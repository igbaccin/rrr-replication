The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy design and historical interpretation. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, have been argued to serve as foundational determinants of economic outcomes. This debate is shaped by competing interpretations of how institutions influence growth, with some emphasizing their capacity to foster inclusive economic opportunities and others highlighting the persistence of extractive arrangements. The discussion is further enriched by the recognition that institutions evolve in response to historical, social, and political forces. This evolution has profound implications for understanding the divergence in economic performance across countries and regions. The interplay between institutions and growth involves power dynamics, cultural legacies, and the distribution of resources in addition to economic efficiency. These considerations underscore the complexity of institutional change and its long-term consequences. Understanding these dynamics is essential for addressing persistent inequalities and designing effective development strategies. This complexity sets the stage for examining how different streams of scholarship approach the question of institutional influence on growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering commitment and economic development. This perspective is reinforced by the observation that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes, with some regions establishing extractive states while others created inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and the rule of law. These institutional differences, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note, shaped long-term economic trajectories by influencing the distribution of power and the incentives for economic activity. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on how political institutions that define property rights and facilitate market exchanges reduce transaction costs, thereby promoting growth. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how institutional development and initial factor endowments led to divergent paths of economic development in the New World, with the United States and Canada achieving sustained growth earlier than other societies. These findings collectively suggest that the quality of institutions is a critical determinant of long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of institutional legacies, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), underscores the importance of historical processes in shaping contemporary economic performance. This emphasis on institutions provides a framework for understanding how historical choices and power dynamics continue to influence economic development today.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective that highlights the interplay between technological progress and institutional change as a driver of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This view is complemented by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, played a crucial role in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, even though these institutions sometimes acted as local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Mokyr (2005, p.3) further elaborates on this by emphasizing the Enlightenment's contribution to the development of useful knowledge and rationality, which underpinned the modern economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These perspectives collectively suggest that technological advancements, supported by institutional frameworks, have been pivotal in shaping economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) add to this discussion by pointing out that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, indicating that structural factors, including institutional and technological developments, were at play long before the Industrial Revolution (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.21). This synthesis underscores the importance of both technological innovation and institutional adjustments in driving long-term economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of how these factors interact with other determinants of economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) extend this discussion by showing how historical events, such as the slave trade, have left enduring legacies that continue to influence economic outcomes in Africa through the persistence of mistrust. This perspective is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the extensive duration of Africa's slave trades and their significant role in influencing subsequent economic development. This finding underscores the enduring impact of historical legacies on social norms and trust, which in turn affect economic performance. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further complicate this narrative by showing how colonial institutions, such as customary law, reinforced patriarchal structures that marginalized women, thereby shaping economic outcomes in ways that extend beyond the immediate effects of the slave trade. These insights reveal the complex interplay between historical events and institutional frameworks in shaping economic development in Africa, suggesting that the legacy of the past continues to influence contemporary economic conditions. This understanding of historical legacies provides a foundation for examining how other factors, such as technological innovation, interact with institutional and historical determinants in shaping long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) highlights how distributional conflicts better explain the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, a perspective that underscores the role of power dynamics in shaping institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This insight aligns with broader findings that historical power dynamics and conflicts significantly influence institutional development, which in turn has implications for long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.30; Stoler 1989, p.6). For instance, Ogilvie's analysis reveals that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial for understanding economic outcomes, as demonstrated by the interconnectedness of the European family system with various market structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These findings collectively suggest that the historical context of power relations and conflicts plays a pivotal role in the evolution of institutions, thereby influencing long-term economic trajectories. This understanding of institutional development through the lens of power dynamics provides a foundation for examining how other factors, such as cultural beliefs and values, interact with these historical determinants in shaping economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.2) challenges the assumption that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a uniformly negative impact on the development of formal education, arguing instead that its influence was more complex and context-dependent (Frankema 2012, p.2). This perspective is reinforced by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who show that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and increased over time, countering the narrative that structural impediments caused persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These findings highlight the importance of re-evaluating traditional economic histories of Africa, which have often overstated the continent's underdevelopment and underplayed its internal dynamics. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) contribute to this reassessment by demonstrating that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the view of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Similarly, Simson (2019, p.11) argues that East African post-independence budget policies were not uniquely clientelist, showing that public spending was shaped by broader economic and political conditions rather than solely by patronage (Simson 2019, p.11). These studies collectively suggest that African economic history is more nuanced than previously assumed, with pre-colonial and colonial periods featuring significant economic activity and development that has been overlooked or misinterpreted. This re-evaluation of historical economic performance provides a more accurate foundation for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-term growth trajectories.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, with significant consequences for economic development. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that historical events such as the slave trade have had lasting effects on trust and economic performance in Africa. These studies emphasize that institutions are shaped by historical processes and power dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further illustrate how institutional frameworks, such as customary law, influenced economic opportunities for women in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, thereby affecting broader economic trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) adds that understanding economic institutions requires examining their interdependencies within a wider institutional framework, rather than in isolation. This approach underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic outcomes. The role of institutions in driving long-run growth is thus a shared finding across these studies, though the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that extractive institutions, even if they do not encourage economic development, are often the result of politically powerful groups seeking to maximize their own rents. In contrast, Ogilvie (2007, p.30) suggests that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, are key to explaining the formation of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This divergence in explanations is settled by the empirical comparison of institutional changes in former European colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), which highlights how colonialism directly influenced the development of institutions and, consequently, long-run economic growth.
