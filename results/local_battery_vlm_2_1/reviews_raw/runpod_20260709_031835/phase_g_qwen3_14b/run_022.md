The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions are widely regarded as crucial determinants of economic outcomes, yet their precise mechanisms and scope of influence remain contested. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on growth, while others highlight the complex interplay between institutions, historical legacies, and other factors. The debate is not merely academic; it shapes how institutions are understood as either enablers or constraints of development. This review explores how different streams of scholarship address the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, focusing on the ways in which institutions are theorized, measured, and contextualized. The discussion begins by examining the foundational argument that institutions are the primary drivers of economic outcomes, as advanced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who argue that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic performance in former colonies. This perspective is contrasted with other streams that explore the contingent and multifaceted nature of institutional effects, such as the role of historical and cultural factors in shaping institutional development. These debates highlight the complexity of understanding institutions as a causal force in economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by historical and comparative analyses of different regions and periods. This perspective is grounded in the observation that institutional frameworks, whether extractive or inclusive, shape the trajectory of economic development by influencing the security of property rights, the rule of law, and the capacity for investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, aligning with the interests of politically influential groups and potentially hindering long-run growth. This insight underscores the importance of understanding the historical and political forces that shape institutional development. North (1989, p.6) further emphasizes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges, which in turn supports economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contribute to this discussion by highlighting the role of institutions in shaping divergent paths of economic development in the New World, linking institutional arrangements to differences in wealth, human capital, and political power. These analyses collectively illustrate that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that influence the conditions under which economic growth can occur. This understanding sets the stage for examining how institutions interact with other factors in shaping economic development.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions that guarantee fair execution of contracts shape long-term economic performance, as illustrated by the role of guilds in western Europe, which helped manage the transfer of skills and maintain economic stability (van Zanden 2009, p.19); this perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments in enabling sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets further notes that technological advancements are closely tied to institutional changes, as they require the necessary adjustments for their successful implementation (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The historical analysis by van Zanden (1999, p.18) reveals that economic development in Europe did not always correlate with improvements in the standard of living, suggesting that the impact of institutions on growth may be complex and context-dependent (van Zanden 1999, p.18). This nuanced view is reinforced by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who highlights the role of intellectual and institutional frameworks in fostering modern economic growth through the promotion of useful knowledge and enforceable contracts (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These findings collectively indicate that institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic performance, but their effects are mediated by broader historical and contextual factors. This understanding provides a foundation for examining how institutions interact with other elements of economic development over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical events, particularly the slave trade, have left enduring legacies that significantly shape long-term economic outcomes in Africa, often through mechanisms like mistrust and institutional legacies. This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial rule entrenched patriarchal structures and customary laws that marginalized women, thereby influencing economic development through gendered institutional frameworks. The persistent culture of mistrust, as identified by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), is further linked to the long-term adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development, as demonstrated by Nunn (2008, p.2), who notes that the slave trades had an adverse effect on long-term economic development. These findings underscore the complex interplay between historical events and institutional legacies in shaping economic trajectories in Africa. Additionally, Frankema (2012, p.2) contributes to this understanding by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education, suggesting that colonial legacies are not uniformly benign. This multifaceted analysis reveals how historical legacies, including mistrust and gendered institutions, continue to influence economic outcomes in Africa, setting the stage for further exploration of how these legacies interact with other factors in economic development.

Stoler (1989, p.6) highlights how colonialism simultaneously constructs the colonizer and the colonized, revealing the complex interplay of class-based logics and racial ideologies that underpin colonial authority and relations of power (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). This perspective underscores the necessity of examining both the internal dynamics within colonial societies and the broader institutional frameworks that shape economic and social structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) similarly argues that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by economic efficiency, emphasizing the interdependence of various institutional systems such as family structures, credit markets, and labor markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These interdependencies indicate that institutions are embedded within broader frameworks and do not operate independently when influencing economic outcomes. For instance, the community-based welfare systems in pre-industrial England, as noted by Solar, were interdependent with labor and land markets, contributing to economic growth before industrialization. This interconnectedness of institutions highlights the need for a more holistic understanding of how historical and institutional legacies shape economic and social structures, both in Europe and beyond. Such insights provide a foundation for exploring how these legacies interact with other factors in economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the assumption that Sub-Saharan Africa was historically underdeveloped, supporting Hopkins (2009, p.18)’s argument that reassessing African economic history can inform more effective policy and institutional reforms. This stream highlights the importance of reinterpreting historical narratives to better understand the mechanisms that drive long-run economic growth. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) show that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, challenging the notion that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings suggest that economic performance in Africa has been more dynamic than traditionally believed, with historical legacies playing a complex role in shaping outcomes. Simson (2019, p.11) further contributes by illustrating that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not uniquely clientelist, indicating that institutional frameworks were more nuanced than previously assumed. This nuanced understanding of historical economic trajectories can guide contemporary policy-making, emphasizing the need to consider diverse factors beyond simplistic institutional explanations. Such insights can help shape more inclusive and effective growth strategies for the region.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the analysis of historical and contemporary institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how European colonization strategies established divergent institutional systems, with some regions developing inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and the rule of law, while others were characterized by extractive states that prioritized resource extraction over long-term growth. This divergence underscores the importance of institutional quality in determining economic outcomes. Similarly, the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) illustrates how historical events, such as the slave trade, have left lasting cultural imprints, including a persistent culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic development. These findings emphasize that institutions are not only shaped by immediate economic interests but also by deep historical legacies that influence trust and cooperation. The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the insights from Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who show how colonial policies, such as the codification of customary law, systematically marginalized women from economic opportunities, thereby affecting overall growth trajectories. This highlights the need to consider the intersection of gender and institutional design in economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contributes by emphasizing the interdependence of various institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that effective economic growth requires the alignment of multiple institutional components. This synthesis reveals a shared understanding of the critical role of institutions in economic growth, yet it also highlights the ongoing debate about the specific mechanisms through which historical and contemporary institutions influence economic outcomes. The empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across regions, as seen in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), provides a concrete basis for evaluating the effectiveness of different institutional models in promoting long-term growth.
