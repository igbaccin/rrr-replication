The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical understanding. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the primary determinant of economic outcomes, while others highlight the complexity of institutional effects and the influence of other factors. A key debate revolves around whether institutions operate as independent causal forces or are shaped by deeper historical, cultural, and economic forces. This literature review explores these streams of scholarship, examining how institutions are conceptualized, their mechanisms of influence, and the conditions under which they drive growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, are the primary determinants of income per capita, as evidenced by the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions in former colonies. Other scholars highlight the interconnectedness of institutions within larger systems, indicating that institutional success relies on their compatibility with other social and economic elements. This review highlights the diversity of perspectives on the role of institutions, the mechanisms through which they influence growth, and the conditions under which they are effective. It begins by examining the argument that institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, as advanced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and supported by their empirical findings. Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering economic commitment and growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective is grounded in the mechanism of institutional design, which shapes the incentives for investment, rule of law, and property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, reflecting the influence of politically powerful groups that prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. This insight highlights how the historical context of colonialism and the power dynamics of ruling elites can shape institutional trajectories. North (1989, p.6) similarly underscores the importance of political institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which in turn support economic development. These findings collectively suggest that the quality of institutions, as defined by their ability to enforce property rights and promote inclusive governance, is a critical factor in determining long-run economic outcomes. The implications of these insights extend to understanding how institutional reforms can be designed to align with the interests of broad societal groups, rather than being constrained by the preferences of a narrow elite. This focus on institutional quality provides a foundation for examining how different types of institutions interact with other factors such as geography and factor endowments in shaping economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies, such as the slave trade, have long-term economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining economic growth, a perspective supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who highlight the persistent impact of historical events on contemporary economic conditions. The paper by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrates that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development, and this mechanism is reinforced by the findings of Nunn (2008, p.2), who shows that the slave trades had an adverse effect on long-term economic development. This historical legacy is further contextualized by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who discuss how colonial rule entrenched patriarchal structures that marginalized women and constrained their economic contributions. These findings suggest that historical events, such as the slave trade and colonial policies, have shaped the economic landscape of Africa in ways that are as significant as institutional frameworks. The implications of these historical legacies extend to understanding how trust and social cohesion, shaped by past events, influence current economic outcomes. This historical perspective provides a foundation for examining how past events interact with contemporary institutional reforms in shaping economic development.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that long-term economic performance is shaped by historical trends, technological change, and ideological shifts, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors. This perspective emphasizes the role of technological innovation and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it as key drivers of sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that mass-uses of technical innovations provide a positive feedback loop, enabling the development of new efficient tools for scientific use and generating new data on natural processes under economic modification. Similarly, van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that institutions such as guilds in western Europe played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, though their effects were complex and sometimes contradictory. These findings suggest that while institutions may influence economic performance, their impact is mediated by broader historical and technological trends. This historical perspective provides a foundation for examining how technological and ideological shifts interact with institutional frameworks in shaping economic development.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism creates both the colonizer and the colonized, necessitating a reexamination of European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority, a perspective supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who highlights the interdependencies among economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. The relationship between institutional systems and colonial history shows how power relations and social hierarchies were imposed on colonized areas and also maintained within the colonizing societies. Similarly, Ogilvie (2007, p.30) emphasizes that the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective welfare systems, contributing to early industrialization. These interconnected institutional frameworks shaped economic and social structures in both Europe and Africa, leaving enduring legacies that continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The analysis of these legacies underscores the complex and reciprocal nature of colonial relationships, demonstrating how institutional and colonial legacies are deeply embedded in the historical development of both regions. This understanding provides a critical foundation for examining how these legacies continue to shape economic and social structures in the modern era.

Reassessing historical economic narratives can help inform contemporary policy and development, which in turn may influence long-run economic growth, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.2). This perspective emphasizes the importance of understanding historical contexts to design more effective institutions and policies. Similarly, Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British colonial rule did not necessarily lead to more benign impacts on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, indicating that the legacy of colonialism is complex and multifaceted. These insights highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of historical processes when developing policies aimed at fostering long-term economic growth. Furthermore, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the assumption that economic growth in Africa was solely a result of European colonial interventions, pointing to evidence of higher per capita incomes before the 1980s. This suggests that historical narratives can be revised to reflect a more accurate picture of economic development, which can inform contemporary policy-making. Such reassessments can lead to more effective strategies for institutional reform and economic development, contributing to long-run growth.

Institutional frameworks play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as highlighted by multiple studies that emphasize the enduring impact of historical institutional arrangements on contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) state that institutions, more than geography or other influences, are the main factor influencing income per capita, pointing out that European colonial approaches resulted in the creation of either extractive or inclusive institutions in various regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes caused by colonialism, which often favored extractive structures over those conducive to long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions is further complicated by the interdependencies among different institutional frameworks, as Ogilvie (2007, p.30) highlights the interconnected nature of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that isolated analysis of individual institutions may not fully explain economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Meanwhile, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate how historical events, such as the slave trade, have left lasting cultural imprints that affect trust and, consequently, economic development, showing that institutions are not only shaped by historical policies but also by deep-seated social norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

A shared finding across these studies is that institutions significantly influence economic development, whether through direct policy mechanisms or through cultural legacies. A clear disagreement persists concerning the degree to which institutional changes are influenced by external pressures, such as colonial actions, compared to internal elements, such as cultural norms and historical influences. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize the role of European colonization in shaping institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), whereas Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) highlight the persistent effects of historical events like the slave trade on trust and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of institutional changes over time, as seen in the analysis of urbanization and population density in relation to subsequent institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36).
