The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions are often positioned as the primary causal force behind economic outcomes, but their influence is not universally accepted or clearly delineated. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality, while others highlight the complex interplay between institutions, culture, and historical legacies. The debate is not merely academic; it shapes how institutions are understood as either foundational or contingent in economic development. This review explores key streams of scholarship that address the causal role of institutions in long-run growth, focusing on their design, historical evolution, and interaction with other factors. The first stream examines the argument that institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes, as illustrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influences long-term economic performance. Their analysis contrasts extractive institutions, which prioritize the interests of a small elite, with inclusive ones, which promote broad-based economic participation. This distinction is supported by empirical evidence showing that countries with more inclusive institutions tend to have higher levels of economic development. The second stream explores the historical roots of institutions, particularly in the context of colonial rule and its lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) suggest that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to institutional changes caused by colonialism and not geographic determinism. They highlight how the disease environment influenced the type of institutions that emerged, with more extractive institutions prevailing in areas where European settlers faced greater health risks. This perspective is complemented by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who investigate how the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which in turn negatively affects economic development. Their findings underscore the importance of trust as a component of institutional quality, suggesting that the long-term effects of historical events can shape the institutional environment. The third stream considers the role of gender and customary law in shaping economic institutions, particularly in Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging neglectful historical narratives. They highlight how patriarchal alliances and customary law were used to codify the subordinate status of women, which in turn affected their access to land and economic opportunities. This perspective adds a gender dimension to the discussion of institutions, emphasizing the need to consider the historical and social contexts in which institutions operate. The fourth stream explores the interdependencies among institutions and their broader economic and social frameworks. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) argues that distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe compared to economic efficiency. She highlights the interdependence of various institutions, such as the family system, labor markets, and credit markets, in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions can be studied in isolation, suggesting instead that they function within complex institutional frameworks. Finally, the fifth stream examines the impact of colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British colonial rule was not more benign in its impact on the origins of formal education, challenging the assumption that colonialism was a neutral or positive force in this regard. His analysis considers the role of malaria ecology and the historical incidence of malaria in shaping educational development, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and other factors. These streams collectively illustrate the multifaceted role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of their historical evolution and broader social and economic contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that drive development, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules in fostering economic commitment and growth. This perspective is grounded in the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of very different institutional frameworks in former colonies, with some promoting the rule of law and investment while others favored extractive states that prioritized resource extraction over development. These differing institutional paths have had lasting effects on economic outcomes, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who note that institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. This insight underscores the complexity of institutional change as it is often driven by the interests of those in power and not by a commitment to broad-based economic development. These findings collectively highlight the central role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories and their enduring impact on long-run growth.

Expanding on this, Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism shaped the identities and power dynamics of both colonizers and colonized, a perspective reinforced by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights how British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa influenced the origins of formal education. These insights reveal that colonialism created complex institutional legacies that persisted beyond the colonial period, influencing economic structures and policies. Similarly, Simson (2019, p.11) discusses how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were influenced by colonial legacies, with shifts in expenditure priorities reflecting both historical and contemporary economic conditions. These findings illustrate that colonialism did not merely impose institutions but also embedded mechanisms that continued to affect economic development long after independence. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) further support this by emphasizing the importance of collaborative research in understanding historical economic data, which is essential for tracing the long-term effects of colonial policies. The interplay between colonial institutions and post-colonial economic policies thus reveals a nuanced legacy that continues to shape economic growth in the region.

This historical context is further illuminated by Van Zanden (2009, p.19) arguing that the skill premium in western Europe fell in the late medieval period and remained low compared to other regions predicting long-term economic performance a claim supported by the reassessment of historical data that challenges conventional narratives (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This reassessment highlights the role of institutions such as guilds, which, despite criticisms for monopolizing skills and restricting entry, may have had beneﬁcial effects on the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Similarly, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the view of persistent underdevelopment in Sub-Saharan Africa by showing that per capita incomes were higher than previously thought before the 1980s, suggesting that economic performance is more complex than previously assumed (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings align with the broader reassessment of historical economic performance, which emphasizes the importance of revisiting historical data to understand long-term economic trends (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further support this by demonstrating that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, challenging the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This stream collectively underscores the need to reassess historical economic performance by challenging conventional narratives and revisiting historical data, which reveals a more nuanced understanding of long-term economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4).

This historical reassessment is exemplified by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrating that historical legacies such as the slave trade have had long-lasting effects on economic outcomes in Africa, shaping development trajectories through persistent cultural norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Their analysis reveals that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust, which negatively affects economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation. This finding is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights how the extensive slave trades in Africa over nearly 500 years significantly influenced subsequent economic development by fostering ethnic fractionalization, which in turn hampers long-term growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). Peters (2004, p.5) adds that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reflects exclusion and class formation, revealing how historical systems of land ownership and control continue to influence contemporary economic structures (Peters 2004, p.5). These studies collectively underscore the enduring impact of historical legacies on economic development, illustrating how past events shape present and future economic trajectories through persistent social and institutional norms. This understanding of historical legacies provides a crucial foundation for examining how institutions interact with these legacies to influence long-term economic growth.

This perspective is further developed by Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizing that sustained economic growth hinges on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective that aligns with Ogilvie’s (2007, p.30) argument that distributional conflicts shape economic institutions more than economic efficiency does. Both scholars highlight the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks, though they differ in their emphasis on the drivers of these changes. Kuznets underscores the role of technological innovation in generating economic surplus, which in turn fuels further research and development, while Ogilvie points to the influence of distributional conflicts in shaping the institutional arrangements that support or hinder economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.3) complements these views by arguing that the Enlightenment’s emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality laid the intellectual foundation for modern economic growth, suggesting that ideological shifts can be a catalyst for institutional change. This interplay between technology, ideology, and institutions reveals a complex mechanism through which economic growth is sustained. Kuznets’ focus on the feedback loops between technological innovation and institutional adaptation highlights the necessity of continuous adjustment in response to changing economic conditions. These perspectives collectively illustrate that economic growth is not solely the result of institutional design but is also shaped by technological progress and ideological transformations that influence how institutions evolve. This understanding of the multifaceted drivers of growth provides a foundation for examining the ways in which institutions interact with these broader forces to shape long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central theme in contemporary economic history, with multiple studies emphasizing their foundational influence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of income per capita compared to geography or other factors as illustrated by the contrasting outcomes of European colonization strategies in different regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who highlight how institutional changes caused by colonialism explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings underscore the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development. The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) suggests that distributional conflicts better explain the formation of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe compared to economic efficiency pointing to the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This contrasts with the view that institutions are primarily shaped by the need to encourage investment and enforce the rule of law, as seen in the cases of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence on the impact of colonial legal systems on land access and gender roles, as documented by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), provides a specific case that highlights how institutional structures can shape economic outcomes through complex social dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).
