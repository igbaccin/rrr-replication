The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with profound implications for understanding historical trajectories and policy design. Institutions shape the incentives and constraints that guide economic activity, influencing investment, innovation, and the distribution of resources. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more significant role. This review explores how different streams of scholarship address these questions, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. One stream emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic development, arguing that inclusive institutions foster sustained growth by promoting rule of law, property rights, and effective governance. Another stream challenges this view, suggesting that alternative factors, such as cultural norms, historical legacies, or distributional conflicts, may explain economic outcomes more effectively. These perspectives highlight the complexity of institutional effects and the need for nuanced analysis. The discussion begins with an examination of how institutions are framed as the primary drivers of economic growth, drawing on empirical evidence from comparative studies of different regions and historical periods. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those shaped by colonialism and historical events, are the primary determinant of long-term economic development, a claim supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who emphasize the role of institutional development and initial factor endowments in shaping divergent paths of economic growth in the New World. This perspective is further reinforced by the observation that colonial powers established very different institutional frameworks across their colonies, with some fostering the rule of law and encouraging investment, while others created extractive states designed to benefit the metropole at the expense of local development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutional differences have had lasting impacts, noting that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were frequently structured to benefit European colonists and to prioritize short-term gains over long-term growth. The impact of such institutions is not limited to the colonial period; it continues to shape economic outcomes today, as evidenced by the persistent disparities in economic development between regions that inherited inclusive institutions and those that inherited extractive ones. These findings highlight the enduring influence of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic performance, setting the stage for further exploration of how specific historical events, such as the slave trade, have shaped institutional quality and, by extension, economic development.

With the historical legacy of institutions established, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that constitutional rules and institutions are essential for fostering commitment and economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England, where political stability and enforceable rules enabled sustained economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that economic growth relies on both technological progress and the institutional and ideological changes that facilitate it, underscoring the relationship between innovation and governance. This perspective is further reinforced by North (1989, p.6), who explains that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and increasing the proportion of economic activity within the broader economy. Mokyr (2005, p.3) complements these views by noting that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality played a pivotal role in shaping the conditions for modern economic growth, as the spread of scientific ideas and the establishment of trust and enforceable contracts became critical to economic progress. These findings collectively illustrate that while institutions are vital, their role is intertwined with other factors such as technological change and ideological shifts, which together create the conditions for sustained economic development. This multifaceted understanding of growth sets the stage for examining how these factors interact in different historical contexts.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that historical economic performance and income levels are shaped by factors such as real wages and urban development, rather than institutions alone, a perspective supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who highlight the role of urbanization in economic growth. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) demonstrate that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, challenging the assumption that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings suggest that economic development is not solely determined by institutional arrangements but is also influenced by wage dynamics and urbanization patterns. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further support this by showing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, indicating that economic performance can be more complex and multifaceted than previously assumed. These insights collectively highlight that while institutions are important, they do not operate in isolation, and other factors such as wage levels and urban development play a significant role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Peters (2004, p.5) argues that distributional conflicts and post-colonial institutional legacies are key drivers of long-run economic growth in Africa, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights the complex interplay between colonial rule and the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. These sources jointly emphasize that economic development in Africa is shaped by historical power dynamics and institutional frameworks that emerged during and after colonial rule. These findings suggest that institutions in Africa are deeply embedded in historical contexts that shaped economic trajectories. Simson (2019, p.11) further contributes by illustrating how post-independence budget policies in East Africa reflected ongoing distributional conflicts, with military spending and debt burdens influencing economic outcomes. These insights show that long-run growth in Africa is shaped by more than just the quality of institutions; historical conflicts and the continuation of exclusionary practices also play a significant role. Understanding these dynamics is essential for grasping the complex relationship between institutions and economic development in the region.

This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight the significant but often overlooked contributions of African women to economic development in pre-colonial and colonial periods, revealing how historical narratives can reshape our understanding of economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These insights suggest that the legacy of colonial rule, including the codification of customary law and the marginalization of women, has had lasting effects on economic structures and institutions in Africa. Stoler (1989, p.6) further underscores the complexity of colonialism by illustrating how it created both the colonizer and the colonized, necessitating a reevaluation of European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority (Stoler 1989, p.6). This multifaceted view of colonial legacies indicates that historical narratives are essential for comprehending the evolution of institutions and their impact on economic growth. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional reforms thus reveals a nuanced picture of Africa's economic trajectory, emphasizing the need to consider both historical and institutional factors in explaining long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by multiple studies emphasizing their influence over geographic or other factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how differing colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, with the latter fostering economic development in regions such as the United States and Australia. This aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), which argues that institutional changes caused by colonialism explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, underscoring the long-term impact of institutional structures on economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the quality of institutions in Africa is influenced by factors beyond ethnic fragmentation, including historical and policy-related considerations, and argues that institutional reforms are essential for achieving economic progress. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further illustrate the importance of trust and its origins in historical events, such as the slave trade, which have had lasting effects on economic development. These findings collectively demonstrate that institutions are central to economic growth, yet the specific mechanisms through which they operate remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) focus on the role of institutional design in promoting or hindering growth, while Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks, suggesting that economic outcomes are shaped by complex institutional systems rather than isolated elements. The historical evidence on institutional legacies and their measurable impact on economic performance, as seen in the comparative analysis of former colonies, provides a concrete basis for understanding the role of institutions in long-run growth.
