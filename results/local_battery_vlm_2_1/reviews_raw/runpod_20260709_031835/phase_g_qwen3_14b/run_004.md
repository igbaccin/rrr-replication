The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy design and historical interpretation. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and social interactions, are argued to shape economic outcomes by influencing incentives, coordination, and the distribution of power. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more substantial role. Some scholars emphasize the causal power of institutions, particularly those that promote the rule of law, secure property rights, and enable efficient governance, while others highlight the limitations of institutional explanations or propose alternative causal pathways. This review synthesizes key streams of scholarship to explore how institutions interact with other forces in shaping economic development. The first stream examines the role of institutional quality in fostering growth, drawing on comparative historical analyses of colonial institutions. The second stream engages with critiques that challenge the primacy of institutions, emphasizing the importance of historical and cultural contexts. The third stream explores the interdependencies among institutions and other social and economic factors, suggesting that institutional effects are often mediated by broader frameworks. Each stream contributes distinct insights into the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic outcomes, revealing the complexity of their role in development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Building on the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), the role of institutional quality in shaping long-term economic trajectories becomes a central focus for further analysis. This claim is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate how the slave trade in Africa created persistent cultural mistrust, which in turn hindered economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation. The mechanism through which institutions shape economic outcomes is further elaborated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who explain that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, reflecting the interests of politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-term growth. This insight is reinforced by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who highlight how initial factor endowments and institutional development shaped divergent economic paths in the New World, emphasizing the role of property rights and political power structures in shaping growth trajectories. These findings collectively underscore the enduring influence of institutions on economic development, revealing how historical legacies continue to shape contemporary economic conditions. This understanding of institutional influence provides a foundation for examining how specific institutional features, such as the rule of law or property rights, interact with other factors to drive long-term economic outcomes.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism shaped institutional and policy frameworks, a claim supported by the broader implications of colonial power structures on long-term development (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). Colonial rule reconfigured internal class dynamics and social hierarchies within both the colonizing and colonized societies, creating institutional legacies that continued beyond the formal end of colonialism. These institutional frameworks influenced the distribution of power, the legitimacy of governance, and the capacity of states to enact policies that promoted economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of institutions in post-colonial states is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but is also shaped by historical processes, including the legacies of colonialism (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the mechanisms through which colonialism influenced institutions were complex and multifaceted, involving both the imposition of external structures and the transformation of internal social relations. The long-term implications of these institutional legacies are evident in the continued challenges faced by many post-colonial states in building effective and equitable governance systems. These findings underscore the need to consider colonial history as a critical factor in understanding contemporary institutional arrangements and their impact on economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth hinges not only on technological advancement but also on the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective aligns with North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who highlight the importance of political institutions in fostering economic growth by ensuring commitment to rules and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The interplay between technological progress and institutional change is further underscored by North (1989, p.6), who argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). These institutional adjustments are essential for the long-term success of economic systems, as demonstrated by the case of France and England in the seventeenth century, where England's institutional stability contributed to its economic superiority (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Mokyr extends this argument by linking the rise of modern economic growth to the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality, which provided the ideological foundation for sustained innovation and growth (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These insights collectively suggest that the evolution of institutions and ideologies is a critical component of economic development, shaping the conditions under which technological progress can be effectively harnessed. This understanding of institutional and ideological dynamics sets the stage for examining how these factors interact with other elements of economic growth.

Peters (2004, p.5) argues that land competition and customary law in sub-Saharan Africa play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, a perspective supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through the codification of customary law, marginalizing women's access to land and economic opportunities. This dynamic is further illustrated by the interplay between institutional frameworks and social dynamics, as seen in the historical context of land allocation and its impact on agricultural productivity. The persistence of these structures has implications for contemporary economic development, as evidenced by the long-term effects of land policies on social stratification and economic exclusion. Simson (2019, p.11) adds to this discussion by examining post-independence budget policies in East Africa, showing how shifts in expenditure priorities, such as the growth of military spending, reflect broader institutional and social dynamics that influence economic trajectories. These findings collectively suggest that the legacy of land competition and the evolution of customary law are integral to understanding economic outcomes in Africa, offering an alternative lens through which to view the role of institutions in long-run growth. This perspective invites further exploration of how institutional and social factors interact in shaping economic development across different regions.

This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who demonstrate that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and showed upward trends, refuting the notion of persistent poverty caused by structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These findings suggest that historical economic narratives are often oversimplified, with alternative explanations emerging from the interplay between local responses and external influences. Frankema (2012, p.2) further contributes to this revision by examining the impact of British colonial rule on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, revealing that colonial policies did not necessarily lead to more benign educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.2). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) adds to this stream by highlighting the role of formal institutions, such as guilds, in shaping economic development in Western Europe, showing how they influenced both the transfer of skills and the regulation of market forces (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These diverse contributions collectively underscore the importance of revising historical economic narratives to account for the complexity of institutional and social dynamics that have shaped long-run growth. This approach invites a more nuanced understanding of economic development that integrates both local agency and global influences.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonial times significantly influenced the economic development of former colonies. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.25), who highlights the dynamic and partly endogenous nature of institutional development in response to environmental and economic conditions. These contributions emphasize that institutions are not static but evolve in response to various factors, influencing economic outcomes over time. The debate over the role of institutions in economic development is further enriched by the insights of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that trust, shaped by historical events such as the slave trade, has a lasting impact on economic performance. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical roots of institutions in explaining economic growth. The specific historical evidence of institutional changes in former colonies, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), provides a clear empirical basis for evaluating the long-term effects of institutions on economic development. These findings collectively highlight the complex interplay between institutions, history, and economic growth, offering a nuanced understanding of the forces that drive long-run development.
