The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape human interactions, are argued to influence economic outcomes by affecting incentives for investment, innovation, and cooperation. A key debate centers on whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play a more fundamental role. Some scholars emphasize the causal power of institutions, particularly those that promote the rule of law, property rights, and political accountability, while others highlight the limitations of this perspective, suggesting that institutions are shaped by broader historical and social forces. This review explores these streams of scholarship, examining how different approaches conceptualize the relationship between institutions and economic growth. It begins by considering the argument that institutions are the primary driver of long-run growth, drawing on evidence from studies that compare historical and contemporary contexts. This stream is followed by an analysis of competing perspectives that challenge the primacy of institutions by emphasizing the role of other variables. Finally, it considers how the scope and conditions under which institutions influence growth are contested, with some scholars identifying specific contexts in which institutional effects are more or less pronounced. These streams collectively illustrate the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic development, highlighting both the explanatory power of institutional analysis and its limitations. This discussion sets the stage for a deeper exploration of the mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering economic commitment and growth. This perspective is further reinforced by the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of very different institutional frameworks, with some regions developing extractive states while others created institutions that encouraged investment and the rule of law. The long-term consequences of these institutional choices are evident in the divergent paths of economic development across former colonies, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who note that institutions and initial factor endowments shaped different trajectories of growth. North (1989, p.6) expands on this by explaining how the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic exchanges and growth. These insights collectively underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes, with the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth being deeply rooted in the historical and political contexts of different societies. This emphasis on institutional determinants sets the stage for examining how specific institutional features, such as the rule of law and property rights, contribute to sustained economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) revise historical narratives of African economic development by highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging the neglectful historical narratives that have dominated the field. Their analysis reveals that colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through customary law, relegating women to marginal lands for food farming while men controlled access to land for cash crops, a dynamic that fueled economic transformations such as the cocoa boom in the Gold Coast. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, countering the view of persistent underdevelopment. Their work indicates that economic growth in West Africa was influenced by internal factors such as agricultural innovations and trade networks in addition to European colonial interventions, as early economic histories have suggested. These findings collectively suggest that African economies were more dynamic and resilient than conventional narratives have acknowledged, with their growth trajectories shaped by a complex interplay of local and external forces. This re-evaluation of historical economic performance sets the stage for understanding how institutional frameworks interacted with these internal dynamics to influence long-term economic outcomes.

Stoler (1989, p.6) highlights the complex and multifaceted legacies of colonialism, emphasizing the need for nuanced historical analysis to understand its long-term effects, a perspective shared by Austin (2008, p.25) and Frankema (2012, p.2). Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that the colonial experience involved a process that reshaped the structures and class dynamics within the colonizing societies themselves in addition to being a process of domination over the colonized; Austin (2008, p.25) further develops this point by critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying the relationship between colonialism and economic development. These insights collectively underscore the importance of considering the internal dynamics of colonial rule, including the class-based logic and political tensions within the colonial state, as critical factors shaping economic trajectories. Such considerations challenge simplistic narratives of institutional determinism and highlight the need for a more integrated analysis of how colonial legacies interact with local conditions to influence long-term economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical events such as the slave trade have shaped long-term economic outcomes in Africa, with the slave trade contributing to a persistent culture of mistrust that negatively affects economic development. This perspective is supported by Peters (2004, p.5), who highlights how land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals exclusion and class formation, further complicating economic trajectories. The mechanisms through which these historical events influence contemporary outcomes are multifaceted, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) showing that both internal and external channels are important in explaining the effects of the slave trade on trust, though the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external one. These findings are reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.2), who emphasizes the long duration of the African slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, and their potential role in shaping subsequent economic development. Moreover, Nunn (2008, p.4) notes the connection between the slave trades and ethnic fractionalization, which in turn has implications for long-term economic growth. The evidence suggests that historical legacies such as the slave trade and land competition have enduring economic consequences, influencing social trust, ethnic divisions, and the structures of land ownership. These insights provide a deeper understanding of how historical events continue to shape economic outcomes in Africa, setting the stage for further exploration of the interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic conditions.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that long-term economic performance is shaped by technological change, skill premiums, and ideological shifts, a perspective supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the relationship between the skill premium and economic growth in Western Europe. This stream emphasizes that technological innovation and the diffusion of knowledge are central to long-term economic development, with the Enlightenment's focus on useful knowledge and rationality playing a pivotal role in fostering sustained growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that economic growth is underpinned by technological advancements and the institutional and ideological adjustments they necessitate, suggesting that the interplay between these factors is essential for sustained development. This perspective challenges the institutional-centric view by demonstrating that long-term economic performance is also influenced by the evolution of skill demands and the intellectual environment that supports innovation. These insights underscore the importance of technological and ideological factors in shaping economic trajectories, offering a broader understanding of the forces that drive long-term growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) challenges the traditional emphasis on economic efficiency by proposing that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.30). This perspective aligns with alternative explanations that highlight the role of power dynamics and inequality in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Stern (2004, p.1) complements this view by critiquing the environmental Kuznets curve, suggesting that environmental models require more nuanced approaches to understand their relationship with economic development (Stern 2004, p.1). Both scholars emphasize that economic institutions are deeply influenced by social and environmental contexts in addition to being the result of rational, efficiency-driven choices. Ogilvie’s analysis of interdependent institutional frameworks in Europe underscores the complexity of institutional systems, where family structures, credit markets, and labor arrangements interact in ways that influence economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Stern’s critique of the EKC highlights the limitations of simplistic environmental models and calls for a more integrated understanding of economic and environmental change. These findings suggest that economic institutions emerge from a complex interplay of distributional conflicts and environmental pressures in addition to arising from a singular focus on efficiency or growth. This broader framework invites further exploration of how these factors interact in shaping economic development across different historical and geographical contexts.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how differing colonial strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, which in turn determined economic outcomes across former colonies. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who emphasize the persistent impact of historical events, such as the slave trade, on economic development through their influence on trust and institutional quality. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in determining economic trajectories over time. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the overemphasis on institutional determinism by arguing that historical narratives should account for the dynamic and endogenous responses of societies to their environments, suggesting that institutions evolve in response to changing conditions. This view is echoed by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who stresses the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks, arguing that economic growth cannot be understood by examining individual institutions in isolation. The empirical evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), which links pre-colonial population density to institutional outcomes, provides a historical comparison that illustrates how institutions can change over time in response to economic and social conditions. These findings suggest that while institutions are crucial for long-run growth, their development is shaped by a complex interplay of historical, social, and environmental factors.
