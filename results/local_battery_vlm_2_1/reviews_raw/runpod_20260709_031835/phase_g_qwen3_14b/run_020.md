The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, cultural norms, or distributional conflicts, play a more prominent role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are the cornerstone of sustained economic growth, while others propose that alternative explanations, such as historical legacies of exploitation or the interplay between cultural values and economic development, offer competing insights. This review explores these perspectives by examining streams of scholarship that highlight the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, as well as those that challenge or refine this causal narrative. The first stream emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, drawing on comparative historical analyses of colonial experiences. The second stream introduces alternative explanations, such as the role of cultural norms and distributional conflicts, in shaping institutional development. The third stream investigates the conditions under which institutional effects on growth are contingent or limited. Each stream contributes to a nuanced understanding of the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering commitment and economic development. This perspective is further reinforced by the analysis of colonial institutions, which show how different institutional frameworks led to divergent economic outcomes in former colonies. For instance, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive states in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast hindered long-term economic development. These findings highlight the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) also contribute to this understanding by showing how institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. Their work underscores the persistence of institutional legacies over time and their influence on economic performance. North (1989, p.6) adds that political and economic institutions significantly influence economic growth, as they shape the rules governing market exchanges and the enforcement of property rights. These insights collectively suggest that institutions are central to explaining long-run economic growth, a theme that continues to be explored in subsequent analyses of institutional determinants.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that institutions guaranteeing the fair execution of contracts, such as guilds in Western Europe, played a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic performance, as demonstrated by the relationship between the skill premium and subsequent economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 1999, p.18). These institutions facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, although their effectiveness was sometimes undermined by their tendency to restrict entry into trades and maintain artificially high pay levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on technological advancement as well as on the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that support it (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.3) further supports this by arguing that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality laid the institutional foundation for modern economic growth, particularly through the establishment of law and order, trust, and enforceable contracts (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These findings collectively suggest that the development and functioning of institutions that ensure fair economic practices are crucial for long-term economic growth. This theme can be further explored in the context of how different regions historically managed to establish such institutional frameworks.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have long-term economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2; Peters 2004, p.5; Frankema 2012, p.2). Their analysis reveals that the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development. This mistrust, they suggest, stems from the historical trauma of the slave trade, which shaped intergroup relations and norms of behavior that continue to influence economic outcomes today. This fractionalization, in turn, has been shown to hinder economic growth by creating environments of uncertainty and insecurity. These findings collectively indicate that historical legacies, such as the slave trade and colonialism, have enduring impacts on economic development that are as significant as, if not more significant than, the role of institutional frameworks. This theme can be further explored in the context of how these legacies interact with contemporary institutional development and economic policy.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) argues that distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than economic efficiency; Austin (2008, p.25) supports this perspective by challenging the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for over-simplifying causation by compressing history. These scholars jointly emphasize that the development of economic institutions is deeply intertwined with the power struggles among different social groups and is not solely driven by the need for efficient resource allocation. For instance, Ogilvie highlights the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, noting that the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. Similarly, Austin critiques the 'simple geography' thesis, advocating instead for a more dynamic, partly endogenous approach that considers how local populations respond to their environments using available technologies. This suggests that institutions are not static but are shaped by ongoing social and economic interactions. The implication of this view is that understanding economic growth requires examining the complex web of distributional conflicts and their resolution within institutional frameworks. This perspective opens the door to further exploration of how such conflicts influence the evolution of institutions in other historical contexts.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that historical narratives in Africa have long underestimated the economic contributions of women, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial periods, and that this oversight has shaped contemporary understandings of economic development. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who challenge the assumption that African economic growth prior to the 1980s was solely a product of European colonial interventions. Instead, they highlight the presence of internal economic dynamism, suggesting that African economies were more resilient and capable of growth before the imposition of colonial rule. These findings are further reinforced by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who demonstrate that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and showed upward trends over time, contradicting the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This cluster of scholarship thus revises the historical economic narrative in Africa by emphasizing the agency of local populations and the presence of internal economic forces that contributed to development. Such a revision complicates the role of external factors in shaping African economic trajectories and underscores the importance of re-evaluating long-standing assumptions about the continent's economic history. These insights provide a more nuanced foundation for understanding the broader mechanisms of long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. Their analysis highlights how extractive institutions, as opposed to inclusive ones, hindered development in regions such as the Congo, while inclusive institutions supported growth in places like the United States. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), who show that the institutional reversal among former colonies can be traced back to the disease environment and the strategies employed by European powers. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges this view by suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis over-simplifies the causal mechanisms at play and by emphasizing the dynamic and endogenous nature of African economic development. This disagreement is further nuanced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who highlight the long-lasting effects of the slave trade on trust and economic development in Africa, showing that institutions are not only shaped by external forces but also by internal cultural dynamics. The historical evidence from the Gold Coast, where women's contributions to cocoa farming were pivotal (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), provides a concrete example of how local agency and institutional frameworks interact to influence economic outcomes.
