The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and policy design. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules and norms that shape human interactions, are argued to be pivotal in determining economic outcomes, particularly in former colonial regions. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography or historical legacies, play a more significant role. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) assert that institutions, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita, emphasizing the impact of colonial strategies on institutional development. Their argument is supported by evidence showing that European colonization led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, with lasting effects on economic performance. This perspective is challenged by Austin (2008, p.25), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis over-simplifies causation by compressing history, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. These competing views highlight the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth and set the stage for further exploration of the mechanisms and conditions under which institutions influence long-term development.

Building on these arguments, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are the primary determinant of economic growth. This perspective is grounded in the observation that colonial powers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, which had lasting effects on economic development. For instance, in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were set up to encourage investment and enforce the rule of law, whereas in the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences have had lasting effects and continue to influence economic outcomes in these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, as they are often shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize their own interests over long-run growth. This suggests that the type of institutions in place can significantly influence the trajectory of economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that institutional development and initial factor endowments have played a crucial role in shaping divergent paths of economic development in the New World. These findings collectively underscore the importance of institutions in driving long-run economic growth and set the stage for examining how specific institutional features interact with other factors to influence development outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge conventional narratives by demonstrating that African women played a significant role in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a contribution historically overlooked. Additionally, the paper highlights how women's marginalization in land access was exacerbated by the central role of men in codifying customary law, yet their labor in food farming and migration for cash crops contributed to economic transformations, such as the Gold Coast becoming a leading cocoa producer (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further contribute to this stream by arguing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the assumption of persistent underdevelopment. They note that early economic histories often attributed growth to European colonial interventions, yet new estimates suggest otherwise (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings collectively indicate that historical economic narratives have been shaped by biases and incomplete data, and that a more nuanced understanding of economic development in Africa requires revisiting long-term trends and recognizing the contributions of previously marginalized groups. This re-evaluation of historical economic performance provides a foundation for understanding how institutional and social structures have influenced development trajectories over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical events such as the slave trade have had lasting effects on long-term economic and social outcomes in Africa, shaping cultural norms that continue to influence development. Their analysis reveals that the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust, which negatively affects economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation. This finding is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the extensive duration of the slave trades in Africa and their role in shaping subsequent economic trajectories. The adverse effects of the slave trade are further compounded by the environment of uncertainty and insecurity it created, which led to increased demand for weapons and reinforced ethnic divisions. These historical legacies are not only economic but also social, as they contribute to high levels of ethnic fractionalization, which are well-documented as impediments to long-term growth. Peters (2004, p.5) adds that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reflects exclusion and class formation, revealing how historical processes continue to shape contemporary social structures. These insights collectively indicate that the legacy of historical events such as the slave trade and colonialism continues to influence economic and social outcomes in Africa, necessitating a deeper understanding of these historical roots to address current developmental challenges.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that it demands, highlighting the interplay between technological progress and institutional change. This perspective is supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who discusses the role of formal institutions such as guilds in shaping economic development in Western Europe, illustrating how institutions can both facilitate and hinder growth depending on their structure and function. The importance of technological innovation is further emphasized by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who attributes modern economic growth to the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality, demonstrating how ideological shifts can drive economic transformation. Stern (2004, p.1) contributes to this discussion by examining the environmental Kuznets curve and its statistical limitations, suggesting that new models may better capture the complex relationship between economic development and environmental factors. These insights collectively indicate that economic growth is shaped by a combination of technological progress, institutional change, and ideological shifts, each playing a distinct yet interconnected role in driving long-term economic development. This multifaceted understanding of growth mechanisms provides a foundation for further exploration of how these factors interact in different historical and geographical contexts.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa provides crucial insights for policy-making and institutional reforms, a perspective supported by the broader implications of historical research on economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Austin 2008, p.25; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This stream emphasizes that understanding the historical evolution of institutions and their interaction with economic forces can inform contemporary efforts to build effective governance structures. These insights suggest that historical research serves a practical purpose, offering valuable guidance for policies that address the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth. The recognition of historical contingencies in institutional development underscores the need for context-sensitive policy approaches, which can enhance the effectiveness of reforms in diverse settings. This understanding of historical processes and their relevance to contemporary policy provides a foundation for further exploration of how historical insights can guide institutional reforms in different economic and social contexts.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. These findings are supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that historical events such as the slave trade have had lasting effects on trust and, consequently, on economic development. The importance of institutions is further reinforced by the insights from Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who emphasize the historical contributions of African women to economic growth, underscoring the need for inclusive institutional frameworks that recognize diverse social actors. Austin (2008, p.25) questions the deterministic perspective on institutions, pointing out that historical processes are more dynamic and internally driven than some institutional theories suggest. This perspective is echoed by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who points out that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily lead to poor institutions, suggesting that other factors such as economic interests and religious affiliations can also influence institutional quality. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is thus settled by examining the historical evolution of institutions, as illustrated by the comparative analysis of different regions and the measurement of institutional quality over time, as seen in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19).
