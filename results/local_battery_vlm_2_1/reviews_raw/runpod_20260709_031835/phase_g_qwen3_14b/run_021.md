The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy interventions. A key intellectual debate centers on whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more decisive role. This question is particularly salient in the context of former colonies, where institutional legacies are often presumed to have enduring effects on economic performance. Scholars have explored how institutions shape economic growth by examining their historical origins, their interactions with other variables, and their influence on trust, property rights, and governance. These inquiries highlight the complex causal mechanisms through which institutions affect long-term development. The interplay between institutional quality and economic outcomes is further complicated by the ways in which historical events, such as colonialism and the slave trade, have shaped institutional frameworks. This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it informs strategies for institutional reform and economic development. Understanding the causal pathways through which institutions influence growth is essential for designing effective interventions that promote sustainable development. This discussion sets the stage for examining how different streams of scholarship have approached the role of institutions in economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Ogilvie (2007, p.30) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, emphasizing how historical and political factors shape institutional frameworks that influence long-term development. These institutional differences, they argue, have had lasting effects on economic outcomes, as evidenced by the persistence of property rights and political constraints in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This interdependence implies that analyzing economic development requires looking beyond individual institutions to understand how they interact within a larger institutional environment. Such insights underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic trajectories, a theme that continues to be explored in subsequent analyses of institutional determinants.

With the role of institutional quality established, Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that institutional adaptation plays a crucial role in shaping economic performance over the long term through technological change and historical trends, a perspective echoed by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes the interplay between advancing technology and the institutional adjustments required to support it. This interplay is evident in the historical evolution of economic systems, where the capacity to supply diverse goods to a population hinges on the alignment between technological progress and the institutional frameworks that enable its application. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how institutions such as guilds in western Europe contributed to the intergenerational transfer of skills, even as they faced criticism for restricting market competition. This dynamic indicates that institutional structures change over time in response to economic demands, which in turn affect long-term growth trajectories. This relationship underscores the importance of institutional flexibility in fostering environments conducive to innovation. The historical evidence from van Zanden (1999, p.18) also illustrates that economic development does not always correlate directly with improvements in living standards, indicating that institutional factors can mediate the effects of growth on societal well-being. These findings collectively suggest that institutional adaptation is a key mechanism through which long-term economic performance is shaped, a theme that continues to inform contemporary analyses of growth determinants.

Highlighting the importance of institutional adaptation, Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that institutional and policy reforms are essential for economic development in Africa, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.11), who highlight the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary governance. This underscores the importance of looking beyond ethnic composition to understand institutional performance. These insights collectively suggest that institutional reforms must be tailored to local contexts, taking into account historical, economic, and social conditions. The implications of these findings extend to policy-making, emphasizing the need for context-specific strategies that address the unique challenges of African economies. This understanding of institutional and policy reforms in Africa provides a foundation for exploring how similar mechanisms operate in other regions, such as Latin America or Southeast Asia.

Expanding the focus beyond Africa, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical legacies, particularly the transatlantic slave trade, have had enduring economic consequences by fostering a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect development outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.2) supports this finding by pointing out that the slave trade, which lasted nearly 500 years, had a significant impact on Africa's economic development trajectory, leaving lasting effects on institutions and social cohesion. The mechanisms through which these legacies operate are multifaceted, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that both internal norms of mistrust and external factors, such as the rule of law, contribute to the economic underperformance of regions historically affected by the slave trade. These insights are further contextualized by Peters (2004, p.5), who argues that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals exclusion and class formation, underscoring how historical processes continue to influence contemporary economic structures. Stoler (1989, p.6) notes that colonialism created complex power dynamics that influenced both the colonizer and the colonized, reinforcing the notion that historical legacies continue to shape the social and economic fabric of societies. These interconnected legacies highlight the importance of understanding how past institutions and events shape present-day economic outcomes, setting the stage for further exploration of how such legacies interact with contemporary policy and reform efforts.

This historical perspective is further nuanced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge prevailing historical narratives by demonstrating that African women played a significant role in economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that has been historically overlooked. Their analysis highlights how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through the codification of customary law, which marginalized women's economic contributions and land access. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced higher per capita incomes than previously assumed before the 1980s, countering the view of persistent underdevelopment. These findings collectively suggest that economic underdevelopment in Africa is not an inevitable outcome of historical or structural conditions but rather a result of specific institutional and policy choices that have shaped economic trajectories. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further contribute to this revision by showing that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and increased over time, challenging the assumption that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This stream of scholarship collectively revises historical economic narratives by emphasizing the agency of African populations and the contingent nature of economic outcomes, suggesting that alternative institutional paths could have led to different developmental trajectories. These insights provide a foundation for re-evaluating contemporary policy approaches that seek to address historical imbalances.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, have lasting effects on economic development, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who highlight how equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This perspective is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate that historical events such as the slave trade have enduring effects on trust and economic outcomes. The role of institutions is further contextualized by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who illustrate how colonial policies, such as the codification of customary law, reinforced gender inequalities and affected economic contributions. These insights are complemented by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who emphasizes the interdependence of various institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that economic growth is shaped by complex institutional systems rather than isolated factors. While the influence of institutions is widely acknowledged, the specific mechanisms through which they affect growth remain a subject of debate, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who notes the complexity of linking institutional quality to economic performance. This debate is settled by the empirical evidence provided by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who show that multiple measures of institutions yield consistent results, reinforcing the central role of institutions in economic development.
