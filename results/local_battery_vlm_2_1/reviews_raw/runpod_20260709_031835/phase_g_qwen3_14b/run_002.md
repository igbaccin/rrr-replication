The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical understanding. Institutions are viewed as foundational to economic performance, shaping incentives, enforcing contracts, and fostering innovation. However, the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain contested, with some scholars emphasizing their direct impact on economic outcomes while others highlight indirect pathways or competing explanations. This review explores the scholarly streams that address these causal relationships, synthesizing insights from multiple sources. A key debate revolves around whether institutions operate primarily through their effect on property rights, the rule of law, and political stability, or whether they interact with other factors such as cultural norms, historical legacies, and geographic conditions. This question is critical for understanding the persistence of economic disparities across regions and the potential for institutional reform to spur growth. The discussion begins with the argument that institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce the rule of law, are the primary determinants of income per capita, as demonstrated by comparative analyses of former colonies. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that institutions shaped by colonial history have long-lasting effects on economic development. These findings set the stage for a broader exploration of how institutions interact with other variables to influence growth outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) report, “Moreover, if we compare the point estimates to estimates using the same specification and sample, but not including the interethnic slave exports control (which are −0.104, −0.126 and −0.107, respectively), we find that the point estimates are barely affected by the inclusion of the control.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a position reinforced by North (1989, p.6), who emphasizes the role of political and economic institutions in shaping long-run development. These institutional differences, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further clarify, are not merely a function of initial conditions but are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. This dynamic is particularly evident in former colonies, where equilibrium institutions often reflect the preferences of European colonists rather than the needs of local populations. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) corroborate this by highlighting how divergent paths of economic development in the New World were influenced by institutional arrangements and initial factor endowments, with the United States and Canada achieving sustained economic growth earlier than other regions. These findings underscore the enduring impact of institutional structures on economic trajectories, setting the stage for further exploration of how specific institutional features interact with other determinants of growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have persistent economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2) who highlights the enduring impact of colonial rule on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. These legacies operate through mechanisms that shape social and economic norms, as evidenced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic development. This mistrust, in turn, influences various aspects of economic life, including trust in political institutions and the effectiveness of firm management practices. Peters (2004, p.5) adds to this by examining how land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals patterns of exclusion and class formation, which are not explained by adaptive customary systems but rather by the imposition of external economic structures. These findings suggest that historical events can shape the trajectory of economic development through deep-seated social and institutional legacies. Nunn (2008, p.2) further emphasizes the long-term economic consequences of the slave trade, noting that its effects extend well beyond the period of the trade itself and continue to influence development outcomes today. These legacies are not confined to economic structures alone but also extend to social and political institutions, as illustrated by the persistent effects of colonial rule on education and land policies. This interconnectedness of historical events and their economic consequences underscores the complexity of long-run economic growth and sets the stage for further exploration of how these legacies interact with contemporary institutional frameworks.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that long-term economic performance is shaped by historical trends and technological change, a perspective supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it. This stream highlights how technological innovation, particularly when integrated with supportive historical conditions, can drive economic development over time. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that mass uses of technical innovations provide positive feedback, enabling the development of new tools for scientific use and generating data on natural processes under economic modification. Similarly, van Zanden (2009, p.19) points to the role of institutions, such as guilds, in shaping economic performance by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, although these institutions may also have had counterbalancing effects by restricting market entry. These findings suggest that while institutions play a role, they are not the sole determinants of long-term economic performance. Instead, the interplay between technological change and historical conditions offers a broader explanation for economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how historical trends and technological advances interact to shape economic outcomes, a theme that can be further explored in relation to the role of institutions in this complex process.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) argues that distributional conflicts and political structures significantly shape institutional formation, offering an alternative explanation for long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.30); North and Weingast (1989, p.2) similarly highlight how constitutional rules and institutional commitments underpin economic performance in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings suggest that the development of institutions is not solely driven by economic efficiency but is deeply intertwined with the power dynamics and political arrangements that govern societies. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.30) emphasizes that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is essential for understanding their economic impact, noting how family systems, credit markets, and labor arrangements collectively influenced early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective aligns with North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who illustrate how institutional stability and credible commitments were crucial for England’s economic success, contrasting with France’s institutional contradictions that led to decline (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Simson (2019, p.11) further extends this analysis by showing how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were shaped by political and economic constraints, revealing that institutional formation is also influenced by historical legacies and external pressures (Simson 2019, p.11). These insights collectively underscore the complex interplay between political structures, distributional conflicts, and institutional development, which in turn shapes long-term economic outcomes. This understanding can inform further exploration of how institutional frameworks interact with other factors such as technological change and historical conditions to influence economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge prevailing assumptions about Africa's economic history by demonstrating that per capita incomes in Sub-Saharan Africa were higher than previously believed before the 1980s, a finding that revises long-standing narratives of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This revision is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who show that real wages in African colonies were historically above subsistence levels and increased over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused enduring poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These studies highlight the importance of recalibrating historical economic data to better understand long-term growth trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) contribute to this stream by arguing that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, challenging alternative interpretations that emphasize later developments (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.21). This recalibration of historical economic performance underscores the need to reassess the role of institutions in shaping growth, as prior assumptions about economic conditions may have distorted the understanding of institutional influences. By providing more accurate historical benchmarks, these studies open new avenues for examining how institutions interact with economic conditions over time. This revised historical context can inform further analysis of the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-term economic outcomes.

Institutional frameworks are central to long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the interplay between formal rules and their enforcement mechanisms. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are the primary determinants of income per capita, as evidenced by the contrasting outcomes in former European colonies. This perspective is reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who highlight the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in seventeenth-century England, showing how credible commitment underpins economic growth through stable and enforceable rules. These findings suggest that institutions shape economic outcomes by creating environments conducive to investment and innovation. However, the extent to which institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than economic efficiency remains a point of contention. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, better explain core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, pointing to the interdependency of various institutional frameworks. This view contrasts with the emphasis on institutional quality and enforceability in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). The historical evidence from the colonial period, particularly the institutional reversal among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36), provides a critical empirical comparison that underscores the role of institutional changes in shaping long-term economic outcomes, thus settling the debate in favor of institutional quality as a key driver of growth.
