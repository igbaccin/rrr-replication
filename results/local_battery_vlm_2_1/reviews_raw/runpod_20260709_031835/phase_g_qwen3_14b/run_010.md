The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical patterns of prosperity and poverty. Institutions are widely regarded as a key determinant of economic performance, yet their causal mechanisms and contextual conditions remain contested. Some scholars emphasize the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions, while others highlight the role of cultural and social factors in shaping economic outcomes. The debate is not merely academic; it informs policy choices regarding institutional reform and development strategies. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that structure economic and social interactions, are argued to influence growth by affecting incentives for investment, innovation, and cooperation. The question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development and the conditions under which they support or hinder growth remains unresolved. This literature review examines three streams of scholarship that explore these issues, focusing on the causal role of institutions, the interplay between institutions and other factors, and the conditions under which institutional effects are most pronounced. Each stream contributes distinct insights into the complex relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) report, “Patriarchal alliances and customary law: The invention of custom One key avenue for the codification of the subordinate status of women in the colonial era was through customary law.”

This perspective is further reinforced by the examination of how institutional changes caused by colonialism explain the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as shown in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). The mechanism through which institutions shape growth is evident in the way they influence the rule of law, property rights, and the concentration of political power, as illustrated by the econometric evidence linking urbanization in 1500 to subsequent institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in seventeenth-century England, emphasizing their role in fostering credible commitments that underpin economic growth. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) underscores how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic expansion. These insights collectively suggest that institutions are foundational to long-run economic development, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. This emphasis on institutions as a causal force continues to inform contemporary analyses of economic performance, as seen in the work of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who link divergent paths of development to institutional differences and initial factor endowments. The shared finding across these studies is that institutions are not merely correlates of growth but are central to its determination, setting the stage for further exploration of how institutional quality can be enhanced to promote sustained economic progress.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that institutions guaranteeing the fair execution of contracts, such as guilds in western Europe, played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic performance by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, even as they faced criticism for potentially restricting market competition (van Zanden 2009, p.19); this perspective aligns with Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These institutions supported the development of new efficient tools for scientific use and influenced the economic surplus available for research and innovation, creating a positive feedback loop that supported long-term growth. The relationship between institutional quality and economic performance is further illustrated by the findings that a lower skill premium in the late medieval period was associated with better long-term economic outcomes, as shown in van Zanden (2009, p.25). This suggests that the ability of institutions to ensure fair and effective economic practices is a key determinant of sustained growth. Additionally, Mokyr (2005, p.3) underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that support law and order, trust, and enforceable contracts, which are essential for efficient resource allocation and economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These insights collectively indicate that the quality and structure of institutions significantly influence the trajectory of economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of how institutional reforms can be designed to enhance long-term economic performance.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have long-term negative effects on economic development in Africa, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2) who highlights the enduring impact of colonial rule on the origins of formal education. These legacies manifest through mechanisms such as the persistent culture of mistrust that emerged from the slave trade, which negatively affects economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation. Nunn (2008, p.2) further demonstrates that the adverse effects of the slave trades on long-term economic development are significant, as they contributed to high levels of ethnic fractionalization, which in turn hinder economic growth. Peters (2004, p.5) adds that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals exclusion and class formation, which are not the result of adaptive customary systems but rather reflect deeper historical processes. This suggests that the historical legacies of exploitation and exclusion have shaped contemporary economic outcomes by creating structural inequalities that persist over time. These findings collectively indicate that the historical experiences of Africa, particularly those involving the slave trade and colonialism, have left deep imprints on the continent's economic trajectory, influencing trust, education, and land distribution in ways that continue to affect development outcomes today.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonial power structures and distributional conflicts shape institutional formation, providing an alternative explanation for long-run economic growth, a perspective supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who emphasizes that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, better explain core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This stream highlights how institutional development is deeply embedded in power dynamics, particularly in contexts where colonial authority and class-based hierarchies interacted to shape economic frameworks. These insights imply that economic institutions are not solely the result of rational choice or efficiency but are deeply influenced by historical power imbalances and social hierarchies. This understanding of institutional formation as a product of distributional conflicts and colonial legacies offers a broader lens for analyzing long-run economic growth, one that integrates the complexities of power and class into the explanation of economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) demonstrate that historical narratives of African economic development have systematically underestimated the contributions of women, particularly in the context of land use and agricultural production, challenging the assumption that economic growth was solely driven by male-dominated structures. This revision of historical accounts is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced higher per capita incomes before the 1980s than previously assumed, countering the narrative of persistent underdevelopment. These findings collectively suggest that long-run economic growth in Africa may have been more robust and less constrained than earlier accounts indicated, as historical records often failed to capture the full scope of economic activity and its drivers. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) add to this by showing that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and showed upward trends, contradicting the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This re-evaluation of historical economic narratives highlights the importance of revisiting and reinterpreting economic data and sources to better understand the trajectories of long-run growth. Such revisions challenge existing assumptions and open new avenues for examining the interplay between institutions, economic performance, and historical contexts.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods has had a lasting impact on economic development. This is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who demonstrate that various measures of institutional quality consistently correlate with economic outcomes. The historical context of institutional formation is further illuminated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who highlight the persistent effects of the slave trade on trust and, by extension, economic development. These findings underscore the importance of institutions in determining economic trajectories over time. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) add to this by showing how institutional arrangements, such as customary law, influenced gender roles and land access, which in turn shaped economic contributions and development. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks, suggesting that economic growth is the result of complex institutional systems rather than isolated policies. The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run growth is also central to Frankema (2012, p.17), who examines how colonial rule influenced the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. A shared finding across these works is that institutions significantly influence economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). The precise mechanisms through which institutions affect growth remain a point of contention, as noted by Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who argues that distributional conflicts may be more influential than economic efficiency. This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of institutional quality and economic outcomes used by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), which provides a robust measurement of the relationship between institutions and growth.
