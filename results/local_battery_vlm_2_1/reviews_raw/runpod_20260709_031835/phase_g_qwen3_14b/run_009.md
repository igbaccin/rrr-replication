The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy-making and historical interpretation. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that structure economic and social interactions, have been identified as a key determinant of economic performance. A core debate revolves around whether institutions are primarily exogenous forces shaping economic outcomes or endogenous responses to historical, cultural, and environmental conditions. This question is particularly salient in regions with complex colonial histories, where institutional legacies are thought to have enduring effects on contemporary development trajectories. The literature highlights how different institutional configurations ranging from extractive to inclusive can generate divergent economic outcomes with inclusive institutions fostering sustained growth and extractive ones perpetuating underdevelopment. This debate has theoretical significance and practical implications as it informs strategies for institutional reform and development interventions. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth is essential for designing effective policies that promote long-term economic prosperity. This understanding is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography, culture, and historical legacies, which shape the trajectory of economic development. The role of institutions involves their direct impact as well as their interaction with other determinants to influence long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, as illustrated by the contrasting development outcomes in former European colonies, where extractive institutions led to persistent underdevelopment while inclusive ones fostered sustained growth. This claim is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who show how historical events, such as the slave trade, shaped institutions and trust, which in turn influenced economic development. The mechanisms through which institutions affect growth are multifaceted, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who highlight how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to benefit politically powerful groups rather than promote long-term growth. These findings are further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who emphasize the role of institutional development and initial factor endowments in shaping divergent economic paths in the New World. The persistence of these institutional legacies is also evident in Nunn’s (2008, p.2) analysis of the long-term economic consequences of Africa’s slave trades, which created environments of mistrust and insecurity that hindered development. These studies collectively underscore the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth, suggesting that their influence extends beyond immediate historical contexts to shape long-term trajectories. This understanding of institutions as a primary driver of growth provides a foundation for examining their interactions with other determinants of development in subsequent analyses.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective supported by North (1989, p.3), who highlights the role of political and economic institutions in shaping economic outcomes. These adjustments are deeply embedded within broader institutional frameworks that govern interactions between individuals markets and states. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes the interdependency of institutions, noting that the European family system was linked with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance, all of which contributed to early industrialization. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering credible commitments that underpin economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England. These interdependencies suggest that institutional change is a complex process, requiring coordinated adjustments across multiple domains. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates on the ideological dimension, showing how the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality laid the groundwork for modern economic growth. This interconnectedness of institutions and ideologies implies that economic development is not merely a function of technological progress but also of the broader socio-political environment that supports and sustains it.

Stoler (1989, p.6) highlights how colonialism shaped both the colonizer and the colonized, with institutional legacies playing a crucial role in long-run economic growth. This is supported by Peters (2004, p.5), who notes that colonial-era perceptions of African landholding practices as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment in agriculture were reinforced by the push for agricultural development, which in turn influenced post-colonial land policies. These institutional frameworks, established during the colonial period, had lasting effects on economic structures, as seen in the way land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals patterns of exclusion and class formation rather than adaptive customary systems. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) demonstrate that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified the subordinate status of women, marginalizing them economically and socially. This institutional legacy continued to shape post-colonial development, as evidenced by the ways in which women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, reinforcing gender inequalities that persisted beyond the colonial era. These findings underscore how colonial institutions created enduring patterns of inequality and exclusion that influenced economic trajectories in the post-colonial period. Such institutional legacies, therefore, serve as a foundational condition for understanding the complex interplay between historical structures and long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.2) challenges traditional views on historical economic performance by arguing that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa did not significantly enhance the origins of formal education, a finding that revises narratives on economic development and growth (Frankema 2012, p.2). This perspective is supported by van Zanden (1999, p.18), who contends that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, thus complicating the assumption that economic progress necessarily leads to improved living conditions (van Zanden 1999, p.18). These studies highlight the importance of re-evaluating the historical performance of economies, as traditional assumptions may not always hold. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further contribute to this reassessment by arguing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, which challenges the view of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This cluster of literature underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of historical economic performance, showing that economic development and growth are influenced by a complex array of factors that may not always align with conventional narratives. These findings suggest that historical economic performance should be viewed through a more comprehensive lens, taking into account a variety of conditions and mechanisms that shape long-run economic trajectories.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by a range of historical and empirical analyses. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize that the type of institutions established during colonial periods, such as those enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions, even when extractive, are often designed to maximize the rents of powerful groups rather than promote long-term growth. These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in determining economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further highlight the persistent effects of historical events, such as the slave trade, on economic development through their impact on trust and social cohesion. Meanwhile, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) illustrate how institutional frameworks, such as customary law, shaped gender roles and land access, influencing economic contributions by women. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) points to the interdependencies among institutions, suggesting that economic growth is better understood through the lens of institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions. These studies collectively show that institutions are pivotal in shaping economic trajectories. The extent to which institutions are shaped by historical legacies such as colonialism or the slave trade remains a point of contention. While Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) focus on the direct impact of institutional design on growth, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the indirect and long-lasting effects of historical events on trust and social norms. This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of institutional quality and trust levels across former colonies, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), which reveal the complex interplay between institutional legacies and economic development.
