The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central issue in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical patterns of prosperity and underdevelopment. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary causal factor in economic growth or if other variables, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more prominent role. Scholars have explored how institutional frameworks shape economic trajectories, emphasizing the importance of inclusive versus extractive institutions in fostering or hindering growth. This literature review examines the contributions of various streams of scholarship that address these questions, focusing on how institutions influence economic outcomes. One stream of research, led by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), argues that institutions are the primary determinant of income per capita, emphasizing the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic development. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive institutions, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, or extractive institutions, as observed in the Congo and the Gold Coast. These institutional differences are posited as the main reason for the divergent economic outcomes in these regions. The significance of this argument lies in its challenge to alternative explanations such as geographic determinism, which attribute economic disparities to environmental factors rather than institutional ones. This stream of research underscores the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-term economic growth, a theme that continues to be explored in subsequent sections of this review. Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are central to long-run economic growth. This perspective is grounded in the observation that different colonial strategies led to the establishment of divergent institutional frameworks, which in turn shaped economic outcomes across regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, as they often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups rather than those that promote broad-based economic development. This insight highlights the importance of understanding how institutional design is influenced by historical and political factors. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) similarly emphasize that institutional development and initial factor endowments have played a critical role in shaping divergent paths of economic growth in the New World. They note that while many societies began with similar levels of per capita product, the divergence in growth trajectories can be traced back to differences in the security of property rights and the structures of the financial sector. These findings collectively underscore the enduring impact of institutional quality on economic development, suggesting that the type of institutions in place has long-term implications for economic performance. This theme of institutional influence on growth provides a foundation for examining how specific institutional features contribute to economic outcomes in different contexts.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that institutional developments in western Europe, such as the emergence of guilds, played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic performance by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, despite their potential drawbacks in restricting market entry. This historical institutional change is further supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that sustained economic growth relies on technological advancements and the necessary institutional adjustments that enable their effective implementation. The interplay between institutional frameworks and technological progress is also reflected in the work of Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21), who argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, highlighting the importance of institutional and economic factors in shaping growth trajectories. These findings suggest that historical trends and institutional changes are deeply intertwined in determining long-term economic outcomes, as evidenced by the relationship between the skill premium and subsequent economic growth observed by van Zanden (2009, p.25). The cumulative effect of these institutional and historical dynamics underscores the importance of understanding the evolution of institutions as a key factor in explaining economic development over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade and colonialism have long-term economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who highlight the persistent impact of historical events on contemporary economic conditions. This mechanism is further complemented by Nunn (2008, p.2), who emphasizes how the slave trades contributed to high levels of ethnic fractionalization, which in turn negatively affects economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds to this by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that colonialism did not necessarily foster benign conditions for educational development. These findings collectively indicate that historical legacies, such as the slave trade and colonialism, have enduring effects on economic outcomes, shaping the trajectory of development in ways that are not always captured by institutional analyses alone. This perspective underscores the importance of considering historical events as significant determinants of long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) challenges the assumption that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, a perspective supported by Austin (2008, p.25) in his critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Both scholars argue that economic outcomes are influenced by the interplay of power dynamics and historical narratives rather than by the mere presence of 'good' institutions. This aligns with Ogilvie's (2007, p.30) assertion that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework must be considered when analyzing economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further contribute to this perspective by demonstrating how historical narratives, particularly those involving the marginalization of women, have shaped economic development in Africa. Their analysis underscores the importance of understanding the social and legal structures that emerged during colonial rule in influencing economic outcomes. These findings collectively suggest that economic institutions are not only shaped by efficiency considerations but also by the distribution of power and the historical legacies that influence social structures. This perspective provides a more comprehensive understanding of the complex factors that drive long-run economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of how these dynamics interact with institutional frameworks.

Institutional frameworks have been identified as pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who emphasize that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. These findings are reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that trust, shaped by historical events such as the slave trade, plays a critical role in economic development. The importance of institutions is further underscored by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who highlight how the codification of customary law under colonial rule affected the roles of women in economic activities, thereby influencing growth trajectories. Meanwhile, Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the notion that institutions alone determine economic outcomes, arguing that historical narratives must account for dynamic and endogenous responses to environmental and economic conditions. This perspective is echoed by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who stresses the interdependence of various institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that economic growth cannot be attributed to a single institutional factor. The empirical evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36) on the relationship between historical population density and current institutions provides a concrete measurement that supports the argument for the enduring impact of institutional legacies. These studies collectively highlight the complex and multifaceted role of institutions in economic development, with the precise mechanisms and historical contexts remaining areas of active scholarly engagement.
