The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central issue in economic history and development studies, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. Institutions are widely recognized as pivotal in shaping economic outcomes, but the mechanisms through which they operate, and the extent to which they are the primary determinants of growth, remain contested. Some scholars emphasize the direct influence of institutional quality on economic performance, while others highlight the interplay between institutions and other factors such as cultural norms, historical legacies, and social fragmentation. This review explores these debates by examining streams of scholarship that address the causal role of institutions in economic development. One stream argues that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, with a focus on the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions. Another stream investigates how historical legacies, such as the slave trade, have shaped contemporary economic outcomes through their impact on trust and social cohesion. A third stream explores the interdependencies among institutions and their broader institutional frameworks. These streams collectively illuminate the complex and multifaceted role of institutions in long-run economic growth. The discussion begins with a stream that underscores the direct influence of institutional quality on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonialism and the slave trade are central to long-run economic growth, as these historical legacies have enduring effects on economic development. The mechanisms through which colonial institutions influence growth are multifaceted, with one key pathway being the establishment of extractive or inclusive institutions that either hinder or promote economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on how equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth, highlighting how institutional persistence can perpetuate underdevelopment. This is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who note that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments, with institutions playing a pivotal role in determining the trajectory of growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) adds that the prolonged slave trades in Africa had a profound and lasting impact on economic development, as they contributed to ethnic fractionalization, which in turn negatively affects growth. These findings collectively underscore how the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, with implications for contemporary policy and development strategies. This institutional legacy thus remains a critical determinant of long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth hinges on technological advancements and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany them, highlighting the interplay between innovation and institutional change as a key driver of long-term economic performance. This perspective is reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that formal institutions, such as guilds in western Europe, played a crucial role in facilitating the transfer of skills and ensuring fair execution of contracts, thus contributing to economic development. Similarly, Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality laid the institutional groundwork for modern economic growth, demonstrating how intellectual shifts can shape economic trajectories. These findings collectively illustrate that institutional adjustments are not merely reactive but are integral to the process of economic transformation. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) further support this view by showing that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes. This shared insight into the role of institutions in fostering economic performance highlights their enduring influence on growth patterns across different historical contexts.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that constitutional rules and institutional frameworks are central to fostering economic growth, as demonstrated by their analysis of seventeenth-century England, where institutional commitments underpinned market development and economic expansion. This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie’s (2007, p.30) emphasis on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, particularly in pre-industrial Europe, where power dynamics rather than efficiency considerations often dictated institutional arrangements. Both approaches highlight the significance of institutional design in influencing economic outcomes, though they differ in their focus: North and Weingast emphasize the importance of credible commitments to rules, while Ogilvie underscores the impact of social and power relations on institutional evolution. These insights are further reinforced by North’s (1989, p.6) discussion of how political institutions that define property rights and facilitate market exchanges reduce transaction costs, thereby contributing to economic growth. This interplay between institutional design and economic performance suggests that the efficiency of institutions is not the sole determinant of growth, but rather that broader institutional frameworks and their alignment with societal power structures are also critical. The recognition of these interdependencies underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of how institutions shape economic trajectories. This perspective sets the stage for examining how different types of institutions interact to influence long-term economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) reports, “If we are going to pursue cultural efﬁciency models, we need much more rigorous theories-and preferably also empirical ﬁndings-about which beliefs and values are economically beneﬁcial, in what ways, and whether they always coexist in the same culture.”

Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa did not significantly enhance the origins of formal education, a claim supported by the broader stream of African economic history and institutional development, which highlights the complex interplay between colonial legacies, education, and post-independence policies in shaping long-run economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; Peters 2004, p.5; Simson 2019, p.11; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This stream reveals that colonial policies often reinforced existing inequalities, particularly in the realm of land distribution and legal systems, which had lasting effects on economic development. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on post-independence economic trajectories. The interplay of these factors highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional development in Africa, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of how education and policy reforms have sought to mitigate these historical legacies.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, with a substantial body of research emphasizing their enduring influence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how colonial institutions established in the 19th and 20th centuries continue to affect economic outcomes today, noting that extractive states, such as those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, hindered development by prioritizing resource extraction over investment in human and physical capital. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate how the legacy of the slave trade has left a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development by undermining cooperation and social cohesion. These findings underscore the importance of institutional legacies in determining economic trajectories. However, the role of institutions is not solely determined by historical legacies; contemporary policy reforms and the evolution of legal and social frameworks also contribute significantly to economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa provides insights crucial for policy-making and institutional reforms, suggesting that the quality of public goods and the development of progressive institutions are not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but also by the ability of societies to establish functional governance structures. This highlights the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional development. The empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across different regions, as discussed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), further illustrates how variations in property rights and political power concentration influence economic outcomes, showing that the institutional reversal among former colonies is closely linked to the disease environment and the initial conditions of population density. These findings collectively emphasize the multifaceted nature of institutional development and its profound impact on long-run economic growth.
