The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. At the heart of this debate lies the claim that institutions shape economic outcomes more profoundly than other factors such as geography or culture. This perspective is exemplified by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the divergent paths of former colonies can be attributed to the types of institutions established during colonial rule, with extractive institutions in places like the Congo leading to long-term stagnation. Regions with inclusive institutions, such as the United States, experienced sustained growth. Other scholars challenge this view, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic development is more nuanced and context-dependent. For instance, Austin (2008, p.25) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing historical processes and failing to account for the dynamic, endogenous responses of societies to their environments. These differing perspectives highlight the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth and set the stage for a deeper exploration of the various streams of scholarship that engage with this question.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, emphasizing the role of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term development trajectories. This perspective is grounded in the observation that different colonial strategies led to divergent institutional setups, which in turn influenced economic outcomes across regions. For instance, the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in some colonies contrasted sharply with the extractive states in others, where institutions were designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional differences, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), were shaped by initial factor endowments and persisted over time, influencing patterns of inequality and economic performance. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on how the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and contributing to economic growth. This mechanism underscores the importance of institutional stability and effectiveness in fostering economic development. The focus on institutions as the central explanatory variable highlights their enduring impact on economic performance and sets the stage for examining how specific types of institutions influence growth outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that sustained economic growth depends on technological progress, institutional change, and historical trends, a perspective that is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes the role of advancing technology and necessary institutional and ideological adjustments in enabling long-term economic growth. This view is further reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the importance of institutions such as guilds in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, even as they may also create local cartels that affect market dynamics. Van Zanden (1999, p.18) contributes to this discussion by showing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, indicating that historical trends and institutional factors may have played a more complex role in shaping economic outcomes than previously assumed. These findings suggest that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its impact is mediated by institutional structures and historical contexts, which together shape the trajectory of long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have long-term economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who highlight the persistent impact of historical events on contemporary development outcomes. This internal channel of mistrust, they argue, is at least twice as significant as the external channel, such as the rule of law or trustworthiness of others. Frankema (2012, p.2) further illustrates how historical colonial policies, such as British rule in sub-Saharan Africa, influenced the origins of formal education, suggesting that these legacies shape economic trajectories through institutional and educational pathways. These findings underscore the enduring influence of historical events on economic development, indicating that the effects of the slave trade and colonialism are not merely short-term disruptions but have deep, lasting implications for trust, education, and institutional quality. Such legacies thus provide a compelling alternative explanation for long-run economic growth, one that is deeply intertwined with the historical context and the social and economic structures that emerged from it.

Stoler (1989, p.6) critiques the oversimplification of causation in colonial narratives, arguing that colonialism simultaneously shaped both the colonizer and the colonized, and that this process requires a reexamination of European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). This perspective challenges the assumption that colonial institutions were unilaterally imposed by European powers, instead emphasizing the complex and dynamic interactions within colonial societies that influenced the development of institutions and economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.25) similarly questions the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, suggesting that historical interpretations often compress complex processes into simplistic narratives, thereby obscuring the agency of local populations and the endogenous responses to environmental and economic challenges (Austin 2008, p.25; Austin 2008, p.23). This critique highlights the importance of considering the multifaceted nature of historical change, where institutions and economic trajectories are shaped through a combination of local and global interactions. Such an approach underscores the need for more nuanced analyses that account for the dynamic and reciprocal relationships between colonizers and colonized, as well as the internal contradictions within colonial systems that influenced long-term economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge prevailing historical narratives about Africa's economic performance and institutions, revealing that African women played a significant role in economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a contribution often overlooked in traditional accounts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This revision of historical narratives is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, countering the view of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.18). These findings suggest that economic performance in Africa has been more dynamic and complex than earlier, more static interpretations have indicated. Simson (2019, p.11) contributes to this revision by demonstrating that East African post-independence budget policies were not uniquely clientelist, showing that public spending was shaped by broader economic and political conditions rather than solely by patronage (Simson 2019, p.11; Simson 2019, p.19). This cluster collectively indicates that African economic history is more nuanced, with institutions and economic outcomes shaped by a range of factors, including gender roles, colonial interventions, and post-independence fiscal policies. These insights have important implications for understanding long-run economic growth, as they highlight the need to consider the diversity of historical experiences and their multifaceted influences on institutional development.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the differences in economic outcomes among former colonies are primarily due to the type of institutions established during the colonial period. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, have lasting effects on economic development, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not conducive to long-term growth. This perspective is challenged by Austin (2008, p.25), who suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causation and that African economic history is more dynamic and endogenous. The debate over the role of institutions is further enriched by the insights of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate that the slave trade has had a persistent negative impact on trust in Africa, which in turn affects economic development. The historical evidence of institutional legacies, such as those found in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), provides a comparative framework to assess the long-term effects of different institutional arrangements on economic growth.
