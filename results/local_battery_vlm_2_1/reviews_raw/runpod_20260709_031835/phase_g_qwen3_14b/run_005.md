The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions are widely regarded as a key determinant of economic outcomes, but the mechanisms through which they influence growth are complex and contested. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, while others highlight the interplay between institutions and other factors such as culture, geography, and technology. This debate underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shape incentives, enforce contracts, and facilitate or hinder economic activity over time. The discussion is further enriched by the recognition that institutions change over time in response to historical events and power dynamics. This evolution can have lasting effects on economic trajectories, as seen in the case of former colonies where institutional legacies continue to influence development outcomes. The role of institutions is thus not only about their presence but also about their nature, durability, and adaptability. These considerations set the stage for a deeper exploration of the different streams of scholarship that have contributed to our understanding of this issue. Acemoglu et al. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, a position supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering economic commitment and growth. This perspective is grounded in the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of divergent institutional frameworks, with some regions, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, adopting institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, developed extractive states aimed at resource extraction. These institutional differences, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), are not merely a function of initial conditions but are shaped by politically powerful groups whose interests may conflict with long-term economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further note that institutional development and initial factor endowments have played a crucial role in shaping divergent paths of economic development in the New World, reinforcing the idea that institutions are central to economic outcomes.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective echoed by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who emphasizes the Enlightenment's role in fostering growth through the promotion of useful knowledge and rationality. This shared emphasis on the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks reveals a mechanism by which long-run growth is achieved: institutions facilitate the diffusion and application of new technologies by creating environments conducive to innovation and knowledge sharing. Stern (2004, p.1) further underscores the importance of institutional settings by critiquing the environmental Kuznets curve, suggesting that statistical models must account for the complex interactions between institutions and economic development. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in supporting growth varies across contexts and depends on their ability to adapt to changing technological and ideological landscapes. The interplay between institutions and technological change, therefore, emerges as a central theme in understanding long-run economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of how specific institutional features shape this process.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) challenges the view that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth by reassessing historical economic data, particularly the role of guilds in western Europe, and argues that the skill premium declined in the late medieval period, predicting long-term economic performance. This reassessment is supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21), who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, providing evidence that contradicts revisionist views emphasizing institutional factors as the sole drivers of economic growth. The cluster highlights that historical economic performance must be understood through a more nuanced lens, one that accounts for the complex interplay of economic conditions, technological change, and institutional arrangements. For instance, van Zanden (1999, p.18) shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that economic performance is not solely determined by institutional quality. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings collectively imply that the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth must be contextualized within broader historical and economic narratives. This perspective sets the stage for further exploration of how alternative factors, such as technological change and global economic integration, interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical events such as the slave trade have had a lasting impact on economic development in Africa, shaping long-term outcomes through persistent cultural and social legacies. This perspective is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the extensive duration of Africa's slave trades and their significant influence on subsequent economic trajectories. The mechanisms through which these historical events affect economic development are multifaceted, including the erosion of trust and the fragmentation of social cohesion. Similarly, Peters (2004, p.5) argues that land competition and exclusionary practices under colonial rule fostered class formation, which continues to influence economic structures in sub-Saharan Africa. These findings underscore the complex interplay between historical legacies and institutional development, revealing how past events continue to shape contemporary economic realities. Frankema (2012, p.2) further contributes to this discussion by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education, suggesting that colonial policies did not necessarily lead to more benign outcomes for educational development. These insights collectively highlight the enduring influence of historical events on economic development, offering a nuanced understanding of the factors that shape long-term economic outcomes in Africa.

This is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who highlight how historical events such as the slave trade have enduring effects on economic development through the persistence of mistrust. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic stability and growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, which can hinder economic development, reinforcing the idea that institutional quality is crucial for long-term prosperity. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) contribute to this discussion by examining how colonial rule influenced the legal status of women, which in turn affected economic development. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that British colonial rule did not necessarily lead to more benign outcomes for educational development, highlighting the complex relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that constitutional rules and institutions are vital for fostering commitment and economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England. These insights collectively show that institutions are pivotal in determining economic trajectories. The extent to which historical legacies shape contemporary institutions remains a point of contention. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that various measures of institutions yield similar results, suggesting a consistent impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes. Frankema (2012, p.17) points to the role of exogenous ecological factors in shaping educational development, indicating that non-institutional factors can also influence economic growth. The historical comparison of institutional development in former colonies, as detailed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), provides a specific empirical basis for evaluating the role of institutions, offering a clear framework for understanding their impact on economic development.
