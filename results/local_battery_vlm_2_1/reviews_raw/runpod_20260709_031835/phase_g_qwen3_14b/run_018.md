The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are posited as key determinants of growth, yet their causal influence is debated. Some scholars emphasize the enduring impact of institutional quality, while others highlight the limitations of such frameworks or propose alternative explanations. The literature explores how institutions shape economic outcomes, often through their influence on property rights, the rule of law, and the capacity for collective action. This review examines key streams of scholarship that address these issues, focusing on the mechanisms through which institutions affect growth and the conditions under which these effects hold. One stream argues that institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes, with historical evidence from colonial experiences underscoring their long-term impact. Another stream challenges this view, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and growth is more contingent and context-specific. A third stream explores how institutions interact with other factors, such as culture or geography, to influence economic development. These streams collectively highlight the complexity of institutional effects and the need for nuanced analysis. The next section examines the argument that institutions, particularly those promoting inclusive governance, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, supported by historical comparisons of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

This claim is reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering credible commitments that underpin economic development in seventeenth-century England. The mechanism through which institutions drive growth is closely tied to the enforcement of the rule of law and the protection of property rights, as seen in the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. This highlights the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments, reinforcing the idea that institutions are central to economic growth. These insights collectively suggest that the quality of institutions, rather than geography or other factors, is the key determinant of long-run economic performance. This theme of institutional quality as a driver of growth provides a foundation for examining the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes in different historical and geographical contexts.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that historical narratives have underestimated the contributions of African women to economic development, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting how patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced gender inequalities. This perspective aligns with broader efforts to revise historical economic narratives by emphasizing previously overlooked factors that shaped long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the assumption that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa, showing that higher per capita incomes existed before the 1980s. These findings suggest that economic performance in Africa was more complex and self-sustaining than conventional accounts suggest. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further complicate the narrative by demonstrating that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and showed upward trends, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This stream collectively reveals that economic growth in Africa was influenced by a range of factors, including internal dynamics and local policies, which have been historically misinterpreted or neglected. These insights contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms driving long-run economic growth, emphasizing the need to re-examine historical records and assumptions about economic development in Africa.

This perspective aligns with broader critiques of economic history frameworks that reduce multifaceted processes to singular causes, suggesting instead that such frameworks may misrepresent the interplay of local responses and external influences (Austin 2008, p.25; Simson 2019, p.11). Simson (2019, p.11) further illustrates this complexity by examining post-independence budget policies in East Africa, showing that these were not uniquely clientelist but involved nuanced allocations of resources that balanced patronage with broader economic considerations (Simson 2019, p.11). The evidence from Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda reveals that while military and capital expenditures were significant, they were shaped by historical legacies, regional instability, and access to international markets, rather than by a singular focus on patronage (Simson 2019, p.11; Simson 2019, p.19). These findings collectively suggest that economic development is best understood through an analysis of institutional and policy dynamics that are context-specific and historically contingent. This approach underscores the importance of re-evaluating historical narratives to capture the full range of factors that influence long-run economic growth.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the role of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes, emphasizing how formal institutions such as guilds in Western Europe influenced the transfer of skills and the regulation of labor markets. This perspective aligns with Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who argues that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, better explain the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Both scholars underscore the interdependence of institutions within broader socio-economic systems, suggesting that isolated analyses of individual institutions may fail to capture their full impact on long-term growth. van Zanden (1999, p.18) further complicates this picture by demonstrating that economic development does not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living, as evidenced by the inverse relationship between real wage growth and regional development in Europe between 1500 and 1800. This finding implies that institutional arrangements may not always translate into tangible economic benefits for the broader population. Mokyr (2005, p.3) adds another layer by linking long-term economic growth to the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality, suggesting that intellectual and ideological shifts can shape institutional development and, by extension, economic outcomes. These interwoven factors, institutional frameworks, distributional conflicts, and ideological transformations, collectively shape the trajectory of economic development in complex and historically contingent ways.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies, such as the slave trade, have had lasting negative effects on economic development in Africa, with the slave trade contributing to a persistent culture of mistrust that continues to hinder economic progress. Their findings are supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights that the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had an adverse effect on long-term economic development in Africa. This persistent mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) note, affects economic development through both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, with the internal channel being at least twice as significant as the external one. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that British colonial rule did not necessarily foster the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that historical legacies of colonialism may have also contributed to structural challenges in education and development. These historical legacies, as Peters (2004, p.5) explains, reveal patterns of exclusion and class formation that continue to shape land competition and economic structures in sub-Saharan Africa. The cumulative effect of these historical legacies, including mistrust, limited educational development, and exclusionary land practices, has created a complex web of challenges that continue to influence economic development in Africa today.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had lasting effects on economic development. Historical legacies, such as those in sub-Saharan Africa, reveal how extractive institutions created persistent challenges in education and land competition, as noted by Peters (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings are supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who highlight the role of the slave trade in fostering a culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic development in Africa. Meanwhile, Austin (2008, p.25) emphasizes the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic responses to environmental and institutional changes, suggesting that institutions are not the sole determinant of economic outcomes. This perspective is further reinforced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who demonstrate how institutional frameworks, such as customary law, shaped gender roles and access to land, influencing economic growth trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) adds that economic institutions are interdependent, implying that their effects on growth must be understood within broader institutional frameworks. The debate over the primacy of institutions versus other factors such as geography or historical responses remains unresolved, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasizing the role of institutions, while Austin (2008, p.25) argues for a more nuanced understanding that incorporates endogenous responses. The empirical comparison of institutional legacies in former colonies, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), provides a specific historical evidence base that can help settle this disagreement by showing how institutions shaped long-run economic outcomes.
