The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the primary determinant of income per capita, overshadowing other factors such as geography or culture. This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, particularly in the context of historical colonial experiences. Others, however, challenge this view by suggesting that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex and contingent, influenced by a range of historical, cultural, and environmental factors. This literature review explores these debates by examining multiple streams of scholarship that engage with the causal role of institutions in economic development. One stream, led by Acemoglu et al., posits that institutions are the key driver of economic growth, as evidenced by the divergent outcomes observed in former European colonies. Another stream, represented by Austin, questions the simplicity of this argument, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and growth is not as straightforward as previously assumed. A third stream, reflected in the work of Nunn and Wantchekon, explores how historical events such as the slave trade have shaped institutional development and, consequently, economic outcomes. These streams collectively highlight the multifaceted nature of the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of each perspective. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth compared to geography or other factors, highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term development trajectories. This perspective is grounded in the observation that different colonial strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional arrangements, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. For example, in regions where extractive institutions were imposed, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, economic growth was stunted, while in areas with inclusive institutions, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, growth was robust. These findings highlight the importance of institutional quality in fostering environments conducive to investment and the rule of law. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, reflecting the influence of politically powerful groups that prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. This underscores the need for institutional arrangements that align with broader developmental goals. North (1989, p.6) similarly emphasizes that political institutions play a crucial role in defining property rights and facilitating market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs and promoting economic activity. These insights collectively illustrate how institutional structures can either enable or constrain economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of the mechanisms through which institutions influence development outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have shaped long-term institutional and economic outcomes in Africa, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2) in his analysis of British colonial rule’s impact on education. The slave trade, as Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrates, had an adverse effect on long-term economic development by fostering ethnic fractionalization, which in turn hindered economic growth through increased mistrust and reduced cooperation. This mechanism is further reinforced by the findings of Hopkins (2009, p.18), who highlights how historical factors, including the legacy of the slave trade, influence the evolution of social institutions and their capacity to address contemporary challenges. Peters (2004, p.5) adds that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reflects exclusion and class formation, illustrating how historical processes have structured power relations that persist into the present. These findings collectively suggest that the legacy of historical events such as the slave trade and colonialism has created enduring institutional and social conditions that shape economic development in Africa. This historical path dependence implies that understanding these legacies is crucial for designing effective policies that address the deep-rooted challenges facing the region.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, highlighting the interplay between innovation and the structures that support it (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective is echoed by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that institutions such as guilds in western Europe played a role in shaping the skill premium and influencing long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These insights suggest that technological progress and the associated institutional frameworks are key determinants of economic development. Van Zanden (1999, p.18) further complicates this picture by showing that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living, indicating that the relationship between economic growth and living standards is not straightforward (van Zanden 1999, p.18). Mokyr (2005, p.3) adds that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality was instrumental in fostering modern economic growth, underscoring the importance of intellectual and cultural shifts (Mokyr 2005, p.3). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) contribute to this discussion by arguing that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, suggesting that long-term economic performance is shaped by a combination of technological, institutional, and historical factors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.21). These findings collectively indicate that economic development is influenced by a complex interplay of technological change, skill distribution, and historical divergence, setting the stage for further exploration of how these factors interact over time.

Austin (2008, p.25) critiques the oversimplification of institutional explanations for economic growth, arguing that historical narratives often compress complex causal relationships into static frameworks, a perspective shared with Ogilvie (2007, p.30) who emphasizes the interdependence of institutions rather than their isolated impact on economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25; Ogilvie 2007, p.30). Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which attributes economic divergence to institutional quality, neglects the dynamic and endogenous nature of African responses to environmental and economic challenges, suggesting that growth can emerge even in contexts of extraction and coercion (Austin 2008, p.25). Ogilvie similarly challenges the assumption that institutional efficiency alone drives economic development, pointing to the role of distributional conflicts and the necessity of examining institutional frameworks as interconnected systems rather than isolated components (Ogilvie 2007, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These perspectives collectively suggest that economic growth is shaped by multifaceted institutional interactions and historical contingencies, which cannot be reduced to a single cause or effect. This nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics sets the stage for further exploration of how different institutional configurations interact with broader historical and socioeconomic conditions to influence long-term economic trajectories.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge simplistic historical narratives of African economic development by highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that contrasts with neglectful historical accounts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This view is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the assumption of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These perspectives jointly emphasize the need to reassess historical economic performance in Africa by considering the roles of women and the potential for economic growth prior to European colonial interventions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further contribute to this reassessment by demonstrating that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, challenging the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This collective argument underscores the importance of revisiting historical economic data and narratives to reveal more complex and nuanced patterns of development. Such findings suggest that African economies were not uniformly stagnant but experienced varying degrees of growth and transformation, influenced by a range of internal and external factors. These insights call for a more inclusive and historically accurate understanding of African economic development that accounts for diverse contributions and trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by multiple studies emphasizing their impact on development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of income per capita compared to geography or other factors, highlighting how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions that continue to influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who show that institutional changes driven by colonialism explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, with equilibrium institutions often designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions is not universally deterministic, as Austin (2008, p.25) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing historical processes, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous interpretation of how institutions interact with economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This view is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging narratives that neglect the role of diverse social groups in shaping institutional and economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is further nuanced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who argue that the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, negatively affecting economic development. Their findings suggest that internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as influential as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This underscores the complexity of institutional effects, as demonstrated by the historical evidence on the slave trade's long-term impact on trust and development.
