The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding the divergent paths of economic development across nations. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical events, play a more pivotal role. This literature explores the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, often emphasizing their capacity to shape incentives, enforce contracts, and foster innovation. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary causal force behind economic growth, as they create the conditions necessary for sustained development. Others propose that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of historical, cultural, and environmental factors, which in turn influence economic performance. This review synthesizes these perspectives, highlighting the multifaceted ways in which institutions interact with other variables to drive long-run growth. The discussion begins with a stream that emphasizes the direct causal role of institutions in shaping economic development, drawing on comparative analyses of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This stream underscores the importance of institutional quality in determining long-term economic outcomes, particularly in the context of colonial legacies. The next section examines a stream that challenges the primacy of institutions by emphasizing the dynamic and endogenous nature of historical processes. Austin (2008, p.25) and Austin (2008, p.23) suggest that institutions change over time in response to a range of factors such as economic opportunities and social dynamics. The following section explores a stream that identifies antecedents to institutional development, such as cultural norms and historical events, which serve as triggers for institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.22). This stream highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other social and historical factors in shaping economic growth. The final section considers a stream that focuses on the role of gender and historical narratives in shaping institutional development and economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This stream emphasizes the importance of recognizing diverse historical contributions to economic development, including those of marginalized groups. The discussion concludes with a stream that examines the role of trust and social norms in shaping institutional quality and economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This stream highlights the importance of understanding the origins of trust and its implications for economic development. Each of these streams contributes to a broader understanding of the complex and multifaceted role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that determine economic outcomes, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules in fostering commitment and economic growth. The mechanism through which institutions influence growth is particularly evident in the case of former European colonies, where differing colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, as they are often shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritize their own interests over long-run growth. This aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who note that institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. The persistence of these institutional differences over time has had lasting implications for economic performance, as seen in the continued disparities in income levels between countries with inclusive and extractive institutions. These findings highlight the important influence of institutions on economic development, as they affect both the starting conditions and the long-term sustainability of growth. This emphasis on institutional quality provides a foundational understanding that can be further explored in relation to other factors that interact with institutional frameworks to influence economic outcomes.

These historical processes are further contextualized by Frankema (2012, p.2), who examines the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, revealing that colonial institutions did not necessarily create more benign conditions for educational development. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional development is also reflected in the work of Nunn (2008, p.2), who argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on long-term economic development, contributing to ethnic fractionalization that continues to hinder economic growth. These findings collectively illustrate how historical events shape the institutional environment, which in turn influences economic outcomes over time.

This historical perspective is expanded upon by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who argues that the intellectual environment shaped by the Enlightenment played a crucial role in fostering modern economic growth, emphasizing the importance of useful knowledge and rationality as foundational elements for development. This perspective is complemented by Stoler (1989, p.6), who highlights how colonial ideologies and power dynamics influenced the creation of social and economic structures that shaped long-term institutional development. These findings underscore the complex relationship between cultural and ideological influences and the development of institutions that support economic growth. The role of colonial legacies in shaping economic and institutional development is thus not only a matter of historical inheritance but also of how these legacies interact with broader ideological and cultural frameworks. This understanding provides a foundation for examining how different regions and periods have experienced varying trajectories of economic and institutional development based on their unique cultural and ideological contexts.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that reconstructing historical economic data is essential for understanding long-term economic performance, as evidenced by the relationship between the skill premium in western Europe and subsequent GDP growth. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who challenge the assumption of persistent underdevelopment in Sub-Saharan Africa by presenting revised estimates of per capita incomes before the 1980s. These findings highlight the importance of data reconstruction in uncovering alternative narratives about economic performance that do not rely solely on institutional explanations. For instance, van Zanden (1999, p.18) demonstrates that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that economic performance may be influenced by factors beyond institutional frameworks. Similarly, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, offering a historical perspective that challenges some institutional explanations of economic growth. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) further emphasize the necessity of collaborative research to improve historical national accounts, as seen in their work on the GDP of the Cape Colony and Ghana. These contributions collectively underscore the value of historical data reconstruction in providing a more nuanced understanding of long-term economic performance and divergence.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) questions the focus on the complexity of institutional development by pointing out that distributional conflicts influence the evolution of core economic institutions, with economic efficiency not being the main driver in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie further emphasizes that the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks complicates efforts to isolate the impact of any single institution on economic outcomes, a point echoed by Simson (2019, p.11), who shows how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were influenced by a complex interplay of domestic and external factors rather than being solely driven by patronage. These contributions collectively suggest that economic growth is shaped by a range of interacting forces, including distributional conflicts, historical legacies, and the evolving nature of institutional frameworks. This understanding of institutions as context-dependent and interrelated has significant implications for how we interpret the long-term trajectory of economic development, shifting the focus from deterministic narratives to more nuanced analyses of institutional evolution. This perspective sets the stage for further exploration of how historical and contemporary factors interact to shape economic outcomes.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the argument that they are the primary determinant of income per capita, with historical examples such as the contrasting institutional frameworks established in the United States and the Congo illustrating this point (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The evolution of institutions is further influenced by historical legacies, as seen in the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, which were designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in economic development, with empirical measures such as protection against expropriation risk and constraints on the executive providing insights into the relationship between institutions and economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36). The influence of institutions is not considered fixed, as some scholars argue that historical narratives should reflect the dynamic and self-generated responses of societies to their environments, challenging the static assumptions of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.25). This highlights a key disagreement regarding the extent to which institutions are shaped by historical legacies versus the agency of societies in adapting to their circumstances. The empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across different regions, such as the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions in various former colonies, provides a basis for evaluating the relative influence of historical legacies and societal agency in shaping economic outcomes.
