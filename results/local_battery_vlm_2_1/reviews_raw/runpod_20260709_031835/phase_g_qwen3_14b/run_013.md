The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued to be pivotal in fostering sustained growth. Some scholars emphasize the causal role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, while others highlight the interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography, culture, and technology. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or if they operate in conjunction with other variables. This review explores these streams of scholarship, focusing on how institutions influence economic development and the conditions under which they do so. The discussion begins with the argument that institutions, particularly those that promote the rule of law and protect property rights, are central to long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) assert that institutions, not geography, are the primary determinant of income per capita, highlighting the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions. Their analysis of colonial strategies and their long-term institutional legacies underscores the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies reflects institutional changes caused by colonialism, which complements this perspective. They provide econometric evidence linking historical population density to current institutional quality, suggesting that institutional development is a key driver of economic growth. These findings are supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and necessary institutional and ideological adjustments. His work highlights the interdependence of technological progress and institutional change, reinforcing the idea that institutions evolve in response to economic and social demands. This synthesis of evidence suggests that institutions play a crucial role in fostering long-run economic growth, with their quality and evolution being central to understanding economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by historical and comparative analyses of different regions and periods, with North and Weingast (1989, p.2) providing complementary evidence on the role of constitutional rules in seventeenth-century England. This stream of scholarship emphasizes how institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the security of property rights, the concentration of political power, and the enforcement of rules that govern economic exchange. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) underscores the importance of political and economic institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which in turn promote economic development. These findings are further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional arrangements and initial factor endowments. The emphasis on institutional quality as a driver of growth suggests that the effectiveness of governance and the inclusiveness of political systems are critical for fostering sustained economic development. This perspective provides a foundation for understanding how institutional reforms can be leveraged to promote long-term economic prosperity.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that long-term economic growth is contingent on technological progress and institutional adjustments, a perspective reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the role of formal institutions such as guilds in shaping economic development in western Europe. These insights suggest that historical trends in institutional evolution, combined with technological innovation, are pivotal in determining economic trajectories over time. Van Zanden (2009, p.25) further illustrates this by showing a clear relationship between the skill premium in the late eighteenth century and subsequent economic growth, underscoring the importance of human capital and institutional frameworks in fostering long-term development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) contribute to this discussion by challenging the assumption that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced persistent underdevelopment, suggesting that historical economic performance may have been more nuanced than previously believed. This aligns with van Zanden (1999, p.18), who argues that economic development does not always correlate with improvements in the standard of living, highlighting the complexity of historical economic trends. These findings collectively indicate that long-term economic performance is shaped by a combination of technological change, institutional arrangements, and historical conditions, with implications for understanding the drivers of sustained growth across different regions and periods.

These findings are complemented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies, particularly the transatlantic slave trade, have had a lasting impact on the development of trust and economic outcomes in Africa, shaping institutional frameworks that persist to this day. This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial rule entrenched patriarchal structures and customary laws that marginalized women, thereby influencing long-term economic and social development trajectories. These findings point to a mechanism where historical events, such as the slave trade and colonial legal reforms, have created enduring patterns of mistrust and inequality that hinder institutional effectiveness and economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.2) further contributes to this understanding by examining the role of British colonial rule in shaping the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that colonial policies did not necessarily foster more benign conditions for educational development. This implies that the historical legacies of colonialism and the slave trade are not only cultural but also structural, affecting the capacity of institutions to support economic development. These insights collectively indicate that the historical conditions under which institutions were formed have significant implications for their long-term functionality and the economic trajectories of African nations. This understanding of historical legacies provides a foundation for examining how contemporary institutions interact with these enduring patterns to influence economic outcomes.

This understanding is further nuanced by Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonial power structures are not merely external impositions but are deeply entangled with the internal class dynamics of the colonizers, shaping the development of institutions in ways that extend beyond economic efficiency (E0169). This perspective highlights how distributional conflicts within colonial societies, as Ogilvie (2007, p.30) notes, are central to understanding the formation and persistence of economic institutions (E0129). These conflicts involve the distribution of resources as well as the negotiation of power and authority, which in turn influences the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) further elaborates on how such institutional frameworks are interdependent, emphasizing that they cannot be studied in isolation but must be understood as part of a broader system of interactions. This interdependency suggests that the legacy of colonial power structures continues to influence contemporary institutional arrangements, shaping the conditions under which economic growth occurs. These insights underscore the need to consider how historical power dynamics shape the trajectory of institutional development and, by extension, long-run economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the interdependent nature of institutional frameworks that influence economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Historical power dynamics, particularly those stemming from colonial rule, have left a lasting imprint on contemporary institutional arrangements, which in turn affect the conditions for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The legacy of colonial power structures is particularly evident in the institutional frameworks that emerged in former colonies, where the type of institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is supported by the observation that the institutional changes caused by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36). The extent to which institutions are shaped by historical legacies versus contemporary reforms remains a point of contention. While some argue that the extractive institutions established during colonial rule continue to hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), others emphasize the potential for institutional change driven by modern reforms and economic development (Frankema 2012, p.17). The empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across different regions and time periods, as seen in the analysis of pre-industrial Europe and African colonies, provides a basis for evaluating the relative influence of historical legacies versus contemporary reforms on economic growth.
