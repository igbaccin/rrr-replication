The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical understanding. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued to be pivotal in determining whether societies experience sustained growth or stagnation. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary causal force behind economic outcomes or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical events, play a more fundamental role. This question is not merely academic; it shapes how policymakers approach institutional reform and how historians interpret the trajectories of economic development. Some scholars emphasize the direct and enduring influence of institutions on economic performance, while others propose alternative explanations that may operate independently of institutional quality. This literature review explores these competing perspectives, highlighting the diverse ways in which institutions are conceptualized and their varying roles in shaping economic outcomes. The discussion begins with a stream that argues for the primacy of institutions in explaining long-run growth, drawing on comparative historical analyses and empirical studies. This stream underscores how different institutional frameworks, such as those established during colonial periods, have had lasting impacts on economic development. The next section considers alternative explanations that challenge the dominance of institutional variables, offering insights into how other factors may interact with or even override institutional effects. These perspectives are not mutually exclusive, and their integration provides a more nuanced understanding of the complex forces that drive economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Building on the contrast between extractive and inclusive institutions highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), the focus now turns to how institutional quality influences economic outcomes in different historical contexts. This perspective is reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutional commitment in seventeenth-century England, noting that the success of economic growth was tied to the degree of commitment to enforceable rules. A key mechanism through which institutions drive growth is by shaping the incentives of politically powerful groups, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who explain that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if they serve the interests of dominant groups rather than promoting long-run growth. This dynamic is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments, with countries that established secure property rights and effective governance achieving sustained growth earlier than others. These findings collectively underscore the central role of institutions in fostering economic development, as they influence the distribution of power, the security of property rights, and the capacity for sustained investment. The implications of these insights extend to understanding how institutional quality can be a decisive factor in shaping economic trajectories, setting the stage for examining how specific institutional features interact with other determinants of growth.

While the previous discussion emphasized institutional frameworks, Kuznets (1973, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) expand the analysis by incorporating the role of technological progress and formal institutions in facilitating economic development. These institutions, while beneficial in promoting skill development, also faced criticism for forming local cartels that could distort market dynamics. This interplay between institutional structures and technological progress is further underscored by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who challenge the assumption of persistent underdevelopment in Sub-Saharan Africa by presenting evidence of higher per capita incomes before the 1980s. Their findings suggest that economic performance in Africa has been more nuanced than previously thought, with policy changes during the 1980s and 1990s contributing to subsequent growth. This indicates that institutional and ideological adjustments are not only historical phenomena but also continue to shape economic trajectories in different regions. The historical analysis by van Zanden (1999, p.18) further complicates the relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe, revealing no clear-cut correlation between economic growth and real wage increases between 1500 and 1800. This suggests that the impact of institutional changes on economic outcomes may be context-dependent and influenced by a range of historical and geographical factors. These insights collectively emphasize the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-term economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of how these dynamics interact with other economic determinants.

The discussion of historical legacies, as illustrated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), underscores how past events continue to shape contemporary economic conditions through their influence on trust and social cohesion. This perspective is supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights the enduring consequences of colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that these historical experiences have shaped institutional and social structures that continue to influence economic outcomes. The mechanisms through which these legacies operate are multifaceted, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) emphasizing the role of both internal and external factors in shaping trust levels, which in turn affect economic development. Peters (2004, p.5) further elaborates on how land competition and exclusionary practices during colonial rule contributed to class formation, rather than adaptive customary systems, reinforcing the idea that historical legacies are deeply embedded in contemporary economic conditions. These findings collectively illustrate how past events have left a profound imprint on the trajectory of economic development in Africa, with implications that extend beyond immediate historical contexts. This understanding of historical legacies provides a critical lens through which to examine the broader role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth.

Stoler (1989, p.6) further elaborates on the enduring effects of colonialism by examining how it structured power relations and production systems, thereby influencing institutional development in the long term. This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial rule institutionalized patriarchal alliances and customary law, reinforcing the subordinate status of women and reshaping economic practices. These mechanisms illustrate how colonial legacies created enduring social and economic structures that influenced long-run growth trajectories. For instance, the codification of customary law under colonial rule marginalized women from land ownership and economic participation, as evidenced by the relegation of women to marginal lands for food farming, which constrained their ability to engage in cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Similarly, Simson (2019, p.11) notes how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were shaped by colonial-era financial practices, such as the reliance on external borrowing and the prioritization of military spending, which continued to affect economic development after independence. These patterns demonstrate how colonial institutions left a lasting imprint on economic structures, influencing resource allocation and governance even after formal colonial rule ended. This understanding of colonial legacies provides a critical lens for examining how historical institutions shape long-term economic outcomes, setting the stage for further exploration of how post-colonial reforms interact with these inherited structures.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the argument that they are the primary determinant of income per capita, with colonial legacies leaving a lasting imprint on economic structures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of extractive institutions, designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, illustrates how historical institutional arrangements continue to affect contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Furthermore, the role of institutions in fostering or hindering development is not solely determined by external factors such as geography or ethnic fragmentation, but also by the quality of governance and the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical contribution of African women to economic growth, often overlooked, highlights the importance of inclusive institutions in promoting development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The ongoing presence of mistrust, which originated in the slave trade, poses a challenge to the effectiveness of institutions and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The historical evidence of how institutions evolved in response to colonial rule, such as the establishment of dual legal systems, provides a comparative framework for understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These findings underscore the complex interplay between historical institutions and economic growth, with the specific historical evidence of institutional changes offering a clear empirical basis for assessing their long-term impact.
