The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the primary determinant of income per capita and long-term growth trajectories. Others emphasize the interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography, culture, and technology, suggesting that institutions do not operate in isolation. A key intellectual tension revolves around whether institutions are the principal cause of economic development or whether they interact with other variables in complex ways. This review explores these streams of scholarship, focusing on how institutions are conceptualized and their causal relationships with economic outcomes. It begins with the argument that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, as advanced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), and then considers alternative perspectives that highlight the interplay between institutions and other factors. The discussion continues with an examination of how institutions are shaped by historical processes and their implications for economic development. Each section synthesizes multiple sources to provide a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in long-run growth. This approach allows for a more comprehensive exploration of the topic, highlighting both the central claims and the complexities that arise in the literature. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) note that one key avenue for the codification of the subordinate status of women in the colonial era was through customary law, which they describe as patriarchal alliances and customary law: the invention of custom.

Building on this institutional focus, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering commitment and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective is further reinforced by the mechanisms through which institutions shape long-term economic outcomes, as seen in the contrasting institutional legacies of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups rather than to encourage economic development, which aligns with North (1989, p.6), who highlights how political and economic institutions significantly influence growth by specifying efficient property rights and facilitating market exchanges. These insights reveal that the nature of institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, directly affects the trajectory of economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that institutions and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World, illustrating how institutional frameworks interact with other factors to influence growth. This shared finding underscores the centrality of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, a theme that continues to be explored in subsequent analyses of institutional development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have shaped long-term economic outcomes in Africa by fostering a persistent culture of mistrust, which negatively affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Their analysis shows that the slave trade's impact on trust operates through both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law, but the internal channel is at least twice as significant as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding aligns with Peters (2004, p.5), who highlights how land competition in sub-Saharan Africa has led to exclusion and class formation, revealing how historical processes have structured contemporary economic and social relations (Peters 2004, p.5). Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this by demonstrating that the slave trades had an adverse effect on long-term economic development, contributing to high levels of ethnic fractionalization that hinder growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). These insights underscore how historical legacies, such as the slave trade and land competition, have created enduring patterns of mistrust and exclusion that continue to influence economic trajectories. This historical context provides a foundation for understanding how institutions, shaped by these legacies, may interact with other factors in shaping economic outcomes, setting the stage for further exploration of institutional development.

Stoler (1989, p.6) challenges conventional understandings of colonial impacts by highlighting how colonialism simultaneously shaped both the colonizer and the colonized, necessitating a reevaluation of European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). These findings suggest that colonialism was an interactive process that reconfigured social and economic relations within both the colonizing and colonized societies. Frankema (2012, p.2) contributes to this reevaluation by showing that British colonial rule did not lead to more benign educational outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa, as previously assumed, but instead entangled educational development with exogenous ecological and historical factors (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.17). This underscores the complexity of colonial impacts, revealing how they were shaped by a range of interrelated forces rather than being straightforwardly determined by colonial policies alone. Such insights call for a more nuanced understanding of how historical narratives and colonial legacies have interacted with local conditions to shape long-term economic trajectories.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that sustained economic growth is contingent upon technological progress and the necessary institutional and ideological adaptations that accompany it, emphasizing the interplay between innovation and structural change. This perspective aligns with van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds in western Europe, played a role in facilitating the transfer of skills, although their impact was complicated by potential market distortions. Similarly, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the assumption that African economic development was solely shaped by colonial interventions, suggesting that pre-colonial conditions and internal dynamics may have contributed to economic trajectories. These findings collectively suggest that while institutions are important, they are not the sole determinants of long-term economic performance, as technological change and historical income levels also play significant roles. Mokyr (2005, p.3) further elaborates on the importance of knowledge and rationality in driving growth, linking the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge to the origins of modern economic expansion. This indicates that the interplay between technological innovation, knowledge diffusion, and institutional frameworks is complex and multifaceted, shaping economic outcomes in ways that go beyond the influence of any single factor. Such insights underscore the need for a broader understanding of economic development that integrates multiple dimensions of historical and institutional change.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the extensive literature on the subject. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita, highlighting how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions that significantly influenced economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes caused by colonialism, emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not conducive to long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The importance of institutions is further underscored by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize that constitutional rules and institutions are crucial for fostering commitment and economic growth, particularly in seventeenth-century England. Their analysis shows that the degree of commitment by the regime to existing rules significantly influenced economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, the role of institutions is not limited to formal legal frameworks; Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) highlight how customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, thereby affecting economic development in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

While the role of institutions is widely recognized, the specific mechanisms through which they influence growth remain a subject of debate. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development, suggesting that internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This contrasts with the view that institutional frameworks alone are sufficient to drive growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who emphasize the long-term consequences of institutional differences established during colonization (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence of institutional differences and their economic outcomes, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), provides a clear empirical comparison that settles the disagreement by showing the lasting impact of institutional frameworks on economic development.
