The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy design and historical interpretation. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure economic and social interactions, are increasingly viewed as pivotal in shaping economic trajectories. This debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary determinants of growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or external shocks, play a more decisive role. The literature explores how institutions influence economic outcomes through mechanisms such as property rights, rule of law, and the quality of governance. Some scholars emphasize the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies, particularly those shaped by colonialism, while others highlight the contingent nature of institutional effects, suggesting that their influence varies with context, such as the presence of trust or the structure of social networks. This discussion highlights the complexity of institutional effects, showing that their role is influenced by a variety of interacting factors. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for identifying pathways to sustained economic development. This review explores key streams of scholarship that address these issues, beginning with the foundational argument that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering commitment and economic development. This perspective is reinforced by the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of vastly different institutional frameworks, which in turn shaped long-term economic outcomes in former colonies. For instance, in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to transfer resources back to the metropole. These differences in institutional quality are not merely historical accidents but reflect the strategic choices made by those in power, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note, with equilibrium institutions often designed to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth. This insight highlights the importance of understanding how institutions evolve and how they can be shaped by the interests of dominant elites. The implications of these findings are significant, as they suggest that the quality of institutions is a crucial factor in explaining divergent paths of economic development across regions and time periods. These insights set the stage for examining how specific institutional features, such as property rights and political constraints, interact with other economic determinants to influence growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that it demands (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective is complemented by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the role of formal institutions such as guilds in shaping economic outcomes during the late medieval period (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions, while beneficial for the intergenerational transfer of skills, also faced criticism for restricting entry into trades and maintaining pay above market levels. The interplay between technological progress and institutional change is further illustrated by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who emphasizes the Enlightenment's contribution to modern economic growth through the promotion of useful knowledge and rationality (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This intellectual shift provided a foundation for more efficient allocation of resources and the establishment of legal frameworks that supported economic activity. Stern (2004, p.1) contributes to this discussion by examining the environmental Kuznets curve, although he argues that its statistical foundation is weak and that alternative models may better explain the relationship between economic development and environmental outcomes (Stern 2004, p.1). These findings collectively suggest that economic growth is a multifaceted process influenced by technological innovation, institutional evolution, and ideological shifts, each of which plays a critical role in shaping long-term development trajectories.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) highlights that historical power dynamics significantly influence the development of economic institutions, particularly in pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts shaped institutional structures more than economic efficiency. These findings suggest that the historical context and power relations within societies play a crucial role in shaping the institutional frameworks that influence economic development. Additionally, Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism created complex power dynamics that affected both the colonizers and the colonized, shaping the internal structures of colonial authority and European class cultures. This implies that understanding the historical evolution of institutions requires examining the broader social and political contexts in which they emerge. These insights contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how institutions are not only shaped by economic factors but also by historical and power-related dynamics, which in turn influence long-term economic growth. This approach provides a foundation for further exploration of how these historical legacies interact with contemporary institutional reforms and economic policies.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa has had a persistent negative impact on economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust that continues to affect intergroup relations. This finding is supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights how historical legacies, such as British colonial rule, have shaped the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa in ways that may have constrained long-term development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further elaborate that the effects of the slave trade on trust operate through both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law, with the internal channel being more significant. This suggests that the psychological and social consequences of historical events can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Peters (2004, p.5) adds that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals patterns of exclusion and class formation, which are not simply the result of adaptive customary systems but are deeply rooted in historical processes. These findings collectively indicate that historical legacies such as the slave trade and colonialism have long-term negative effects on economic development, shaping institutions, social norms, and economic structures in ways that continue to influence contemporary outcomes. This perspective provides a foundation for understanding how historical events contribute to the persistence of underdevelopment in certain regions.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, challenging the views of Pomeranz and Parthasarathi, and this perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who similarly revise historical economic narratives by demonstrating that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s. These findings are part of a broader effort to reassess long-standing assumptions about economic underdevelopment in Africa, suggesting that the continent's economic performance was more favorable than previously assumed. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) contribute to this revision by highlighting the significant economic contributions of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods, which were often overlooked in historical narratives. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further support this revision by showing that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, contradicting the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These studies collectively indicate that historical economic narratives in Africa have been shaped by biases and incomplete data, leading to an underestimation of the continent's economic potential and resilience. This revised understanding of Africa's economic history underscores the importance of re-evaluating the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as it suggests that historical legacies may not be as deterministic as previously believed.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by multiple studies that emphasize their influence over other factors such as geography or historical legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods, whether extractive or inclusive, has had a lasting impact on economic development, with inclusive institutions fostering sustained growth. Similarly, the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlights how historical events, such as the slave trade, have influenced economic development through their effects on trust and social cohesion, which are integral to the functioning of institutions. These findings suggest that the quality of institutions is a critical determinant of economic outcomes. The role of institutions can be complex, as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) demonstrates, emphasizing the interdependence of various institutions within broader frameworks and indicating that the effectiveness of one institution may rely on the presence of others. This interdependence complicates the understanding of how institutions drive growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) provide historical evidence of how institutional changes, such as those related to land rights and gender roles, have influenced economic development in Africa, showing that institutions are not static but have evolved over time. This evolution underscores the need to consider the historical context in which institutions operate. The empirical comparison of institutional quality across regions, as used by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), provides a measurable framework to assess the impact of institutions on economic growth, offering a concrete basis for evaluating their role.
