The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it addresses whether and how the rules, norms, and organizations that govern societies influence their economic trajectories. The stakes are high intellectually because understanding this relationship can provide insights into why some countries prosper while others stagnate or decline. At its core, the debate centers around two main positions: one emphasizes institutions as the primary driver of economic growth, while the other highlights the role of geographic factors and initial conditions. Proponents of institutional primacy argue that inclusive institutions fostering secure property rights, rule of law, and political stability are crucial for sustained economic development. Those focusing on geographic factors contend that natural endowments, climate, and disease environments play a more significant role in determining long-run growth. The interplay between these positions is complex, with some scholars suggesting that institutions mediate the impact of geography, while others view geography as a more direct determinant. This disagreement underscores the need to examine how institutions operate within different historical and geographical contexts to influence economic outcomes over the long run.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. This perspective is complemented by North's work, which underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15), however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more nuanced than straightforward causal models might suggest.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The establishment of settler colonies led to the creation of institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment, fostering economic development over time. Extractive states were established in regions with high European mortality rates to quickly transfer resources back to the metropole and promote local growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2002, p.33) argue that these institutions persisted long after colonial rule ended and continue to shape economic outcomes today.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show how differences in European settlement patterns led to varied institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced the trajectory of economic growth in former colonies. The interaction between European colonization strategies and local conditions resulted in diverse institutional landscapes that significantly impacted long-run development. This finding underscores the importance of initial institutions in shaping future economic pathways. The interplay between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization played a central role in determining long-run economic development. Thus, understanding the legacy of colonial institutions is crucial for grasping the current economic disparities observed across former colonies.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. For instance, Ogilvie and Landes both identify beliefs as part of an institutional structure, but they diverge on the role these beliefs play in shaping economic outcomes. The differences between these perspectives highlight the complexity of understanding how beliefs influence economic systems: for Landes, culture is a driving force behind Europe's economic success. However, Ogilvie suggests that the economic institutions themselves were more directly shaped by distributional conflicts rather than cultural values (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), this distinction underscores the importance of considering multiple factors in assessing institutional development. The implications are clear: understanding the role of distributional conflicts is crucial for comprehending how institutions influence long-run economic growth, as these conflicts can drive the formation and evolution of economic systems.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for reducing transaction costs. The Bank of England's creation and the introduction of new financial instruments, such as long-term bonds, were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. This financial revolution enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is essential for both a robust capital market and sustained economic development. Furthermore, the political institutions that specified and enforced these property rights played a critical role in this process. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) illustrate that England's success was due to both its economic performance and the underlying institutional changes. This perspective is further supported by the evidence from capital markets, which shows that the institutional framework in England was more conducive to economic growth than previously thought. North (1989, p.6) recognizes that institutions are not the sole factor at play; as economies grow, increasing specialization and division of labor multiply aggregate exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP. This implies that while institutions are crucial for reducing transaction costs, they must also adapt to the evolving complexity of economic systems.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that long before 1800, Europe had already begun to diverge from Asia economically, driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. The high productivity in the traded goods sector of north-western Europe was due to merchant distribution and finance, which played a crucial role during this period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden (1999, p.1) shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. This implies that while merchant distribution and finance were significant drivers, they did not translate directly into improved living standards for all (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.16). Pamuk (2007, p.23) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes in Europe, leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This suggests that while merchant distribution and finance were important, they operated within a broader context of institutional flexibility and structural change (Pamuk 2007, p.16; van Zanden 1999, p.9).

These factors operated within a broader context: the growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49; van Zanden 2009, p.19). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that the expansion of scientific societies and journals facilitated the dissemination of knowledge, reducing access costs and fostering innovation. This institutional support for knowledge sharing was instrumental in driving technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) complements this view by noting that technological advancements often require institutional adjustments to be efficiently integrated into the economy. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) qualifies this perspective by noting that while institutions like guilds played a role in skill transfer, they also acted as local cartels, potentially restricting entry and inflating wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dual role of institutions suggests that while they can facilitate technological progress, their impact on economic growth is complex and context-dependent. Institutions must balance the benefits of knowledge dissemination with potential barriers to entry to ensure sustained economic performance.

Nunn argues that Africa's economic development has been significantly impeded by the legacy of slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade affected immediate economic outcomes and left lasting impacts on institutions and social norms. Nunn and Wantchekon complement this perspective by showing how these effects are independent from external factors such as the rule of law or trustworthiness of others, instead driven by internal norms of mistrust (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This culture of mistrust significantly hinders economic development, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The importance of trust in economic development is well-documented. Sokoloff and Engerman further elaborate that the extreme inequality resulting from the slave trades led to institutional structures that favored elites at the expense of broader competition, thus impeding growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Nunn notes that some societies like the Kongo kingdom were able to support trade despite the presence of slave trades due to their centralized government and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while slavery had a profound adverse effect on economic development, its impact was not uniform across all African societies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show how British colonial rule influenced economic performance through education. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, driving demand for missionary education (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15). These changes led to a growing market for exports, which tied tightly to national economies' growth patterns. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that poverty in Africa has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered, as the persistence of poverty is still unclear (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies African economic history by compressing different historical periods and paths. When settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with their fiscal interests. This dynamic led to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elucidates this by showing how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but also by the ability to develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that African economies have the potential for growth if they can establish effective institutions that provide incentives for investment, a crucial factor in development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by both North and Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that the quality of institutions determines the incentives for investment. Institutions are the rules of the game, which shape economic performance. They affect economic outcomes through their impact on property rights and transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure private property rights, facilitated by effective political institutions, is critical to long-term economic growth. The English financial revolution, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which was crucial for both capital markets and growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores how institutional changes can foster economic development by reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, setting up either inclusive or extractive states based on the local conditions they encountered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how institutional frameworks directly influence long-term economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and growth.

The historical context of Africa further illustrates the importance of institutions in economic development. Hopkins argues that African economies have the potential for growth if they can establish effective institutions that provide incentives for investment, a crucial factor in development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on how extractive institutions hinder economic progress. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but also by the ability to develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding underscores that effective institutions are essential for fostering investment and growth, regardless of cultural or ethnic factors. The historical evidence from Africa shows that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous yet still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. Therefore, institutional quality is a key determinant of economic performance, as it shapes the incentives for investment and growth.
