The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, as it addresses how societies can foster enduring prosperity. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions that aim to promote sustained economic development. One live disagreement centres on the nature and impact of institutions themselves. Some scholars emphasize the efficiency-driven evolution of institutions, while others highlight distributional conflicts and shared beliefs as key drivers. Additionally, there is a debate over whether European settlement patterns significantly influenced institutional outcomes in colonized regions.

The literature on how institutions shape economic growth includes evidence that differences in colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups. Acemoglu et al. argue that the presence of European settlers was crucial: where they settled, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin's work challenges this perspective by suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation and compresses different historical periods and paths; colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions. This adaptation could lead to higher production equilibria, contrary to hypotheses that focus solely on European settlement patterns (Austin 2008, p.15).

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are instrumental in shaping long-term economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and securing property rights. Secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are pivotal in reducing costs per transaction, thereby encouraging economic growth through increased specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework underpins Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers, significantly influenced the long-term development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Specifically, extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often hindered economic growth in these regions. These findings underscore how institutional frameworks can either foster or impede long-term economic growth depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argued that the development of credible commitment mechanisms, such as the Glorious Revolution, ensured that political leaders were bound by rules that protected private property. These institutions reduced transaction costs and encouraged investment in capital markets, contributing to long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate this with their analysis of New World societies, where the presence or absence of secure property rights significantly influenced economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how these institutional frameworks, shaped by initial factor endowments and colonial policies, perpetuated inequality over time. This underscores the critical role of institutional design in economic outcomes, particularly when considering how different societies evolved from similar starting points to vastly different levels of prosperity (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts are pivotal to grasping pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie and Austin both show how the institutional frameworks in pre-industrial societies were shaped by the interests of dominant groups. The institutional evolution in pre-industrial Europe was driven by these conflicts and a pursuit of optimal economic outcomes. Austin's analysis highlights how colonial administrations adapted their policies to maintain control over resources and labour, illustrating that institutions were shaped by the need for social stability and political legitimacy (Austin 2008, p.15).

This perspective challenges the idea that economic efficiency was the primary driver of institutional change, suggesting instead that institutions were often designed to reinforce existing power structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin's work supports this view by showing how colonial policies were adjusted in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating that institutions evolved to manage distributional conflicts and to maximise economic efficiency. This dynamic is evident in the way institutions like serfdom and guilds were maintained despite their potential inefficiencies, as they served to preserve the interests of dominant groups over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). These findings underscore how distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa engendered a lasting culture of mistrust. This historical trauma has had profound effects on contemporary African societies, shaping their economic development. The persistent low levels of trust are not merely a residual effect of other factors like colonialism or recent events; they are directly linked to the legacy of the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2). Moreover, this mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals, with internal norms of mistrust being at least twice as significant as external environmental factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. This internalisation of mistrust has far-reaching implications for economic development, as it permeates social interactions and undermines cooperative endeavors essential for growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). However, while Nunn highlights that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, he does not address the extent to which this mistrust might have been mitigated by institutional reforms or changes in governance structures.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, emphasizing that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not inherently determine the quality of public goods or the rule of law. The extent to which institutions in Africa are dysfunctional is linked to a variety of factors beyond ethnic diversity, including colonial legacies and governance structures (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that colonial policies could create conditions for institutional development if they aligned with local demand and cost-benefit calculations. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that economic performance was influenced by a mix of factors including transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This implies that while colonial rule could set certain conditions for institutional development, local initiatives and adaptations played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr's emphasis on useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth is complemented by Kuznets' observation that technological changes require institutional adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technology and institutions suggests a mutually reinforcing relationship where technological innovations drive economic growth, which in turn necessitates institutional adaptations to sustain this growth. Institutional progress complements technological advancements, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable over the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights how modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, necessitating significant societal adjustments. These adjustments must be managed carefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society, which could hinder economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating technological progress but does not diminish the central role of knowledge accumulation as the driving force behind long-term economic growth.

North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic development. Concurrently, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita by hindering investment and economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings highlight how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies establishing rule-of-law-enforcing institutions while others developed extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This disparity in institutional frameworks illustrates how political decisions and power dynamics shape economic trajectories over the long run. The historical evidence of colonial policies and their enduring effects on current economic conditions shows that institutions are indispensable instruments for understanding long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
