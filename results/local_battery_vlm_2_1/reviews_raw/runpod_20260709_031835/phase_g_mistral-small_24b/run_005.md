The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake is whether institutional frameworks can be credibly identified as causal drivers of sustained economic performance, or if they are merely symptoms of deeper structural factors. A live disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions themselves drive economic outcomes, versus acting as intermediaries for other underlying causes such as geography or culture. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on economic growth, highlighting how secure property rights and effective governance can foster investment and innovation (North 1989, p.6). Others argue that institutional changes are often responses to broader historical forces, suggesting a more complex interplay between institutions and economic development.

Institutions matter because they shape the incentives and constraints under which individuals operate. For example, North and Weingast identify credible commitment as a key factor in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who argue that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, with some colonies establishing extractive states while others developed institutions that encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The divergence in these institutional paths has had lasting effects on economic development. Moreover, the historical context in which institutions evolve is crucial. North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England highlights how political factors, such as credible commitment underpinned by secure property rights, were instrumental in driving economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underscores the idea that institutions are not static but evolve in response to political and economic pressures.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies, as seen in Acemoglu et al.'s work (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In their study of colonialism's impact on institutional development, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established extractive states in high-mortality regions and settler institutions in low-mortality areas. This divergence in institutions was instrumental in determining the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies. Extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists persistently hindered development by enforcing property rights and the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Settler institutions fostered an environment conducive to economic growth by encouraging investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence was a direct outcome of colonial policies. The population density and prosperity in these regions influenced the strategies pursued by European colonizers, which in turn shaped the institutions that were established (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). High mortality rates led to extractive states focused on resource extraction, while lower mortality rates allowed for more stable settler societies. The resulting institutional frameworks had profound and lasting effects on economic development.

The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This development reflected an increased security of property rights over time, a critical factor for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War was pivotal in this institutional evolution. This dynamic is central to economic growth: the size of the transactions sector grows proportionally with GNP as markets become more sophisticated. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that while institutions play a critical role in economic performance, other factors such as bureaucratic structures can also influence outcomes.

Shifting focus to contemporary Africa, the literature on colonial legacies and economic policies explores how historical governance structures shaped development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can provide valuable insights into institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This perspective aligns with Frankema's (2012, p.15) findings on formal education, which demonstrates that British colonial rule fostered educational development more effectively than other colonial powers. The mechanism through which this occurred involved changes in social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, highlighting how institutional frameworks influenced individual decisions and broader societal outcomes (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this perspective by noting that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed. This suggests that while colonial institutions laid a foundational role, post-colonial governance structures also played a critical part in shaping economic development trajectories.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting social and economic dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show this mistrust is internalised within individuals and communities. They argue the legacy of slavery has left deep-rooted norms of mistrust which persist across generations in Africa, with significant implications for contemporary economic outcomes. This mistrust manifests both internally and externally, affecting interpersonal relationships as well as broader societal structures such as the rule of law. Austin (2008, p.2) offers a nuanced view by highlighting that colonial institutions, shaped in part by these mistrustful dynamics, were not uniformly detrimental. In some contexts, colonial administrations adapted their policies to encourage indigenous economic activity, demonstrating a degree of responsiveness to local incentives and actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that while the legacy of slavery created enduring challenges, post-colonial governance structures also played a critical role in shaping Africa's economic development trajectories.

This interplay between historical legacies and institutional adaptation underscores the complex nature of Africa's economic growth.

This historical context is also evident in New World societies, where Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions shaped economic development trajectories by influencing the distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power. They show that the institutions in societies with greater inequality favored elites through government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4); Broadberry and Gardner 2022 (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2)). This mechanism is evident in how factor endowments led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. This dynamic was not uniform across all New World societies; for example, regions with more egalitarian institutions saw different economic outcomes. The impact of these institutional structures on long-term growth varied based on local conditions and policies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further demonstrate that the timing and scale of export production, closely tied to foreign trade, influenced economic development in Anglophone African economies. This underscores how specific institutional frameworks and market dynamics can drive or hinder growth.

Building on this framework, Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.49) posits that technological progress is the primary driver of long-term economic growth. This perspective highlights how advancements in technology and knowledge creation necessitate specific institutional adjustments to harness their full potential. Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores this by noting that epochal innovations, which are major breakthroughs in human knowledge, have historically constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These technological shifts require corresponding institutional changes to mitigate conflicts arising from economic growth and ensure the sustainability of development. For instance, the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in resolving growth-induced conflicts without weakening society's political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of complementary institutional developments to sustain economic growth driven by technological advancements. For example, the creation of scholarly societies and formal institutions aimed at reducing access costs were crucial in facilitating the diffusion of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49).

Institutional development plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, which are critical for market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, some designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, others extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutions significantly influenced current income per capita across these regions, demonstrating how institutional design directly impacts economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation and compresses different historical periods and paths; he shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping long-run growth. The creation of financial instruments like those developed during the English financial revolution exemplifies how institutions can dramatically reduce transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

North (1989, p.9) argues that economic growth is fundamentally linked to the evolution of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.29), who highlight how the political factors underpinning economic growth include not just the rules governing exchange, but also the institutions enforcing these rules and the commitment to them (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illustrate this by showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This points to a fundamental agreement: institutions matter significantly in shaping economic growth trajectories. The precise mechanisms and historical contexts through which institutions exert their influence remain a subject of debate. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations adapted institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation highlights how local responses can shape institutional outcomes and long-term growth. The empirical comparison between European colonies with different settlement patterns demonstrates the varying effects of institutions on economic development; high mortality rates led to extractive institutions, while lower mortality facilitated the establishment of more growth-promoting institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).
