The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy and explain historical trajectories. There exists a live disagreement about whether institutions matter for economic growth as much as other factors, such as culture or geography. This debate involves scholars who argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth due to their role in securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). On the other hand, some researchers propose that distributional conflicts are a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This debate is crucial because it determines whether institutional reforms can drive economic growth or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs and values, are more significant. The resolution of this disagreement has implications for how economists and policymakers approach development strategies in both historical and contemporary contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The authors demonstrate that the mortality rates of Europeans led to different colonization strategies and associated institutions. In areas with high European mortality, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to these institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. In this context, institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes today, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary development trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth. They show the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically declined transaction costs by increasing security in property rights over time. This pattern reflects increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The evolution of these institutions was pivotal in establishing secure property rights, fostering economic growth by ensuring political commitment to rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

This evidence suggests that the institutional changes were critical for economic development. The success of these institutions was remarkable, as shown by capital market evidence. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates on this point by emphasizing that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security over time (North 1989, p.9). This highlights the importance of credible commitments in economic growth.

Colonial rule in Africa significantly influenced institutional change through the negotiation and imposition of colonial policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions were shaped by the interactions between African societies and colonial powers. In particular, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa was contingent on a complex interplay of factors, including the perceived benefits of education as determined by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the success of colonial institutions hinged on their ability to align with local needs. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this point by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This indicates that colonial institutions could foster economic growth under certain conditions, such as when they facilitated export production and integrated with local endowments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The variability in export production patterns across different countries highlights the importance of specific political and institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes during this period (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Simson (2019, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that the role of women in African economic development is often overlooked. The paper shows how colonial institutions impacted land access for women, who were relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This gendered division of labor was reinforced by customary law under colonial rule and had significant implications for economic growth. The codification of customary law during this period solidified the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to resources crucial for economic advancement. This institutional framework shaped agricultural practices and influenced broader economic policies. Simson (2019, p.11) illustrates how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were heavily influenced by these colonial legacies. The shift from imperial subsidies to domestic military spending and the high debt burden reflect the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic decision-making. This pattern underscores how institutional frameworks established during colonial rule continue to shape long-run economic growth trajectories, even as new policies emerge post-independence.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). They propose a mechanism where the transatlantic slave trade created an environment of mistrust between ethnic groups, which has persisted over centuries and continues to affect economic outcomes. This culture of mistrust is not merely an artifact of historical events but a deeply ingrained social norm that influences contemporary behaviors and attitudes. The slave trade's impact on trust extends beyond intergroup dynamics; it also affects intra-group relationships and individual perceptions of trustworthiness (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the legacy of the slave trade, are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law or the perceived trustworthiness of others. This internalization of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Austin qualifies this mechanism by noting that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). While the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is significant, the institutional changes imposed by colonial governments also played a role in shaping African economies. This adaptation suggests that while the culture of mistrust is a persistent factor, it interacts with other historical and institutional variables. The cultural norms established during this period continue to influence contemporary economic behaviors, illustrating the complex interplay between historical events and modern economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts are the primary drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie argues that institutional structures such as serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution rather than efficiency. This perspective contrasts with the view that institutions are primarily driven by shared beliefs or exogenous cultural factors. These distributional conflicts led to institutions that reflected the interests of dominant groups, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument by showing how inequalities in New World societies advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. They note that differences in factor endowments across colonies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that affected long-term economic development. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts shape institutions and influence their enduring impact on economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms through which these conflicts translate into institutional change can vary widely. In some cases, as highlighted by Ogilvie, institutions were designed to maintain power and control over resources, while in others, they served to perpetuate social hierarchies and limit economic opportunities for marginalized groups. This variation underscores the complexity of distributional conflicts and their multifaceted influence on institutional development.

Institutional development is fundamentally shaped by distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. highlight that institutions often reflect the power dynamics of politically dominant groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This aligns with Ogilvie's argument that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were designed to maintain control over resources and perpetuate social hierarchies, rather than being driven by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These findings underscore the significance of power dynamics in shaping institutional frameworks. The impact of these conflicts is not confined to their initial establishment but extends to their enduring effects on economic outcomes (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further underscored by the interaction between property rights and transaction costs. North posits that secure property rights are essential for reducing transaction costs, which in turn fosters economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that extractive institutions can persist due to the interests of politically powerful groups, even if these institutions hinder long-term growth. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique aligns with North's emphasis on secure property rights as a critical dimension in reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Despite the consensus on the importance of institutions, there is disagreement over the specific mechanisms through which distributional conflicts translate into institutional change. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Austin shows that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of these interactions (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This includes the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The divergence in economic outcomes among former European colonies underscores the role of these institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
