The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics and economic history. The stakes are high because the answer informs policy debates about whether institutional reforms can drive sustained economic development. On one side, scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and secure markets are crucial for fostering growth. On the other hand, there are those who contend that distributional conflicts and political dynamics play a more significant role in shaping core economic institutions. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for how governments approach economic policy and development strategies.

There is a live disagreement between scholars who view institutions as primary drivers of growth versus those who see them as secondary to other factors such as distributional conflicts or political dynamics. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, which in turn influenced long-term economic development. Similarly, North emphasizes that institutions matter because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms, which are critical for a growing economy (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions as the primary driver of growth lacks universal acceptance. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that institutional evolution is more complex than it appears in some models (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth involves multiple factors including indigenous economic and political action. Ogilvie also challenges the efficiency-driven view of institutions, arguing instead that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective shifts the focus from institutional efficiency to the underlying power dynamics and conflicts within societies.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled. While some scholars emphasize the importance of property rights and secure markets, others point to distributional conflicts and political dynamics as key factors. This tension highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other economic and social factors to drive growth.

Building on this, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) identify secure property rights as a linchpin for economic growth. Their argument hinges on how political institutions shaped economic outcomes by securing long-term property rights, which facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. The establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 1694 exemplifies this dynamic (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework reduced transaction costs and fostered a stable environment for long-term capital markets. This institutional framework was instrumental in driving England's economic growth by providing a stable environment for long-term investments. North and Weingast also emphasize that the success of these institutions depends on the regime's commitment to enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The stability provided by secure property rights is part of a broader institutional framework that includes effective enforcement mechanisms. This highlights how the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth through secure property rights and effective governance, setting the stage for sustained long-term economic development.

Acemoglu et al.'s research on colonial institutions shows that European colonizers introduced diverse institutional frameworks in their colonies, which significantly shaped long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions ranged from extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole to more inclusive ones that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation was driven by European mortality rates and settlement patterns, where high mortality led to extractive institutions. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The persistence of these institutions is deeply rooted in initial colonial strategies and the political dynamics they engendered. Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights how the nature of colonial rule, particularly the institutional legacies it left behind, continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). For instance, regions that experienced extractive institutions during colonial times often struggle with weak property rights and ineffective governance, which hinder long-term economic growth. This dynamic is evident in countries like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where extractive states were established, leading to persistent underdevelopment.

Similarly, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) shared that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These conflicts led to the establishment of institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, which were more influenced by power dynamics than efficiency considerations. The specific mechanisms through which these distributional conflicts operated varied widely. For example, in regions where elites held significant power, they often implemented policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance, as seen in colonial Latin America, where institutions favored the elite class and perpetuated inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic highlights how institutional arrangements can be skewed to benefit those with political influence, thereby shaping long-term economic outcomes. Ogilvie's observation nuances this perspective by noting that shared beliefs within a society also contribute to its institutional structure, suggesting cultural factors are intertwined with power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interplay between distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs underscores the complexity of institution-building processes.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn and Austin highlight the historical significance of African slave trades spanning nearly 500 years. This prolonged period is far longer than colonial rule which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to about 1960. Nunn's evidence shows that the impact of these trades on economic development was profound. The Kongo kingdom, for example, had a centralized government and well-developed trading networks, which facilitated trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that pre-colonial institutions could have supported economic growth if not undermined by slave trades an implication that Austin's analysis qualifies. Austin argues that colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic action and changed their institutional prescriptions accordingly. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic shows that while African institutions were capable of adapting, the legacy of the slave trade created enduring challenges for economic development.

This perspective is further nuanced by Frankema and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), who argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated the development of formal education. The demand for missionary education was driven by African political leaders' cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, indicating that Africans actively sought out these educational opportunities. This active engagement with formal education under British colonial rule suggests that the institutional framework established during this period created a foundation for economic development. This framework is further supported by evidence showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), highlighting the economic impact of these educational institutions. Simson's analysis notes that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive (Simson 2019, p.2). This qualification suggests that while British colonial rule may have laid a foundation for educational development, the subsequent political and economic management of these institutions was crucial for sustaining their benefits.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets argues that the major breakthroughs in advancing human knowledge constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, and these epochal innovations spread to substantial parts of the world, driving long-term economic development. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advances, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from modernization. Kuznets highlights that only if such conflicts are resolved without excessive costs can modern economic growth be possible. The sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully resolving these growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr notes that the growth of economic reasonableness was far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress suggesting that while institutions are important for sustaining economic growth they are not as fundamental as technological advancements. This perspective underscores the idea that while institutional frameworks can facilitate or hinder economic growth, it is the growth of useful knowledge that ultimately drives long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally about how political and economic structures facilitate or hinder development. North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) emphasis on institutions designed to maximize rents, which can either foster or stifle long-term development depending on their design. Austin (2008, p.15) further illustrates this by showing how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. The shared finding is that institutions matter profoundly for economic growth. The precise mechanisms through which these operate remain a subject of debate.

This debate centers around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This view contrasts with North and Weingast's (1989, p.2) assertion that credible commitment underpins growth, suggesting that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of long-term capital market development and the role of fiscal crises in shaping institutional changes (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
