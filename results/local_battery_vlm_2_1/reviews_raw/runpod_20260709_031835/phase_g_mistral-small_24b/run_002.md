The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. The stakes are high because if institutions decisively shape growth, then the right policies could transform economies over generations. At issue is whether institutions are the primary driver of growth, or merely one factor among many. One position holds that institutions create incentives for investment by ensuring property rights and contract enforcement, thus driving economic activity. A rival view proposes that other factors, such as culture, determine which institutions get adopted in a society. Still another perspective contends that institutional reforms can be informed by historical evidence to eliminate poverty, particularly among Africa's bottom billion people. The question is whether the right policies could transform economies over generations.

One stream of scholarship argues that European settlers established different types of institutions across their colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that European mortality rates influenced the kind of institutions they set up in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In high-mortality areas, Europeans established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, whereas in low-mortality areas, they created more inclusive institutions. Austin's analysis supports this view by showing how colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic and political action, adjusting institutional prescriptions accordingly (Austin 2008, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective underscores the importance of historical context in shaping institutions, which in turn influences long-term economic outcomes.

Another stream investigates the mechanisms through which institutions operate. North argues that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth because they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view is supported by Hopkins' work on African economic history, which shows how institutional reforms can inform policy to eliminate poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.8). These authors stress that institutions influence both immediate effects and long-term implications for economic development (Author Year p.N). Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis underscores the role of colonial rule in codifying customary law, which reinforced patriarchal alliances and marginalized women’s access to land (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective illustrates how institutions shape economic outcomes as well as social structures.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial in promoting economic growth by shaping the environment for market exchanges and long-term investments. This institutional framework fostered economic transactions while ensuring political support for long-term investments. These findings highlight a pattern where initial institutional frameworks, whether colonial or domestic, have enduring effects on economic trajectories, shaping the long-term prospects of nations. This suggests that while institutions drive growth, their effects can be contingent on broader socio-economic conditions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elucidate the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and enforced (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework fosters a stable environment for economic activity, where investors can confidently engage in long-term ventures without fear of expropriation or arbitrary changes in policy. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England exemplifies how secure property rights can drive economic development. This stability in property rights and enforcement mechanisms is critical for sustained growth, as it provides the necessary predictability for market exchanges and investments.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) agree that institutions are often shaped by distributional conflicts as well as efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows how institutions like serfdom and guilds were maintained to preserve power dynamics as well as for economic efficiency. This perspective is echoed in Akyeampong and Fofack's examination of African colonial history, where patriarchal alliances and customary law codified the subordinate status of women. The mechanism by which distributional conflicts shape institutions involves the entrenchment of rules that benefit dominant groups, even if these are not the most economically efficient. This institutional framework can then limit economic growth by restricting competition and innovation. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that factor endowments also play a role in shaping institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In societies with extreme inequality, such as those in the New World colonies, institutions often evolved to protect elite interests, further reinforcing economic disparities. This dynamic underscores that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they interact with other factors like resource distribution and political power to shape institutional outcomes.

Mokyr argues that technological progress is crucial for long-run economic growth as it drives the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) echoes this idea and explains how modern technology demands institutional adjustments to efficiently use innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows that such adaptations must resolve growth-induced conflicts peacefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. This dynamic underscores that while technological progress is essential for long-run economic growth, the institutional environment must also evolve to support and sustain these advancements.

This interplay between technology and institutions is further complicated by the need for coordination to address failures in access costs and rectify policies. This implies that while technology drives growth, institutions play a critical role in facilitating its efficient use and sustaining long-term economic development. Institutions must adapt to technological changes as well as social and political realities of their time.

Institutional reforms in Africa hold significant potential for alleviating poverty by enhancing economic outcomes, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) both argue (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These scholars highlight that targeted institutional changes can foster development by providing incentives for investment, which is essential for growth. One mechanism through which this occurs is the enhancement of public goods provision; Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that institutional quality, rather than ethnic fragmentation, is a key determinant of economic performance. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that while institutional reforms are crucial they must be tailored to specific historical and geographical contexts to ensure sustained poverty reduction (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The implication is that while institutional improvements can drive growth, their effectiveness depends on contextual adaptations.

The literature on budget policies and land systems in Africa focuses on specific institutional dynamics without directly addressing long-run economic growth (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Simson (2019, p.2) shows that East African countries' post-independence budgetary practices were not as unproductive as commonly perceived. Budget allocations, particularly in Kenya and Tanzania, saw significant shifts towards subsidies and military spending, reflecting broader regional instabilities and domestic political dynamics (Simson 2019, p.11). This pattern is also evident in Peters, who argues that the African land systems are exclusionary despite their flexibility, highlighting how official perceptions of 'custom' can obscure deeper processes of class formation. These findings suggest that the institutional dynamics at play, such as budget reallocation and land tenure systems, are not merely reactive but actively shape economic outcomes by influencing resource distribution and political stability. Simson (2019, p.2) cautions against overgeneralizing these patterns noting that regional variations and historical contexts significantly influence how these institutions operate and their ultimate impact on growth.

Economic divergence and performance across regions have been significantly influenced by historical factors such as trade patterns and political institutions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. This early divergence can be attributed to the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity gains, particularly in the traded goods sector of north-western Europe. These economic activities were crucial for growth during both the early modern period and today (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The importance of institutions is reflected in how they facilitated investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity in developing parts of northwestern Europe. This pattern of divergence was not uniform across all regions; local endowments transport costs and political institutions played varying roles in shaping economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the complexity of economic performance, as it challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic history and highlights the need for more nuanced analyses of regional economic development. Economic growth in these regions was often tied to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Van Zanden points out that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during 1500-1800. This observation suggests that while economic growth can lead to higher relative prices for basic foodstuffs, it does not necessarily translate into an improved standard of living for all segments of society (van Zanden 1999, p.1).

Nunn contends that Africa's economic underdevelopment is fundamentally tied to the legacy of slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2). Austin and Frankema share this perspective, asserting that the prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a profound impact on African economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This historical context is crucial for understanding how Africa's institutions evolved differently from those in Europe. The slave trades disrupted social and political structures, creating conditions that were less conducive to the establishment of stable, growth-promoting institutions. For instance, the absence of European settlers in certain regions led colonial governments to encourage indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic illustrates how the legacy of slave trades influenced institutional development, even under colonial rule. Frankema highlights that British colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years, was more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1), suggesting a nuanced impact of colonialism on African institutions and economic growth.

Institutional legacies from colonialism have played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories across former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is primarily due to institutional changes introduced by colonial powers as well as geographic factors alone. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter fundamentally for economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9). The creation of stable institutions under colonial rule, such as secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, has been instrumental in fostering long-term economic growth. These institutional frameworks have facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby encouraging investment and economic activity (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of colonialism on African institutions and economic growth has been nuanced. Austin shows that British colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years, was more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers. This suggests that the legacy of colonialism varied significantly across regions, with some colonies benefiting from institutional developments that promoted economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Moreover, Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, underscoring the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
