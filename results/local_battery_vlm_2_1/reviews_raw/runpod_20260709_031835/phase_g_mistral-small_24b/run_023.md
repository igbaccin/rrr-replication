The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development studies and has significant implications for understanding the persistence of inequality across regions. The debate revolves around how different institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over extended periods. One key position argues that institutions facilitating secure property rights and rule-based governance are crucial for sustained economic growth, while another view emphasizes distributional conflicts and political dynamics as primary drivers behind core economic institutions. A third position focuses on the long-term impact of colonial policies and their institutional legacies. These differing perspectives highlight the complexity of institutional influence on economic development, where institutions are both shaped by historical contexts and themselves shape future economic trajectories.

The role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth is a recurring theme in studies examining the relationship between institutions and long-run economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to lower transaction costs, were pivotal in enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This political commitment to rule-based governance is seen as a critical factor in ensuring economic growth and market development.

The persistence of certain institutional frameworks can also be attributed to their alignment with the interests of politically powerful groups. Acemoglu et al (2001) argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates in colonies, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al (2002), who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which can hinder long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional legacies of colonialism thus play a significant role in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies.

The impact of colonial policies on institutional frameworks and subsequent economic outcomes is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al (2001) who show that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This highlights how historical contexts and colonial policies can shape institutions, influencing long-run economic growth. The legacy of these institutions persists, affecting economic development even after independence.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth continues to evolve as new evidence and theoretical frameworks emerge. Institutions are both shaped by historical contexts and themselves shape future economic trajectories.

In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they tended to establish extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional inertia underscores the deep-seated impact of colonial legacies on economic development, illustrating how historical policies can have lasting effects even after independence. The interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes reveals that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is intrinsically linked to the institutions introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) underscores how simplistic views of causation in economic development can obscure the complexity of historical institutional evolution. This perspective challenges the notion that colonial institutions uniformly determined long-run economic outcomes by showing how local incentives and political actions influenced institutional changes, even under colonial rule. Austin illustrates this through his examination of African commodity production: where European settlers were absent, indigenous economic activities often aligned with colonial interests, leading to institutional adaptations that reflected these dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This finding is consistent with Ogilvie's argument that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie contends that the core economic institutions of serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were products of power struggles and shared beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the institutional legacies of colonialism varied based on local conditions and political contexts. Ogilvie's call to transform institutions from mere accessories into indispensable instruments in economic history underscores the need for a deeper understanding of these dynamics, emphasizing that institutions are shaped by complex interactions between local actors and broader political economies. This approach reveals how colonial legacies can have varied impacts on long-run economic growth depending on the specific institutional paths taken during and after the colonial period.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasize that technological progress and efficient innovation use are crucial drivers of growth, requiring both institutional adjustments and ideological shifts. This process involves adopting new technologies and creating the necessary social infrastructure to support them. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology often conflicts with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, necessitating significant societal changes. These institutional adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts without undermining political stability. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) also notes that economic reasonableness, the ability to make rational decisions in economic matters, is not as irreversible as technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while institutional adjustments are necessary for leveraging technological advancements, the sustainability of these improvements depends on continuous efforts to maintain and enhance economic rationality. The interplay between technology and institutions is dynamic, requiring ongoing adaptation and coordination to ensure long-term economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more positive impact on formal education compared to other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education grew as local leaders and families saw increased advantages in acquiring these skills (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). For example, this education allowed Africans to access new economic opportunities created by colonial economic policies. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) point out that the growth of export production, often tied to foreign trade, was not uniform across countries; it varied based on local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability suggests that while British rule facilitated educational progress, its impact on economic development was mediated by these contextual factors. Therefore, the positive influence of British colonial education policies did not uniformly translate into economic growth across sub-Saharan Africa.

These institutions were pivotal in fostering secure property rights during the seventeenth century. This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth, as specialized exchanges multiplied aggregate transactions. The English financial revolution illustrates that political institutions specifying efficient property rights can lower transaction costs, thus promoting economic development. North and Weingast extend this argument by highlighting the role of credible commitment in enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They show that a regime's commitment to rules governing exchange is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as evidenced by capital market performance during this period. However, the broader implications of this institutional evolution are qualified by research on France’s economic performance before the French Revolution, which suggests that institutions alone may not be sufficient for economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the culture of mistrust which emerged during this period persists to this day. This mistrust is argued to account for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, as it affects both external environments and internal norms within societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn establishes a direct link between Africa's slave trades and its underdevelopment by comparing the duration of these trades with that of colonial rule, highlighting how the former spanned nearly 500 years while colonial rule lasted only about 75 years. The slave trade's legacy is further illustrated by the observation that sustained trade with Europeans was facilitated by centralized governments and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom. This historical context suggests that the absence of such institutions in other regions may have hindered their ability to engage in productive trade, thereby limiting economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). However, the broader implications of these findings are qualified by research on France’s economic performance before the French Revolution, which suggests that institutions alone may not be sufficient for economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-established by historical evidence showing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms foster economic development (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, which in turn encourages investment and specialization. North highlights that the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby promoting long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

Colonial experiences further illustrate this principle: European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were developed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The impact of these institutional differences is evident in the varying economic trajectories of former colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among these regions can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors alone (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions is not absolute. Research on France's economic performance before the French Revolution shows that while institutions are crucial, they alone may not be sufficient for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This finding is supported by evidence from capital markets, which indicates that France and England were almost at parity in economic performance despite their differing institutional frameworks. Therefore, other factors such as fiscal policies and bureaucratic structures also play significant roles in shaping long-run economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions alone can drive economic development. While North and Weingast argue for the centrality of credible commitment underpinning growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), Austin questions whether institutions are a sufficient explanation, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement can be found in the economic performance metrics of former European colonies. These metrics show that regions with institutions enforcing property rights and rule of law experienced higher long-run growth compared to those with extractive institutions, even when controlling for geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
