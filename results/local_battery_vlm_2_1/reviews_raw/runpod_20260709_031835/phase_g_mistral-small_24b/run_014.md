The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that underscores the interplay between societal structures and economic performance. At stake are fundamental understandings of how economies evolve over centuries and what factors sustain prosperity. The live disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes; whether they do so primarily by enhancing efficiency, mitigating distributional conflicts or fostering secure property rights. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they enhance economic efficiency by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9) and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6), while others contend that the primary role of institutions is to mitigate distributional conflicts in pre-industrial economies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Additionally, some research highlights how institutions shape long-run growth through their impact on secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The centrality of institutions in economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism and settlement. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to vastly different institutional outcomes in colonies, with some establishing extractive states while others set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced current income per capita, illustrating how early institutional choices can have enduring economic consequences. Moreover, North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth by fostering secure property rights, which are crucial for sustained economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Kuznets 1973, p.6).

For example, the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution led to a significant decrease in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself.

Acemoglu et al (2001) show that different European colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes in colonies. These differences in early institutional frameworks had enduring economic consequences, directly influencing current income per capita. The variation in institutional outcomes highlights how initial choices can shape long-term economic development trajectories. Acemoglu et al (2002) extend this argument by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the long-term development of former colonies was significantly influenced by the interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. She advocates for a deeper integration of institutional analysis into historical research to better understand economic performance over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). However, she challenges the view that institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations, arguing instead that distributional conflicts play a significant role in their development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the evolution of property rights and other institutional frameworks involves managing power dynamics within society. The implications of this are profound for understanding long-run growth trajectories: institutions may not always be designed to maximize overall productivity, but rather to serve specific interests and manage conflicts among different social groups.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both assert that technological progress is fundamental to long-term economic development. They argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge propels sustained economic growth. This perspective suggests that the generation and diffusion of new technologies are crucial for fostering economic advancement. Mokyr similarly emphasizes that technological progress is a key driver in reducing access costs to knowledge, which in turn facilitates economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Institutions in this framework complement and sustain growth. Kuznets points out that institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that while technological progress is the primary engine, institutions play a supportive role by ensuring that these advances are effectively integrated into society. Mokyr's analysis of coordination failures further underscores this point: without complementary institutional progress, economic improvements might be limited or even reversed due to rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Kuznets also notes that resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully requires a strong political fabric, highlighting the importance of stable institutions in managing societal tensions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is essential, institutional stability is crucial for maintaining long-term economic growth.

In contrast, the impact of inequality and slave trade on economic development highlights how specific historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions favoring elites. These elites implemented policies curbing competition, which perpetuated inequality. This institutional framework, shaped by initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power, constrained economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Nunn's analysis of Africa's slave trades further illustrates how historical institutions can hinder development. He shows that the prolonged period of slave trades left lasting adverse effects on African economies, distinct from more recent colonial influences or other factors. However, Nunn also notes that centralized governments with developed markets could facilitate trade, suggesting that strong institutions might mitigate some negative effects of historical inequalities (Nunn 2008, p.19). This underscores the complex interplay between historical contexts, institutional development, and long-term economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) re-evaluate historical economic performance in Africa, challenging the notion of persistent poverty by highlighting periods of growth tied to foreign trade. The authors argue that African economies experienced significant fluctuations tied to export markets during the colonial era, with per capita income levels above subsistence in many Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) underscore this dynamism, noting that growth patterns in African economies were closely linked to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This implies a nuanced relationship between Africa's economic performance and external factors, particularly trade dynamics, which played a pivotal role in shaping periods of growth and decline.

The implications for long-term economic development are significant: the historical data suggests that while foreign trade was instrumental in driving economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depended on various factors including local endowments. This highlights the importance of understanding how external influences interact with local conditions to shape economic trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg note that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is necessary to fully grasp the long-term picture of economic development. This suggests that while trade dynamics were crucial, internal factors such as income distribution and specific historical conditions also played a critical role in shaping African economies over time.

This is consistent with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9); (van Zanden 1999, p.1) who argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was evident even before 1800. This assertion challenges views that attribute the great divergence solely to modern industrialization. This mechanism parallels modern economic development, where service sectors play a pivotal role. Van Zanden suggests that the relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe from 1500 to 1800 is not straightforward (van Zanden 1999, p.1; van Zanden 1999, p.9). Van Zanden's analysis of wage data indicates that economic growth did not uniformly translate into improved living standards across Europe. This nuanced view qualifies the direct impact of economic development on living standards, highlighting regional disparities and the complex interplay between urbanization and wages. The shift in economic development to North Sea countries, particularly Holland and England, further illustrates how institutional changes can drive productivity gains but do not uniformly improve living standards.

Institutional changes have played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), while North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings underscore how institutional frameworks can enhance economic efficiency, with the English financial revolution serving as a prime example. The evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England highlights how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for sustained economic development.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth further illustrates this point. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions established by Europeans in high-mortality colonies persistently affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of inclusive institutions in colonies like the United States and Australia led to significant economic development. This divergence shows how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on a region's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This insight aligns with North’s argument that the development of economic institutions reflects increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.6).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another critical aspect. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective complements North’s view on the importance of property rights, highlighting how institutional development can be influenced by underlying social dynamics. Sokoloff and Engerman further elaborate on this by showing that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions per se drove economic growth, or if other factors like factor endowments were more influential. North and Weingast argue that institutional changes in England were revolutionary (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) while Sokoloff and Engerman suggest that differentials in paths of development are attributable to differences in institutions arising from varying degrees of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this, showing that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth as previously thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), highlighting that institutional changes can be less revolutionary than other factors.
