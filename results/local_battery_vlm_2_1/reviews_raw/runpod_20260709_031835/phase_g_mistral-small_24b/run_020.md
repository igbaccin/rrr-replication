The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history, as it addresses how different political, legal, and social frameworks can either foster or hinder sustained economic development. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancement, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure investment environments are crucial for long-term economic prosperity. Others contend that institutional frameworks are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Additionally, there is a rival perspective that emphasizes the impact of colonial policies on institutional development, suggesting that the legacies of these policies have enduring effects on economic growth.

The tension between these views highlights the complexity of understanding how institutions operate within different historical and geographical contexts. This review will explore how various scholars have approached this question, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth. The first stream considers the direct impact of institutional frameworks on economic development, highlighting how secure property rights and effective governance can drive sustained growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. North (1989, p.9) describes this as a political model where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, leading to more efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. This institutional framework significantly lowered transaction costs per exchange, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. The success of these institutional changes is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which shows a remarkable alignment with economic growth during this period. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) elaborates that as economies grow, political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs. The aggregate volume of exchanges increases due to specialization and division of labor, causing the transactions sector to grow proportionally larger in GNP. This suggests that while institutional changes reduced individual transaction costs, the overall economic complexity increased, creating a dynamic interplay between institutional efficiency and economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions, challenging efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that distributional concerns frequently override efficiency considerations. In pre-industrial Europe, institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by the need to manage conflicts among different social groups, rather than solely by economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This dynamic is illustrated in New World societies, where initial endowments of land, natural resources, and labor led to extreme inequality. Elites leveraged government policies to maintain their advantages, restricting competition and sustaining inequality through institutions that favored their interests (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). However, while distributional conflicts are a powerful force shaping institutions, they do not act in isolation. The persistence of these institutions is also influenced by the broader economic context and the evolving power dynamics within societies.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. establish that European settlers established divergent institutions based on mortality rates, low mortality encouraged settlement and inclusive institutions, while high mortality led to extractive states focused on resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional divergence was driven by the settlers' expectations of longevity and their ability to influence local governance. Where Europeans anticipated staying long-term, they implemented institutions that protected property rights and encouraged investment. This institutional framework shaped economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and growth. Acemoglu et al. note that these institutions served to maximize rents for the colonizers, often at the expense of long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence has been a significant barrier to economic growth in many former colonies. Thus, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape the economic trajectories of these regions, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can help eliminate poverty by informing institutional reforms. He argues that while ethnic fragmentation may influence economic outcomes, it is not the only determinant; dysfunctional societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg note that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They reconstruct real wage levels in British colonies, showing that economic development was influenced by these wages and their distribution over time. The lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates for Africa leaves many questions unanswered (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg's work suggests that a deeper understanding of these historical economic indicators could provide insights into future growth possibilities. This implies that while colonial institutions may have had significant impacts, the long-term effects on economic development are complex and multifaceted.

Mokyr (2005, p.49); (Kuznets 1973, p.1) identify that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in driving long-term economic growth. This suggests that the accumulation and diffusion of technological advancements are critical drivers of economic progress. The institutional adjustments required for efficient use of these innovations are active facilitators of growth.

Mokyr further elaborates on this by explaining how coordination failures can impede economic sustainability if not rectified through appropriate policies (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This indicates that while technological progress is essential, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure long-term economic improvement. Kuznets's observation that modern technology requires significant ideological and institutional adjustments supports this view (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This complexity underscores the need for institutions that can flexibly support ongoing technological advancements.

The implications are clear: while institutions play a crucial role in facilitating growth, the primary driver of long-term economic development is the continuous generation and application of useful knowledge. The ability to harness this knowledge through effective institutional frameworks is what ultimately determines a society's economic trajectory, making it essential for policymakers to focus on both technological innovation and institutional adaptation.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade was instrumental in shaping African societies by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This mistrust persists today, significantly impacting trust levels. The slave trade's legacy is not merely historical; it has had lasting effects on social cohesion and economic development. Austin elaborates on this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This oversight underscores the importance of understanding how institutions have evolved over time, which is crucial for grasping the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) further posits that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions by adjusting their institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). The impact of the slave trade on trust levels varied based on local responses and adaptations to the colonial environment. This dynamic interplay between historical events and institutional adaptations highlights the multifaceted nature of African economic development, which is influenced by both internal and external factors.

The dynamic interplay between historical events and institutional adaptations highlights that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This influence is evident in the contrasting outcomes between colonies where settlers established institutions enforcing the rule of law and those where extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources. Similarly, North argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England, exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development.

The role of institutions is further underscored by their ability to adapt and respond to local conditions. Austin's analysis reveals that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive capacity is crucial for understanding the varied impact of historical events on trust levels and economic outcomes in Africa. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium in response to local incentives, illustrating how institutions can evolve to meet the needs of both colonizers and indigenous populations.

The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, which aligns with North's assertion that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.6). This highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the comparative analysis of income per capita across former European colonies. The reversal in relative incomes shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, particularly those designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, have a lasting impact on economic development (North 1989, p.9; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This empirical comparison settles the debate by demonstrating how different institutional frameworks lead to varied economic outcomes.
