The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics, with scholars debating whether institutions are foundational to development or merely ancillary factors. The stakes are high because the answer dictates policy priorities for developing nations. One camp asserts that institutions are pivotal, shaping incentives and constraints that drive growth. This view posits that secure property rights, rule of law, and effective governance foster economic activity by reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment (North 1989, p.6; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Another position contends that other factors such as culture or geographic endowments are primary drivers, with institutions playing a secondary role. This debate underscores the need to understand how institutions interact with these factors to influence growth trajectories. A third stream of thought emphasizes colonial legacies and their enduring impact on institutional frameworks. The extent to which institutions set by colonizers continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies is crucial for understanding the persistence of economic disparities (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments are cited as key factors in this process, facilitating market exchanges and driving economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement. The evolution of these institutions, as described by North and Weingast (1989, p.29), was instrumental in reducing transaction costs and increasing the volume of market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). However, the extent to which these institutional changes were revolutionary is debated, with some research suggesting that economic performance in France before the French Revolution was not significantly different from England's. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other factors such as geographic endowments and cultural influences in shaping long-run economic growth. The impact of financial institutions on reducing transaction costs remains a critical factor in understanding economic development, even when considering broader determinants of growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The mechanism through which these colonial institutions affected growth was the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they tended to establish extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole rather than fostering local investment and development. This pattern is evident in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where institutions were structured to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). In contrast, regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences had enduring effects on economic outcomes, shaping the trajectory of development in these colonies over centuries.

The persistence of these institutional legacies is a critical factor in understanding the divergence in economic performance among former European colonies. Even when controlling for other covariates such as geographic endowments or initial conditions, the quality of institutions established during colonial rule remains a significant predictor of current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This finding underscores the profound and lasting impact of historical institutional choices on economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is directly linked to these institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting that geographic factors alone cannot account for the observed differences in long-run growth.

The slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust within Africa, which has persisted and significantly impedes long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) posit that the slave trade's impact on trust is multifaceted. The internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, indicating that individuals' norms of mistrust play a critical role in shaping intergroup relations (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings suggest that the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond immediate economic disruptions and has deeply ingrained social consequences. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates that while colonialism is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of Africa's underdevelopment. The four slave trades spanning nearly 500 years have had enduring effects on African economies (Nunn 2008, p.2). When considering how these historical events shaped economic development, it becomes clear that the legacy of mistrust and weakened institutions are crucial factors in understanding contemporary economic challenges.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) assert that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation and efficient use of useful knowledge, with technological progress and institutional adjustments playing crucial roles. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this idea by highlighting how epochal innovations, major breakthroughs in human knowledge, have historically constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to substantial parts of the world, facilitating economic growth through their interplay with institutional changes. This process is not merely about technological advancement; it also requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve conflicts that arise from these technological shifts. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that modern technology often clashes with traditional rural modes of life and extended family patterns, necessitating substantial social and political adaptations. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for long-run economic growth, as it ensures that innovations are efficiently utilized without undermining societal stability.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) reassess African economic performance by challenging prevailing narratives about per capita income levels and post-independence budget policies. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more robust economic foundation than previously acknowledged. This perspective challenges the notion that Africa's economic underdevelopment is an inevitable consequence of colonial legacies or inherently weak institutions. Simson adds nuance to this narrative by examining East Africa's post-independence budget policies, which were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed. The shift in expenditure towards subsidies and military spending was driven by regional instability and growing domestic dissent rather than purely rent-seeking behaviors. Simson (2019, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) qualify this pattern of economic activity with evidence that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores the importance of context-specific institutional arrangements in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting that while certain policies may have been effective in one setting, they might not translate universally across different regions. The complexity of these interactions suggests that a monolithic view of African economic performance overlooks the diverse trajectories and institutional adaptations that have occurred over time.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining colonial policies and labor dynamics, rather than focusing on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. This view is shared by Austin (2008, p.15). The mechanism that links these policies to economic outcomes involves the interplay between colonial administrations' fiscal needs and indigenous economic actions. Colonial governments often encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent, aligning with their fiscal interests. These administrations also adapted institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by local economic and political dynamics. This adaptation suggests a more nuanced relationship between colonial policies and economic growth than is typically acknowledged.

Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, thereby calling for a deeper understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on long-term economic growth. This perspective underscores the need to examine how institutions are adopted in specific contexts rather than assuming universal applicability. This nuanced view of institutional adaptation suggests that the impact of colonial policies on African economies was varied significantly based on local contexts and administrative responses.

The downstream implication is that a more granular understanding of these dynamics can provide insights into contemporary African economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue for a deeper exploration of real wage divergence across British Africa, suggesting that this historical data can offer valuable lessons for future growth possibilities. By reconstructing real wage levels and trends over the colonial period, they aim to assess both temporary and structural effects on long-term African welfare development. This approach highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in shaping current economic policies, thereby offering a pathway to more effective strategies for sustainable growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the evolution of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms is critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al. who show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks, with some colonies adopting extractive states that hindered long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often maximized rents for colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical context of colonial rule highlights how institutional changes introduced during this period significantly affected the trajectory of economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is directly linked to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutional frameworks continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. North and Weingast emphasize the political factors underpinning economic growth, noting that a regime's commitment to rules governing property rights is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The interplay between institutions and long-term economic growth is further complicated by cultural factors. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts trust levels necessary for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal norm of mistrust, exacerbated by historical events, highlights how institutional frameworks must account for both external environmental factors and internal cultural norms to effectively promote growth. The evidence from capital markets in the seventeenth century illustrates that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This shared finding across multiple studies shows that credible commitment underpins growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2), but also that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The precise remaining disagreement is whether the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or geographic factors. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in long-run development (North 1989, p.9).
