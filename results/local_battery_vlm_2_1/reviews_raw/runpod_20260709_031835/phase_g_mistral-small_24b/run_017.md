The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. At stake intellectually is the degree to which institutions are determinants of prosperity over the very long run. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions influence growth directly through property rights and transaction costs, or if they are intermediaries for other factors like culture or population density. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial in determining economic outcomes by shaping incentives and constraints on human behavior. These scholars focus on how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms foster economic activity. Conversely, others suggest that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper cultural beliefs or exogenous factors, thereby diminishing their direct role in growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue for the importance of institutions by examining how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) highlight the role of credible commitment underpinned by stable property rights as a key factor for economic growth. These views emphasize that institutions directly influence economic performance by creating an environment conducive to investment and economic activity.

This direct influence is exemplified by the specific institutional developments highlighted by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This security was critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that as political institutions evolve to specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, the costs per transaction decrease while the size of the transactions sector grows proportionately with GNP. This pattern underscores how institutional changes facilitate market exchanges and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.6). They argue that the regime's commitment to these rules is pivotal. This suggests that while institutions are vital, their effect on growth may vary depending on broader socio-political factors. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering secure property rights and efficient market exchanges, but their impact can be context-dependent.

Mokyr argues that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth by increasing useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). The growth of knowledge fosters innovation and improves productivity, which in turn drives economic expansion. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that technological advancements require institutional adjustments to be efficiently integrated into society, noting that these changes must be compatible with prevailing social structures for sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by suggesting that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements may not be fully realized or could even be reversed over time due to rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technology is a critical driver, its impact can be amplified or diminished depending on how well institutions adapt to support it.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping institutions and thus economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were more influenced by distributional struggles than efficiency concerns. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this perspective to New World societies, arguing that institutions in these regions evolved to advantage elites through policies that restricted competition. The mechanism linking distributional conflicts to institutional outcomes involves elite capture of the political process. Elites, driven by their self-interest, shape institutions to maintain their privileges and restrict economic opportunities for others (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in New World societies where extreme inequality persisted due to institutions that protected elite interests at the expense of broader economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) advises that distributional conflicts are pivotal; they do not exist independently of other elements such as beliefs and ideologies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These elements can reinforce or challenge institutional arrangements, adding layers of complexity to how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping the institutional frameworks that persisted after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. illustrate this through contrasting colonization strategies adopted by Europeans, which led to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo and Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans settled permanently, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment (Acemoglu et al., 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, hindered economic progress by discouraging investment and innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions post-independence further entrenched economic disparities among former colonies. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutional legacies and long-run economic outcomes, suggesting that while colonial institutions played a pivotal role, their impact was mediated by various contextual factors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that African economies were more economically advanced in the early 20th century than previously thought. They demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by this time, challenging previous characterizations of Africa's economic history. For example, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa has been underutilized in comparative studies on economic growth and development, which has led to an incomplete understanding of the region’s economic performance. The availability of large amounts of economic data for both the colonial era and post-independence periods suggests a more nuanced picture of African economic history than previously acknowledged. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) support this by reconstructing real wage levels in nine British African colonies from 1880 to 1965, providing evidence that poverty was not always a persistent historical phenomenon but varied over time. This variation underscores the importance of understanding both short-term fluctuations and long-term trends in economic development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further emphasize that these trends were often tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The interplay between these external influences and internal conditions shaped the economic trajectories of African countries, highlighting how institutional frameworks and market dynamics co-evolved during colonial rule. These findings suggest that historical narratives about Africa's economic performance must account for both domestic and international factors to fully grasp the complexities of long-term economic development in the region.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2) argue that historical factors have significantly influenced African economic development. This influence extends beyond direct institutional impacts and includes broader socio-economic and cultural dynamics. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights how colonial policies, particularly those related to indigenous commodity production, were often shaped by the interests of colonial governments rather than purely economic imperatives (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged material interest in the status quo adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political actions. This dynamic suggests a more nuanced relationship between colonial policies and African economic development. The findings of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further illustrate how historical factors, such as the slave trade, have left enduring cultural legacies that continue to affect contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Their research shows that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts current levels of intergroup trust. This mistrust, in turn, has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for effective cooperation and institutional functioning. This internalization complicates efforts to address the legacy of historical injustices through institutional reforms alone.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with historical legacies playing a crucial part. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly across regions, leading to the establishment of different institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies where Europeans settled and implemented rule-of-law institutions, these systems encouraged investment and economic development. In areas where extractive states were established, resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole, hindering local growth. This variation in institutional frameworks persists today, influencing current levels of income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

Institutional continuity is a key factor in understanding long-run economic growth. North emphasizes the significance of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin adds that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.15).

Institutional design and enforcement are pivotal in shaping economic outcomes. North and Weingast highlight that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is evident in seventeenth-century England, where political factors were crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The internalization of mistrust due to historical injustices complicates efforts to address these legacies through institutional reforms alone. Acemoglu et al. point out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This dynamic is evident in the colonial context, where institutions were often tailored to benefit European colonists at the expense of local economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The precise historical evidence that settles this remaining disagreement is the measurement of income per capita across former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in institutional frameworks established during colonization significantly influenced current levels of income, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
