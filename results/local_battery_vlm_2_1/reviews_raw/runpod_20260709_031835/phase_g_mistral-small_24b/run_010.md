The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in understanding why some regions thrive while others stagnate over centuries. At stake is the ability to predict and influence economic trajectories, with implications for policating and development strategy. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they merely complement other factors such as geography or cultural beliefs. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial because they determine how resources are allocated, who has power, and what incentives exist to invest in the future. Others contend that institutions are secondary to geographic endowments or cultural values, which fundamentally shape a society's economic trajectory.

One key position posits that institutions can be either inclusive or extractive. Inclusive institutions foster growth by encouraging investment, innovation, and widespread participation in economic activities; extractive institutions concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, stifling growth. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that European colonies with extractive institutions tend to have lower per capita income today than those with inclusive ones (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). The enduring impact of these colonial-era institutions underscores their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, suggesting a path dependency where initial institutional choices have lasting effects.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments were pivotal for long-run economic growth in historical contexts, with the creation of financial institutions and secure property rights playing central roles. North (1989, p.9) highlights how "the English financial revolution", marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional shift reflects an enhanced security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that as economies grow, political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs per exchange (North 1989, p.6). This pattern underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating market exchanges and driving economic development.

Their analysis shows that the regime's commitment to rules governing public choice was a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework enabled England to outperform France despite similar economic conditions, highlighting the transformative power of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights technology as a primary engine of growth. Mokyr elaborates on this idea, emphasizing that technological progress requires complementary institutional developments to be sustained over time. The interplay between technological innovation and institutions is crucial for long-term economic success: without the right institutions to support and coordinate technological advancements, the gains from new knowledge can dissipate. Kuznets (1973, p.1) provides historical examples where the lack of such coordination led to conflicts that impeded growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). However, Mokyr notes that even with institutional progress, economic improvement is not guaranteed; it requires a balance between technological and institutional developments to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay suggests that while institutions may facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, their role is contingent on the broader context of knowledge growth and technological change.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule facilitated formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers. As a result, demand for missionary education increased, reflecting a cost-benefit calculation that favored education within this institutional framework.
This dynamic is tied to broader economic outcomes; Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) reveal that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This pattern underscores how educational progress can translate into improved economic performance. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also highlight that growth in export production varied significantly across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability suggests that while British colonial rule may have fostered educational development, the broader economic impact was contingent on a range of contextual factors.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were not just efficient responses to economic needs but were heavily influenced by power dynamics and conflicts over resource distribution. The persistence of serfdom, for example, can be explained by the ability of landowners to maintain control over labor through coercive means rather than through any inherent efficiency in the system (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to colonial settings, where institutions that concentrated wealth and power in the hands of elites were maintained despite their inefficiencies. This concentration of resources resulted from the ability of elites to impose policies that restricted competition and preserved their dominant status (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). While Ogilvie (2007, p.12) focuses on how these distributional conflicts shaped institutions, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that these institutions, in turn, reinforced inequality by limiting opportunities for social mobility. This dynamic suggests that economic growth was constrained not just by institutional inefficiencies but also by the entrenched power structures that maintained them.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that European colonial policies laid the foundation for institutions that profoundly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. European settlers' varying mortality rates shaped their colonization strategies and institutional designs; where Europeans faced high mortality, they established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole rather than invest in local development. This institutional legacy has persisted, with former colonies initially endowed with extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita in the present day. The persistence of these institutions highlights how colonial policies can have enduring effects on economic outcomes, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies linked to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. However, the relationship between institutions and growth is complex; while extractive institutions hinder development, they do not necessarily prevent it entirely.

The literature on institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa focuses on specific interventions and their impacts rather than directly addressing how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. When European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production to expand, creating incentives for institutional changes that responded to local economic dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). For example, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium illustrates how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous actions. Hopkins further emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This highlights that while institutional reforms can lead to short-term poverty reduction, their impact on long-run economic growth depends on sustained investment and adaptive policy changes. However, Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that the new institutionalist approach faces challenges in explaining why some countries adopt institutions favorable for economic growth while others do not (Austin 2008, p.15). This implies that the effectiveness of institutional reforms in Africa may vary widely depending on local contexts and historical paths. The complexity of institutional change underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how specific interventions can shape long-term economic outcomes in diverse settings.

However, these theories highlight the importance of shared beliefs in influencing institutional structures. This shows that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local dynamics. North demonstrates how the creation of financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs and facilitated economic growth by providing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9).
