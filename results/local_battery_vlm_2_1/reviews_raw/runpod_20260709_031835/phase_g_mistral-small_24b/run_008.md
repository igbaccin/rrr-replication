The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. The core disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures is crucial for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others posit that institutional frameworks are merely one component among many, with cultural beliefs and historical paths also exerting substantial influence on economic outcomes. This debate underscores the need to understand how institutions interact with other variables to foster or hinder long-term growth. The extent to which institutions shape economic trajectories remains a contentious issue, particularly when considering the enduring impact of colonial legacies and the varied institutional frameworks they imposed (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth; Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) share this perspective and argue that extractive institutions can hinder long-term development. North observes that the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial instruments, which lowered transaction costs, was a critical factor in economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional change facilitated market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms. These extractive institutions, which prioritize the interests of colonizers over local populations, can persist and significantly affect current income per capita in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. However, these institutions may have been designed to maximize rents for European colonists, which did not necessarily promote long-run growth. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial legacies on economic trajectories, where extractive institutions can persist and hinder development over generations.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the English Parliament's success in limiting the crown's power to extract resources from private actors was pivotal. The shift towards secure property rights created an environment where long-term investments became viable; this is because investors could be confident that their returns would not be arbitrarily seized. This mechanism was crucial for fostering economic growth as it incentivized investment and innovation, which in turn drove productivity gains. However, she also highlights that the efficiency of institutions is not always the primary driver; distributional conflicts can play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. This suggests that while secure property rights are essential for growth, the political dynamics surrounding their enforcement must also be considered. The interplay between institutional design and political power structures thus emerges as a critical factor in understanding long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) posit that institutions in New World societies shaped long-run economic growth by reinforcing inequality through elite-advantaging policies. These institutions restricted competition and favored the interests of powerful elites, which in turn hindered broader economic development. The pattern of unequal land distribution and resource allocation in these societies created significant barriers to entry for non-elite groups, perpetuating a system where wealth and political power remained concentrated among a select few (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this dynamic by highlighting how technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments. When institutions fail to adapt or are designed to protect elite interests, they can impede the efficient use of innovations. This institutional rigidity exacerbates inequality and limits economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) adds that resolving conflicts arising from technological changes without excessive costs is crucial for sustaining long-term growth. This underscores the importance of institutional flexibility and inclusivity in fostering economic progress.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that economic development in Europe during the early modern period did not uniformly translate into improved living standards. This lack of a straightforward correlation suggests that the benefits of economic development were not evenly distributed. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how guilds, which played a crucial role in skill transfer, also acted as local cartels that restricted entry into trades and kept wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dynamic illustrates how institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic progress by shaping the distribution of skills and wealth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) observe that high productivity in northwest Europe's traded goods sector was driven not only by production but also by merchant distribution and finance, indicating that service sectors played a pivotal role in early modern economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). These findings suggest that the fruits of development were unevenly distributed. This unevenness aligns with van Zanden's observation that economic development did not always lead to improved living standards for all segments of society, particularly during the seventeenth century when economic activity shifted towards the North Sea region (van Zanden 1999, p.9).

The historical impact of slave trades on Africa's economic development and ethnic fractionation highlights how specific historical events can shape long-term socio-economic outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Nunn demonstrates that the prolonged period of slave trades in Africa, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, significantly influenced subsequent economic development. This historical context is crucial for understanding how external shocks can disrupt institutional frameworks and impede long-term growth. One specific mechanism through which these events shaped outcomes was the disruption of local governance structures. For instance, the Kongo kingdom's centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). However, the reliance on foreign firms and intermediaries in Africa represents a significant departure from patrimonial dependence on decaying state institutions for political resources (Peters 2004, p.19), highlighting how external interventions can alter internal dynamics. This shift underscores that while historical events like slave trades can have profound impacts on economic development, they do not directly address the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth. The complex interplay between historical events and institutional frameworks suggests that understanding Africa's economic trajectory requires a nuanced examination of both exogenous shocks and endogenous institutional responses.

The slave trade has been argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.15), which persists and affects trust levels, thereby impacting long-run economic growth independently of institutional factors. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trade has deeply ingrained norms of mistrust within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Hopkins (2009, p.18) supports this by noting that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional outcomes, indicating that trust issues may persist even in ethnically homogeneous societies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This internalized mistrust can hinder economic development by reducing social cohesion and undermining collective action, which are crucial for effective governance and economic growth. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this perspective by noting that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15), suggesting that not all colonial legacies are uniformly detrimental to trust-building efforts. This highlights a nuanced view where specific colonial interventions can mitigate some of the negative impacts of historical mistrust, offering pathways for institutional reforms that could foster long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2), along with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge conventional narratives about African economic history by highlighting the contributions of women and the effectiveness of post-independence budget policies. They collectively underscore that these factors were crucial for economic growth, even though they do not directly address how institutions influence long-run economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) document how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified customary law, which often reinforced the subordinate status of women. This institutional framework, while not explicitly aimed at economic development, had profound implications for resource allocation and productivity. Women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, which limited their access to more profitable cash crops (Simson 2019, p.11). This institutional bias against women's economic participation highlights a specific mechanism through which colonial policies shaped economic outcomes, even if they were not primarily designed with economic development in mind. Simson (2019, p.11) notes that post-colonial expenditure shifts, such as increased military spending, were also driven by regional instability and domestic dissent, further complicating the relationship between institutions and economic growth. This suggests that while institutional reforms can foster long-run economic growth, the path is fraught with complexities stemming from historical legacies and contemporary challenges.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) illustrate how export production patterns varied significantly due to factors including transport costs and political institutions. These variations highlight how specific institutional contexts can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing trade dynamics. This pattern underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between historical legacies, contemporary policies, and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge the oversimplification of African economic history by rejecting the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. This perspective argues that poverty in Africa is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, but rather a result of complex interactions between institutions and economic outcomes over time.

One key mechanism through which this interaction occurs is colonial policy adaptation. Austin (2008, p.2) shows how colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local economic activities (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning with the interests of the colonies themselves. This dynamic illustrates that institutional evolution was not unidirectional but responsive to local conditions and actions.

They emphasize the importance of examining long-term trends, including income distribution, to assess future growth prospects. This approach underscores that African poverty is influenced by specific historical and geographical conditions as well as structural impediments.

The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding economic development in Africa. By recognizing the adaptive nature of institutions and the role of local actions, it becomes clear that long-run economic outcomes are shaped by a complex interplay of factors rather than being predetermined by structural constraints. This nuanced view of African economic history challenges simplistic narratives and opens avenues for more effective policy interventions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they create the framework within which economic activities operate and evolve. Institutions, defined broadly to include formal rules and informal norms, shape incentives and constraints for economic actors. North (1989, p.6) shows that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and fosters growth. This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can be extractive and hinder long-run economic development.

The historical context of institutional development is crucial for understanding its impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonies with high mortality rates were subjected to extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This finding underscores the enduring influence of colonial-era institutions on long-run economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) further illustrates how local actions and adaptations shaped institutional responses in African contexts, highlighting the importance of indigenous economic and political dynamics.

The adaptability of institutions over time is another key factor. North (1989, p.9) discusses how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term economic growth. This institutional evolution shows that economic growth can be driven by changes in property rights and their enforcement mechanisms.

The complexity of institutional change is also evident in the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, thereby impeding economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) emphasis on how population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, shaping the long-run development of former colonies.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into institutional reforms that can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Englebert's findings, cited by Hopkins (2009, p.18), show that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments, indicating that societies can be both heterogeneous and capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

The agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. North shows that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). There remains disagreement over the extent to which extractive institutions can be overcome by local actions and adaptations. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), whereas Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies settles this disagreement by showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, played a central role in long-run development.
