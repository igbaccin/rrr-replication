The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding why some regions prosper while others stagnate. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors like geography or culture play more significant roles. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are shaped by specific historical and political contexts, or whether they emerge from broader social and cultural forces. This disagreement is evident in the varying emphasis placed on the role of property rights, political stability, and colonial legacies in fostering economic growth. The debate is further complicated by the question of whether institutional changes are driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Some argue that institutions evolve to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, while others contend that they are shaped by broader social norms and beliefs. This literature review will explore these competing perspectives, focusing on how different scholars understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Institutions are not just static structures but dynamic entities that can either facilitate or hinder long-run economic development, depending on their design and enforcement. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of colonial institution established was determined by the Europeans' mortality rates and settlement patterns; areas with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were more likely to develop extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. explain that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions persistently affected current income per capita, illustrating how colonial policies shaped economic outcomes in the long run (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. also note that while these institutions were designed to maximize short-term gains they did not necessarily encourage economic development or private property rights (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.33). This suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions may have hindered long-run growth by preventing institutional changes that could benefit local populations.

Distributional conflicts were key drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie argues that the persistence of serfdom, communal structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds can be better understood through the lens of distributional conflicts rather than efficiency. This is because these institutions often served to entrench power for elites at the expense of broader economic development. For example guilds ensured that elites maintained a monopoly over certain trades (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman show how in New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies and restricted competition. They emphasize that the differing factor endowments in these societies resulted in extreme wealth disparities from their inception, which persisted over time and shaped the evolving institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective suggests that extractive institutions arose from internal power struggles within societies as well as colonial policies. The implication is that long-run economic growth can be stunted when institutions are designed primarily to serve the interests of a privileged few rather than fostering broad-based development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments were pivotal for long-run economic growth. These institutions facilitate market exchanges by specifying property rights and ensuring their enforcement, thereby reducing transaction costs. This mechanism is evident in the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which dramatically lowered the cost of transacting and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, including the evolution of public choice institutions, fostered secure property rights and economic growth by ensuring that the regime was committed to these rules. This commitment is crucial for understanding how institutions underpin long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The development of financial institutions such as the Bank of England exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to a more efficient allocation of resources, thereby promoting economic growth. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is nuanced and depends on their specific functions within a society. The role of credible commitment in institutional development is paramount; it ensures that property rights are secure over time, which is essential for fostering long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

Thus, the historical context of institutional development reveals that while certain institutions can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs, their effectiveness depends on broader political and social commitments within a society.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share that technological progress is an essential driver of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Technological advancements do not merely enhance productivity; they also necessitate institutional adjustments to facilitate their efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that the introduction of new technologies often creates conflicts within societies, particularly between traditional and modern ways of life (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These conflicts require resolution through institutional changes that support economic growth without compromising social stability. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by highlighting the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge and technology (Mokyr 2005, p.54), thereby ensuring sustained economic improvement. The sustainability of such improvements depends on continuous institutional progress complementing technological advancements. This interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is crucial for long-term economic growth, as it ensures that new technologies are not only developed but also effectively integrated into society. The evolution of national states with authority based on loyalty and a shared sense of community plays an important role in resolving conflicts arising from technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in supporting economic growth is contingent upon their ability to adapt to new technological paradigms while maintaining social cohesion.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28)) challenge the prevailing narrative of African economic history by demonstrating that per capita incomes in many parts of Africa were not as low as previously thought. They show that, by the early twentieth century, several Anglophone African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence. This challenges the conventional view that African economies have consistently lagged behind other regions due to structural impediments; it suggests instead that these economies had significant growth potential during this period. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) attribute this economic performance to a complex interplay between foreign trade, export markets, and local endowments and transport costs, as well as political institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) provide further evidence by reconstructing real wage levels in British African colonies from the late nineteenth century through to independence, showing that poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their work underscores the importance of understanding long-term economic trends and the factors influencing income distribution within Africa to assess future growth prospects accurately.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the legacy of slavery has left deep scars on African societies. They find that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This enduring mistrust is deeply rooted in individuals' internal norms. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor, creating a complex interplay between economic and political incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that the mistrust fostered by the slave trade was compounded by colonial policies that influenced institutional prescriptions. The implications of this mistrust are profound, as it affects various aspects of societal functioning, including economic development and political institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that understanding these historical legacies is crucial for comprehending contemporary African economies, as they reveal the intricate pathways through which past events shape present realities (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) demonstrates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs per transaction. This institutional framework fosters a growing proportion of GNP dedicated to market exchanges, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. Austin (2008, p.2) echoes this perspective, highlighting how European economies today are understood through patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience that stretch back centuries. These long-term institutional frameworks are crucial for comprehending current economic conditions. Shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), influencing how institutions evolve and function over time. This interplay between shared beliefs and institutions underscores the complexity of economic growth, where political incentives and historical legacies play pivotal roles in shaping contemporary outcomes.
