Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. The question of how institutions influence economic outcomes has been central to debates among economists and historians. One key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions determine economic performance. On one side, some scholars emphasize the direct causal impact of institutions on economic growth, arguing that certain institutional frameworks foster prosperity while others hinder it. On the other hand, rival explanations suggest that geographical factors or cultural beliefs may be more determinant in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic development.

For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates. In places with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. In regions like the United States and New Zealand, settler institutions enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights that institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience are critical to understanding long-term economic growth.

To understand how institutions shape economic outcomes, it is essential to consider their role in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.6) posits that political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs per exchange. This reduction in costs is crucial for economic growth, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutions.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) advocates for transforming institutions into indispensable tools for economic historians, emphasizing their importance in understanding long-term economic outcomes. She argues that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which may prioritize rents over long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) assertion that equilibrium institutions are designed to maximize rents for European colonists.

Institutions also play a pivotal role in the context of colonialism and its lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al.  (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, attributing this phenomenon to institutional changes introduced by colonial powers. These changes were influenced by population density and prosperity, which shaped European policies and subsequently affected long-run development trajectories.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by the new economic history of Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that understanding Africa's dismal economic record requires examining the quality of public goods provided by African governments, highlighting the importance of progressive institutions in fostering development. This perspective underscores the need for institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These institutions played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes. The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the introduction of innovative financial instruments, dramatically lowered transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This reduction was not merely an economic phenomenon but also reflected enhanced security in property rights over time, an essential dimension for both capital markets and broader economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident from the capital markets' performance, which indicates that institutional changes were pivotal in driving economic development. The impact of bureaucratic structures on economic growth remains a point of contention. While some argue that elaborate bureaucracies can hinder growth, others suggest that they may not be as detrimental as previously thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic performance.

Colonialism's institutional legacy underpins contemporary economic disparities among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s seminal work illustrates how European colonizers established divergent institutions based on mortality rates, shaping long-term economic trajectories. In regions with high European mortality, extractive states were formed to rapidly exploit resources and transfer them to the metropole. In contrast, where Europeans settled in large numbers due to low mortality rates, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence was a direct result of colonial policies designed to maximize European interests. Acemoglu et al.'s findings, supported by various measures of institutions including constraints on the executive and property rights, underscore that these colonial-era institutions have persisted and continue to influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies has been a key factor in the reversal of relative incomes among these regions. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century played a pivotal role in shaping long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is not straightforward; Acemoglu et al. also highlight that politically powerful groups may resist institutional changes if they fear losing their rents or political power, thereby maintaining extractive institutions even when they hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is shared by Kuznets, who posits that advancements in technology necessitate institutional adjustments to harness their potential effectively. Their shared finding is that technological progress requires complementary changes in institutions to ensure sustained economic improvement. For example, Kuznets highlights that modern technology conflicts with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, necessitating significant adaptations for successful integration (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological innovation and institutional change underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is relatively irreversible, the development of institutions supporting this progress can be less predictable and more susceptible to setbacks. He notes that without complementary institutional advancements, rent-seeking behaviors could erode the benefits of technological improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technology drives growth, the sustainability of such growth hinges on the ability to develop and maintain supportive institutions.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are central to understanding economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The institutions Ogilvie examines, such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, are better explained by power struggles over resources than by efficiency considerations. These institutions arose from the need of elites to maintain control over labor and resources. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this analysis to New World societies, showing how initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power shaped institutional development. They argue that the extreme inequality prevalent in these societies led to government policies that restricted competition and entrenched elite advantages. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of institutions that favored elites at the expense of broader economic growth. Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing beliefs as independent drivers of institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). While shared beliefs can influence institutions, they are often secondary to material interests and power dynamics. This nuance is crucial for understanding how institutions evolve in response to both ideological and material pressures.

Austin argues that the impact of colonial administrations on African economies was nuanced and context-dependent (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Colonial rule often shaped economic outcomes by altering indigenous institutions and incentivizing commodity production. When European settlers were absent, colonial governments frequently supported local commodity production to secure fiscal stability. This support was contingent upon the material interests of both colonizers and the colonized, leading to institutional adaptations that responded to economic pressures from below (Austin 2008, p.15). While Austin's analysis centers on the varied impacts of colonial policies, Nunn and Wantchekon delve into the cultural legacies of colonial rule. They posit that the Atlantic slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and significantly influences contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is deeply ingrained in social norms and has substantial implications for economic development, as trust is crucial for various economic activities ranging from international trade to firm management practices. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings suggest that the internalized norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external factors like the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin emphasizes the importance of local context and historical specificity in understanding economic development.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights for institutional reforms and poverty alleviation among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not determined by ethnolinguistic fragmentation but rather by the presence of functional institutions. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that British colonial rule facilitated formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, suggesting that specific historical contexts shaped institutional outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that export production varied significantly between and within countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The authors posit that the early modern period saw significant shifts in economic development within Europe, driven by merchant distribution and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This perspective aligns with van Zanden's (1999) findings on the geographical patterns of silver and grain wages across European cities, which indicate a complex relationship between economic development and living standards. Van Zanden notes that economic growth did not uniformly translate into improved living conditions; instead, it led to varied outcomes depending on regional factors (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This uneven distribution of economic benefits echoes Broadberry and Gupta's observation that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled ones. The implication is that institutional changes, such as those in agriculture and finance, played a crucial role in driving economic growth during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These institutions did not uniformly benefit all segments of society, suggesting that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth was nuanced and context-dependent.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that women's contributions to economic growth in Africa have been underestimated, shared finding with Simson (2019, p.2). Their role was integral during both pre-colonial and colonial periods when they were "relegated to marginal lands for food farming" (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This pattern of land allocation resulted from the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men in access to cash crops. The economic revolution propelled by cocoa production in the Gold Coast, for instance, was driven by male farmers migrating in search of farmland (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This male-dominated narrative is incomplete: women also played a crucial role in agricultural activities and contributed significantly to household income through food farming. This gendered division of labor shaped economic growth during the colonial period. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances and further marginalized women's access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). In East Africa, post-independence budget policies were not exclusively clientelist or unproductive; instead, they reflected broader structural changes in public expenditure patterns, such as the growth in military spending due to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the allocation of resources and the distribution of political power. The nature of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced subsequent economic trajectories. European colonizers implemented diverse institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. Settler colonies, such as the United States and Australia, developed inclusive institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Conversely, extractive institutions were imposed in regions with high mortality rates, where Europeans aimed to rapidly transfer resources back to their home countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions has profoundly affected current income levels, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial legacies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the political and fiscal dynamics that shaped colonial administrations. In East Africa, post-independence budget policies were influenced by broader structural changes, including increased military spending due to regional instability and domestic dissent. These policies reflected a complex interplay between clientelism, public expenditure patterns, and institutional adaptations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England in 1694, exemplifies how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduced transaction costs, facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence shows that institutions matter by providing the necessary framework for long-term capital markets and economic development.

Institutional frameworks are shaped by distributional conflicts as well as efficiency considerations. The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were influenced by power struggles among different social groups rather than purely efficient arrangements (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize long-run growth; instead, they often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups who seek to maintain their rents and control. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributable to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors alone (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a crucial role in shaping the long-term development of these colonies.

The significance of institutions extends beyond historical contexts to contemporary economic reforms. The new economic history of Africa highlights how institutional quality is essential for providing incentives for investment, which is vital for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Good institutions ensure that public goods are provided effectively, regardless of ethnic fragmentation or homogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks to inform current reforms and eliminate poverty among marginalized populations. Ultimately, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident across diverse historical and geographical contexts. Institutions matter because they determine the allocation of resources, distribution of political power, and provision of public goods (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The specific historical evidence that settles any disagreement regarding institutional influence on economic growth lies in the measurement of transaction costs and property rights enforcement, as shown by North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33).
