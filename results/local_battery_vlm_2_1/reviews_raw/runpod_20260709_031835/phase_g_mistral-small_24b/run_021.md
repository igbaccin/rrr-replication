What role do institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth? This question is central to understanding why some nations thrive while others stagnate over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, and population density hold more explanatory power. One camp argues that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or inclusive, directly influences a nation's economic trajectory. Another perspective posits that while institutions matter, their impact is often overstated, and other variables play equally significant roles. The debate hinges on whether institutions should be viewed as endogenous to broader socio-economic processes or as exogenous forces shaping these processes. This question is intellectually crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development in underdeveloped regions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had profound effects on the long-term economic performance of colonies. Austin (2008, p.2) acknowledges the significance of institutional differences but argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This debate underscores how institutional legacies from colonial times can have enduring impacts on economic development, shaping modern economies through persistent institutional frameworks.

North (1989, p.9) (Hopkins 2009, p.8) posits that institutions are pivotal in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, both of which are essential for long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.6) explains how the emergence of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement can lead to falling transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic development. This perspective is echoed by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who emphasizes that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. These arguments collectively underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in creating an environment conducive to economic growth through reduced transaction costs and secure property rights.

Institutions are not only shaped by efficiency concerns but also by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize economic growth; instead, they often reflect power dynamics and the interests of politically dominant groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) support this view by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. These arguments collectively highlight that institutional design is often influenced by the interests of those in power, which can lead to institutions that do not necessarily foster economic development.

The debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth remains vibrant, with scholars continuing to explore how different types of institutions influence economic outcomes and the extent to which these institutions are shaped by broader socio-economic factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates have significantly affected current income per capita. They argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates; in regions with high mortality, they implemented extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional design was not conducive to long-run economic growth but rather aimed to maximize short-term rents for the colonizers. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further elaborate that the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. This institutional framework, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, did not encourage economic development but instead maximized rents for European colonists. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) caution that while various measures of institutions show similar results, it is crucial to consider the specific context and mechanisms through which these institutions operate. This underscores the importance of understanding not just the presence of institutions but also their underlying motivations and effects on economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice are pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic growth. They emphasize the role of political institutions in specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms to facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. This institutional framework is crucial for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This institutional evolution was driven by fiscal crises that necessitated trade-offs between political actors, ultimately shaping economic institutions to support growth.

However, North (1989, p.9) also notes that while these institutions are critical, the size of the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor. This implies that the benefits of secure property rights are amplified by the growing complexity of the economy. These commitments were pivotal in fostering a stable environment for economic growth, as evidenced by the success of capital markets during this period. This institutional framework not only governed economic exchanges but also ensured that these rules could be enforced and adapted over time.

The success of these institutions in fostering growth is contingent on their ability to adapt to changing economic conditions and maintain credible commitments over time. This underscores the importance of understanding not just the presence of institutions but also their underlying motivations and effects on economic outcomes, a theme that carries into subsequent discussions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) both argue that unequal institutions in New World societies favored elites through government policies. This institutional inequality restricted competition and perpetuated economic disparities over time. In particular, Sokoloff and Engerman show how the initial factor endowments of these societies led to extreme wealth inequality from the outset, shaping the development of institutions that reinforced this imbalance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Nunn's work on Africa's slave trades highlights a similar dynamic: the historical trauma of slavery has left lasting impacts on economic development, with institutional inequalities playing a crucial role in perpetuating underdevelopment. This is evident in how societies with centralized governments and developed trading networks, like the Kongo kingdom, were better positioned to engage in sustained trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn and Wantchekon note that while external factors such as the rule of law contribute to trust, it is the internal norms of mistrust fostered by historical injustices that have a more profound impact on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Colonial rule in Africa shaped institutional change through both exogenous factors and local initiatives, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This process was driven by the interaction between colonial policies and indigenous social structures. However, local leaders and families also played a crucial role by making cost-benefit calculations based on the new institutional environment, demonstrating that endogenous factors are essential in shaping economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) underscore this point, noting that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions could support significant economic development when aligned with local initiatives (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that the interplay between exogenous institutional changes and endogenous adaptations is critical for understanding Africa's long-run economic growth.

Mokyr's emphasis on useful knowledge and Kuznets' focus on technological innovations reveal the necessity of institutional adjustments for efficient and sustainable economic growth. This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial; Kuznets notes that modern technology often conflicts with traditional societal structures, requiring significant adaptations to facilitate economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr furthers this by emphasizing the need for coordination failures to be addressed through policies that support technological progress, ensuring its sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress is essential for sustaining the benefits of technological advancements. Without complementary institutional developments, economic improvements might be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and other inefficiencies (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the intricate relationship between technology and institutions in driving long-run economic growth: while technological progress is pivotal, it must be supported by robust institutions to ensure sustained development.

The distributional approach posits that these institutions emerged as solutions to power struggles between various social groups, where each sought to maximize their share of resources. For instance, the persistence of serfdom in many European regions can be attributed to the balance of power between landowners and peasants, with landowners leveraging institutional arrangements to maintain control over labor. Similarly, craft guilds and merchant guilds served as mechanisms for skilled workers and traders to protect their economic interests against competition from less-skilled laborers or new entrants into their trades. Austin (2008, p.15) elaborates on this by showing that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development. This suggests that the evolution of institutions is not merely a function of efficiency but is deeply rooted in the power dynamics and resource allocation struggles within societies.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the framework within which economic activities unfold and incentives are structured. The historical development of institutions is deeply intertwined with the power dynamics and resource allocation struggles within societies (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development underscores that institutions reflect the interests of politically powerful groups (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The creation of secure property rights is a critical dimension in this process. For instance, the establishment of the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This historical example illustrates how institutions can emerge from fiscal crises to create long-term economic benefits by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement.

The role of colonial administrations in shaping institutions further highlights this dynamic. Colonial authorities adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating a responsive relationship between institutional development and the interests of both colonists and indigenous populations (Austin 2008, p.15). The extractive nature of some colonial institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, exemplifies how institutions can be shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

This historical evidence underscores that institutions matter in economic growth. Institutions are not static but evolve in response to political and economic pressures, reflecting the interests of those who hold power within societies. The persistence of extractive institutions in some regions highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on long-run economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). While distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions like serfdom or guilds than efficiency considerations, shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the development of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This shows how deeply rooted historical factors continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The new economic history of Africa demonstrates that good institutions, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment essential for development, matter significantly in shaping long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise remaining disagreement is over the extent to which historical institutional choices can be reversed or mitigated. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-term growth, suggesting a persistent legacy of colonial institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic actions, indicating some flexibility in institutional development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence settles this disagreement by showing that while extractive institutions can persist, they are not immutable. The dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development indicates that changes in power dynamics and resource allocation struggles within societies can lead to shifts in institutional frameworks over time. This is evident in the adjustments made by colonial administrations in response to local economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15).
