The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians alike. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop and prosper over extended periods. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes driven by political and social dynamics. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions are fundamentally determined by geographic conditions, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes. On the other hand, others posit that institutional frameworks evolve through complex interactions between political actors and societal structures, with significant implications for economic growth. A third perspective suggests that distributional conflicts play a more significant role than efficiency considerations in shaping core economic institutions. The interplay of these perspectives highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The debate over how institutions shape long-term economic outcomes is well illustrated by Acemoglu et al.'s argument that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on the critical role of institutions in economic growth, particularly through the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). These scholars share a finding that institutional frameworks are pivotal in determining long-run economic performance. The argument is that efficient property rights and their enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth by encouraging investment and market exchanges. This institutional perspective challenges neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as primary determinants of growth.

However, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood within patterns and paths of institutional evolution going back to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the complexity of institutional development and the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time, particularly in response to indigenous economic and political action. Austin's argument aligns with North and Weingast's emphasis on the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England as crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This shared finding highlights that institutional frameworks are not static but evolve in response to changing political and social dynamics, which in turn shape long-run economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in their former colonies. The nature of these institutions was shaped by the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in different regions. In areas with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework persisted over time and had profound effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) find that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than geographic factors alone. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that while extractive institutions were prevalent, they were not always uniform across all colonies. This variation underscores how the specific policies and strategies employed by colonial powers shaped the institutional frameworks that would ultimately determine economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time suggests that historical legacies play a crucial role in shaping contemporary economic development, even as new institutions are introduced or existing ones reformed.

The emergence of specific institutions has been pivotal in fostering long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of the Bank of England (North 1989, p.9) and new financial instruments were crucial for long-term economic development, while North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The institutional developments in England helped reduce transaction costs by specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms. For instance, North argues that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the Hundred Years' War was a product of fiscal crises. This political model highlights how institutional changes can drive economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights over time.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share that technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr's research emphasizes the role of useful knowledge in driving economic development. He argues that the accumulation of practical, applicable knowledge leads to technological advancements, which then fuel economic growth. Kuznets extends this idea by highlighting that technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth over long periods. However, Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be relatively monotonic and irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness is less so; without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that although technology is a fundamental driver, its benefits are maximized when supported by appropriate institutions.

The slave trades in Africa directly impeded long-run economic development through their impact on trust and institutions (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within African societies, which persists today. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates is twofold: it affects both individuals' internal norms and their external environment. This period far exceeds the duration of colonial rule in Africa, which lasted approximately 75 years. The adverse effects of the slave trade on institutions are compounded by the lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets that could support economic growth during this time; without these structures, sustained trade did not occur (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the legacy of mistrust and weak institutions created by the slave trades continues to hinder Africa's economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) re-evaluated African economic history by challenging the notion of persistent poverty due to structural impediments. They argue that African economies exhibited growth patterns tied closely to foreign trade and export markets during colonial rule, a period marked by significant economic activity despite prevailing narratives of stagnation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) support this view, highlighting how the timing and scale of economic growth varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions; these factors shaped the expansion of export production, which in turn drove overall economic growth. This underscores that African economies were dynamic and responsive to market forces, rather than being uniformly constrained by structural impediments. However, the extent to which these growth patterns translated into sustained long-term development remains a subject of debate.

Distributional conflicts and historical paths significantly shape economic development trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the institutional explanations for economic development are incomplete because they overlook these crucial factors (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's work highlights that colonial institutions were not static but evolved in response to indigenous economic activities. Colonial administrations adjusted their policies based on local incentives, suggesting that institutional frameworks were more fluid and responsive than often assumed. This dynamic interplay between local actions and institutional adaptations challenges the notion of a singular path to economic growth driven solely by institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this critique by examining pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective underscores that the adoption of seemingly efficient institutions can be better explained by power dynamics and social struggles rather than purely economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). However, while Austin acknowledges the complexity of institutional evolution, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests a more nuanced approach to integrating institutions into long-term research programs. She advocates for viewing institutions as indispensable tools in economic history that can reveal deeper insights when analyzed through a broader lens than just efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce efficient transaction costs. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) shows that the development of institutions such as the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on extractive versus inclusive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth.

Moreover, the evolution of political institutions is crucial in creating an environment conducive to economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) demonstrate that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the commitment of regimes to rules governing property rights and their enforcement. This perspective underscores how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic prosperity.

Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) extends this analysis by highlighting how colonial institutions often evolved in response to local economic and political actions, rather than being uniformly imposed. This dynamic interaction between local conditions and colonial policies shaped the institutional landscape of former colonies, influencing their long-run development trajectories.

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter profoundly for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The precise nature of this influence remains a subject of debate. While Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), Austin's analysis reveals how local adaptations to colonial institutions can lead to varied outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from the Bank of England's role in reducing transaction costs provides a clear case where institutional changes directly contributed to economic growth. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by showing that the specific institutional arrangements, such as those governing property rights and financial markets, are pivotal in determining long-run economic outcomes.
