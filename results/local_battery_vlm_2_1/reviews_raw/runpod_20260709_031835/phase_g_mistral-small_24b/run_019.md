The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal to our understanding of global development disparities and the enduring effects of historical processes on contemporary economies. Institutions are the rules of the game, which structure political, social, and economic interactions. The live disagreement centers around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some scholars emphasizing the direct impact of institutional quality on growth, while others highlight the mediating role of geographic factors or the path-dependent nature of institutional change. Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, in turn, significantly influenced current income per capita by encouraging or hindering investment and economic development. The debate extends beyond colonial legacies to include the broader question of how political and economic institutions interact to foster growth. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth, emphasizing the importance of institutions governing public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This argument underscores a fundamental principle: institutions matter because they shape the incentives for long-term investment and economic activity.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The central mechanism of this influence was the establishment of extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutions, characterized by poor enforcement of property rights and weak rule of law, hindered long-term investment and economic development in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Politically powerful groups often design institutions to maximize their own rents while fostering economic development. The implications of this institutional legacy are far-reaching: former colonies with extractive institutions continue to grapple with economic underdevelopment, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The creation of financial institutions and development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) shows that the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 was a key factor in this process. This institution led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. The political model outlined by North (1989, p.9) highlights how trade-offs between certain rights and revenues were crucial during fiscal crises, ultimately shaping economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective underscores the importance of political commitment to institutional rules, which are essential for long-term economic stability. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that while institutions were crucial in England's case, they might not be universally revolutionary, as evidenced by France's comparable economic performance despite different institutional structures (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that the impact of financial institutions on economic growth may vary depending on specific historical and political contexts.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both assert that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective highlights the role of technological progress as a key mechanism for sustained economic growth, suggesting that innovations drive economic development by increasing productivity and efficiency. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for harnessing these innovations effectively. Kuznets elaborates on this dynamic, noting that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological adaptations to be efficiently integrated into the economy (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that while institutions may facilitate or hinder economic growth, it is ultimately the advancements in knowledge and technology that propel long-term development. Mokyr adds a cautionary note about coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors that can undermine sustained economic improvement. These factors underscore the importance of complementary institutional progress to ensure that technological gains are not eroded over time (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The shared finding is that institutions play a secondary role in supporting the primary driver of technology; they do not initiate growth, but merely sustain it.

In contrast, Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) examine the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by focusing on historical periods and paths of institutional evolution. They argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting that European economic growth during the early modern period was driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when settlers were not present to compete for African labour. This dynamic led colonial administrations to adjust their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The colonial impact on African economic development highlights how institutional change was influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors during colonial rule (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. He argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This perspective underscores how African societies can develop progressive institutions regardless of their ethnic composition. He shows that demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by both African political leaders and families. This dynamic influenced the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is necessary. They argue that assessing long-term economic development requires exploring how these determinants relate to other parts of colonial Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced view suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, African initiatives and responses were equally critical in shaping economic outcomes.

The African slave trade, by fostering a culture of mistrust, has had enduring effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust is explained by its effect on others' trustworthiness and through individuals' internal norms of mistrust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, complementing studies that document trust’s importance for various aspects of societal function (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn identifies the slave trade as a critical factor in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, challenging views that attribute underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. The prolonged duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, underscores their profound and lasting influence on African economies (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context highlights how the slave trade's legacy of mistrust continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa today.

The historical legacy of institutions on economic growth is evident in both colonial and post-colonial contexts. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), but it aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that institutions matter in explaining economic outcomes. This shared emphasis on institutional impact highlights how colonial policies, shaped by European interests, continue to influence long-run economic trajectories.

The role of institutions is further underscored by North's assertion that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, illustrates how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and foster a long-term capital market. Hopkins builds on this by showing that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8), shared finding (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This points to a broader consensus: institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges are pivotal in shaping economic growth.

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are detailed in North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework, which included credible commitment to rules and enforcement mechanisms, underpins long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. corroborate this by showing how equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) underscores the importance of institutional design in economic outcomes.
