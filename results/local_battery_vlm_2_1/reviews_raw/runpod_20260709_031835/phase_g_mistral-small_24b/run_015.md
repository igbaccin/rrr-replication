The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that underpins our understanding of why some countries prosper while others lag behind. At stake intellectually is the identification of causal mechanisms that can explain persistent disparities in global economic development. Institutions matter because they structure incentives, allocate resources, and constrain behavior over time. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions drive growth through efficient resource allocation or through distributional conflicts. One position posits that institutions facilitate economic growth by ensuring secure property rights, enforcing contracts, and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Some scholars argue that political struggles over power and resources primarily shape institutions, which can result in extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This debate is particularly salient in the context of colonialism and its enduring effects on economic development.

The central role of property rights in facilitating long-run growth has been a cornerstone of institutional theory. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who note that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law encouraged investment in settler colonies, leading to higher income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-driven view of institutions, arguing that distributional conflicts are a more accurate explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This view posits that power struggles often influence the shaping of institutions more so than purely economic considerations. Acemoglu et al. present evidence from their study on colonialism, showing that extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.33; Ogilvie, 2007, p.12). This highlights the importance of understanding the political and historical contexts in which institutions are formed.

Acemoglu et al. identify two primary colonization strategies employed by Europeans: settler colonies and extractive states, the latter of which were designed to transfer resources rapidly back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework was critical in determining economic outcomes. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions did not foster growth because they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists.

Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the persistence of these colonial institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The institutional legacies of colonialism have influenced long-run development by shaping the economic policies and governance structures in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, which were originally designed to benefit European colonists, have created barriers to economic growth that persist even after independence.

Building on this idea of institutional persistence, North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They highlight the critical role of institutional changes that led to increased security of property rights, which is essential for long-term capital markets. For instance, North underscores that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflects an enhanced security of property rights over time. These institutional changes were instrumental in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs per exchange (North 1989, p.6). While emphasizing the importance of these institutions, they also recognize that the bureaucratic structure inherited from earlier regimes was not necessarily an obstacle to economic growth. This suggests that the specific institutional framework and its enforcement mechanisms are more critical than the overall bureaucratic complexity. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in how they enforce property rights and create stable conditions for investment.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view, noting that major breakthroughs in human knowledge have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates on the role of coordination failures, suggesting that without complementary institutional progress, economic improvements might not be sustainable. This perspective emphasizes the need for policies to rectify such failures, ensuring that technological progress is complemented by institutional advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets notes that technological progress varies in pace and affected sectors over centuries and among world regions (Kuznets 1965). These differences imply that while technological advancements are pivotal, their impact on economic growth depends on regional and temporal contexts. This suggests that although technology is a primary driver, its effectiveness in fostering economic growth varies significantly based on local conditions.

Sokoloff and Engerman assert that institutions in New World societies were shaped by inequality, which had long-lasting effects on economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade played an instrumental role in fostering these inequalities. Nunn and Wantchekon illustrate how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which continues to affect intergroup trust today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).) This mistrust is deeply rooted and persists across generations, influencing social capital and economic interactions. The internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external factors such as rule of law or others' trustworthiness. Nunn further elaborates on this by showing that Africa's slave trades had a profound adverse effect on its economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional legacy of inequality and mistrust, stemming from historical slave trade practices, persists in shaping contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of past institutions on long-run economic growth.

Extending this perspective to colonial Africa, Frankema and Simson (Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2) show that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education among African families and leaders was driven by cost-benefit calculations that shifted with new institutional frameworks. This pattern of educational development highlights how colonial policies can have long-lasting effects on societal structures, even if they do not directly address the institutional framework as a cause of economic growth. Simson elaborates on this point by noting that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were more complex than simply being clientelist or unproductive (Simson 2019, p.2). This complexity underscores the enduring impact of colonial educational policies on subsequent public spending patterns, particularly in sectors like defense and subsidies.

Simson provides a detailed example of how these policies played out in Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30% of spending in the early 1990s (Simson 2019, p.11). This shift in budget allocation had significant implications for capital and recurrent expenditures. Colonial educational policies laid a foundation for post-independence education systems, but their influence on economic growth was complex. For instance, the high debt burden experienced by countries like Tanzania and Kenya during the 1960s and 1970s highlights how post-colonial economic challenges can complicate the relationship between education and long-run economic growth. The transition from colonial rule to independence brought about significant changes in budgetary policies, including increased military spending due to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This dynamic illustrates that while British colonial rule facilitated educational development, its impact on long-term economic outcomes was mediated by broader post-independence political and economic factors.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women played a pivotal role in food farming and cash crop production, despite being relegated to marginal lands under colonial rule. The codification of customary law during this period reinforced their subordinate status, yet women's contributions were essential for economic development. This dynamic is echoed by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who argues that the new economic history of Africa highlights the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) show that patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule often marginalized women's roles in economic activities. This reveals a complex interplay between institutional frameworks and gender dynamics in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

This gendered perspective is important as Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) share a common posture that the literature on African poverty has overlooked the long-term economic performance of Anglophone Africa. This is evident in their focus on real wage levels as an indicator of economic development, which reveals that many African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to demonstrate that poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon in Africa. This suggests that institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes by facilitating growth in export production. The timing and scale of growth varied both between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Consequently, the impact of these institutions on economic development was complex and multifaceted.

This oversight is significant because Austin (2008, p.2); Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) jointly argue that specific institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe shaped economic outcomes through complex interactions that were not always efficient. This perspective posits that historical economic phenomena can be explained by the interplay of political, social, and economic factors. The colonial administration's approach to indigenous commodity production illustrates this dynamic: when European settlers were absent, local governments encouraged commodity production to align with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie extends this idea by proposing that distributional conflicts often drove the core institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Austin's (2008) discussion on institutional evolution highlights how these arrangements can persist over time, influencing long-term economic trajectories. The adoption of institutions favorable for growth was not uniform across countries. This variation underscores the nuanced role of institutions in shaping pre-industrial Europe’s economic outcomes, suggesting that while they are crucial, their impact is mediated by local contexts and specific historical circumstances.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks, with some promoting rule of law and investment while others established extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional development highlights how the establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The persistence of these institutions over time is critical to understanding their impact on long-term economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) emphasizes that institutional evolution is a complex process influenced by local contexts and specific historical circumstances, which can lead to varied outcomes even within similar regions (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced view aligns with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that institutions are shaped by underlying power dynamics and competing interests within society, further underscoring the importance of considering the local context in institutional analysis (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial in this reversal, with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North's (1989, p.9) assertion that secure property rights and effective enforcement are essential for economic development, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6).

The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique is supported by Hopkins's (2009, p.18) work, which shows that institutional reforms can inform economic development and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). The variation in institutional frameworks across different regions highlights the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that while they are crucial, their impact is mediated by local contexts and specific historical circumstances.
