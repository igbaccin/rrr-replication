The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic progress. One live disagreement centres on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Proponents of institutional determinism argue that the quality and type of institutions directly determine economic performance, while others posit that geopolitical factors or cultural beliefs play a more significant role. Furthermore, there are competing views on whether institutions evolve endogenously in response to economic conditions or are exogenously imposed through colonial legacies. This debate underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and their impact on long-term economic trajectories.

The influence of European colonization strategies on the establishment of institutions in colonies highlights the exogenous imposition of institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions, as seen in the United States and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive institutions in colonies like the Congo were designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, with inclusive institutions fostering long-term growth and extractive ones hindering it. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that colonial policies introduced by European powers shaped the institutional landscape of former colonies, directly impacting their relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2) complements this view by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, which adjusted institutional prescriptions in response to local economic conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adjustment highlights how exogenous factors can influence the evolution of institutions, shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The role of transaction costs and property rights in facilitating market exchanges underscores the endogenous evolution of institutions. North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions matter significantly in economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who emphasizes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Power dynamics shaped serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds as much as economic considerations did (Author Year p.N). This endogenous evolution of institutions highlights how internal factors can drive institutional change, influencing long-term economic trajectories.

The interplay between institutional quality and economic development is further illuminated by the impact of colonial legacies on African economies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The legacy of these institutions continues to shape African economies today, underscoring the lasting impact of exogenous institutional frameworks.

Acemoglu et al. show how European settlers established extractive institutions in high-mortality regions. This approach maximized resource extraction for colonizers while fostering local development had enduring effects on economic outcomes. The Europeans' desire to maximize rents from colonies shaped these institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). A key mechanism through which these extractive institutions persisted is the reluctance of politically powerful groups to initiate institutional change that would not directly benefit them. This dynamic highlights how colonial legacies continue to shape economic trajectories in former colonies by reinforcing institutions that prioritise short-term gains over sustainable development.

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights were crucial for economic growth in England (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They contend that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered an environment where property rights were protected. This was achieved through a series of political transformations that established credible commitments to enforce these rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. These institutions provided increased security for property rights over time, which was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged with Parliament in return for revenues, laying the groundwork for these institutional changes (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions was underpinned by political factors, particularly the regime's commitment to enforce rules governing economic exchange.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores this perspective by highlighting how technological progress has historically necessitated institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This relationship between technological advancement and institutional change is not unidirectional; it requires a reciprocal adaptation where institutions evolve to accommodate new technologies, and technological progress in turn shapes these evolving structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further illustrates how conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully for sustained growth to occur (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while knowledge accumulation is a primary driver, the institutional framework plays a crucial role in facilitating this process.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a profound adverse effect on long-run economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) assert that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. This enduring mistrust significantly impedes economic development by undermining social cohesion and hindering collective action (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Such mistrust is an internal norm deeply ingrained in individuals' psyches as well as an external environmental factor; it affects their attitudes toward others and their willingness to engage in cooperative ventures. Nunn further elaborates that the extensive duration of Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, lasted much longer than the period of colonial rule, which was approximately 75 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). This temporal disparity underscores the profound and lasting impact of the slave trade on African economic development. Additionally, Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that centralized governance structures, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, facilitated sustained trade with Europeans. Nunn (2008, p.2) also notes that many local coastal societies lacked these institutional capacities, which impeded their ability to engage in productive trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of mistrust and weak institutions, both products of the slave trade, continues to shape African economies today by limiting trust-based interactions and hindering economic growth.

Colonial administrations could encourage indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labor. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contradicting the notion of uniform colonial exploitation (Austin 2008, p.15). This mechanism is supported by Hopkins, who highlights that institutions enabling investment are crucial for development and can be shaped by local actions that create incentives for policy changes (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that while colonial policies could influence economic outcomes, poverty in Africa cannot be solely attributed to these historical factors. They emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of long-term economic development trends in Africa, including the distribution of income and real wages (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, their impact on long-run economic growth was complex and intertwined with other structural factors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share a view that distributional conflicts shaped institutions in pre-industrial Europe as much as efficiency considerations did. These authors argue that the core economic institutions of serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds resulted from power struggles among different social groups seeking to maximize their own interests. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights how shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights influenced institutional development in Latin America, leading to economic failures due to the absence of property rights and contract enforcement. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this perspective by showing that societies with greater inequality in the New World developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This pattern suggests that distributional conflicts influenced long-term economic development trajectories as well as shaping institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions against overemphasizing institutional explanations, noting that while institutions are crucial, they should be integrated into a broader research program that considers multiple factors influencing economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework for secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. This institutional structure facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs, which are essential for economic development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups often hinder long-run growth, as seen in the context of former European colonies where extractive institutions were prevalent (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were not conducive to economic development because they were shaped by the interests of colonial powers rather than the needs of local populations. The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in determining the long-run development trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The persistence of institutional legacies from colonial times underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic outcomes. Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can also influence the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, as seen in Latin America's economic failures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These findings highlight that distributional conflicts and power dynamics within societies shape institutions over time. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore multifaceted, involving both the historical context and contemporary political and economic factors.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). By examining the experiences of different regions, it becomes clear that effective institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The historical evidence from Africa shows that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This underscores the importance of institutional quality over ethnic composition in determining economic outcomes.
