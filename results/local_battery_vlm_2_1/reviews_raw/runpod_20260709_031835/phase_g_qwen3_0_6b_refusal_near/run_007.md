The role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin is a central theme in contemporary economic analysis, with scholars arguing that it contributed to divergent outcomes based on institutional characteristics. (Komlos 1998, p.16) highlights the divergence between living standards and biological well-being, suggesting that socioeconomic environments did not always foster health as previously thought. (Komlos 1998, p.2) further argues that the persistence of extractive institutions, such as those in the Soviet Union, played a key role in maintaining economic development despite the challenges of authoritarian regimes. However, this perspective is not without controversy. (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.9) points out that while central planning may have influenced industrialization, the extent of its impact depends on institutional features like property rights and the availability of labor. The disagreement between these claims stems from differing interpretations of how institutions shape economic performance. (Allen 2001, p.29) notes that the regression analysis supports the idea that economic performance is influenced by institutional factors, while (Allen 2001, p.9) emphasizes that such factors are not the sole determinant of outcomes. The historical evidence, as shown in (Temin 2002, p.9), demonstrates that the expansion of the supply frontier during the Second Thirty Years War provides a measurement of how these institutions affected industrialization, thus resolving the debate between central planning's role and its broader implications.
