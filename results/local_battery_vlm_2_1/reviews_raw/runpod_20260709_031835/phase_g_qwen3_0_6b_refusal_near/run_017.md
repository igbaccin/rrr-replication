The literature on the role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin is grounded in a synthesis of multiple streams of scholarship, which collectively argue that central planning led to the divergence in living standards and biological well-being among populations during this period. This divergence, as supported by (Komlos 1998, p.16), highlights the role of physical stature in shaping socioeconomic outcomes, suggesting that historical institutional conditions did not always foster health. However, the argument is not without controversy. (Komlos 1998, p.2) notes that the French data, which extend back into the seventeenth century, provide the earliest known records of such conditions, suggesting that the Soviet Union’s central planning may have exacerbated these issues. Yet, the claim that the reversal in relative incomes reflects changes in institutions rather than economic development is contested. (Temin 2002, p.16) argues that the evidence supports an asymptotic approach to an equilibrium share, which may be the correct model for all time, while (Temin 2002, p.9) suggests that the barriers to international commerce during the world wars and Great Depression created a disequilibrium that led to the stagnation of industrialization.
