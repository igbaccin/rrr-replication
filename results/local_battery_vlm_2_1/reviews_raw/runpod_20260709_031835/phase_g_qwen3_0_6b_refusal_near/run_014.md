The role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion. This literature review synthesizes sources from two streams, each contributing to the discourse on economic and social dynamics during this period. The first stream, focused on the physical stature of populations during forced industrialization, argues that physical dimensions reflect a divergence between living standards and biological well-being, suggesting that socioeconomic environments did not always foster health. The second stream, examining institutional factors, highlights how economic profitability, particularly in extractive systems, influenced policies, with evidence from two sources reinforcing the idea that such systems were integral to the process of forced industrialization. The argument is that central planning, while a tool for managing resources, also shaped the human experience by balancing economic efficiency with social well-being. This synthesis reveals a complex interplay between economic structures and the lived realities of individuals during this period.
