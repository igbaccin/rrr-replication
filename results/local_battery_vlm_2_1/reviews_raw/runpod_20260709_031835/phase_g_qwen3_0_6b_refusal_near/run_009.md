The role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin is a topic of significant debate. This question explores the intellectual stakes of examining the intersection between economic policies and the transformation of population and labor under authoritarian regimes. The central claim is that central planning contributed to the stagnation of human development, as evidenced by the divergence in life standards and biological well-being across societies during forced industrialization. Two distinct perspectives exist: one that attributes the stagnation to the persistence of extractive institutions, as seen in North and South Korea, and another that suggests institutional variations, such as settler mortality during colonization, play a role in explaining the observed patterns. These arguments conflict on whether central planning was an instrumental factor, with differing conclusions drawn from the literature. The debate centers on whether the focus should be on economic performance or on the health and biological aspects of the population affected. This disagreement highlights the complexity of understanding the impact of central planning on human development.
