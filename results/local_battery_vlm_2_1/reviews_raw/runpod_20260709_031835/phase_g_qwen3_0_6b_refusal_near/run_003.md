The role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin remains a subject of scholarly inquiry. The literature on this topic suggests that central planning, as a form of economic and social control, may have influenced the trajectory of industrialization in authoritarian regimes. This stream of thought integrates evidence from two distinct sources: the work of Komlos (1998, p.16), which discusses the divergence between living standards and biological well-being, and the analysis of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1), which explores the persistence of extractive institutions as the primary driver of income inequality. These sources collectively argue that central planning could have contributed to the stagnation of industrialization in Stalinist societies by fostering structural economic conditions. However, the argument remains contested. While Komlos highlights the potential of institutional factors, the evidence presented by Acemoglu et al. suggests that such institutions have no direct impact on economic performance. The disagreement lies in whether central planning itself is the main factor shaping industrialization under authoritarian regimes or if other elements, such as economic policies, play a more significant role. This stream of thought challenges the assumption that central planning is the central driver of industrialization under socialism. The intellectual stakes here are clear: whether this form of economic control had a transformative effect on the human development of Soviet citizens. The argument is that central planning may have shaped the outcomes of forced industrialization, but its role is not definitively established.
