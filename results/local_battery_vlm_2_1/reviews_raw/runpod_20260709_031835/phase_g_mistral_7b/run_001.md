The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within the realm of economic history, as scholars grapple with understanding the factors that have led to disparities among nations in terms of wealth and development. At stake is an explanation for why certain societies have prospered while others have stagnated or regressed over time. This review delves into the literature addressing this question, highlighting the key disagreements and findings that have emerged from various streams of scholarship.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They posit that Europeans adopted different strategies in their colonies, with some establishing rule-of-law systems that encouraged investment, while others set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The results from Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) regressions using various measures of institutions support this claim (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They contend that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming but played crucial roles in the agricultural revolutions of regions like the Gold Coast, which became the world's leading producer of cocoa (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15). Furthermore, colonial rule reinforced customary law that further marginalized women in access to land and resources (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.20).

In a related but distinct stream of scholarship, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins posits that societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional, while ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins, 2009, p.18).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They contend that interethnic slave exports are associated with lower levels of trust, which has negative consequences for economic development (Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011, p.27). Furthermore, they find that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel in explaining this effect (Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011, p.4).

In a competing explanation, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) propose that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. They argue that political power was important to elites in the colonies because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

Frankema (2012, p.2) offers another perspective by arguing that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. Frankema's study demonstrates that factors like malaria ecology, pre-colonial state centralization, and native African resistance against colonial rule played a role in shaping educational development (Frankema, 2012, p.17).

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that conflicts within institutions offer a compelling explanation for the emergence of specific economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This claim is supported by North (1989, p.9), who argues that political and economic institutions play a significant role in determining economic growth, and North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who emphasize secure rights as crucial for economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) proposes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework contributes to early industrialization and capitalism. For instance, the European family system, according to Laslett, was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie_2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.27)). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England, according to Solar, was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie_2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)).

They highlight the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North&Weingast_1989 (North and Weingast 1989, p.30)). This increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself, was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War (North_1989 (North 1989, p.9)).

In this context, the development of specific economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe can be understood as a response to distributional conflicts within an interdependent institutional framework.

This stream of literature suggests that the emergence of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe was driven not only by efficiency considerations but also by distributional conflicts within an interdependent institutional framework. Understanding these dynamics can provide valuable insights into the long-run economic growth in Europe, offering a more nuanced perspective on the role of institutions in shaping economic development.

In regions where settlers established rule-of-law and investment-encouraging institutions, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth thrived. The results across various measures of institutions corroborate this assertion.

For instance, relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor, illustrating how these institutional changes have persisted and influenced long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Furthermore, the impact of European colonization on Africa's economic development is not solely limited to the establishment of institutions. Nunn provides evidence that Africa's slave trades had a detrimental effect on current economic development in Africa. The Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans, but the slave trades hindered the development of similar institutions elsewhere (Nunn 2008, p.2).

Englebert's work, cited by Hopkins, reveals that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly impact the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous and capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Hopkins further argues that larger populations in Africa, which could resist European encroachment, occupied epidemiological zones that imposed a high rate of mortality on Europeans (Hopkins 2009, p.10). In such regions, the European presence was either marginal, in which case unproductive institutions remained in place, or took the form of colonial rule, which introduced extractive types of economic control that were poorly suited to long-term development. The Atlantic slave trade is a particularly telling case, reducing the population from a level that was previously relatively high and encouraging the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth.

Thus, Hopkins's work suggests that policy interventions aimed at strengthening state institutions could potentially contribute to institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa, bypassing some of the historical obstacles identified by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48). It is essential to take into account the unique context and historical development of each region when designing and implementing such policies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing view that African economic growth above subsistence was primarily driven by European colonial interventions. Instead, they argue, many Sub-Saharan African economies had achieved levels of per capita income comparable to those in Asia by the early twentieth century, as evidenced by Allan McPhee's (1926) claims about West Africa's economic growth being due to railways, malaria prophylaxis, and British rule. This finding contradicts the assertions of earlier economic histories of Africa, which attributed any growth above subsistence to European colonial influences.

The authors support their claim by noting that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or 1960, a period when per capita incomes stagnated or declined in many African countries. While some developing economies were aligning with Europe and North America during the same period, others were not. Broadberry (2021) provides insights into patterns of growth and development over centuries, while the field of African economic history has experienced a 'renaissance' based on newly digitized sources of data and innovative techniques (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016).

For instance, they cite evidence of long-distance trade networks, urbanization, and state formation in West and East Africa prior to European colonization. These factors contributed to the development of institutions that facilitated economic growth and shaped the trajectory of African economies even after European intervention.

One implication of this stream's findings is that policy interventions aimed at strengthening institutions could potentially contribute to institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa, bypassing some of the historical obstacles identified by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48). As Hopkins (2009, p.18) stresses, it is crucial to consider the specific context and historical trajectory of each region when designing and implementing such policies.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and others posit that the growth of useful knowledge was pivotal in fostering long-term economic growth, particularly during the Industrial Revolution in the British Isles (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This claim is substantiated by evidence showing an increase in intellectual societies during this period, focusing on matters pertaining to political economy such as free trade and efficient taxation (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

One mechanism through which the growth of useful knowledge contributed to economic growth was by creating a conducive environment for technological innovation. For example, Mokyr (2005, p.54) points out that intellectual societies served as platforms for idea exchange, resulting in the development of new technologies and practices that boosted productivity (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Another condition that facilitated this process was the establishment of institutions that protected and enforced property rights.

The growth of useful knowledge also had downstream implications for the nature of economic growth. This finding aligns with broader literature suggesting that knowledge diffusion plays a significant role in shaping the trajectory of economies over time.

The adoption of barbed wire significantly contributed to agricultural development by reducing costs associated with land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This technological innovation, initially introduced in the late 19th century, marked a turning point in the region's agrarian landscape.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides evidence that the widespread use of barbed wire led to an increase in improved farmland by 20 percentage points from 1880 to 1890 compared to a minimal change in the preceding decade. This shift was particularly pronounced in counties that had adopted herd laws, which regulated livestock grazing on open ranges.

The paper highlights how the adoption of barbed wire made it possible to effectively enforce property rights and control livestock grazing on individual plots. Moreover, the increased certainty of property rights likely attracted more investment into the region, contributing to its overall economic development.

In a broader context, this stream of literature underscores the significance of technological innovations and institutional changes in shaping agricultural development and, by extension, long-run economic growth. The adoption of barbed wire serves as an example of how seemingly mundane inventions can have far-reaching consequences for the trajectory of economies (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Under colonial rule, the codification of customary law reinforced the disadvantage of women in land access within the context of cash crops (Lawrance, Osborn, and Roberts 2006).

Emmanuel Akyeampong and Hippolyte Fofack (2014) further emphasize that the invention of customary law was a key avenue for the codification of the subordinate status of women in the colonial era. Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts, which facilitated the reinforcement of patriarchal alliances (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

While African women faced substantial restrictions under customary law, it is essential to acknowledge that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa also played a role in shaping educational development. The impact of this educational development on gender roles and economic growth is a topic for further exploration within this stream of literature.

Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011) paper provides evidence suggesting that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that interethnic slave exports are strongly correlated with lower levels of trust between groups, even after controlling for other factors that might influence trustworthiness. Furthermore, they demonstrate that this effect is not primarily due to the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others but rather on the internal norms of mistrust within societies that were heavily involved in the slave trade.

The importance of trust in economic development is well-established in the literature (Knack & Keefer, 1997; Fafchamps, 2006; Tabellini, 2007; Algan & Cahuc, 2010). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings imply that the slave trade may have hindered economic growth in Africa by fostering a culture of mistrust. This cultural legacy could potentially make it more difficult for Africans to engage in cooperative activities, invest in long-term projects, or participate in markets, all of which are essential for sustainable economic development.

In addition, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) also suggest that the external environment, such as the rule of law, plays a role in shaping trust levels within societies. This finding opens up avenues for further research into how institutional arrangements might be designed to promote trust and, consequently, economic growth in Africa.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run.

One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the creation of legal systems that favored the elite. For instance, in Spanish America, the elite successfully lobbied for laws that protected their property rights while limiting the rights of indigenous peoples and slaves (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This resulted in a situation where the elite controlled most of the productive land and resources, making it difficult for others to compete economically.

In Portuguese Brazil, a similar pattern emerged. The sugar industry was dominated by large plantations owned by a small group of wealthy families, who used their political influence to maintain their control over the industry (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This concentration of economic power among the elite restricted competition and hindered the development of a more diverse economy.

The persistence of these institutions over time had significant downstream implications. This vulnerability persisted until the late 20th century, when the Brazilian government implemented policies aimed at diversifying the economy.

This stream of literature highlights how institutions that advantaged elites in New World societies can contribute to the persistence of inequality over the long run. By restricting competition and limiting access to economic opportunities, these institutions hinder economic development and perpetuate initial inequalities. This finding underscores the importance of considering the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in contexts where significant inequalities exist.

Institutions, particularly formal ones like guilds in western Europe, significantly shaped long-run economic growth by reducing skill premiums, as suggested by van Zanden (2009, p.19). This reduction was achieved through the fair execution of apprentice contracts, a mechanism that ensured the intergenerational transfer of skills without monopolizing them or artificially inflating relative pay (vanZanden_2009 (van Zanden 2009, p.19)). The emergence of these formal institutions in western Europe played a crucial role in this process, as they addressed issues related to apprenticeship contracts that were problematic elsewhere (vanZanden_2009 (van Zanden 2009, p.1)).

The fairness and effectiveness of guilds, however, have been subject to criticism. Historical reassessments have shown that guilds played a significant role in shaping long-term growth by reducing skill premiums and facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills.

This stream of literature underscores the importance of understanding the role of formal institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of western Europe. The fair apprentice contracts enforced by guilds led to a substantial decrease in skill premiums compared to other parts of the world economy, paving the way for sustained economic development. Goldin (2006, p.7) highlights a quiet revolution in the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States. This transformation was primarily driven by the generation born in the late 1940s. Goldin (2006, p.7) provides examples such as Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, who graduated near the top of her Stanford Law School class in 1952 but could not secure a lawyer position and had to settle for a secretarial role.

The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes (Goldin, 2006, p.3). These changes include the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, which made "nice" jobs available, and the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s that greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women.

One specific mechanism established by this stream is the role of the generation born in the late 1940s as a catalyst for the increased involvement of women in various aspects of American life. This generation, which came of age during a time of social upheaval and expanding opportunities, was particularly adept at navigating the changing landscape and taking advantage of new opportunities (Goldin 2006, p.7).

The downstream implication of this mechanism is that the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles contributed to broader societal changes. For instance, as more women entered the workforce, they began to challenge traditional gender roles and push for greater equality (Goldin 2006, p.7). This shift had far-reaching consequences for American society, including changes in family dynamics, attitudes towards women's rights, and the broader cultural landscape.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) extend Angus Maddison's groundbreaking work on estimating GDP and population in the world economy by focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This collaborative effort among scholars, known as The Maddison Project, aligns with the broader scholarly interest in institutions as fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

The project's central goal is to revise and extend Maddison's estimates, acknowledging that earlier data may have significant margins of error due to partial information and subjective assumptions (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

One specific mechanism that The Maddison Project elucidates is the role of data quality in estimating GDP and population in different historical periods and regions. This highlights the importance of ongoing research to improve data quality and reduce these errors.

This improved understanding can, in turn, inform policies aimed at promoting economic development in these regions.

The Maddison Project's emphasis on collaboration among scholars and the importance of accurate data quality aligns with the broader scholarly consensus that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007; North 1989; North & Weingast 1989). By extending and revising Maddison's estimates, this project contributes to our understanding of the institutional factors that have driven economic development throughout history.

Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that the British industrial revolution was propelled not just by male labor but also by the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor. This shift in focus reveals a hitherto unacknowledged aspect of the industrial revolution's driving forces.

He supports this claim by demonstrating that a significant group of families during the period had fathers who were absent or deceased, indicating marital instability and male abandonment (Humphries 2013, p.16). This turbulence in family structures provided a substantial pool of cheap and amenable labor, particularly among women and children.

For example, Bailey's recent multi-sourced study of matrimonial conflict suggests that desertions increased from the seventeenth century, consistent with contemporary perceptions that runaway husbands were becoming more common (Bailey cited in Humphries 2013).

The inventions of this period further contributed to the availability of cheap and amenable labor. For instance, the power loom and spinning jenny reduced the need for strength or skill, making it possible for women and children to work in textile factories (Humphries 2013, p.16). This shift increased the labor force and allowed for a more efficient use of resources, significantly contributing to the momentum of the industrial revolution.

The implications of this stream of literature extend beyond the British context. As other scholars have noted, the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a critical factor in the industrialization processes of various countries (Kuznets 1966). Understanding these dynamics can provide valuable insights into the economic development of other regions as well.

Simson's (2019) work challenges the prevailing assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Simson contradicts this view by demonstrating that these countries' budgets were not significantly different from other developing nations in terms of subsidies, interest payments, and military spending.

According to Simson (2019, p.11), the high debt burden in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda was a result of external factors rather than mismanagement. In Kenya, for instance, subsidies and interest peaked at 30% of spending in the early 1990s, while Tanzania experienced an 18% peak in debt payments (Simson_2019, p.11). Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief compared to its neighbors.

Simson also highlights a shift towards growth in military spending post-independence, which was a response to regional instability and domestic dissent. Under colonial rule, defense was an imperial matter rather than the responsibility of individual colonies (Simson_2019, p.11).

Beyond the capital cities, employment distribution did not change significantly, with jobs increasingly favoring comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward (Simson_2019, p.19). This trend suggests that high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the decline in skill premiums in Western Europe compared to other parts of the world economy was due to increased educational opportunities and better-educated labor forces. This downstream implication suggests that the high-level allocation of wage resources in post-independence Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda could have contributed to a more skilled workforce over time.

European class cultures were strategically restructured to establish and maintain social distinctions essential for imperial control, as argued by Stoler (1989, p.6) and supported by various scholars. This restructuring played a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that underpinned imperial control and long-run economic growth.

For instance, racist ideology, fear of the Other, and preoccupation with protecting European women from sexual assault by Asian and black males were not merely justifications for continued European dominance but also a critical part of a class-based logic aimed at controlling potential dissent among white colonials (Stoler, 1989, p.6).

Simultaneously, as Stoler points out, there is a scarcity of literature that examines the internal political tensions among Europeans themselves within the colonial state (Stoler, 1989, p.4). Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979), for example, have studied the distinct class interests within the colonial state, providing valuable insights into this under-explored aspect of imperial control.

These findings suggest that the European class cultures restructured for imperial control contributed to the creation of institutions that advantaged Europeans at the expense of the colonized, thereby shaping long-run economic growth. This downstream implication is supported by other scholars who have studied the impact of colonialism on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001; Acemoglu et al. 2002; Nunn, 2008).

Institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly during the colonial period. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the establishment of institutions, which in turn affected current income levels across countries. These institutions were shaped by distinct class cultures within the colonial state, as Berman (1979) have studied.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) found that European colonies with institutions promoting rule of law and investment experienced higher economic growth compared to those with extractive states. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) underscore the significant contributions made by African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods, which were often overlooked or subjugated by the codification of customary law under colonial rule.

On the other hand, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) suggest that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting intergroup trust.

The disagreement between these scholars lies in the extent to which European colonial institutions were beneficial or detrimental to economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue for the positive impact of institutions that enforced rule of law and encouraged investment, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the negative effects of the slave trade on trustworthiness and intergroup relations.
