The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in contemporary development economics, as it offers insights into the persistent disparities among nations and regions. This literature review examines various perspectives on this issue, highlighting the causal link between institutions and economic growth, the factors that trigger their formation, and the subsequent impacts they have on development trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutional structures established during European colonization have a profound impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that the varying strategies adopted by Europeans in their colonies led to different associated institutions, with some promoting investment and the rule of law, while others were extractive in nature (Acemoglu et al., 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offer a complementary perspective, demonstrating that African women played significant roles in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods. However, their contributions were often marginalized through the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15)).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) discusses the implications of the new economic history of Africa for policies affecting institutional reforms and poverty reduction among the bottom billion people in Africa. He argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins, 2009 (Hopkins 2009, p.18)). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) investigate the impact of the slave trade on intergroup trust in Africa, finding that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their work underscores the importance of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011 (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) extend their earlier work by demonstrating that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They emphasize the role of political power in enabling elites to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al., 2002 (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48)). Frankema (2012, p.2) focuses on British rule in sub-Saharan Africa, arguing that formal education was primarily facilitated by the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

These scholars argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutions that had profound effects on current income levels across countries.

On the other hand, in colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

They highlight political power as essential to elites in plantation agriculture colonies, enabling them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34). This exploitation led to the development of extractive economies, which had long-lasting effects on economic growth.

Interestingly, North (1989, p.9) also emphasizes the importance of institutions in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that focus primarily on population and savings (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.3). He argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, enhancing long-term capital markets and economic growth. This finding underscores the downstream implication that well-functioning institutions can foster economic growth by reducing the costliness of measuring separable dimensions of goods and services.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a lasting impact on the development of trust within African societies, which in turn has been linked to long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2)). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that this effect is not primarily due to the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others, but rather on individuals' internal norms of mistrust. This internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

He argues that the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans, while smaller local societies could not (Nunn 2008, p.19). This lack of sustained trade likely contributed to the persistence of mistrust within African societies.

Downstream, this culture of mistrust has been linked to long-run economic growth. Thus, the slave trade's impact on trust within African societies may have had far-reaching consequences for the continent's economic development over time.

In pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts, not efficiency, are proposed as a primary cause of the core economic institutions that emerged (Ogilvie 2007; Peters 2004). Ogilvie argues that these interdependent institutional frameworks, which encompass labor markets, credit markets, and welfare systems, were shaped by conflicts over wealth distribution. For instance, the European family system was intertwined with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to accumulate savings, and collective insurance and pension provision (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This comprehensive institutional structure may have contributed significantly to early industrialization and capitalism.

Peters further supports this notion by highlighting the masked processes of exclusion and deepening social divisions in sub-Saharan Africa's perceived negotiable landholding and use (Peters 2004). He contends that an examination of who benefits and loses from such instances is crucial, as it reveals the formation of classes and the capture of benefits by national and local elites. This focus on distribution aligns with Ogilvie's argument about the interdependence of institutions in pre-industrial Europe.

Moreover, Peters (2004, p.5) points out that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa often overlook considerable social inequality within many customary systems. This observation underscores the importance of understanding distributional conflicts in shaping institutional development, a theme echoed by Ogilvie's (2007, p.27) analysis of pre-industrial Europe.

The fundamental institutions of representative government, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Mokyr (2005, p.54), play a pivotal role in fostering economic growth and market development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) contend that secure rights, demonstrated in seventeenth-century England, are crucial for economic growth, while Mokyr (2005, p.54) posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the financial revolution in England was a critical factor in its long-run success, suggesting that even if other forces would have ultimately led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively.

The institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize, are essential for economic growth and market development.

In this context, the Enlightenment idea that when individuals work for their own good, they normally also contribute to the welfare of society, is significant.

The downstream implication of these findings is that representative government institutions, when effective in enforcing rules and fostering a conducive environment for the growth of useful knowledge, can significantly contribute to long-run economic growth.

The authors note that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15). This de-privileging of women in access to land was reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Lawrance, Osborn, & Roberts, 2006). For instance, Polly Hill (1963) documents how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farm land for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911.

The invention of custom served as a key avenue for this codification (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.20). Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts. These native courts often perpetuated the subordinate status of women by enforcing customary laws that favored men.

In this context, it is worth noting that the economic revolution driven by cocoa farming in the Gold Coast disproportionately benefited men. This illustrates a downstream implication of the codification of customary law: it reinforced gender disparities in economic opportunities and outcomes.

Thus, Akyeampong and Fofack's work underscores the crucial role that institutions played in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing gender dynamics and economic outcomes in Africa. Their findings highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of the impact of colonial rule on African societies, one that takes into account the ways in which institutions were codified to reinforce existing power structures.

The consolidation of colonial rule played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, as evidenced by the work of Frankema (2012, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10). Frankema's study underscores the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries in facilitating formal education across sub-Saharan Africa before 1940, a crucial factor in human capital development that contributed to economic growth. In addition, the consolidation of colonial rule, as measured by the extent of pacified rule and native African resistance against it, significantly influenced malaria ecology, which in turn affected educational development (Frankema 2012, p.17).

On the other hand, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) provide evidence that many Sub-Saharan African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding suggests that these economies were not entirely dependent on European colonial interventions for growth, as previously assumed.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly contributed to agricultural development by facilitating property rights enforcement and reducing costs of land protection (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This innovation, introduced in the mid-nineteenth century, transformed the region's landscape and economy.

Prior to its introduction, herd laws were enacted in some counties to manage livestock grazing on open ranges. These laws were largely ineffective due to their complexities and difficulties in enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The near-universal adoption of barbed wire alleviated these problems, making it easier to define and protect property boundaries, thereby reducing conflicts over land use and promoting agricultural development.

The paper presents evidence that counties adopting herd laws experienced a significant increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This suggests that the new technology facilitated property rights enforcement, leading to increased investments in agricultural improvements.

In a broader context, this stream of literature underscores the role of technological innovations in shaping economic development. This finding complements the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, which highlights the importance of property rights enforcement and cost reduction in promoting long-run development (North & Weingast 1989; Mokyr 2005).

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a significant factor in shaping the long-run economic growth associated with the British industrial revolution (Humphries 2013, p.16). Humphries's study challenges the traditional focus on men and high wages, arguing that a more accurate understanding of the industrial revolution is achieved by considering the large and growing numbers of women and children among pauper populations.

This shift in focus highlights the economic contributions of female labor during this critical period. Humphries demonstrates that marital instability and male abandonment were common, with some historians suggesting that as many as 18% of boys grew up separated from yet-living fathers (Humphries 2013, p.16). This finding is consistent with contemporary perceptions that runaway husbands were becoming more common. The presence of a significant group of families whose fathers, while yet-living, were not present, alongside the perhaps 18% whose fathers had died, testifies to the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16).

In the United States during the past century, the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles was primarily driven by changes in demand for female office workers, making 'nice' jobs available (Goldin 2006, p.7). This shift, as argued by Goldin (2006, p.7), was due to various exogenous changes, including the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers.

Goldin's work builds upon a historical context where women's employment opportunities were limited. Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, for instance, graduated near the top of her Stanford Law School class in 1952 but was offered a position as a legal secretary instead of a lawyer (Goldin 2006, p.7). This situation changed with the increased demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, making "nice" jobs available.

The growth of educational institutions played a significant role in this transformation.

In essence, the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States was a result of the convergence of rising demand for female office workers and expanding educational opportunities. This shift had profound implications for gender dynamics within families and the labor market, as women gained newfound independence and opportunities for personal growth and financial stability.

The quality of institutions, as exemplified by the guilds in late medieval Europe, played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium, which in turn contributed to long-run economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.1). In this period, formal institutions such as the guilds emerged in western Europe and addressed issues related to apprenticeship contracts and the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19). While these institutions have faced criticism for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into their trades, they played a significant role in maintaining a relatively high skill premium (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

This focus on institutional quality has important implications for understanding long-term economic performance. This finding aligns with the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, which shows that strong and fair institutions can foster innovation, efficiency, and economic development (North & Weingast).

It is crucial to acknowledge that the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is intricate and context-specific. The quality of institutions varies across time and space, and their impact on growth may be influenced by factors such as cultural norms, political stability, and technological change. Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology is not compatible with traditional institutions and social structures, such as family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which were prevalent in earlier times and had to be replaced by more appropriate ones for effective participation in the modern economic system (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate that credible commitment underpins growth by encouraging investment and reducing uncertainty. They show how the Magna Carta established a framework of limited government that allowed for the rule of law, property rights, and economic growth in England (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This work qualifies Kuznets's assertion about institutional adjustments necessary for effective participation in the modern economic system.

Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions established during European colonization played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the distribution of political power and property rights . Their findings suggest that extractive institutions, which limit economic opportunities for most of the population, hinder growth, while inclusive institutions, which promote broad-based economic participation, foster growth. This work further substantiates Kuznets's claim about institutional adjustments as essential factors for economic growth .

European class cultures were strategically restructured within imperial control to preserve social distinctions, which in turn fostered the development of cultural infrastructure in colonies [Stoler (1989, p.6)]. Stoler's work suggests that this restructuring was not merely a means of justifying continued European rule and white supremacy but also an essential component of the power dynamics within colonial societies.

Stoler argues that racist ideologies, fear of the Other, and obsession with white prestige were not just tools for controlling the indigenous population but also mechanisms for controlling dissent within the European community.

For example, Stoler points out that there is a lack of studies examining the internal political tensions among the British themselves in colonial India or Africa, indicating that such issues were overlooked in favor of maintaining social distinctions.

This restructuring had significant downstream implications for the development of cultural infrastructure in colonies, as European institutions were tailored to suit local conditions while preserving social distinctions.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that these policies, while often criticized for prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, can be seen as a response to historical factors such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation and European encroachment, which hindered long-term development [Hopkins (2009, p.18)].

Simson's study on Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda sheds light on the specific mechanisms of these budget policies. Contrary to common assumptions, Simson (2019, p.11) finds that while subsidies and military spending increased in the independence period, they did not necessarily displace capital or recurrent spending, nor were they inherently unproductive [Simson (2019, p.11)]. Instead, changes in employment composition and private returns to public employment suggest a shift towards more skilled and female professions, potentially constraining rather than easing the distribution of patronage [Simson (2019, p.11)].

This finding underscores the complexity of post-independence budget policies in Africa. The downstream implication is that understanding the nuances of these budget policies can provide insights into effective strategies for promoting long-term economic growth in Africa.

This finding sheds light on how European encroachment has contributed to Africa's dismal economic record.

The debate between these scholars revolves around the primary factors shaping African economies: colonial-era institutions (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)) or the cultural legacies of the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon, (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)). For instance, studies could examine the relationship between historical institutions and contemporary economic growth or investigate how trust levels in various African societies correlate with their institutional quality.
