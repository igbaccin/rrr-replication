The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for our understanding of global development and policy interventions. At the heart of this discussion lies the disagreement over the role of institutions in fostering economic progress, particularly in the context of European colonialism and its aftermath.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that in some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans adopted strategies that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to economic growth (AcemogluEtAl_2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)). In contrast, in other colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, European strategies were extractive, transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, resulting in poor institutions and stagnant economies (AcemogluEtAl_2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19)).

However, it is essential to note that not all scholars agree on this point. Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins_2009 (Hopkins 2009, p.18)). This perspective challenges the assertion that European colonization was the primary determinant of current income levels, as it implies that other factors, such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation, could also play a significant role.

In a related but distinct stream of scholarship, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight the case of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast who migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling an economic revolution (Akyeampong&Fofack_2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15)). However, they also note that colonial rule reinforced women's subordination by codifying customary law, which further marginalized them in terms of land access (Akyeampong&Fofack_2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20)).

This scholarship underscores the complexity of institutions' role in shaping long-run economic growth. It suggests that while European colonization may have established certain institutional frameworks, these were not always conducive to economic development. Moreover, other factors, such as women's contributions and ethno-linguistic fragmentation, also play crucial roles in this process. As we delve deeper into the literature, we will explore how these factors interact and what implications they have for our understanding of long-run economic growth.

Greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.2) study highlights this mechanism: in societies with extreme inequality, institutions were designed to maintain elite status, thereby limiting economic opportunities for the majority.

The authors demonstrate that these institutions took various forms, such as property rights systems that favored elites or financial structures that excluded the masses. For instance, they show how inequalities in landholding patterns affected institutional development in Latin America and the Caribbean.

Moreover, Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) provide evidence supporting this pattern by showing how European settlers established institutions in the New World based on the level of inequality they encountered. In regions with high inequality, they created extractive institutions that exploited the local population rather than promoting economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The persistence of these elite-advantaging institutions over time can be seen in their impact on competition and innovation. Engerman, Haber, and Sokoloff (2000) argue that where elites controlled key sectors of the economy, they stifled competition and discouraged entrepreneurship, further perpetuating inequality. In contrast, societies with more equitable institutions fostered innovation and economic growth (Engerman et al. 2000).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing notion that African economies did not experience significant growth prior to European colonial interventions. They argue that many Sub-Saharan African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.10).

This claim is supported by their assertion that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.1). This period, however, was one when at least some other developing economies were converging with Europe and North America (Broadberry, 2021).

The implication of this finding is that the conventional wisdom may have underestimated the economic growth achieved by African societies prior to European colonialism. Furthermore, Broadberry and Gardner suggest that new contributions to historical national accounting and innovative techniques in African economic history have provided insights into patterns of growth and development over centuries (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016).

This discovery complicates the understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa. If these economies had achieved significant growth prior to European colonialism, it suggests that indigenous institutions may have played a more substantial role in promoting economic development than previously thought. Further research is needed to understand the specific mechanisms through which these institutions facilitated growth and how they were affected by European colonization.

Post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson's study challenges the notion that these countries' post-colonial expenditure was primarily geared towards patronage. Instead, he demonstrates that budget allocations were influenced by factors such as debt burdens and military spending.

The high debt burden in Tanzania and Kenya resulted from external factors, including colonial subsidies for defense and the peaking of interest payments (Simson 2019, p.11). In Uganda, the lower debt payments can be attributed to less access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief compared to its neighbors.

Another significant post-colonial expenditure shift was the growth in military spending. Simson (2019, p.11) explains that this increase responded to regional instability and domestic dissent, rather than nepotistic practices within public sector recruitment. The high-level allocation of wage resources during this period constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage.

Beyond military spending, changes occurred in the composition of employment, favoring comparatively skilled and female professions (Simson 2019, p.11). This shift did not rule out unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment but showed that from the 1980s onward, high-level allocation of wage resources generally constrained the distribution of patronage.

The work of Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) suggests that precolonial labor market conditions, an essential aspect of institutions, played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa by influencing real wages. Their research challenges the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments, as they demonstrate that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012, p.2).

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880-1965) to address the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012, p.2). Their findings show that real wages in Africa were not stagnant but exhibited significant growth, particularly in regions with a high degree of commercialization and less income inequality between different racial groups (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012, p.28).

One specific mechanism established by this stream is the impact of varying degrees of income inequality on long-run wage income growth. This finding calls for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development, as the recorded differences in long-run wage income growth patterns suggest that historical African poverty may not have been as persistent as previously assumed (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012, p.28).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and others argue that for economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology is insufficient; institutional and ideological adjustments are also essential to effectively participate in the modern economic system. According to Kuznets, modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions such as family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery, which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world (Kuznets_1973).

In his seminal work, Kuznets explains that modern technology necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, occupational status groups, and societal views. For instance, the dynamic drives of modern economic growth require a reaching out geographically and a continuous expansion to less developed areas (Kuznets_1973). However, these adjustments are not without challenges.

Thus, while Kuznets emphasizes the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments for economic growth, Simson illustrates that these changes may not always lead to a more equitable distribution of resources. Consequently, understanding the mechanisms through which institutions shape long-run economic growth must account for both the potential benefits and challenges associated with such transitions.

The increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century (Goldin 2006, p.7). This shift, as argued by Goldin (2006, p.7), resulted from various exogenous changes that made "nice" jobs available for women.

Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's first job interview after graduating near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class exemplifies this trend. Despite her impressive credentials, she was offered a position as a legal secretary and no California law firm extended an offer for a lawyer position (Goldin 2006, p.7). This illustrates the limited opportunities available for women in professional careers during that time.

The increased demand for female office workers can be traced back to the shift from manual to clerical work, which required less physical strength and was more suitable for women (Goldin 2006, p.7).

Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s further altered female labor supply.

Institutional changes also played a significant role in making part-time work readily available and dispensing with barriers to the employment of married women. For example, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination based on sex in hiring, employment, and apprenticeship (Goldin 2006, p.7).

This increased involvement of women in the labor force had profound downstream implications. As more women entered the workforce, they gained greater economic independence and were able to contribute more to household income (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This, in turn, led to a reduction in family size as families became less reliant on children for economic support (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intricately linked to labor dynamics, as evidenced by the British industrial revolution. This period saw the widespread availability and utilization of cheap and amenable female and child labor, a factor that Humphries (2013, p.16) argues was instrumental in the adoption of labor-saving technologies.

The traditional view of the British industrial revolution often focuses on men and high wages, but Humphries (2013, p.16) contends that this perspective is incomplete. He demonstrates that a significant proportion of boys grew up separated from their fathers due to marital instability, desertion, or paternal mortality, indicating the prevalence of family breakdown during this period. This finding aligns with other historians' depictions of the eighteenth century as a time of considerable marital instability (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Moreover, Humphries (2013, p.16) underscores that the availability of a substantial group of families whose fathers were absent, either deceased or separated, testifies to the turbulence of the times. This situation created a pool of cheap and amenable labor, which, in turn, facilitated the adoption of labor-saving technologies during the British industrial revolution.

The implications of this labor dynamic extend beyond the industrial revolution. As more women and children entered the workforce, they contributed to economic growth by reducing family size, as families became less reliant on children for economic support (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This downstream implication underscores the interconnectedness of institutions, labor dynamics, and long-run economic growth.

European class cultures were strategically restructured to maintain imperial control by creating and reinforcing social distinctions among the colonizers themselves, thereby ensuring the stability and legitimacy of their rule (Stoler 1989, p.6). This reconfiguration was not solely aimed at subjugating the indigenous populations but also served as a means to manage potential dissent within the European colonial community.

Stoler's work (1989) highlights that the European class cultures in the colonies were shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including racist ideology, fear of the Other, and obsession with preserving European prestige. These elements served as critical tools for maintaining social order among the colonizers and ensuring the continuity of their rule.

One significant implication of this class-based restructuring was the creation of a system that kept potentially subversive white colonials in line (Stoler 1989, p.6). This finding is supported by studies such as those by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979), which emphasize the distinct class interests within the colonial state. These works underscore that the European class cultures in the colonies were not monolithic but rather comprised different factions, each with its own set of priorities and concerns.

Moreover, this class-based logic extended beyond the colonies, as it influenced the internal dynamics of the European societies themselves (Stoler 1989, p.4). For instance, studies such as Dodwell (1926), Kincaid (1971), Spear (1963), and Edwards (1969) examine the social rankings among British colonials in India but fail to address the internal political tensions within the European community. This omission underscores the need for further research into the class-based dynamics of European societies during the colonial period.

In the realm of economic history, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and others propose that distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping the institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the efficiency-centered explanations often associated with these institutions' development.

For instance, Laslett's work on the European family system suggests an interdependency with labor markets, credit markets, and collective insurance systems, potentially contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Solar's study of community-based welfare in pre-industrial England reveals a connection between labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, which may have furthered economic growth before industrialization (ibid.).

Moreover, Acemoglu and Johnson's cross-country comparisons hint at interdependencies among institutions that regulate interactions between private citizens ('contracting' institutions) and those that constrain political and economic elites ('property rights' institutions). This suggests the need to examine entire institutional frameworks rather than isolating individual institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Furthermore, Putnam's theory of 'social capital', which refers to features of social organization that can improve society's efficiency by facilitating coordinated actions, highlights the role of cultural beliefs and values in fostering institutions conducive to economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These 'civic' cultures, with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (ibid.).

The emergence of guilds in western Europe during the late medieval period significantly influenced the skill premium by ensuring fair apprenticeship contracts and addressing labor market issues (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions, often criticized for monopolizing skills and forming local cartels, also played a crucial role in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the quality of these institutional arrangements was instrumental in shaping the skill premium in western Europe compared to other parts of the world economy (van Zanden 2009, p.1). The paper emphasizes that guilds, which had emerged as formal institutions, addressed problems related to apprenticeship contracts and labor market issues, issues that were not effectively managed elsewhere (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

However, these institutions have been criticized for their attempts to maintain a higher relative pay by restricting entry into their trades. Despite this potential counterbalance, the beneficial effects of guilds on skill transfer may outweigh any negative consequences from monopolistic behavior (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

In addition, van Zanden's work supports the broader argument that institutional quality is a crucial factor in shaping long-run economic growth.

Moreover, North and Weingast (2005) argue that credible commitment underpins growth . They show that institutions that guarantee fair contracts, such as those provided by guilds, foster economic development by reducing uncertainty and promoting trust among market participants . This finding aligns with van Zanden's work, further highlighting the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-run economic growth.

This negative effect was not only due to the external environment created by the slave trade, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, but also stemmed from an internal culture of mistrust among individuals (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

Nunn and Wantchekon's findings underscore the importance of trust in shaping economic development, supporting recent studies that highlight its significance for economic growth, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade disrupted traditional African societies, leading to a weakening of social bonds and increased mistrust among groups involved in the trade. This mistrust has persisted over time, impeding economic development in Africa.

The slave trades disrupted local societies and economies, making it difficult for them to recover and develop sustainably. The long-lasting impact of these trades can be seen in the lingering mistrust among communities that were once involved in the trade.

Furthermore, the negative consequences of the slave trade on trust and economic development were exacerbated by the weak institutional environment in Africa at the time (Nunn 2008, p.19). Sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly contributed to agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck (2010, p.8), Hornbeck (2010, p.32)). This innovation, which became nearly universal in the region, enabled farmers to effectively demarcate and protect their lands from encroachment by livestock.

In the aftermath of the herd law era, barbed wire's introduction marked a turning point in agricultural development. Prior to its widespread adoption, counties with herd laws experienced minimal improvements in farmland (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). However, following the implementation of barbed wire, these same counties saw significant increases in improved farmland and total land under cultivation (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

The impact of barbed wire on agricultural development can be further appreciated when considering its influence on property rights enforcement. By providing an efficient and cost-effective means to demarcate boundaries, farmers were better able to protect their lands from trespassing livestock, thereby enhancing security of property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This improved security likely encouraged investment in agricultural infrastructure and technology, contributing to the overall development of the American Plains.

In this context, barbed wire can be seen as a key factor in the shift from open-range grazing towards more intensive farming practices that characterized the American Plains during the late 19th century. This transition not only led to increased agricultural productivity but also paved the way for further economic growth and development in the region.
