The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of ongoing debate among scholars, as they grapple with understanding the complex interplay between political structures, historical events, and socio-economic development. At the heart of this discourse lies a live disagreement: while some argue that the foundational institutions established during European colonization significantly impact current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), others contend that African women played a crucial role in economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

They document how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling an economic revolution (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

These conflicting perspectives highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The next section delves into the implications of colonial rule for institutional reforms and its potential impact on poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.10).

The paper by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and complementary evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) suggest a significant role of institutional changes resulting from European colonialism in shaping current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during colonization varied significantly, with some colonies adopting laws that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, while others were set up as extractive states intended to transfer resources rapidly back to the metropole. This variability in institutional development, according to Acemoglu et al. has had lasting effects on current income levels.

The paper provides evidence that political power was crucial to elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offer a related perspective, suggesting that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their findings indicate that both the internal environment (i.e. norms of mistrust) and external factors (i.e. rule of law and trustworthiness of others) contribute to this mistrust, but the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust could have long-term implications for economic development, trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

Together, these papers suggest that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism play a significant role in shaping current income levels across countries by influencing the development of property rights, political power dynamics, and cultural norms. The lasting impact of these institutional changes on economic growth highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions are established and evolve.

The creation of political institutions that establish and protect property rights is crucial for long-term economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This period saw the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). These lower costs enabled the emergence of political institutions that specified 'efficient' property rights, fostering economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) offers insights on why these institutional changes were more effective in England than other economies. He argues that focusing on the English financial revolution can help overcome limitations of the new institutionalist approach associated with Douglass North (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin suggests that the adoption of favorable institutions for economic growth varied across historical contexts, highlighting the need to account for specific circumstances when studying long-term economic development.

They argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules. In England, this commitment was evident in the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years-War (North 1989, p.9).

Moreover, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that the success of these institutions depended on the sovereign's commitment to them. This commitment ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced effectively, promoting economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Jeffrey Williamson's work provides further support for this thesis (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), suggesting that secure rights played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and others argue that the growth of useful knowledge was pivotal in driving long-term economic growth, particularly during the Industrial Revolution in the British Isles (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Intellectual societies proliferated in this period, focusing on issues such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's impact on commerce, and more efficient taxes (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These intellectual communities played a crucial role in fostering the growth of useful knowledge, which in turn contributed significantly to economic development.

One specific mechanism that these sources jointly establish is the importance of law and order in promoting economic growth.

An implication of these findings is that the development of strong institutions that can enforce contracts and promote law and order is crucial for long-run economic growth. Therefore, the growth of useful knowledge, facilitated by strong institutions that enforce contracts and ensure law and order, can be seen as a key driver of long-run economic growth.

Goldin's (2006) study reveals that the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century was a significant factor in the subsequent rise of women's employment, education, and family roles. This shift was driven by various exogenous changes, such as the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin's findings are supported by Rona Jaffe's novel and movie, The Best of Everything (1958), which portrays the struggles of a young woman graduating from Radcliffe in the 1950s who lands a secretarial position with aspirations of becoming an editor (Goldin 2006, p.7).

This development, in turn, had profound implications for women's education levels. As more women entered the workforce, they gained access to higher education opportunities, further fueling their employment prospects and contributing to a virtuous cycle of educational attainment and labor force participation (Goldin 2006, p.7).

This stream of literature highlights the importance of understanding the historical evolution of gender roles and the impact of changes in demand for specific occupations on women's employment, education, and family roles. The findings underscore the importance of considering both supply-side factors, such as educational attainment, and demand-side factors, such as the growth of certain industries or occupations, when examining long-term trends in gender inequality.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) extend Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, arguing that continued collaboration among scholars can contribute to a better understanding of long-run economic growth. The Maddison Project, initiated by Angus Maddison in the 1960s, aimed to quantify long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) emphasize that estimates of past national accounts are subject to errors due to incomplete data and assumptions. They argue that these margins of error can be estimated objectively, as demonstrated by Feinstein & Thomas's method (Feinstein & Thomas, 1986).

This improved understanding of historical GDP and population data can inform research on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it provides a better foundation for analyzing trends over time.

The impact of the slave trade on current economic development and industrial revolution is a complex issue that has been explored by scholars such as Nunn and Humphries. According to Nunn (2008, p.2), Africa's slave trades had a long-lasting negative impact on the development of institutions in Africa, which in turn hindered economic growth. This claim is supported by evidence showing that the African continent experienced four slave trades for nearly 500 years, compared to approximately 75 years of official colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). Furthermore, Nunn argues that the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans only when it was found by the Portuguese (Nunn 2008, p.19).

In a separate study, Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that a more accurate understanding of the British industrial revolution is achieved by considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor, rather than focusing on men and high wages. This perspective complements Nunn's work by shedding light on the economic consequences of family breakdown and male abandonment during this period (Humphries 2013, p.16). Humphries also provides evidence of the turbulence of times, with war, empire building, and labor mobility straining men's links to wives and children (Humphries 2013, p.2).

Together, these studies suggest that the long-lasting effects of the slave trade on Africa's institutions and the availability of cheap labor in Britain played significant roles in shaping economic growth during the industrial revolution.

The British alliance with educated nationalists during the post-World War II nationalist ferment and the eve of independence in sub-Saharan Africa played a significant role in shaping land access for women, which in turn affected their economic contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.2).

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming under colonial rule, a practice reinforced by the central role of men in codifying customary law (Lawrance, Osborn, & Roberts 2006).

This educational advancement may have indirectly empowered women, as formal education often leads to improved land rights and economic opportunities. Akyeampong and Fofack caution that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances, perpetuating the subordinate status of women in access to land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The literature by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) challenges the prevailing notion that African economies were persistently poor due to structural growth impediments. Instead, they argue for a history of economic growth in many Sub-Saharan African countries prior to the 1980s.

They question the assumption that economic growth in Africa was primarily due to European colonial interventions, citing examples such as malaria prophylaxis and railways (Broadberry&Gardner_2022, p.10).

One implication of this research is a reevaluation of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. This line of inquiry could provide new insights into the mechanisms that underpin economic growth in Africa and inform policies aimed at promoting sustainable development.

For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the quality of institutions plays a crucial role in determining economic outcomes. They suggest that the presence of extractive institutions, which are designed to exploit rather than protect property rights, hinders economic growth. Inclusive institutions, which protect property rights and encourage investment, foster economic development. Understanding the types of institutions present in African economies prior to the 1980s could help explain why some countries experienced growth while others did not.

The paper by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Peters (2004, p.5) challenge the efficiency-based explanations for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, arguing instead that distributional conflicts played a more significant role. These scholars suggest that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework contributed to early industrialization and capitalism in Europe.

Similarly, Solar (cited in Ogilvie, 2007) posits that the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system; this distinctive framework may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.

Peters (2004, p.5), on the other hand, focuses on Africa, showing how a perception in official circles that 'custom' is static can have certain advantages for groups seeking to keep officials ignorant of what they are doing and changing. This misperception of communal landholding practices as inhibiting individual investment in agriculture was fueled by the push towards agricultural development during the final decade of colonial rule (up to c.1960). Critics like Jean-Philippe Platteau (cited in Peters, 2004) have pointed out that such unproblematic visions ignore considerable social inequality in many customary systems as well as evidence of encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood and/or commercial benefits, of state appropriation, and illegal sales by 'traditional' leaders.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) propose that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating economic disparities over the long run. This rival explanation to the role of institutions in promoting long-run economic growth highlights a specific mechanism: elite-advantaging institutions.

They contend that where there was extreme inequality, institutions were designed to advantage elites, limiting the access of much of the population to economic opportunities (Sokoloff & Engerman, 2000, p.2).

One downstream implication of this mechanism is that members of elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time, perpetuating the persistence of inequality. This path of institutional development may in turn have affected growth (Sokoloff & Engerman, 2000, p.12).

Furthermore, Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000) findings are supported by North and Weingast, who demonstrate that the English Glorious Revolution of 1688 established institutions that limited the power of the monarchy and protected property rights, thereby fostering economic growth. However, in contrast to Sokoloff and Engerman's focus on extreme inequality, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize the importance of credible commitment underpinned by a shared national identity in shaping institutions that promote economic growth.

In the scholarly discourse on economic growth in less developed countries, Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits an alternative perspective that challenges the notion of technology and institutional adjustments as primary drivers of long-term economic development. He argues that these factors are indeed crucial but may not be the main catalysts for growth as suggested by the central theme of this literature review.

Kuznets contends that advancing technology requires compatible institutions and social views, which were often absent in less developed countries during earlier times (E0097). Furthermore, modern economic systems necessitate rapid changes in labor force location, structure, and societal relations, a dynamic drive of growth that has far-reaching impacts on family formation, urbanization, and man's role in society (E0098).

Within this stream, Kuznets's findings are echoed by Hopkins (1973), who highlights the need for institutional changes to accommodate industrialization in less developed countries. He demonstrates that the transition from a rural agrarian society to an urban industrial one necessitates significant adjustments in social structures and economic institutions, such as the emergence of wage labor, market-oriented agriculture, and modern financial systems (Hopkins, 1973).

Moreover, Hornbeck (2015) provides empirical evidence supporting Kuznets's assertions. In his study on the impact of colonialism on economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa, he finds that the introduction of modern economic systems and institutions by European powers led to rapid urbanization, changes in labor force structure, and shifts in societal values, all of which are key drivers of economic growth as identified by Kuznets (1973, p.1).

European class cultures were strategically restructured to reinforce imperial control, according to Stoler (1989, p.6). This process involved the selective adaptation of social norms and power dynamics within Europe itself, as well as in its colonies, to maintain order and subdue potential dissent among both indigenous populations and European underlings.

Stoler's work highlights how colonial authorities employed racist ideologies, fear of the Other, and concerns about white prestige to establish and preserve social hierarchies that benefited the imperial elite (E0177). For instance, these strategies served to create a sense of 'colonial community' and context that bolstered colonial authority and regulated relations of production and power.

Simultaneously, Stoler points out that many historical studies on European colonization have overlooked the internal political tensions among Europeans themselves (E0178). This oversight is significant because understanding these tensions can shed light on how class-based dynamics within the colonial state contributed to the maintenance of imperial control.

By focusing on the internal workings of the colonial state, Stoler's work complements studies by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979), which also examine distinct class interests within the colonial state. While these scholars provide valuable insights into the political dimensions of colonial rule, they do not explicitly link their findings to long-run economic growth.

The selective restructuring of European class cultures to maintain imperial control offers a glimpse into the complex social dynamics that underpinned colonial rule. To fully understand how these processes shaped long-term economic growth, it is necessary to explore additional mechanisms and consider how the economic interests of various actors within the colonial system influenced the development and evolution of institutions over time.

This exploration will be undertaken in the subsequent sections, where we delve into studies that examine the role of specific institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on factors such as wage labor, market-oriented agriculture, and modern financial systems (e.g. Acemoglu et al. 2001; North & Weingast, 1989).

Hopkins argues that European encroachment was often resisted by larger populations in Africa, which occupied epidemiological zones that imposed a high rate of mortality on Europeans.

The Atlantic slave trade is a particularly telling case, as it reduced the population from a level that was previously relatively high and encouraged the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth.

In this context, Hopkins suggests that institutional reforms could play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa by addressing the legacy of extractive institutions established during European colonization. This proposition aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions established during European colonization have profound and lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Hopkins's paper underscores the importance of understanding how the state can be leveraged to improve institutional quality and, in turn, foster long-term economic growth. As such, policymakers may wish to consider his call for a shift towards 'bringing the state back in' as a means to address poverty in Africa. This argument provides a valuable perspective that complements the existing literature on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, emphasizing the potential for policy interventions to shape institutional development and ultimately influence economic outcomes.

The subsequent sections will delve into additional mechanisms through which institutional reforms could impact long-term economic growth in Africa, further exploring Hopkins's proposition that 'bringing the state back in' may hold promise as a strategy for reducing poverty on the continent.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly contributed to agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This assertion is grounded in the near-universal implementation of barbed wire fencing, which enabled farmers to secure their lands more effectively against encroachment by livestock and prevented overgrazing.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) presents evidence that herd laws, aimed at regulating livestock movement, became less prevalent after 1880, coinciding with the widespread adoption of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8).

The benefits of barbed wire extended beyond land protection. By securing property rights, it facilitated long-term investments in farming infrastructure and fostered the growth of agricultural markets.

In this context, the adoption of barbed wire can be seen as an institutional innovation that improved property rights enforcement, promoting agricultural growth in the American Plains.

As we move forward, it is worth considering whether similar institutional innovations could be leveraged to address challenges faced by agricultural development in other contexts, such as sub-Saharan Africa. The potential for policy interventions to shape institutional development and ultimately influence economic outcomes remains an important area of inquiry (Hopkins ).

The emergence of formal institutions in western Europe during the late medieval period, as argued by van Zanden (2009, p.19), played a significant role in shaping the skill premium. This claim is supported by the guilds, which ensured fair execution of apprentice contracts and facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, despite their criticism for forming local cartels and restricting entry into their trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

This finding is consistent with the broader literature that emphasizes the role of institutions in economic growth (North & Weingast 1989; North 1989).

This raises questions about the overall effect of such institutions on economic growth and development.

Furthermore, the skill premium, as measured by van Zanden (2009, p.19), is also suggested to be a predictor of long-term economic performance. This finding aligns with Kuznets' (1973) assertion that factors affecting economic growth in less developed countries are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

The literature on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth reveals a consensus that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism have significantly impacted current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). A point of contention arises regarding the effects of these institutions on the intergenerational transfer of skills and local economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies varied greatly, with some leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In contrast, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the role African women played in economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods, suggesting that attempts by these institutions to form local cartels could potentially counterbalance the benefits of skill transfer (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The skill premium, as measured by van Zanden (2009, p.19), is also found to be a predictor of long-term economic performance, aligning with Kuznets' (1973) assertion that factors affecting economic growth in less developed countries are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This finding, however, does not explicitly address the effects of institutions on skill transfer and local economic development.

To resolve this disagreement, a thorough examination of historical evidence from both colonizer and colonized regions could provide valuable insights. For instance, comparing the impact of European colonization strategies on skill transfer in societies such as the United States (where institutions enforced the rule of law) with that in regions where extractive states were established (such as the Congo or the Gold Coast) could shed light on this issue (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
