The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centered on the causal mechanisms and the extent to which institutional structures influence economic development. This literature review examines various perspectives that shed light on this question, focusing on the impact of European colonization, the contributions of African women, the implications for institutional reforms, the culture of mistrust induced by the slave trade, and the influence of British rule in facilitating formal education.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that different strategies adopted by Europeans led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies promoting investment and rule of law, while others were set up for resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). On the other hand, Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) challenge this narrative by highlighting the significant contributions made by African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Under colonial rule, however, these gains were reversed, as customary law reinforced women's subordination.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) extend this discussion by investigating the lasting impact of the slave trade on intergroup trust in Africa. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds another layer by examining the role of British traders and Protestant missionaries in facilitating formal education in sub-Saharan Africa before 1940.

Finally, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. He contends that cultural theories, such as Putnam's theory of social capital, offer valuable insights into the relationship between institutional structures and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

This claim is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that these institutional changes account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

In some cases, they established extractive states with the intention of quickly transferring resources to the metropole, leading to lower income levels.

Nunn (2008, p.2) provides evidence that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.2). This finding adds a layer of complexity to the relationship between European colonialism and economic growth, suggesting that certain aspects of colonialism may have had detrimental effects.

The development of political institutions that secure property rights and enforce them effectively plays a crucial role in promoting long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This is evident in the case of pre-industrial Europe, where the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework significantly contributed to early industrialization and capitalism.

This, in turn, facilitated the growth of a long-term capital market essential for economic growth.

They argue that the success of the financial revolution was remarkable, as shown by evidence from capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further qualifies this implication, stating that cultural theories, such as Putnam's theory of social capital, also play a role in encouraging institutions that favour economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Mokyr's (2005, p.54) seminal work underscores intellectual factors as a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. He argues that the expansion of useful knowledge played a crucial role in spurring technological change and industrialization, which ultimately led to the unprecedented economic growth witnessed during this period.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the Enlightenment era saw an explosion of intellectual activity focused on political economy, with scholars exploring issues such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's impact on commerce, and more efficient taxation systems. Central to Mokyr's argument is the Enlightenment idea that individual self-interest often benefits society as a whole, provided individuals do not engage in redistribution or rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Moreover, Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that the Industrial Revolution was not solely a British phenomenon but rather had its roots in various nations, each with different institutional foundations supporting growth (Mokyr 2005, p.3). For instance, early modern states like the Dutch Republic and Venice exhibited institutional structures that facilitated economic growth, albeit to a lesser extent than Britain during the Industrial Revolution (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

In this context, it is worth noting that Mokyr's work complements North and Weingast's (1989) analysis of seventeenth-century England. While North and Weingast focus on the role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth, Mokyr highlights intellectual factors as another essential component driving long-term prosperity. Together, their findings suggest that a combination of secure institutions and a thriving intellectual environment can create an ideal setting for sustained economic development (shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Mokyr 2005, p.3)).

As we delve deeper into the literature, it becomes increasingly clear that intellectual growth and institutional development are intertwined in shaping long-run economic growth.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor emerges as a significant factor in the British industrial revolution, according to Humphries (2013, p.16). This assertion is grounded in the observation that inventions during this period were often designed to reduce the need for skilled adult workers.

Instead, he contends that considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor offers a more accurate perspective. This claim is substantiated by evidence suggesting that a significant group of families during this period had fathers who were absent or deceased, indicating the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Furthermore, Humphries's findings are supported by historical accounts depicting the eighteenth century as a period of considerable marital instability, which in turn led to family breakdown and male abandonment, resulting in large numbers of women and children among pauper populations (Humphries 2013, p.16).

The implications of this stream of literature suggest that the British industrial revolution was shaped not only by the availability of skilled adult labor but also by the presence of a significant group of families with absent or deceased fathers. This finding underscores the complexity of the factors driving long-term economic growth, emphasizing the importance of understanding the role of various demographic groups in shaping historical economic developments.

As we move forward, it becomes evident that the interplay between different demographic groups and their roles in economic growth is a crucial aspect to consider when examining long-run economic development.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement, thereby contributing to long-run economic growth (Hornbeck (2010, p.8), Hornbeck (2010, p.32)). This shift, as Hornbeck (2010, p.8) argues, was underpinned by the decline in herd laws that had previously regulated cattle grazing on common lands. With barbed wire's advent, ranchers could effectively enclose their properties, thus increasing control over their land use and fostering agricultural improvements.

This development was particularly significant as it allowed farmers to cultivate crops more efficiently, even in arid regions of the American Plains. Consequently, agricultural output increased, and farmers were better able to sustain their livelihoods during periods of drought or other adverse weather conditions.

This change facilitated the emergence of a market for land and encouraged investment in agricultural improvements. As a result, the American Plains experienced a surge in economic growth during the late 19th century, as farmers were able to produce more goods with greater efficiency.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth for less developed countries, stating that these nations must adopt advanced technology, undergo institutional and ideological adjustments, and gain access to tested data. The paper argues that modern economic growth necessitates changes in labor force location and structure, occupational status groups, and societal views (Kuznets_1973, p.4).

In terms of agricultural development, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide evidence that institutions play a crucial role in determining the level and direction of technological adoption. They demonstrate that European colonization led to the creation of extractive institutions in Africa, which hindered technological progress and economic growth (AcemogluEtAl_2002). In regions where European powers introduced more inclusive institutions, such as those in Latin America, technological advancement and economic growth were encouraged.

The shift towards individual property rights, as seen in the American Plains with the widespread use of barbed wire (Hornbeck_2010), is another example of how institutional changes can facilitate technological adoption and economic growth. The emergence of a market for land encouraged investment in agricultural improvements, leading to increased output and greater efficiency (Hornbeck_2010).

The codification of customary law under colonial rule, a specific aspect of institutions, significantly shaped gender dynamics and economic outcomes in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20)). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made substantial contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods.

Polly Hill (1963) documented how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. This agricultural boom had consequences for women, however.

The introduction of dual legal systems under colonial rule enabled the codification of patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women.

The British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education and economic development, which are argued to have contributed to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Frankema's 2012 paper argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa primarily influenced formal education through the impact of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2). Hopkins, in his 2009 work, highlights how European encroachment in regions with larger populations and more resistant native African societies led to the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

Frankema's research reveals the significant role of British traders and missionaries in promoting formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. The Protestant missionaries, for instance, established schools to educate African converts and promote their faith. Similarly, British traders needed educated Africans to serve as clerks, interpreters, and agents in their businesses (Frankema 2012, p.17).

Hopkins' work underscores the impact of European encroachment on the development of institutions that hindered long-term growth. Conversely, areas under direct colonial rule introduced extractive economic control systems that were poorly suited for long-term development.

The downstream implication of these findings is that the facilitation of formal education and economic development by British colonial rule likely contributed to long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. The effectiveness of these efforts was influenced by factors such as the resistance of native African societies and the presence of unproductive institutions before European encroachment.

In the context of New World societies, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)) argue that greater inequality leads to institutions disproportionately benefiting elites, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This finding challenges the conventional wisdom that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12)) contend that in societies with extreme inequality, the developed institutions serve to maintain elite status, often limiting opportunities for much of the population. For instance, in Latin America, the concentration of wealth among a few led to the establishment of institutions that reinforced this inequality, such as land tenure systems and labor regulations that favored the elite.

This institutional setup, in turn, had implications for economic growth. This finding does not imply that institutions are not crucial for economic growth; rather, it underscores the role of inequality in shaping the nature of these institutions.

This notion aligns with the findings of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) who argue that economic institutions are crucial for long-run economic growth, particularly in less developed countries .

Therefore, addressing inequality and promoting more equitable institutions could be a key strategy for fostering long-term economic growth in New World societies. This argument underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between inequality, institutions, and economic growth, a theme that will carry into the next section.

Goldin's (2006) work offers insights into the changes in women's employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century. The increased involvement of women was primarily driven by exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, a finding shared with Goldin (2006, p.7). This shift was facilitated by the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3).

Goldin's (2006) research underscores the role of exogenous factors in shaping women's employment patterns. The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various external changes, including the increased relative demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes made 'nice' jobs available and removed barriers to the employment of married women.

In a qualifying study, Goldin (2006, p.7) provides an illustrative example of this mechanism through Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's first job interview after graduating near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class. Despite her impressive academic achievements, no California law firm offered her a position as a lawyer, instead offering her a secretarial position with hopes of becoming an editor. This anecdote highlights the barriers women faced in entering traditionally male-dominated professions and the role of exogenous changes in overcoming these barriers.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) contribute to the ongoing efforts to refine and expand Angus Maddison's estimates of global GDP and population by focusing on the early modern period and Asia. The Maddison Project, a collaboration among scholars, aims to extend and revise these estimates, recognizing that data from earlier periods may have larger margins of error but can still be improved upon (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.3). This endeavor is significant as it allows for a more detailed understanding of long-term economic growth than when Maddison first initiated his project in the 1960s (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.2).

In this context, Bolt and van Zanden's work builds upon Maddison's strategy of producing numbers despite potential uncertainties, encouraging scholars to challenge and refine these estimates through new research (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.2). One such example is the Hitotshibashi team's study on Asian national accounts, which experimented with various margins of error for their data (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.3). This downstream implication underscores the collaborative nature of the Maddison Project and its impact on advancing our understanding of long-term economic growth.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically restructured to preserve social hierarchies, which in turn fostered the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler 1987; Stoler 1989). This reconfiguration served multiple purposes, including the maintenance of colonial authority and the management of potential dissent among European underlings.

The work of Ann Laura Stoler (1989, p.6) provides a comprehensive analysis of this phenomenon, highlighting how fear of the Other, racist ideology, and preoccupation with white prestige were integral to the class-based logic that dictated colonial rule. According to Stoler, these factors were not merely justifications for continued European dominance and white supremacy but also instrumental in maintaining order among European colonials (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Moreover, Stoler's research underscores the importance of understanding the internal political tensions within the colonial state. She cites various studies that focus on social rankings among the British in India and French interaction with the colonized, but these works often overlook the distinct class interests within the colonial state (Stoler 1989, p.4). Works by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979) address this gap by examining the internal political tensions among Europeans in the colonies.

This strategic restructuring of European class cultures had downstream implications for the development of cultural infrastructure in colonies. As Stoler argues, the creation of a sense of community and context within the colonial setting allowed for the perpetuation of colonial authority and the establishment of specific relations of production and power (Stoler 1989, p.6). This finding aligns with the work of other scholars who have explored the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they demonstrate how institutions can be manipulated to serve the interests of those in power (Acemoglu et al. 2001; Nunn & Wantchekon 2011).

The quality of institutions, as measured by the fair execution of apprentice contracts in guilds, plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that in the late medieval period, the skill premium in western Europe fell sharply compared to most other parts of the world economy, and this decline can be attributed partly to the emergence of institutions that ensured fair apprentice contracts, such as guilds.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) contends that these institutions had a twofold impact on economic growth. Firstly, they facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, ensuring that knowledge was passed down efficiently from master craftsmen to apprentices. Secondly, by guaranteeing fair contracts, guilds mitigated the potential negative effects of cartelization, as they prevented monopolistic practices from artificially inflating wages and stifling competition.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth can also be observed downstream, in the development of specific industries. For instance, Hornbeck (2010, p.8) demonstrates how the introduction of barbed wire in the American Plains facilitated agricultural expansion by enabling more efficient land management and resource utilization, an outcome that was contingent upon the legal recognition of property rights and the enforcement of these rights through institutions.

Simson's (2019) investigation into post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda challenges common assumptions that these countries' spending prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial expenditure. Contrary to widespread belief, Simson argues that the three nations did not exhibit exceptionally unproductive budgeting practices.

In the early 1990s, Kenya's subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30% of total spending, displacing both capital and recurrent expenditure (Simson 2019, p.11). Tanzania also faced a high debt burden, with interest payments reaching 18% of expenditure, while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief (Simson 2019, p.11).

Beyond the capital cities, the geographic distribution of employment did not change significantly, with jobs increasingly favoring comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward (Simson 2019, p.19). This shift in employment composition constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage, suggesting that nepotistic or unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment were not widespread.

Simson's findings underscore a downstream implication: post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda did not prioritize patronage networks to the detriment of socially beneficial spending as commonly assumed. Instead, they demonstrate a more nuanced picture, with a focus on managing debt burdens and maintaining employment opportunities across various sectors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10)(Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1) challenge the widely held belief that European colonial interventions were the main drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 1980s. Contrary to earlier economic histories, which attributed any growth above subsistence to European influence, these scholars argue that many African economies had per capita income levels comparable to Asia's largest economies during this period.

The authors substantiate their claim through a critical review of existing studies, pointing out that most of them rely on data from the mid-20th century or later, a period marked by stagnant per capita incomes in Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). In contrast, they highlight recent contributions to historical national accounting and innovative techniques in African economic history that have shed light on growth patterns over centuries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1).

Broadberry and Gardner contend that these new findings reveal that many Sub-Saharan African economies had reached levels above subsistence by the early 20th century.

Simson's findings (2019) further support this downstream implication by demonstrating that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda did not prioritize patronage networks to the detriment of socially beneficial spending. Instead, they focused on managing debt burdens and maintaining employment opportunities across various sectors (Simson 2019). This nuanced picture reinforces Broadberry and Gardner's argument that African economies had a level of development comparable to Asia before the 1980s, implying that European colonial interventions may not have been as crucial for growth as commonly believed.

In the examination of institutions' role in shaping long-run economic growth, a consensus emerges that European colonial interventions significantly influenced current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)). The extent to which these interventions were detrimental or beneficial remains a topic of debate.

Acemoglu and colleagues (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that European colonization established institutions with varying impacts on economic growth. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering long-term growth. On the other hand, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states with a focus on transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, hindering economic development (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)).

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) emphasize the significant contributions of African women to pre-colonial and colonial economic growth. They note that European rule reinforced customary law, which often relegated women to marginal lands for food farming and subordinated their access to land in the context of cash crops (Akyeampong & Fofack, (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15)). This perspective suggests a more nuanced view of colonial interventions' impact on African economies.
