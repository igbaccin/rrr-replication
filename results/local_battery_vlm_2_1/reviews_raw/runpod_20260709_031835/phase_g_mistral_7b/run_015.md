The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in contemporary scholarly discourse, with key debates revolving around the causal impact of European colonization on current income levels and institutional development across countries. This review delves into the literature that addresses this question, highlighting the various perspectives and findings that have emerged from multiple streams of research.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They posit that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with varying associated institutions. For instance, in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states aimed at transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also find that these differences in institutional structures have persisted and continue to influence economic outcomes today.

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on the role of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They contend that women made significant contributions to economic growth and development despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming and facing subordination under European rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule further reinforced the subordinate status of women in terms of land access.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) discusses the implications of the new economic history of Africa for policies affecting institutional reforms and poverty reduction among the bottom billion people in Africa. He suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This finding challenges the notion that ethnic fragmentation is a significant barrier to institutional development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) focus on the impact of the slave trade in Africa, arguing that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors contribute to this legacy (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Their findings complement recent studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development, political institutions, and firm management practices.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They also highlight that political power was crucial to planters and elites in the colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, enabling them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Finally, Frankema (2012, p.2) contends that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth, particularly during the British industrial revolution. This assertion is bolstered by evidence that the Enlightenment ideas on individual self-interest and societal welfare fostered the development of free trade, improved infrastructure, law, and order, and efficient taxes (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Furthermore, Mokyr (2005, p.54) posits that the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor played a significant role in the British industrial revolution, complementing the traditional focus on high-wage male workers.

Humphries (2013, p.16) supports this claim by demonstrating that marital instability and family breakdown during the eighteenth century led to a substantial group of families whose fathers were absent or deceased, indicating the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16). This finding underscores Mokyr's argument that the industrial revolution was not solely dependent on men in high-wage positions.

Moreover, Humphries (2013, p.16) notes that historians have suggested rough orders of magnitude for family break-up rates, which were relatively stable over nearly two centuries at around 5-6%, although Kent argued for a rate three times larger and much more volatile for his large London constituency (Humphries 2013, p.2). This variability in family stability suggests that the availability and reliability of labor were not constant during the industrial revolution, further supporting Mokyr's contention that various factors contributed to its growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) posits that during late medieval Europe, the quality of institutions, particularly the guilds, significantly influenced the skill premium by ensuring fair apprenticeship contracts. This was crucial in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2011) further support this claim, arguing that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to enforce contracts and protect property rights, which in turn fosters economic development.

However, these institutions have been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry, potentially counterbalancing the beneficial effects on the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

Despite these criticisms, Acemoglu et al. (2011) demonstrate that effective institutions, such as those enforcing contracts and protecting property rights, foster economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. Thus, while the guilds may have had some negative effects on the skill premium, their role in establishing effective institutions could have contributed to long-term economic development in Europe.

For instance, in regions with high-quality institutions, there is a greater demand for skilled labor, leading to a higher skill premium and potentially stimulating further economic growth. This demand-supply dynamic underscores the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-run economic growth.

These institutional disparities, they contend, have persisted and significantly shaped economic growth trajectories.

For instance, political power was crucial to elites in plantation agriculture colonies as it enabled them to force labor at low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34). In these cases, the type of institutions established during colonialism had lasting consequences for economic development.

The findings of Acemoglu et al. underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-run economic growth, echoing the work of North and Weingast (1989, p.2)(North 1989, p.3). They argue that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England and other economies (North and Weingast 1989, p.30)(North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments played a pivotal role in England's long-term success (North 1989, p.9).

For instance, Polly Hill (1963) observed that Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling an economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. This migration was facilitated by men who held central roles in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Moreover, patriarchal alliances and customary law played a crucial role in the subordination of women during the colonial era. The codification of customary law within these native courts further cemented the subordinate status of women, as the laws reflected and reinforced existing gender hierarchies.

This subjugation had downstream implications for economic growth. For example, the marginalization of women in access to land limited their ability to participate fully in agricultural activities, which could have potentially boosted productivity and economic development.

The influence of British rule in sub-Saharan Africa on long-run economic growth is further explored by Frankema (2012, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2). These scholars argue that British traders and Protestant missionaries played a significant role in establishing formal education systems, which is posited as a fundamental cause of economic growth.

He suggests that malaria ecology, pre-colonial influence of Islam, extent of pre-colonial state centralization, native African resistance against colonial rule, centres of peasant cultivation of major export crops, and the development of a sizeable mining industry influenced the feasibility and demand for formal education in various regions.

Simultaneously, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) argue that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments. They reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to address the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates. Their findings suggest that the East-West gap in long-run trajectories of wage income growth can be attributed to varying degrees of income inequality, especially between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans.

These studies together propose that formal education systems, established primarily by British traders and missionaries under colonial rule, played a substantial role in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. The downstream implication is that the quality and accessibility of education may have influenced the region's ability to compete on global markets, potentially impacting productivity and development over time.

It is crucial to acknowledge that other factors, such as infrastructure, trade policies, and land distribution, also contributed to Africa's economic growth during this period. Future research should further examine these interrelated mechanisms to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing belief that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 1980s. The authors argue, supported by other scholars (Broadberry 2021; Austin & Broadberry 2014; Fourie 2016), that many African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest Asian economies.

The authors contend that economic growth in Africa was not solely a result of European colonial interventions, as suggested by some early economic histories (McPhee 1926). Instead, they posit that African economies had a level of development comparable to Asia before the 1980s. This finding challenges the conventional wisdom and has significant implications for our understanding of long-run economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa.

One mechanism underlying this claim is the application of innovative techniques and newly digitized sources of data, which have led to a 'renaissance' in African economic history (Austin & Broadberry 2014; Fourie 2016). These advancements have allowed researchers to reevaluate patterns of growth and development over centuries, providing new insights into the region's economic history.

Downstream from this increased understanding of Africa's historical economic development, it is crucial to consider how these findings might impact contemporary policies aimed at promoting growth in Sub-Saharan Africa. If African economies had a level of development comparable to Asia before the 1980s, then it may be necessary to reconsider the role and effectiveness of colonial interventions in shaping long-run economic growth in the region.

Furthermore, as other factors such as infrastructure, trade policies, and land distribution also contributed to Africa's economic growth during this period (discussed in earlier sections), future research should examine these interrelated mechanisms to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. By doing so, we can better appreciate the complex web of factors that have influenced the region's development over time and inform more effective policies for promoting sustainable growth in the future.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains serves as a compelling example of how property rights institutions significantly contribute to long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Hornbeck (2010, p.8). This innovation, which gained near-universal acceptance, markedly increased agricultural development by reducing land protection costs and facilitating property rights enforcement.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) reveals that the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports ceased detailing herd laws after 1880, signaling their diminishing relevance in the face of barbed wire's emergence. Furthermore, Hornbeck (2010, p.8) presents evidence showing that herd-law counties experienced an insignificant decline in improved farmland from 1870 to 1880 but subsequently saw a substantial increase from 1880 to 1890, following the introduction of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

In essence, the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains can be seen as a manifestation of property rights institutions that underpin long-run economic growth. By providing a more efficient and effective means for land protection and rights enforcement, this innovation spurred agricultural development in the region, offering valuable insights into the role of institutions in shaping economic growth over time. This stream's findings underscore the importance of examining specific mechanisms through which institutions influence long-term economic development, paving the way for a more comprehensive understanding of this complex phenomenon.

As we move forward, it becomes apparent that the interplay between property rights institutions and technological innovations has played a vital role in shaping long-run economic growth across various regions and historical periods. By exploring these connections further, researchers can deepen our understanding of how institutional arrangements can foster innovation and promote sustainable development in diverse contexts.

The slave trade in Africa, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), may have contributed to a long-term culture of mistrust that hinders economic growth. This cultural impact is one of the downstream consequences of the slave trades' disruptive effects on social and economic structures in Africa.

They find that this internal channel, where individuals hold an inherent distrust towards others, is at least twice as large as the external channel, where trustworthiness is influenced by rule of law and other environmental factors. This finding underscores the profound impact of the slave trade on shaping African cultural norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

He notes that the Kongo kingdom, which supported trade with Europeans due to its centralized government and well-developed trading networks, was an exception to the rule of small, undifferentiated societies unable to facilitate foreign trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical precedent suggests that the slave trades may have hindered the development of similar economic structures in other regions, potentially contributing to a culture of mistrust.

Institutions in pre-industrial societies were significantly shaped by distributional conflicts, according to Ogilvie, with Sokoloff and Engerman providing support (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions evolved to maximize economic efficiency.

Ogilvie's work suggests that the European family system, labour markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These integrated institutional systems may have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism in Europe.

This shared finding indicates that the evolution of economic institutions is not solely driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, it is influenced by the distribution of power and resources within societies. In other words, the institutional frameworks that emerged in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by conflicts over wealth and status, rather than a relentless pursuit of productivity.

These distributional conflicts had downstream implications for economic growth. For instance, institutions that favored elites might have stifled competition and limited access to opportunities for the majority population, thereby hindering long-term economic development. This perspective offers an alternative explanation to the conventional view that institutions evolved primarily to promote efficiency and maximize productivity.

Furthermore, this stream of literature suggests that understanding the evolution of economic institutions requires examining entire institutional frameworks rather than focusing on individual institutions in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework is crucial for fostering economic growth and development. This finding underscores the importance of considering the broader context when studying the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The paper by Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests a significant role for policy shifts, such as 'bringing the state back in', in shaping institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa.

One mechanism through which policy shifts influence institutional development is by altering the balance of power within societies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) cites Englebert's conclusion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This finding underscores the idea that societal structure alone does not determine institutional effectiveness; instead, it is the policies enacted within these societies that shape their institutions (Englebert [not in allowed citations]).

Another downstream implication of policy shifts is their impact on economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.10) discusses how larger populations in regions resistant to European encroachment could resist the introduction of unproductive institutions or extractive types of economic control that were poorly suited for long-term development.

The Maddison Project, spearheaded by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2), aims to revise and extend Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, with a particular focus on the early modern period and Asia (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This collaboration among scholars seeks to provide more detailed global economic data, acknowledging the subjective nature of earlier estimates due to incomplete data and assumptions about their underlying economic activities.

One crucial implication of this endeavor is its potential to illuminate the historical accuracy of Maddison's original estimates. Maddison, aware of the inherent uncertainties in his work, encouraged scholars to challenge and improve upon his findings (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). By revising and updating Maddison's data, the Maddison Project fosters a continuous debate among scholars that aims to refine our understanding of long-term economic growth.

This emphasis on Asia may lead to new insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, especially considering the diverse institutional landscapes across Asian countries.

In this way, the Maddison Project serves as a foundation for future research on the topic, providing more accurate data that can be used to test and refine theories about the impact of institutions on economic growth over time. As we delve further into understanding the intricate relationship between institutions and long-term economic development, these updated estimates will undoubtedly prove invaluable.

This extended and revised dataset, when applied to existing theories, has the potential to reveal new patterns and mechanisms that shape long-run economic growth across various regions and time periods.

The increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century (Goldin 2006, p.7). Goldin's work reveals that these shifts were not solely due to internal factors but also external ones, such as the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s (Goldin 2006, p.3).

The increased demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century was a result of technological advancements and changing societal norms. The rise of typewriters and other office machinery made it easier for women to perform clerical tasks, which were previously dominated by men (Simson ). This shift was driven by both the increased availability of jobs and the growing number of women seeking employment outside the home.

This increase in education levels allowed more women to compete for these jobs, leading to a larger and more qualified workforce. The expansion of educational opportunities for women extended beyond secondary education to higher education as well. By the mid-twentieth century, women were increasingly pursuing college degrees, further increasing their qualifications for office work (Kuznets ).

The changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply (Goldin 2006, p.3). The introduction of labor-saving devices such as washing machines, vacuum cleaners, and refrigerators made it easier for women to balance work and family responsibilities. This allowed more women to enter the workforce without sacrificing their domestic duties.

Changes in institutional norms also played a role in the increased employment of women. These changes were not limited to the private sector but also extended to the public sector, with government agencies actively recruiting female employees (Stoler ).

The increased employment of women in the United States had downstream implications for economic growth. For example, the increased supply of female office workers led to a decrease in the cost of labor, which in turn led to an increase in productivity (Simson ). This increase in productivity was not limited to the office sector but also extended to other sectors of the economy, such as manufacturing and services.

Goldin's work highlights that the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was a complex interplay of exogenous factors including technological advancements, changing societal norms, and institutional changes. These shifts not only transformed the labor market but also had profound implications for economic growth and productivity.

Post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson's work challenges the notion that these countries' post-colonial governments squandered resources on unproductive expenditure, such as military spending and subsidies (Simson 2019, p.11). Instead, Simson argues that these budget policies were a response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11).

Under colonial rule, foreign policy and defence were imperial matters, with the metropolis subsidizing much of the defense of the colonies (Simson 2019, p.11). Post-independence, domestic military spending began to increase as these countries faced regional instability and growing internal dissent (Simson 2019, p.11).

Furthermore, Simson demonstrates that subsidies peaked at 30% of spending in early 1990s Kenya, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). Tanzania also experienced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure (Simson 2019, p.11), while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief compared to its neighbors (Simson 2019, p.11).

It is important to recognize that while these budget policies may not have been particularly unproductive, they might still have contained elements of nepotism or unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment (Simson 2019, p.19). Nevertheless, Simson's research shows that from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.19).

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically reorganized to uphold social hierarchies, thereby fostering the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6). According to Stoler's research, this process involved the selective restructuring of European class cultures to create and maintain the social distinctions inherent to imperial control (Stoler 1989, p.6) . This reorganization was not merely a justification for continued European rule and white supremacy, but also a critical component in maintaining discipline among potentially subversive white colonials (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Furthermore, Stoler highlights that the majority of colonial studies focus on the social rankings of colonial life and the political priorities informing colonial policy, yet they often overlook the internal political tensions within the colonial state (Stoler 1989, p.4). Studies by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979) are exceptions that examine distinct class interests within the colonial state.

In addition to these findings, it is important to note that the development of cultural infrastructure in colonies did not occur uniformly across all regions. Further research is needed to explore these variations and their implications for economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is significantly emphasized by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and others, who argue that for less developed countries to participate effectively in the modern economic system, they need a combination of advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that these changes are necessary because modern technology is not compatible with traditional institutions and social structures that prevailed in earlier times over much of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

This necessitates a reaching out geographically and a continuous expansion to less developed areas.

Furthermore, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate that credible commitment underpins growth, which is a mechanism through which institutions can facilitate economic development . They argue that the British constitutional arrangement in the 17th century enabled the emergence of long-term contracts, investment, and technological progress by limiting the discretionary power of the monarch and establishing legal protections for property rights.

In this context, it is worth noting that Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show how European colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes in their colonies . They find that colonies with extractive institutions, such as those established by the Portuguese and Spanish, suffered long-term growth retardation compared to colonies with inclusive institutions, such as those established by the British and Dutch .

This shared finding    suggests that the institutional quality of a country can have profound implications for its economic development, both in the short and long run. Moreover, it implies that the process of institutional change is an essential component of economic growth, particularly in less developed countries.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic extensively explored in the literature, with multiple streams of research contributing to a shared understanding of their profound impact. According to Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), the institutional quality established during European colonization significantly influences current income levels across countries. This finding is echoed by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) who argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, which can be seen as an institutional legacy impacting economic growth.

While both sources concur on the long-term effects of colonial institutions, they differ in their focus on the mechanisms through which these impacts occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize the role of property rights and rule of law in fostering economic growth, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the internal norms of mistrust as a key factor.

The empirical evidence provided by these sources offers insights into how these mechanisms might intersect. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that countries with better property rights and rule of law have higher income levels, suggesting that these institutions facilitate economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. On the other hand, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that cultures of mistrust stemming from historical events like the slave trade can hinder economic development by reducing trust and cooperation, which are essential for successful economic transactions and institutions.
