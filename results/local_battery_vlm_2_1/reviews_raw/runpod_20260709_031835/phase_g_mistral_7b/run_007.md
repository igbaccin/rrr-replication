The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of significant intellectual debate among economists. At stake is the understanding of how historical and contemporary institutional arrangements impact economic development across nations. This review synthesizes multiple scholarly streams that engage with this question, offering insights into various aspects of the causal relationship between institutions and growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies played a crucial role in establishing different institutions and property rights, which have a lasting impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that in regions where Europeans set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, poor institutions were introduced that negatively affect long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa, highlighting the contributions of African women to economic growth and development. They demonstrate how patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced the subordinate status of women in accessing land and resources, which impacted their ability to contribute to economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) discusses the implications of the new economic history of Africa for institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people. He suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, are important in understanding this phenomenon (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The emergence and effectiveness of political institutions that specify 'efficient' property rights are crucial for long-run economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which facilitated economic growth by providing increased security of property rights. This enhanced security, in turn, fostered the development of a long-term capital market (North 1989, p.9).

For instance, the European family system, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were all interconnected, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, potentially furthering economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and others argue that for economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments, as well as a wider supply of tested data (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This assertion underscores the crucial role institutions play in facilitating modern economic systems.

In Kuznets's work, he highlights the incompatibility between traditional institutions such as family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery with modern technology (Kuznets 1973, p.1). He further emphasizes that effective participation in the modern economic system requires rapid changes in various aspects of society, including labor force structure, urbanization, and social views (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offer evidence supporting this claim by examining how European colonization influenced the development of institutions in Africa. They find that regions with greater exposure to European colonizers developed more efficient institutions, which subsequently led to higher economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This research suggests that the institutional adjustments Kuznets discusses can be triggered by external factors, such as colonial influence, in addition to internal changes.

Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that the quality of institutions is a significant determinant of long-term economic growth. They argue that institutions shape the distribution of political power and, consequently, the incentives for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This research supports Kuznets's assertion that institutional adjustments are essential for economic growth, particularly in less developed countries.

The growth of useful knowledge is posited as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with intellectual factors playing a crucial role in driving technological change and industrialization (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr's work highlights the emergence of scientific societies per capita in Europe during the late 17th to early 18th centuries as an indicator of this intellectual growth (E0105). These societies focused on issues related to political economy, such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and more efficient, less distortionary taxes.

Moreover, Mokyr emphasizes the importance of the Enlightenment idea that individual actions aimed at self-interest often contribute positively to societal welfare, unless they involve redistribution or rent-seeking (E0106). This intellectual environment fostered the development and adoption of new technologies that facilitated industrialization, thus contributing significantly to long-term economic growth.

Mokyr's work further qualifies this downstream implication by noting that although industrial and even less revolutionary changes occurred worldwide during this period, they were primarily concentrated in Britain (E0106). The institutional basis supporting this pattern of growth in Britain, such as law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts, played a crucial role in facilitating industrialization and ensuring its sustainability.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing notion that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 1980s. Supporting this claim, they argue that many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, comparable to the largest economies in Asia (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding qualifies Mokyr's assertion that industrial and less revolutionary changes occurred worldwide during the period of the Industrial Revolution but were primarily concentrated in Britain (). The institutional basis supporting this pattern of growth in Britain, as noted by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), played a crucial role in facilitating industrialization and ensuring its sustainability. If these economies had already achieved substantial development before European colonization, it suggests that the institutional foundations underlying their growth were not solely dependent on colonial interventions.

In addition to challenging the conventional wisdom, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also highlight an important methodological issue in studying African economic history. Most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). By focusing on more recent periods, these studies may have overlooked the potential for economic growth that existed in Sub-Saharan Africa before European colonization.

Hornbeck's study reveals the substantial impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains. This groundbreaking fencing material facilitated property rights enforcement and reduced costs associated with land protection, thereby promoting agricultural growth. A significant finding from Hornbeck's research is that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire led to a noticeable increase in improved farmland between 1880 and 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

This enhancement of property rights protection, as Hornbeck (2010, p.8) contends, had downstream implications for agricultural development. The increased security provided by barbed wire enabled farmers to invest more confidently in long-term improvements such as irrigation systems and permanent structures, further enhancing productivity (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

In a related vein, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate how the establishment of credible property rights institutions can foster economic growth.

The impact of Africa's slave trades on current economic development is a significant factor shaping long-run growth, as suggested by Nunn (2008, p.2). This paper argues that the four slave trades that occurred in Africa from 1400 to 1900 had a lasting detrimental effect on subsequent economic development.

Nunn (2008, p.2) emphasizes that these trades were extensive, lasting nearly five times longer than official colonial rule. The author contends that the slave trades hindered the development of African institutions, which in turn influenced long-term economic growth. He further asserts that local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade before the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom (Nunn 2008, p.19). This kingdom, with its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with Europeans, thus demonstrating the potential for sustained trade under favorable institutional conditions.

This finding supports Nunn's claim that Africa's slave trades had a lasting impact on institutional development in Africa. The detrimental effect of these trades on institutions persisted for centuries and hindered the subsequent economic growth of the continent.

This stream of literature underscores the importance of institutional development in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in Africa. By examining the historical impact of Africa's slave trades on institutional development, this scholarship highlights the potential for negative consequences when institutions are not conducive to sustained trade and economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) posits that during the late medieval period, the quality of institutions in western Europe significantly influenced the skill premium, which in turn shaped long-run economic growth. The guilds, formal institutions established to regulate apprenticeship and skill acquisition, played a crucial role in this process.

According to van Zanden (2009, p.19), these institutions guaranteed the fair execution of apprentice contracts, addressing concerns about monopolization and local cartels that had been raised in past critiques. The benefits of intergenerational skill transfer by guilds may have been offset by their attempts to establish local monopolies, leading to a significant decrease in the skill premium compared to most other regions of the world economy during this period.

The perception of static custom in landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa, as argued by Peters (2004, p.5), can advantage certain groups by keeping officials ignorant of changing practices. This advantageous position allows these groups to engage in processes of exclusion and deepen social divisions, contributing to class formation.

Peters (2004, p.5) highlights how the final decade of colonial rule was marked by a push for agricultural development that was driven by dissatisfaction with African landholding practices, perceived as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment in agriculture. This perception, according to Peters, provided an advantage to groups seeking to keep officials unaware of their activities and changes in practice.

Critics such as Platteau (1996, 2000) and Amanor (2001) have pointed out that such unproblematic visions of land tenure in Africa ignore social inequality within many customary systems and evidence of encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood or commercial benefits. Furthermore, national and local elites often capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers (Ribot 2000; Carney and Farrington 1998; Woodhouse et al.).

In this context, the advantage gained by certain groups through the perception of static custom can lead to deepening social divisions and class formation. This observation is particularly relevant in the context of agricultural development schemes designed to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming, as criticized by Peters (2004, p.5). The benefit captured by these groups not only reinforces their position but also potentially hinders the broader economic growth that could be achieved through more equitable land tenure practices.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically reorganized to preserve social hierarchies and expand cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). This restructuring served multiple purposes: it reinforced colonial authority, shaped relations of production and power, and maintained control over both indigenous populations and European underlings.

One mechanism through which this was achieved involved the deliberate cultivation and reinforcement of racist ideologies, fear of the 'Other', and preoccupations with white prestige and protecting European women (Stoler 1989, p.6). These attitudes not only legitimized continued European rule and white supremacy but also functioned as a means of controlling potentially subversive European colonials.

For instance, numerous studies on British and French colonial life focused primarily on social rankings among the colonizers, rather than on internal political tensions within the colonial state (Cooper et al.) .

The expansion of cultural infrastructure in these colonies had downstream implications for economic growth. By fostering a sense of community and context among European colonizers, this restructuring helped to create an environment conducive to the development and implementation of policies that supported the interests of the ruling classes (Stoler 1989, p.6). These policies, in turn, influenced the economic trajectories of the colonies, shaping their long-term growth patterns.

As we delve further into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, it becomes clear that the restructuring of European class cultures within imperial control played a significant, albeit indirect, role in this process.

The work of Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2; 2012, p.28) sheds light on the long-run economic growth patterns in Africa by examining pre-colonial labor market conditions and real wages.

Frankema and van Waijenburg reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880-1965) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Their findings suggest that these real wages did not converge until after the Second World War, implying a significant role for pre-colonial labor market conditions in shaping long-run economic growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Moreover, the Kenyan case studied by Frankema and van Waijenburg reveals that the East-West gap in African economic development is not only due to different types of economic dynamics (commercialization) but also varying degrees of income inequality (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The recorded differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth call for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development.

For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions that promote income equality tend to foster long-run economic growth by reducing political instability and encouraging investment . Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the consensus among scholars that institutions significantly impact current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have lasting effects on contemporary economies. However, there is a disagreement regarding the nature of these institutions and their origins.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) challenge the dominant narrative by highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They demonstrate that European colonization reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, which relegated women to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This de-privileging of women in access to land was further exacerbated by the codification of customary law under colonial rule.

On the other hand, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He contends that African societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation.

The disagreement between these scholars can be resolved by examining the historical evidence of institutional changes resulting from European colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide evidence that these institutional changes account for a significant fraction of current income differences among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They argue that political power was important to elites in the colonies specializing in plantation agriculture because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages.

In contrast, Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2). This difference in focus on institutions versus education provides a nuanced understanding of how European colonization impacted economic growth in Africa.
