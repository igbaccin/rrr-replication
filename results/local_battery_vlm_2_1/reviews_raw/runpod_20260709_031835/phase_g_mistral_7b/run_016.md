The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate, as researchers grapple with understanding the factors that have led to varying levels of prosperity across nations. This literature review will delve into this contentious issue, focusing on the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, and examining the competing explanations and supporting evidence presented by various scholars.

At the heart of the debate lies the question: what specific institutional arrangements foster long-term economic growth? One prominent school of thought, represented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), argues that the institutions established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that European settlers adopted different strategies in their colonies, with some establishing strong rule-of-law systems and encouraging investment, while others set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) counter this view by emphasizing the significant economic contributions made by African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women's subordination in access to land under colonial rule hindered their ability to contribute effectively to agriculture, which was a major driver of economic development (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15).

Meanwhile, Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies aimed at institutional reforms and poverty reduction among the bottom billion people in Africa. He notes that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins, 2009, p.18).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offer another perspective, arguing that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors contribute to this effect, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011, p.4).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also explore the impact of European colonialism on economic growth, finding that institutional changes resulting from this period account for a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies. They argue that political power was crucial to the elites in plantation-focused colonies because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

Lastly, Frankema (2012, p.2) sheds light on the role of British rule in facilitating formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. He contends that this was primarily due to the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema, 2012, p.2).

European colonialism's impact on institutions and economic growth is a significant focus in the literature. In one extreme, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.

The results from Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) regressions support their argument, showing that the institutions established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels. Moreover, they find similar results using various alternative measures of institutions, such as constraints on the executive, an index of law and order tradition, a measure of property rights, a measure of rule of law, and the efficiency of the judiciary (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor.

Nunn (2008, p.2) provides an additional perspective, arguing that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa. He suggests that sustained trade did not occur until African societies with centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed markets and trading networks were able to support trade with the Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.2).

For economic growth, a country requires advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets argues that modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions and social views, necessitating rapid changes in society to accommodate it (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further emphasizes the importance of intellectual growth as a catalyst for economic development, with scientific societies proliferating during the Enlightenment period (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

In Kuznets's view, modern technology requires advanced institutions and social views to function effectively. He highlights steam and electric power, large-scale plants, and urbanization as examples of technological advancements that necessitate changes in societal structure and ideology (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

The implications of these findings suggest that for economic growth, countries must adapt their institutions and ideologies to accommodate technological advancements.

In the ongoing exploration of institutions' role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) offer compelling insights into the impact of precolonial labor market conditions on this development trajectory. The authors challenge the persistent assumption of historical African poverty by reconstructing and comparing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies from 1880 to 1965, revealing significant growth over time (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Frankema and van Waijenburg's findings suggest that precolonial labor markets in Africa were not characterized by persistent poverty but rather displayed a trend of increasing real wages. By revealing the precolonial labor market's role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, Frankema and van Waijenburg's work complicates the notion that the lack of investment in education and institutions was the primary factor hindering economic development on the continent. Instead, their findings emphasize the importance of considering labor market conditions as a crucial element of institutional arrangements that impacted economic growth.

The emergence of 'bringing the state back in' as a policy approach signifies a shift towards recognizing the importance of effective governance and institutions in economic development, particularly in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This shift is evident in the work of Hopkins and Broadberry & Gardner.

He contends that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly impact the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Instead, he suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

They argue that many Sub-Saharan African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This finding implies that effective governance and institutions indigenous to Africa have played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

While European colonialism may not have been the sole driver of economic growth in Africa, its presence did introduce extractive types of economic control poorly suited for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This suggests that while indigenous institutions have contributed to economic growth, external factors have also had a profound impact.

Greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.2) research substantiates this claim by demonstrating how the unequal distribution of wealth in these societies resulted in institutions that solidified elite power, thus limiting opportunities for economic advancement among the majority population.

For instance, they cite examples from Latin America where landholding patterns and labor systems were structured to maintain elite control over resources and production (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). These institutions limited access to economic opportunities for the majority population, thereby perpetuating inequality over time.

Furthermore, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggest that these elitist institutions had downstream implications on economic growth. This finding is supported by the work of Mokyr (2005, p.54), who argues that technological progress plays a significant role in shaping long-term economic development .

Post-independence budget policies in Africa, particularly subsidies and interest, were not as unproductive or patronage-driven as commonly assumed (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson's research on Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda reveals that these countries experienced high debt burdens due to post-colonial budget policies, which may have hindered long-run economic growth.

Simson (2019, p.11) argues that the allocation of wage resources in the independence period constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage. In response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent, military spending increased towards the end of the 1960s and 1970s (Simson 2019, p.11). Beyond the capital cities, the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly, while jobs increasingly favoured comparatively skilled and female professions (Simson 2019, p.11).

The high-level allocation of wage resources, however, did not necessarily translate into productive investments. As interest payments peaked at 30% of spending in Kenya and 18% of expenditure in Tanzania, capital and recurrent spending were displaced (Simson 2019, p.11). Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to less access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief (Simson 2019, p.11).

These high-debt burdens, in turn, had downstream implications on economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological progress plays a significant role in shaping long-term economic development. With resources diverted towards debt servicing and military spending, investments in technology and innovation may have been neglected, thereby hindering economic growth over the long run.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly contributed to agricultural development by reducing land protection costs and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This shift marked a crucial transformation in the region's agrarian landscape, as documented by Hornbeck (2010, p.8).

The introduction of barbed wire was accompanied by the decline of herd laws, which had previously regulated livestock grazing on open ranges. Prior to the widespread use of barbed wire, counties with herd laws experienced minimal improvements in farmland from 1870 to 1880 (Hornbeck 2010 (Hornbeck 2010, p.8)). After the adoption of barbed wire, these same counties saw a substantial increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

This shift towards more secure property rights allowed for increased investment in agricultural improvement, as farmers could now protect their lands more effectively. This pattern is consistent with the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, which highlights the importance of secure property rights for long-term development (Mokyr 2005; Hopkins 2009).

Moreover, the improved enforcement of property rights also had downstream implications for technological progress. With more secure land tenure, farmers were better positioned to invest in machinery and other innovations that could boost agricultural productivity (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

In this context, the adoption of barbed wire can be seen as a catalyst for agricultural development in the American Plains. By reducing the costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement, it enabled farmers to invest more effectively in their lands, leading to increased agricultural improvement and technological progress over time.

The improved agricultural productivity resulting from this shift also had broader implications for economic growth and development in the region. As agriculture became more productive, there was a concomitant increase in rural income levels, which could then be reinvested in other sectors of the economy (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This dynamic is reminiscent of the role that agricultural development played in Europe's early industrialization process (Goldin 1996).

Women's involvement in employment, education, and family roles underwent significant changes in the United States during the past century, driven primarily by increased demand for female office workers (Goldin 2006, p.7). This shift was facilitated by a confluence of factors, as Goldin (2006, p.7) notes.

The early twentieth century saw an increased relative demand for female office workers, making "nice" jobs available to women (Goldin 2006, p.3). This demand was fueled by the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3).

This dynamic is reminiscent of the role that increased demand for labor played in driving women's employment in Europe during industrialization (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The increased involvement of women in employment led to broader implications for their education levels. As women entered the workforce, they demanded better educational opportunities to improve their job prospects and career advancement (Goldin 2006, p.7). This dynamic is supported by evidence from Kuznets (1973, p.1), who found a strong positive relationship between economic development and educational attainment for both men and women.

The increased demand for female office workers also had implications for family roles. This dynamic is a shared finding with Humphries (1983), who found that the increasing employment of married women in Britain during the nineteenth century led to changes in family structure and dynamics.

The Maddison Project, spearheaded by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), aims to extend and refine Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia, which aligns with the topic's claim that institutions are crucial for long-run economic growth (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that estimating national accounts in the past, especially in distant periods, is subject to certain margins of error due to partial data and assumptions about the links between these data and economic activities they represent. However, as Maddison anticipated, disagreements among scholars have led to new work aimed at quantifying long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

This work underscores the importance of continued collaboration among scholars to improve our understanding of long-term economic growth, particularly in understudied regions such as Asia.

This collaborative spirit is evident in projects like the one led by Acemoglu et al. which focuses on understanding European colonialism's impact on institutions and economic growth in Africa and other parts of the world (Acemoglu et al. 2001; Acemoglu et al. 2002).

In this context, the Maddison Project's focus on Asia offers a valuable opportunity to address a significant gap in our understanding of long-term economic growth. As Bolt and van Zanden note, data for medieval England may be better than, for example, nineteenth-century Sub-Saharan Africa or pre-Colombian Latin America (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). By extending and refining Maddison's estimates for these regions, scholars can gain a more nuanced understanding of the role institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth across different historical contexts.

The European class cultures in colonies were strategically restructured to preserve social hierarchies and bolster imperial control (Stoler 1989, p.6; Peters 2004, p.5). Stoler's work underscores how the colonial authorities manipulated racial ideologies, fear of the Other, and anxieties over white prestige to maintain order among both Europeans and colonized subjects. For instance, European rulers used racist ideology as a tool to control dissent within their ranks by emphasizing the perceived threat from Asian and black males (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Simultaneously, Peters' research reveals that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa masked processes of exclusion and deepening social divisions, necessitating a focus on who benefited and lost from such instances of 'negotiability' (Peters 2004, p.5). This selective restructuring of European class cultures not only maintained social distinctions among the colonizers but also contributed to the exploitation and marginalization of the colonized.

Furthermore, this manipulation extended beyond the realm of racial ideologies and landholding practices. Peters' work also highlights how agricultural policies during colonial rule were designed to raise productivity through modernized farming, which often disproportionately benefited European agricultural officers and national and local elites at the expense of African farmers (Peters 2004, p.9).

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor played a pivotal role in shaping the British industrial revolution, according to Humphries (2013, p.16). This shift away from reliance on skilled adult male labor was a significant consequence of the period's economic, social, and political conditions.

This finding is supported by other historians' depictions of the period as marked by economic, social, and political turmoil (Sharpe, Snell, Kent, Bailey). The paper suggests that these conditions strained men's links to wives and children, making male-breadwinner households less reliable sources of support.

Moreover, Humphries (2013, p.16) emphasizes the presence of a significant group of families whose fathers were yet-living but absent, in addition to those whose fathers had died. This turbulence testifies to the challenges faced by men during empire building, war, and labor mobility.

The implications of this shift are further explored by Vincent (1990), who provides accounts of poverty in working-class memoirs. These sources suggest that poor law historians' views of poverty were influenced by the availability of cheap female and child labor during the industrial revolution.

In the realm of late medieval economic development, van Zanden's (2009, p.19) work provides compelling evidence that the quality of institutions played a pivotal role in shaping the skill premium, a factor instrumental in long-run economic growth. The author contends that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, in western Europe during this period significantly influenced the skill premium by ensuring fair apprentice contracts and fostering intergenerational skill transfer (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

These institutions were not without criticism, as they potentially formed local cartels and maintained artificially high wages. Nonetheless, van Zanden argues that the benefits of guilds on skill transfers could outweigh their attempts at monopolization (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

The significance of this finding is further underscored when considering that the skill premium in western Europe during the late medieval period was markedly lower than in most other parts of the global economy (van Zanden 2009, p.1). This pattern, according to van Zanden, can be attributed to factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors.

This implication is supported by the broader literature on economic growth, which emphasizes the importance of human capital and institutional quality for sustained development (Kuznets_1973, Mokyr_2005).

Thus, van Zanden's work adds to our understanding of the mechanisms driving long-run economic growth by highlighting the role of institutions in shaping the skill premium during the late medieval period. This finding, however, operates as a rival cause to the main topic, as it suggests an alternate explanation for long-term economic performance that does not involve the specific variables under investigation in this review.

Nevertheless, the evidence presented by van Zanden underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes and provides a foundation for further research into the role of institutional quality in determining skill premiums across different historical periods and geographical contexts.

In contrast to efficiency-based explanations of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and North (1989, p.9), along with North and Weingast (1989, p.30), propose a compelling account centered on distributional conflicts. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework has been recognized by studies like Laslett's work on the European family system, which suggests that this interlinked institutional system contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Similarly, according to Solar, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system; this distinctive framework may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Moreover, cultural theories have been applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efficient' rules of guilds. This shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and impact the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

In this light, it becomes evident that the complex interplay between economic institutions, cultural factors, and collective action shapes pre-industrial Europe's long-run economic growth.
