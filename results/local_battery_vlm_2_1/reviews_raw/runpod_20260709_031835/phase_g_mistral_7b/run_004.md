The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centered on the impact of European colonization and its aftermath on contemporary income levels across countries. At stake is understanding the historical trajectories that have led to the economic disparities observed today, particularly in Africa.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels. They posit that differences in colonization strategies resulted in varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies fostering investment and rule of law while others created extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further demonstrate that these institutional changes account for a significant fraction of current income differences among former European colonies.

On the other hand, Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that ethno-linguistic fragmentation and population density played crucial roles in shaping Africa's economic history. He suggests that societies with higher populations could resist European encroachment, leading to the development of unproductive institutions or colonial rule that introduced extractive types of economic control ill-suited for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Meanwhile, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods.

The work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offers an additional perspective, arguing that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today.

Frankema (2012, p.2) adds to this discussion by focusing on the role of British rule in facilitating formal education in sub-Saharan Africa.

These scholars posit that Europeans adopted varying colonization strategies, leading to different associated institutions with contrasting effects on economic development.

Conversely, in colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, stifling economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

Relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor, underscoring the profound impact of these institutional shifts on economic growth over time.

These findings highlight the significance of institutions established during European colonization in shaping long-run economic growth across non-European societies.

This stream of literature suggests that the institutional legacy of European colonization continues to influence current income levels and economic growth patterns in non-European societies, with profound implications for understanding the long-run determinants of economic development in these regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women made substantial contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. One mechanism through which this occurred was the relegation of women to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15). Polly Hill's documentation of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast highlights how their migration in search of farm land for cocoa propelled the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911 (Lawrance, Osborn, & Roberts, 2006).

Under colonial rule, customary law codification reinforced women's subordinate status in terms of land access (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15). Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts. This dual legal system allowed for the invention of customary law that further entrenched women's subordinate status (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.20).

This stream of literature suggests that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth must account for how colonial rule codified customary law and its impact on gender dynamics and economic outcomes in Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms, which can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Simson (2019, p.11) challenges common assumptions about post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, arguing that these countries' budgets were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending.

In the context of Africa, institutional reforms have played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. This finding challenges the notion that ethnic heterogeneity necessarily leads to poor institutions and underdevelopment.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman suggest that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework played a crucial role in early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, the European family system was intertwined with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions. This complex institutional system may have contributed to economic growth in pre-industrial Europe.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. In societies with extreme inequality, elites were better able to maintain their status, but at the expense of economic opportunities for the majority (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). This path of institutional development may have affected growth, as it limited access to economic opportunities for many.

Secure rights, particularly political liberties, were crucial for economic growth in seventeenth-century England and other economies (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Kuznets 1973, p.1). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, suggesting that even if other forces would have eventually led to growth under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. The implication is that institutions, specifically those providing secure rights, were necessary for economic growth and political freedom.

He emphasizes the need for changes compatible with modern technology, which was not compatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, all of which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world.

The paper by North and Weingast focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by rules, emphasizing the importance of secure rights in ensuring effective enforcement and change.

In seventeenth-century England, this commitment was demonstrated through the Glorious Revolution, which established a constitutional monarchy and the rule of law.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) further qualifies this downstream implication by explaining that effective participation in the modern economic system necessitates rapid changes in various aspects of society. This includes changes in family formation, urbanization, and man's views on his role and the measure of his achievement in society, all of which were facilitated by the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) study on the American Plains offers compelling insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The near-universal adoption of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement, two fundamental causes of long-term economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.8).

Evidence reveals that the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped detailing herd laws after 1880 and entirely ceased reporting their status after 1884, suggesting a shift in focus towards barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This change coincided with an increase in the improved fraction of farmland by 20 percentage points from 1880 to 1890, and a similar increase in total improved land (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). These findings underscore the significance of barbed wire as a tool for enforcing property rights and promoting agricultural development.

It is important to note that not all counties had equal access to barbed wire. Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) research shows that herd-law counties, which were less wooded, experienced an insignificant decline in the improved fraction of farmland from 1870 to 1880 but a substantial increase after the introduction of barbed wire. This qualification highlights the uneven distribution of benefits and potential challenges in implementing such institutional changes.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that European class cultures within imperial control were strategically restructured to preserve social hierarchies, fostering the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies. Stoler's work underscores that this process was not merely a matter of justifying continued colonial rule and white supremacy but also an intricate class-based logic aimed at maintaining order among European settlers and subordinates (Stoler 1989, p.4).

One mechanism through which this occurred was the selective reconfiguration of social norms around race, gender, and prestige.

This restructuring had downstream implications, particularly in terms of cultural infrastructure development. This focus on class-based infrastructure expansion, in turn, contributed to the long-run economic growth of colonies by facilitating the efficient extraction and management of resources.

However, it is essential to acknowledge that this stream of literature primarily focuses on European imperialism in Asia and Africa, providing a partial perspective on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge emerges as a pivotal driver of long-term economic growth, particularly in Western Europe during the late medieval period. This claim is supported by both Mokyr (2005, p.54) and van Zanden (2009, p.19). According to Mokyr, intellectual advancements were the mainspring of economic growth, with societies that fostered innovation and knowledge creation experiencing greater prosperity. He highlights how the Enlightenment ideas promoting individual self-interest for societal welfare contributed significantly to economic development during this period.

On the other hand, van Zanden argues that the institutional quality in Western Europe played a crucial role in facilitating the growth of useful knowledge. These institutions were instrumental in shaping the skill premium in Western Europe during the late medieval period, which was lower compared to most other parts of the world economy (van Zanden 2009, p.1).

In this context, it is worth noting that the guilds, despite being criticized for monopolizing skills and forming local cartels, had beneficial effects on the transfer of skills over time. This suggests a complex interplay between institutional development, knowledge creation, and economic growth during the late medieval period in Western Europe.

By focusing on the growth of useful knowledge as a driver of long-term economic growth, this stream of literature sheds light on the mechanisms that underpinned economic development during the late medieval period in Western Europe. It also highlights the importance of institutional quality and the role of formal institutions like guilds in facilitating the transfer of skills and promoting economic growth. Humphries's research reveals that a substantial proportion of boys grew up separated from their fathers due to marital instability, desertion, and male abandonment during the eighteenth century. This finding suggests that the high wage economy was not as reliable as previously thought, given the fragility of men's ability and commitment to provide support (Humphries 2013, p.16).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of considerable interest among scholars. A common thread that emerges from the literature is the significant impact of European colonization on current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). There is significant disagreement regarding the specific factors driving this influence.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a substantial impact on economic growth. They assert that in colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions enforcing the rule of law, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was fostered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast where Europeans established states for rapid resource extraction and transfer to the metropole, economic growth was hindered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming in the context of cash crops, with their subordinate status reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective sheds light on the importance of considering gender dynamics when examining long-term economic growth.

The disagreement between these two perspectives can be reconciled through a focus on specific historical evidence. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that societies could be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the impact of European colonization on economic growth may not solely depend on the ethnic makeup of a colony but also on the specific institutions established.

Moreover, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact could have hindered the development of productive institutions in colonies where the slave trade was prevalent. Therefore, understanding the extent and impact of the slave trade within different colonial contexts may provide further insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
