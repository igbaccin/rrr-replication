The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, as they strive to elucidate the factors that underpin the disparate trajectories of development across nations. At stake is an explanation for the observed income level and economic performance disparities, offering insights into potential avenues for policy initiatives aimed at fostering growth and alleviating poverty. A central dispute revolves around the impact of institutions established during European colonization, with some scholars asserting that these institutions have exerted a substantial influence on current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), while others contend that alternative factors, such as population dynamics or cultural legacies, hold more sway (Hopkins 2009, p.10; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

In the first stream, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) present a robust argument for the causal role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. They argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with varying associated institutions, which have had enduring effects on current income levels across countries. In contrast, in regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, resulting in lower income levels today.

The paper by Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in former European colonies. They contend that Europeans adopted varying colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In contrast, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole.

The results of Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) regressions using various measures of institutions support their view that these institutional differences account for current income disparities among former European colonies. Nunn further investigates the impact of Africa's slave trades on subsequent economic development, arguing that they had a negative effect on current economic development in Africa. He suggests that sustained trade did not occur until African societies with centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed markets and trading networks, such as the Kongo kingdom, emerged (Nunn 2008, p.2).

Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.48) subsequent work builds on these findings by providing evidence that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. They demonstrate that relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor, and this shift can be attributed to the establishment of extractive institutions during the colonial period (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Institutions in pre-industrial societies were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution, challenging efficiency-based explanations (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie argues that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework was crucial for early industrialization and capitalism. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

They suggest that where there was extreme inequality, and institutions favored elites and limited the access of much of the population to economic opportunities, members of elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12).

These findings imply that understanding the development and persistence of economic institutions requires a holistic approach that considers their interdependencies. Furthermore, they suggest that inequality played a significant role in shaping these institutions, with societies experiencing greater inequality tending to develop institutions that further entrenched this disparity. This insight complements the previous section's claim that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism accounted for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The paper by Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue for a more nuanced understanding of the role of indigenous agency in shaping economic institutions, suggesting that this factor may have been overlooked in previous models of economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths, necessitating qualification and methodological modification to account for this omission.

Hopkins (2009, p.18), on the other hand, examines the new economic history of Africa and its implications for policies affecting institutional reforms, particularly focusing on ethno-linguistic fragmentation's impact on public goods provision by African governments.

This insight is significant because it highlights the need to consider indigenous agency as a determinant of institutional choice, a factor that may have been overlooked in previous models.

Moreover, Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the Atlantic slave trade reduced Africa's population, which in turn influenced the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth. This finding complicates the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by demonstrating how historical events can have lasting impacts on institutional development and economic growth.

The emergence of political institutions that specify 'efficient' property rights and provide effective enforcement is a key factor in long-run economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North (1989, p.9) provides evidence of this through the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting. This decline is described as the English financial revolution, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a critical factor for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself.

North (1989, p.9) further argues that since we derive utility from the various attributes of goods and services rather than from the entities themselves, it is the costliness of measuring the separable dimensions that is critical in this study. This relationship between the benefits derived from rule specification and the costs of measurement has been significant in the history of property rights and is at the heart of many issues related to the structure and effectiveness of enforcement.

They focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, not just the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules.

If other forces would have ultimately led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. Thus, institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

In terms of downstream implications, Jeffrey Williamson's recent work provides further support for this thesis (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). However, it is essential to note that while secure property rights are crucial for long-term economic growth, they do not guarantee it. Other factors such as population, geography, and technological change also play significant roles in shaping economic development.

The insights from North's work and its subsequent qualifications highlight the importance of institutions in promoting long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. This finding adds to the growing body of literature that emphasizes the role of institutions in economic development, complicating the traditional focus on population and savings as primary determinants of economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as intellectual factors have been instrumental in driving technological change and industrialization (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr's work underscores the significance of intellectual advancements, arguing that societies with a higher concentration of scientific societies per capita experienced more rapid economic development during the period from 1600 to 1800 (Mokyr 2005, p. 54). This emphasis on intellectual factors complicates the traditional focus on population and savings as primary determinants of economic growth.

Moreover, Mokyr's findings are supported by evidence that the Industrial Revolution, while primarily British, was fostered by an institutional basis that promoted efficiency, law and order, and enforceable contracts (Mokyr 2005, p. 3). This institutional support facilitated technological change and growth, even in regions such as the Dutch Republic and Venice, which had early institutional foundations for economic development (Mokyr 2005, p. 3).

In this context, it is worth noting that while intellectual factors contribute to economic growth, they do not guarantee it. Other factors such as population, geography, and technological change also play significant roles in shaping economic development. This broader perspective highlights the multifaceted nature of the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, emphasizing the interplay between various factors in promoting sustainable and efficient economic development over time.

The British industrial revolution was characterized by a reliance on cheap and amenable female and child labor, challenging the traditional focus on men and high wages (Humphries 2013, p.16). Humphries's paper argues that this overlooked factor played a significant role in the revolution, as the availability of such labor allowed for increased productivity and economic growth.

The large numbers of women and children among pauper populations were, in part, the result of family breakdown and male abandonment during this period (Humphries 2013, p.16). This finding is consistent with other historians' depictions of the eighteenth century as a period of marital instability, which was associated with economic, social, and political conditions (Humphries 2013, p.16). The presence of a significant group of families whose fathers were absent, either due to death or abandonment, testifies to the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16).

The availability of cheap labor enabled industrialists to lower production costs and increase profits, thereby contributing to the growth of the economy. However, it also led to exploitative working conditions for women and children, as they were often paid less than men and subjected to long hours and hazardous work environments (Humphries 2013, p.16).

The use of female and child labor during the British industrial revolution was not unique, but its extent and impact were significant. In other regions, such as colonial America, the availability of slave labor played a similar role in driving economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, Acemoglu et al. 2002). However, the use of slave labor was characterized by even more extreme exploitation and violence than the use of female and child labor during the British industrial revolution.

The availability of cheap and amenable labor was not the only factor driving economic growth during the British industrial revolution. Other factors, such as technological innovation and access to capital, also played important roles (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, the use of female and child labor was a key factor that enabled these other drivers of growth to be effectively harnessed.

The political stability offered by colonial rule facilitated large-scale infrastructure projects that indirectly contributed to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, thereby influencing long-run economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This stability, enabled by the British colonial administration, allowed for the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries to promote formal education in the region before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2). The construction of schools, hospitals, and railroads by these agents of colonialism provided a foundation for educational development that persisted beyond their direct involvement.

Moreover, the establishment of formal education systems laid the groundwork for increased access to knowledge, which is crucial for economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.17). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) demonstrate that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, suggesting that the region was not persistently mired in poverty as previously assumed. This growth in income levels could be attributed to the improved human capital resulting from increased access to education, which facilitated economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

However, it is important to note that the benefits of formal education were not evenly distributed across all populations (Acemoglu et al.). The colonizers prioritized educating European settlers and Asian migrants over native Africans, creating a persistent income inequality between these groups. This disparity in educational opportunities had long-term consequences for economic growth, as it limited the potential for human capital development among native African populations (Acemoglu et al.).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that for less developed countries to achieve long-run economic growth, they need not only technological advancements but also institutional and ideological adjustments. This claim is supported by Kuznets' (1973) assertion that modern economic growth requires institutional changes, as well as advancing technology and a wider supply of tested data.

They contend that many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s. This growth, however, did not continue at the same pace during the second half of the twentieth century, a period when other developing economies were converging with Europe and North America (Broadberry&Gardner_2022).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores the necessity of institutional changes for modern economic growth. He explains that the industrial revolution required new institutions and social views to accommodate large-scale plants, urbanization, and labor force participation. These adjustments were not compatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world (Kuznets_1973).

They note that the field of African economic history has experienced a 'renaissance' based on newly digitized sources of data and innovative techniques, but most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960 (Broadberry&Gardner_2022).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes the importance of reliable data for economic growth. He argues that effective participation in the modern economic system by the labor force necessitates rapid changes in its location and structure, in the relations among occupational status groups, and even in the relations between labor force and total population. Consequently, he contends that high aggregate growth rates are associated with rapid changes in economic structure, urbanization, family formation, and man's views on his role and the measure of his achievement in society (Kuznets_1973).

The European class cultures in imperial control shaped the expansion of cultural infrastructure, but did not directly contribute to long-run economic growth (Stoler (1989, p.6), (Stoler 1989, p.4)). Stoler's analysis reveals that these cultures were restructured to maintain social distinctions and authority within colonial societies, with a particular focus on racial ideologies and the protection of European prestige. This class-based logic, as Stoler argues, was not only aimed at controlling indigenous populations but also at regulating potentially subversive white colonials (Stoler 1989, p.6).

In this context, the expansion of cultural infrastructure can be seen as a byproduct of these European class cultures. For instance, the creation and maintenance of the sense of colonial community served to legitimize and sustain colonial authority, thereby contributing to the development of cultural institutions (Stoler 1989, p.6). However, it is essential to note that this expansion did not translate into direct economic growth, as the primary aim was maintaining social control rather than promoting economic development.

Furthermore, Stoler's work underscores the importance of examining the internal political tensions among Europeans within colonial societies, which have been largely overlooked in previous studies (Stoler 1989, p.4). This emphasis on the complexities of European class dynamics within imperial control provides a nuanced understanding of how these cultures shaped the expansion of cultural infrastructure.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains serves as a compelling example of how property rights institutions can shape agricultural development. As Hornbeck (2010, p.8) demonstrates, this innovation significantly increased agricultural productivity by reducing land protection costs and facilitating property rights enforcement.

Hornbeck's study reveals that the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports, which detailed herd laws and their status, ceased providing such information after 1884, suggesting a shift in focus towards barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). By comparing herd-law counties with those without herd laws, Hornbeck found that the former experienced an insignificant change in agricultural development from 1870 to 1880. However, after the introduction of barbed wire, these counties saw a substantial increase in improved farmland between 1880 and 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

The impact of barbed wire on property rights institutions is further illustrated by its role in reducing conflicts over open-range grazing. This, in turn, allowed for more efficient use of resources, fostering agricultural growth.

In a broader context, the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains exemplifies how the development of institutions can drive economic growth by facilitating resource allocation and reducing transaction costs. This insight aligns with the findings of other scholars who have emphasized the role of property rights institutions in fostering long-run economic growth (e.g., North 1989; North & Weingast 1989).

Simson's (2019) research challenges the common assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Contrary to this view, Simson argues that these countries' budgets exhibited a more nuanced allocation of resources.

In the early 1990s, Kenya's subsidies and interest peaked at 30% of spending, displacing both capital and recurrent expenditure (Simson 2019, p.11). Tanzania also experienced a high debt burden with interest payments reaching 18% of expenditure, while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief compared to its neighbors (Simson 2019, p.11). Another important post-colonial expenditure shift was the growth in military spending, which increased in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent under colonial rule (Simson 2019, p.11).

Beyond the capital cities, the geographic distribution of employment did not change significantly, while jobs increasingly favored comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward (Simson 2019, p.19). These trends suggest that high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage.

In a related vein, changes occurred not only in the composition of employment but also the private returns to public employment. Over this period, the high-level allocation of wage resources on the whole constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.19). This finding underscores Simson's argument that post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that ongoing collaboration among scholars is crucial for extending and refining Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This endeavor aims to produce and update global economic data with greater precision.

The Maddison Project, as championed by Bolt and van Zanden, recognizes the inherent uncertainties in past economic data due to limited resources and assumptions about the relationships between data points and the activities they represent (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The further back in time one goes, the larger these margins of error become. However, the project members have experimented with various methods for estimating these errors, with the Hitotshibashi team's study on Asian national accounts offering the most detailed analysis (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

Maddison himself embraced this challenge, encouraging scholars to work together and refute his estimates when necessary. This approach has led to a wealth of research on long-term economic growth, as many scholars have responded to Maddison's pioneering work (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). By extending and revising Maddison's data, the Maddison Project aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of global economic development over time.

This collaborative effort is particularly significant for Asia, where historical data has been scant and often inadequate.

The Maddison Project's emphasis on collaboration and refinement underscores the importance of ongoing research in shaping our understanding of long-run economic growth. By working together to improve data quality, scholars can address uncertainties and biases that have plagued historical economic analysis, ultimately leading to a more accurate and comprehensive account of global development over time.
