The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of significant scholarly debate, as it sheds light on the factors that have contributed to the divergent paths of development across nations. At stake is an understanding of how historical events and societal structures have influenced contemporary income levels and economic performance.

A prominent disagreement revolves around the impact of European colonization on institutions and their subsequent effects on economic growth. On one hand, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with varying associated institutions that have had a lasting influence on current income levels across countries. Specifically, they contend that in regions where Europeans settled and established rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was fostered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). On the contrary, in colonies with extractive states, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, European presence led to the establishment of institutions that were detrimental to long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Moreover, the codification of customary law during this period served to solidify the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

These perspectives on institutions and their role in shaping economic growth offer valuable insights into historical events and their contemporary implications. It is crucial to take into account other factors that may have impacted institutional development and economic outcomes, such as population dynamics and the effects of European traders and missionaries. In the following sections, we will explore how these factors have contributed to the evolution of institutions in sub-Saharan Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa had lasting impacts on institutional development and poverty reduction policies in the region. Their study reveals a culture of mistrust persisting today, which they attribute to the slave trade. This mistrust, according to their findings, stems from both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) provides evidence supporting this claim by demonstrating that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, while ethnically homogeneous societies can be dysfunctional (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This finding suggests that the slave trade may have contributed to the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth by fostering a culture of mistrust.

Moreover, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the Atlantic slave trade reduced population levels and encouraged the development of institutions that were poorly suited for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

The paper by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) collectively suggest that conflicts over resource distribution played a significant role in shaping the economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging efficiency-based explanations.

He contends that the European family system, labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interlinked institutional system, according to Ogilvie, may have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further support this claim by arguing that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. In societies with extreme inequality, institutions were designed to maintain elite status, thereby limiting economic opportunities for the majority (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). This path of institutional development may have affected growth, as members of elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time.

It is important to acknowledge that while these conflicts over resource distribution impacted institutions, they did not always result in inefficient outcomes. He cites Richardson's work, which contends that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efficient' rules of guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

In this process, African women's contributions to economic growth and development were often marginalized.

The dual legal system introduced by colonial rule, European courts based on common and civil law and native courts, provided a key avenue for this codification (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This legal framework, shaped by patriarchal alliances, further marginalized African women's economic contributions.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) collectively argue that for economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments are essential. These factors create the necessary conditions for modern economic participation and growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that modern technology is not compatible with traditional institutions such as family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world. He posits that rapid changes in economic structure, occupational status groups, and societal views are required for effective participation in the modern economic system by the labor force (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Meanwhile, Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He suggests that the Enlightenment ideas, such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and less distortionary taxes, played a significant role in promoting economic growth during the Industrial Revolution (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

He further emphasizes that institutional support, such as law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts, were crucial in facilitating efficient allocation of resources during the Industrial Revolution (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

These factors provide the necessary conditions for modern economic participation and growth, while ideological shifts such as the Enlightenment ideas contribute to creating an environment conducive to economic development.

British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa played a significant role in shaping the development of infrastructure that, in turn, influenced the formation of institutions contributing to long-run economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British rule facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. This educational expansion was instrumental in fostering an environment conducive to institutional development. Supporting this claim, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that many Sub-Saharan African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s.

The presence of British traders and missionaries promoted formal education, which in turn facilitated institutional development. Frankema (2012, p.2) notes that malaria ecology does not accurately reflect historical incidence, as it is arguably endogenous to educational development. To account for this, the paper includes a dummy variable for pre-colonial influence of Islam and an index measuring native African resistance against colonial rule. These factors suggest that areas with stronger pre-colonial state centralization or more resistance to British rule may have had less access to formal education and thus slower institutional development.

Despite initial opposition, the influence of British traders and missionaries eventually led to extensive educational expansion. This educational expansion facilitated institutional development, contributing to long-run economic growth.

While the authors do not explicitly discuss the role of education in this growth, their findings suggest that the institutional development facilitated by educational expansion under British rule may have been a significant factor.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly boosted agricultural development by facilitating property rights enforcement and reducing costs of land protection (Hornbeck (2010, p.8), Hornbeck (2010, p.32)). This innovation, introduced during the late 19th century, transformed the region's agricultural landscape, enabling farmers to secure their lands more effectively.

Barbed wire's impact on property rights was profound. Prior to its introduction, herd laws were widely used to manage grazing rights and prevent overgrazing (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). However, these laws proved ineffective due to the difficulty of enforcing them across vast, open spaces. With barbed wire, farmers could easily demarcate their lands, making it possible to enforce property rights more efficiently. This development reduced conflicts over land use and fostered a more stable agricultural environment.

The enhanced ability to secure land led to a surge in agricultural improvements. This shift suggests that barbed wire played a crucial role in promoting agricultural development by reducing the costs associated with land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) join The Maddison Project in advocating for the continued collaboration of scholars to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population. This strategy is aimed at improving our understanding of historical economic growth, even when a solid basis for these numbers does not exist (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.3).

The Maddison Project's approach is grounded in the recognition that estimates of national accounts in the past, especially in distant history, are subject to significant margins of error due to partial data and assumptions about economic activities (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.3). These margins of error can serve as a basis for objective estimates, although they are based on somewhat subjective assessments of the potential errors in the underlying data (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.3).

Maddison himself acknowledged this and adopted a strategy of producing numbers even when a solid basis did not always exist, hoping that scholars would disagree and work to show his estimates were incorrect (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.2). This strategy has been highly successful, inducing many scholars to work on long-term economic development quantification and providing a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.2).

This collaboration aims to improve our understanding of long-term economic growth by filling data gaps and refining assumptions about economic activities, even when a solid basis for these numbers does not exist.

This shared emphasis on extending and revising Maddison's estimates highlights the crucial role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. By improving our understanding of historical economic development, these collaborative efforts contribute to the refinement of institutional analyses that shape economic growth, as they provide more accurate data for researchers to analyze and draw conclusions from.

The composition of the workforce during the British Industrial Revolution was significantly influenced by inventions that reduced the need for skilled labor, leading to an increased use of unskilled female and child labor [(Humphries_2013)(Humphries 2013, p.16); (Goldin_2006)(Goldin 2006, p.7)]. Humphries argues that a more accurate understanding of the British industrial revolution is achieved by considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor, rather than focusing on men and high wages. He presents evidence of family instability during this period, with fathers often absent or deceased [(Humphries_2013)(Humphries 2013, p.16)]. Goldin, on the other hand, focuses on the United States, demonstrating a quiet revolution in women's employment, education, and family roles over the past century. She attributes this shift to various exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth of educational institutions [(Goldin_2006)(Goldin 2006, p.3)].

One specific mechanism that emerges from these sources is the impact of technological advancements on labor composition.

Another downstream implication of this shift in labor composition is the emergence of a new set of social and economic challenges. Goldin's work also highlights the social and economic implications of this shift, particularly in terms of women's roles within families and society [(Goldin_2006)(Goldin 2006, p.7)].

European class cultures in colonies were selectively restructured to maintain social distinctions, contributing to imperial control [(Stoler_1989)(Stoler 1989, p.6)]. Anne Stoler's work suggests that these restructurings involved the creation of a 'colonial community' and a set of relations of production and power that aimed to control both indigenous subversives and potentially subversive European underlings in the colonies [(Stoler_1989)(Stoler 1989, p.6)].

The restructuring of class cultures also masked processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation in Africa, necessitating a focus on who benefits and loses from instances of 'negotiable landholding and use' [(Peters_2004)(Peters 2004, p.5)]. Peters argues that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa have long been perceived as promoting individual (male) investment in agriculture, but in reality, they often served to exclude certain groups and deepen social divisions [(Peters_2004)(Peters 2004, p.5)].

This finding is shared by other scholars who have highlighted the problems with unproblematic visions of customary land tenure systems that ignore social inequality and encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood and commercial benefits [(Platteau_1996)(Peters 2004, p.9)].

In late medieval Europe, the quality of institutions, as exemplified by guilds, played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium within western Europe (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Guilds, which emerged as formal institutions to address issues such as fair execution of apprentice contracts and monopolization of skills, were instrumental in this process (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that these institutions had a significant impact on the intergenerational transfer of skills, although they have been criticized for attempting to form local cartels and keeping relative pay above market clearing levels. However, the beneficial effects of guilds on skill development may be counterbalanced by their cartel-forming tendencies (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

The paper underscores that the skill premium in western Europe fell significantly compared to most other parts of the world economy during this period, with factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors contributing to these global patterns (van Zanden 2009, p.1). This finding aligns with the broader literature on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (North_1989, North&Weingast_1989, AcemogluEtAl_2001, AcemogluEtAl_2002).

Moreover, the skill premium measured in this way is also a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.1). This downstream implication highlights the importance of understanding the role of institutions in shaping not only the immediate economic landscape but also the long-term development trajectory of regions.

The impact of Africa's slave trades on current economic development is a crucial yet complex issue in the discourse surrounding institutional shaping and long-run economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that these trades led to the evolution of institutions that had a negative impact on contemporary African economies.

Nunn's (2008) central claim is that Africa, for nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, experienced four slave trades, which lasted significantly longer than official colonial rule (E0137). This prolonged trade activity had profound effects on the continent's institutions. For instance, Vansina (1990) notes that local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade before the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom (E0138). The Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This scenario suggests that the slave trades may have hindered the development of more robust economic institutions in other regions, which could have had lasting negative effects on their long-term economic performance.

This demographic decline, in turn, may have impeded the development of productive agricultural systems and industries, further contributing to the current economic challenges faced by many African countries.

Simson's (2019) study challenges the widespread assumption that post-independence budget policies in African countries were unproductive and prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. This perspective is countered by Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) research, which demonstrates that real wages in African colonies were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies from 1880 to 1965, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments. They found that the Kenyan case, in particular, revealed significant differences in long-term growth trajectories, calling for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Simson (2019, p.11) delves into the budget policies of Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda during their post-independence periods. Contrary to common assumptions, Simson argues that these countries' budgets did not prioritize patronage networks excessively or neglect socially beneficial spending. Instead, high debt burdens and military spending peaked in the 1960s and 1970s in response to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11).

Furthermore, Simson (2019, p.11) shows that from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage. Changes in employment composition and private returns to public employment over this period underscore this shift (Simson 2019, p.19).

The literature agrees that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They demonstrate that regions with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, experienced higher economic growth compared to those with extractive states, like the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the historical contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and this deprivileging was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

However, a point of disagreement arises when considering the role of African women in post-colonial economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced the subordinate status of women, while Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has a significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The historical evidence that settles this disagreement can be found in the impact of colonial rule on formal education. Frankema (2012, p.2) shows that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2). This educational development may have contributed to improved public goods provision, thereby diminishing the negative effects of ethno-linguistic fragmentation on economic growth.
