In the realm of economic history, a contentious debate unfolds surrounding the role institutions play in shaping long-term growth. At the heart of this discourse lies the question: what impact do historical institutional arrangements have on contemporary income levels across nations? This review delves into the scholarly literature that attempts to answer this question, highlighting diverse perspectives and providing a synthesis of key findings.

At one pole, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization significantly influenced current income levels by establishing distinctive institutions and property rights. They posit that in regions where Europeans established law-abiding societies that encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was fostered. Conversely, in areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were set up with the intention of transferring resources quickly to the metropole, economic development was hindered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

On a different front, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) contend that African women played a crucial role in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) proposes that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests a need for qualification and methodological modification to account for these complexities (Austin 2008, p.2).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) propose that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Secure political and economic institutions, particularly those of representative government, significantly contribute to long-run economic growth, according to North and Weingast's seminal work (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). The authors demonstrate this claim through the historical example of seventeenth-century England, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Moreover, North and Weingast argue that these institutions were critical because they provided security for property rights, essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The authors illustrate this point by showing how the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of fiscal crises, highlighting the significance of political factors in economic development (North 1989, p.9).

In addition, North and Weingast emphasize that the effectiveness of these institutions depends on their ability to enforce and change rules. They suggest that the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules plays a critical role in determining economic growth and the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Furthermore, North and Weingast's work supports Jeffrey Williamson's findings on the relationship between institutions and economic growth.

The institutional changes resulting from European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries, particularly in former colonies where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to diverse institutional outcomes. In some cases, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In other instances, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that these institutional differences have lasting effects on economic growth. They find that countries with better institutions, as measured by various indices, experience higher income levels today. This relationship holds even after controlling for other factors, such as geography and initial conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

They suggest that the relatively rich societies in 1500, such as those in West Africa or South America, became relatively poor due to the establishment of extractive institutions during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

Nunn (2008, p.2) provides additional evidence for this mechanism by showing that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa. He argues that these trades weakened African institutions, making it difficult for them to recover after the end of colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19).

The paper by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) collectively suggest that distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These conflicts, as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues, led to the establishment and persistence of certain institutional frameworks that intertwined labor markets, credit markets, community-based welfare systems, and family structures.

Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. The authors argue that this path of institutional development may have affected growth, as where there was extreme inequality, and institutions favored elites and limited the access of much of the population to economic opportunities (Sokoloff & Engerman 2000, p.12).

These findings underscore the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, as acknowledged by some studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). If such interdependencies are widespread, then we cannot study one institution in isolation; instead, we have to look at entire institutional frameworks (Acemoglu & Johnson, cited in Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This insight has implications for the understanding of long-run economic growth, as it emphasizes the importance of holistic analyses that account for the complex interplay between various institutions and their historical evolution.

The paper by Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue for a revised model of economic growth that acknowledges the role of indigenous agency in shaping institutional choices, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

These include the apparent difficulty of testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively and the insufficiency of the theory for explaining why institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2).

He argues that the European presence was either marginal or took the form of colonial rule, which introduced extractive types of economic control poorly suited for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This finding complements the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002), who demonstrate the negative impact of European colonialism on current income levels in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and others argue that the growth of useful knowledge was instrumental in driving long-run economic growth, particularly during the Industrial Revolution in the British Isles. Mokyr contends that intellectual societies, which emerged to discuss issues related to political economy such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order, and efficient taxes, played a significant role in this growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Furthermore, these intellectual communities served as safeguards against the exploitation of resources by 'roving bandits' or greedy individuals (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

These intellectual societies fostered an environment that encouraged the efficient allocation of resources and the development of trust, establishing a foundation for sustainable growth. Mokyr highlights that while industrial and even less revolutionary developments were not exclusive to Britain, it was the British context that experienced the most significant impact (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This suggests that the presence of intellectual societies may have contributed to the unique trajectory of economic growth in the British Isles during the Industrial Revolution.

The development of these intellectual communities also had implications for the role of law and order in economic growth. Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate the negative impact of European colonialism on current income levels, argue that effective institutions are crucial for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, 2002). Mokyr's work implies that the presence of intellectual societies in the British Isles may have contributed to the development and enforcement of such effective institutions, further supporting the argument that these communities played a significant role in driving long-run economic growth during the Industrial Revolution.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) posits that the emergence and quality of institutions in Western Europe during the late medieval period significantly shaped the skill premium, contributing to long-run economic growth.

The quality of these institutions, as van Zanden suggests, had a profound impact on the skill premium, which in turn influenced long-term economic performance (van Zanden, 2009, p.1). The guilds, often criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into their trades, played a dual role: they facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills while also attempting to form local cartels to maintain higher relative pay above market clearing levels (van Zanden, 2009, p.19).

This stream of literature suggests that the skill premium, shaped by institutional quality and factor proportions, served as a predictor of long-term economic performance in various parts of the world economy during the late medieval period. The qualitative difference in institutional development between Western Europe and other regions thus contributed to a comparative advantage in long-run economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) underscore the significance of continued collaboration among scholars in shaping long-run economic growth through the extension and revision of historical estimates of GDP and population. They argue that these revisions are crucial for understanding the drivers of economic development, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia.

This project acknowledges that estimates of national accounts from the past are subject to certain margins of error, particularly as one goes back in time (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Scholars can collaborate and experiment with various margins of error to produce more objective data that contributes to a better understanding of long-term economic development.

This collaborative effort has led to a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project.

Their work underscores the importance of focusing on Asia, a region that has been underrepresented in historical economic analyses but is crucial for understanding global economic development.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This technological innovation, which gained widespread use during the late 19th century, marked a turning point in the region's agrarian landscape.

Hornbeck's study (2010) reveals that prior to the introduction of barbed wire, herd laws were a common means of land management in the Plains. These laws sought to control livestock movement and prevent overgrazing. After 1884, the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped reporting details on these laws, suggesting their decline in relevance following barbed wire's rise (Hornbeck 2010, p.8).

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire led to a significant increase in improved farmland between 1880 and 1890. In herd-law counties, the fraction of improved farmland increased by 20 percentage points, while total improved land saw an 11 percentage point increase during this period (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). These findings suggest that barbed wire not only reduced costs associated with land protection but also facilitated property rights enforcement, leading to increased agricultural development in the American Plains.

Moreover, Hornbeck's research underscores the importance of secure property rights as a catalyst for economic growth. The improved ability to define and protect land boundaries allowed farmers to invest more effectively in their properties, ultimately contributing to the region's agricultural expansion. This finding complements the work of North and Weingast (1989, p.30) who argue that secure property rights are essential for long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and promoting investment.

In this context, it is worth noting that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains was not a given but rather the result of specific historical conditions. These factors combined to create a unique set of circumstances that made the widespread adoption of barbed wire possible.

This case highlights the role of institutions, in this instance, property rights enforcement mechanisms, in shaping long-run economic growth. As we move forward, it is crucial to consider how similar factors may have influenced institutional development and economic growth in other regions of the world.

British rule played a significant role in shaping formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, which has long-term implications for economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940, facilitated the development of formal education in this region.

The role of these actors can be traced through their impact on educational institutions and curricula. For instance, missionary schools were established to educate African children in Christian values, literacy, and numeracy skills (Frankema 2012, p.2). These schools not only provided basic education but also served as a means to spread Western culture and ideologies.

Moreover, the British traders' need for skilled laborers led them to invest in vocational training programs that focused on teaching practical skills such as carpentry, masonry, and printing (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This emphasis on vocational education was a departure from traditional African educational systems, which primarily focused on oral learning and apprenticeships.

The development of formal education under British rule also had downstream implications for income levels in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.2) finds that the expansion of formal education led to higher real wages and a reduction in poverty, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.17).

The impact of British rule on educational development was not uniform across all regions. For example, areas with a strong pre-colonial influence of Islam or high levels of native African resistance against colonial rule may have experienced different educational trajectories (Frankema 2012, p.17).

Simson's (2019, p.11) study challenges the prevalent view that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were unproductive, focusing primarily on patronage networks instead of socially beneficial spending. In contrast, Simson demonstrates that these policies led to high levels of subsidies and interest, significantly impacting resource allocation.

Simson (2019, p.11) reveals that Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda's post-independence budgets were marked by substantial subsidies and interest payments, which peaked at 30%, 18%, and remained smaller in the respective countries during specific periods (Simson 2019, p.11). This high debt burden had a considerable influence on expenditure allocation.

Furthermore, Simson's work highlights a shift towards military spending following independence, as domestic defense became the responsibility of individual colonies rather than the metropolis (Simson 2019, p.11). As regional instability and internal dissent grew during the 1960s and 1970s, military spending escalated in response.

Beyond capital cities, employment patterns did not significantly change, but jobs increasingly favored skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward (Simson 2019, p.19). While this trend may suggest nepotistic or unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment, Simson's findings show that high-level allocation of wage resources generally constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage.

In the realm of British industrialization, Humphries (2013, p.16) and others propose a significant shift in focus towards the role of unskilled female and child labor in fostering long-run economic growth. Humphries argues that these labor forces were not only abundant but also amenable to the technological innovations characterizing the British Industrial Revolution.

Humphries (2013, p.16) presents evidence of marital instability during the eighteenth century, suggesting that a considerable number of families experienced separation or male abandonment. This condition, in turn, led to an increased availability of women and children within the pauper populations, contributing to the labor force (E0089). Furthermore, Humphries asserts that these groups were often overlooked in historical analyses due to the focus on high-wage male labor (E0090).

While Humphries's work emphasizes the availability of female and child labor during the British Industrial Revolution, other scholars have investigated its impact. For instance, Goldin (1991) highlights that the industrial revolution was a period of rapid urbanization, which led to an increase in the demand for unskilled labor, particularly among women and children (E0367). This increased demand, in turn, helped to drive down wages for these groups, making them more affordable for manufacturers.

Goldin's (2006) work builds upon the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century, a key factor driving the quiet revolution in women's employment, education, and family roles. This shift was not merely a matter of expanding job opportunities; it also entailed a transformation in societal expectations and norms regarding gender roles (Goldin 2006, p.7).

The increased demand for female office workers can be attributed to the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3). This surge in educated women led to their employment in increasingly complex roles within offices, contributing to a change in societal perceptions about women's capabilities and potential.

The shifts from one phase to the next were not only due to changes in supply and demand but also to alterations in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s (Goldin 2006, p.3). These technological advancements made it possible for women to balance work outside the home with domestic responsibilities, further enabling their participation in the labor force.

The quiet revolution in gender dynamics also dispelled barriers to the employment of married women, making part-time work readily available (Goldin 2006, p.3). This change allowed more women to maintain a balance between work and family life, contributing to the normalization of women's employment outside the home.

For example, Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, who graduated near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class, was initially offered a position as a legal secretary and no California law firm offered her a position as a lawyer. These examples illustrate how societal expectations about women's roles were gradually changing, allowing for more opportunities in education and employment.

For economic growth in less developed countries, Kuznets (1973, p.1) identifies advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data as crucial requirements. According to Kuznets's paper, these elements are essential for effective participation in the modern economic system by labor forces, particularly in less developed areas.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology is not compatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which were prevalent in earlier times over much of even the developed world. Thus, to facilitate economic growth, these outdated institutions and social views need to be replaced by more appropriate ones.

This dynamic drive of modern economic growth requires geographical reaching out and continuous expansion to less developed areas, emphasizing the importance of institutional adjustments for economic progress in these regions.

For instance, the British Industrial Revolution saw a significant shift from rural to urban living and a reconfiguration of labor force dynamics, with women and children often playing crucial roles in the manufacturing sector (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Colonial authorities reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law that limited women's access to land and resources in Africa, thereby hindering their economic contributions.

A key mechanism through which this subordination occurred was the introduction of a dual legal system under colonial rule. European courts based on common and civil law coexisted with native courts, leading to the codification of customary law that reinforced women's subordinate status (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This is evident in the case of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast, who migrated in search of farmland for cocoa production.

The downstream implication of this subordination was a limitation on women's economic potential. For instance, Polly Hill (1963) documented how the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule deprived women of access to land and resources crucial for cash crop production. This not only restricted their contributions to economic growth but also perpetuated gender inequality that persists to this day.

Moreover, the authors find that this internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as important as external factors such as the rule of law and trustworthiness of others in explaining current levels of intergroup trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This finding complements other studies that highlight the importance of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Knack & Keefer 1997; Greif 1989; Putnam 2000; Bloom et al. 2008).

Further downstream implications of this mistrust can be seen in the difficulties faced by African countries in building effective institutions. For instance, Acemoglu et al. It is possible that the slave trade played a role in shaping these institutions by fostering mistrust and undermining cooperation between different ethnic groups.

The literature consistently underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in Africa (Acemoglu et al., 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27); Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15)). European colonization significantly impacted the institutional landscape of Africa, with regions experiencing heavier colonization exhibiting lower levels of institutional quality today (Acemoglu et al., 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)).

There is a disagreement regarding the specific factors shaping these institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies played a pivotal role in establishing property rights and rule of law, which in turn influenced current income levels (Acemoglu et al., 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)). In contrast, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) suggest that the subordinate status of African women during colonial rule was instrumental in shaping institutions by undermining cooperation between different ethnic groups (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20)).

The historical evidence sheds light on this disagreement. Hopkins (2009, p.18) points out that the Atlantic slave trade reduced population levels and fostered institutions inimical to long-term growth (Hopkins, 2009 (Hopkins 2009, p.10)). Moreover, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate how European colonization reinforced the de-privileging of women in access to land, which could have contributed to mistrust and undermined cooperation between ethnic groups (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15)).
