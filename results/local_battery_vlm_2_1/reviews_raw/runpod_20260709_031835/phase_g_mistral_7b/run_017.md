In the realm of economic history, the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth is a contentious yet vital issue. Scholars debate whether and how European colonization and its institutional legacies have influenced current income levels across countries. This review synthesizes key findings from various studies that address this question directly or indirectly, offering insights into the complex interplay between institutions, economic growth, and historical events.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the differing colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to distinct institutional structures, which in turn have significant impacts on income levels today. They contend that where Europeans established law-abiding societies that encouraged investment, such as in the United States and Australia, current income levels are high.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) shift focus to pre-colonial and colonial Africa, examining the contributions of African women to economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) discusses the implications of the new economic history of Africa for institutional reforms and poverty reduction. He suggests that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments, but that societies can still be dysfunctional or capable of upholding the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) extend their earlier work, focusing on the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism and their impact on the relative income levels among former colonies. Frankema (2012, p.2) delves into the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, attributing its facilitation to British traders and Protestant missionaries, as opposed to direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) offers a different perspective, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations. He suggests that interdependencies among individual institutions within wider frameworks are crucial to understanding their impact on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

As we delve deeper into the complexities of institutional development and their role in shaping long-run economic growth, it becomes clear that a nuanced understanding of historical context is essential. The interplay between institutions, cultural factors, and economic outcomes is intricate and multifaceted, requiring further research to fully comprehend the dynamics at play.

The literature on institutional development and long-run economic growth highlights the significant impact of institutions established during European colonization. In one extreme, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In contrast, in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

This variation in institutional structures has far-reaching consequences for current income levels across countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Furthermore, they find that this influence accounts for a considerable fraction of current income differences among nations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.34) extend their argument to explain the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies.

The effects of European colonization on economic development are not limited to institutions established by extractive states. Nunn (2008, p.2) contends that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa. He argues that sustained trade did not occur until African societies with centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed markets and trading networks emerged (Nunn 2008, p.19).

One key mechanism through which this occurred is the subordination of women in access to land and resources during colonial rule.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that European colonization reinforced the de-privileging of women in access to land in the context of cash crops, particularly for cocoa farming. Polly Hill (1963) has documented how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911.

The introduction of a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts facilitated this process. This subordination of women had downstream implications for economic growth as their contributions to agriculture and trade were limited, hindering the development of productive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) provide further evidence supporting the role of historical institutions in shaping current African economies. They argue that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their estimates show that both internal and external factors contribute to this mistrust, but the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. This finding underscores the importance of addressing internal norms of mistrust in order to promote economic development and trustworthiness in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a fundamental driver of economic growth, particularly in less developed countries. According to Mokyr (2005, p.54), intellectual advancements concerning issues of political economy such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and more efficient, less distortionary taxes were significant contributors to the Industrial Revolution. Kuznets further supports this claim by stating that modern technology necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, family formation, urbanization, and man's views on his role and the measure of his achievement in society.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights the Enlightenment idea that when individuals work for their own good, they normally also contribute to the welfare of society, unless they choose to engage in redistribution and rent seeking. This ideology, he argues, was instrumental in promoting economic growth during the Industrial Revolution. Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by stating that effective participation in the modern economic system requires rapid changes in labor force location and structure, relations among occupational status groups, and even in the relations between labor force and total population.

The widespread adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as Hornbeck (2010, p.8) argues, significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement. This mechanism was instrumental in fostering a more secure environment for farmers to invest in their lands, thereby promoting agricultural growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides empirical evidence to support this claim. He demonstrates that counties with herd laws, which regulated livestock movement and thus necessitated the use of barbed wire, experienced a substantial increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire. Specifically, herd-law counties had an insignificant 6 to 7 percentage point decline in the improved fraction of farmland from 1870 to 1880; however, they experienced a significant 20 to 3 percentage point increase from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This pattern suggests that barbed wire played a crucial role in enforcing property rights and facilitating agricultural development.

The downstream implication of this mechanism is further qualified by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights the importance of secure property rights for long-run economic growth. Kuznets argues that effective participation in the modern economic system requires rapid changes in labor force location and structure, relations among occupational status groups, and even in the relations between labor force and total population. The enhanced ability to protect land through barbed wire undoubtedly contributed to these necessary transformations, thereby fostering agricultural development and setting the stage for broader economic growth.

In this context, it can be argued that the widespread adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains not only enabled farmers to protect their land more effectively but also played a crucial role in enforcing property rights, thereby fostering agricultural development and contributing to long-run economic growth. This finding complements the broader literature on institutions and economic growth by identifying an antecedent that flowed into the causal variable as a trigger for development. The Maddison Project, led by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2), aims to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) acknowledge that estimates of national accounts in the past, especially in distant periods, are subject to certain margins of error. They assert that these errors can be estimated, as demonstrated by Feinstein and Thomas's method and its application in several studies on the topic (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Members of the Maddison Project have also experimented with various margins of error, with the Hitotshibashi team's study on Asian national accounts being the most detailed (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

This collaboration among scholars not only aims to improve the accuracy of historical GDP and population estimates but also intends to stimulate further research in this area. By inducing many scholars to work on these themes, they hope to quantify long-term economic development more accurately than ever before (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). As we delve deeper into understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, it becomes clear that such collaborative efforts are essential for refining our understanding of historical economic dynamics.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and others argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, were the primary driver of the development of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the traditional view that institutions emerged to maximize efficiency or promote growth. Instead, Ogilvie posits that institutions evolved to address conflicts over wealth distribution, a crucial factor shaping social and political dynamics during this period.

These interlinked institutional systems, according to Laslett, contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. Similarly, Solar argues that the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This distinctive framework may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.

This finding underscores the importance of considering entire institutional frameworks rather than focusing on individual institutions in isolation.

Furthermore, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) offer evidence that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This finding supports the idea that economic institutions emerged to address distributional conflicts, as secure property rights can help mitigate disputes over wealth distribution.

The British influence on African education and economy, as facilitated by British traders and Protestant missionaries, played a significant role in raising real wages above subsistence level in sub-Saharan Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This claim is supported by Frankema's work, which argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education primarily through the influence of traders and missionaries rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2).

This educational expansion, in turn, contributed to the rise in real wages. The authors find evidence of this trend in nine British African colonies from around 1880 to 1965.

This finding supports the idea that British influence led to economic growth in Africa, as it implies that African wages were sufficient to support a population living at or above subsistence levels.

However, it is important to note that the rise in real wages was not uniform across all regions and populations. Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight differences in long-term growth trajectories between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This disparity underscores the complexities of African economic development under British influence and suggests that further research is needed to fully understand these dynamics.

The paper by Humphries (2013, p.16) and Goldin (2006, p.7) offer a compelling argument that revisits the traditional narrative of the British industrial revolution. Rather than focusing solely on men and high wages, these scholars advocate for considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor as a crucial factor in this historical event.

Humphries (2013, p.16) delves into the socio-economic conditions of the eighteenth century, revealing that family breakdown and male abandonment were common during this period. He cites evidence suggesting that up to 18% of boys grew up separated from their yet-living fathers, indicating the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16). Goldin (2006, p.7), on the other hand, examines the shifts in female employment and education patterns in the United States over the past century. She highlights that the increased involvement of women in various roles underwent a quiet revolution, primarily driven by changes in demand for female office workers, educational institutions, household production technology, and institutional changes (Goldin 2006, p.7).

The implication of this combined evidence is twofold. First, it underscores the importance of acknowledging the role of women and children in the industrial revolution, challenging the conventional focus on men and high wages. Second, it suggests that understanding the dynamics of family structures, employment patterns, and educational opportunities for women can provide a more nuanced perspective on economic growth during this period.

European class cultures were intentionally restructured to maintain colonial authority and establish specific relations of production and power in imperial control (Stoler 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.9). This process involved the creation and enforcement of social distinctions that served to regulate both indigenous populations and European colonials.

One mechanism through which this occurred was the establishment of racial ideologies, which functioned as a means of justifying colonial rule and white supremacy while also serving as a tool for controlling dissent among European underlings (Stoler 1989, p.6). For instance, Stoler argues that the fear of sexual assault by Asian and black males on European women was not only used to justify continued European rule but also served as a directive aimed at maintaining order within the colonial community.

Simultaneously, political and economic institutions were developed to facilitate the extraction of resources from colonized territories (North 1989, p.9). North highlights the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments as key examples of this process. These institutions not only led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs but also reflected increased security of property rights, which was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth.

For example, the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years-War, reflecting the need for political and economic institutions that could provide stable revenue streams for the colonial authorities.

Peters's (2004) work and Simson's (2019) findings offer a compelling perspective on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Both scholars argue that the perception of static customary landholding systems in the region masks processes of exclusion and class formation, which can be advantageous for certain groups seeking to maintain power and control.

Peters (2004, p.5) demonstrates how the final decade of colonial rule was marked by a push towards agricultural development, fueled by a prejudice against African landholding practices that were seen as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment in agriculture. This prejudice, according to Peters, led to instances where national and local elites captured the benefits of decentralization and 'traditional' managers, deepening social divisions and furthering exclusion.

Simson (2019, p.11), on the other hand, challenges the common assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Instead, Simson argues that these countries experienced a high debt burden and growth in military spending, which was a response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent. These budget policies, while not necessarily meritocratic, did not significantly ease the distribution of patronage.

However, it is important to note that these findings are specific to the contexts examined and may not be universally applicable across all sub-Saharan African countries.

The literature suggests that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) who argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. However, the nature and extent of these institutions' impact are not uniformly agreed upon.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) assert that European colonizers established different types of institutions across their colonies, with some promoting investment and the rule of law, while others were extractive in nature. Conversely, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops.

The disagreement between these two streams lies in the emphasis on European colonization's role versus that of African societies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) focus on the institutional changes brought about by European colonizers, while Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the economic contributions made by African women before and during colonial rule.

A key piece of historical evidence that helps settle this disagreement is the extent to which European colonization influenced African societies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide empirical evidence showing that European colonies with institutions promoting investment and the rule of law experienced economic growth, while those with extractive institutions remained stagnant. On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate how African women were able to propel economic revolutions despite the subordinate status imposed upon them by colonial rule.
