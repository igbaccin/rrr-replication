The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a question of paramount importance in contemporary scholarship, as the intellectual stakes extend beyond academic discourse to inform policy decisions and development strategies. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement concerning the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth, with some scholars emphasizing the significance of institutions, while others propose alternative explanations.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a profound impact on current income levels across countries. They posit that the strategies adopted by Europeans in their colonies varied significantly, with some establishing rule-of-law systems that encouraged investment, while others set up extractive states intended to transfer resources rapidly back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)). Furthermore, they demonstrate that different measures of institutions yield very similar results in terms of their impact on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19)).

In a related but distinct stream, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) explore the contributions made by African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Moreover, they highlight how patriarchal alliances and customary law were instrumental in the codification of women's subordinate status under colonial rule (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20)).

This stream complements Acemoglu et al.'s claim by emphasizing the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, although from different perspectives. In the former case, it is the colonial-era institutions established by Europeans that are central to the argument, while in the latter, it is the customary laws and social structures that predate European colonization but were codified under colonial rule that are of interest. Together, these works highlight the complex interplay between historical institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of this relationship.

The paper by Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and North's (1989, p.9) works underscore the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, specifically focusing on the impact of European colonialism. These studies collectively argue that institutional changes resulting from colonial rule have significantly influenced current income levels across countries.

The authors demonstrate that these institutional differences have had lasting consequences for income levels. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further extend this argument by showing how these institutional changes accounted for the reversal of relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor.

North's (1989) work complements this stream by emphasizing the significance of political and economic institutions in determining economic growth. North argues that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England played a crucial role in England's long-term success, highlighting the importance of secure property rights for economic development. This finding supports Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) claim that the institutions established during European colonization have had lasting effects on income levels across countries.

The implication of this stream is that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism have played a crucial role in shaping current income levels across countries, with different strategies leading to varying institutional outcomes and long-term economic consequences. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and specific institutional arrangements established during European colonization to fully comprehend contemporary income disparities among former colonies.

The authors demonstrate how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system in Africa, which codified the subordinate status of women through customary law. This legal system, in turn, affected women's access to land and resources, thereby impacting their contributions to economic growth.

Polly Hill (1963) documents the migration of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. However, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15).

Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts, which enabled the codification of customary law (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.20). This legal system perpetuated gender inequality by reinforcing the subordinate status of women in African societies.

Distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe significantly shaped the development of economic institutions, offering an alternative explanation to efficiency-based theories (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie's study highlights the interdependency of various institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that this integrated system may have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. For instance, the European family system was linked with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets for savings accumulation, and collective provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

In addition, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantage elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework may have furthered economic growth by enabling elites to maintain their status while limiting access for much of the population to economic opportunities.

One downstream implication of these findings is that studying one institution in isolation would fail to capture the full picture of pre-industrial Europe's economic development. Instead, it is essential to examine entire institutional frameworks to understand how they have influenced each other and contributed to economic growth. This insight underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when analyzing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Furthermore, Ogilvie's study demonstrates that cultural beliefs can also play a significant role in shaping economic institutions and their efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, Richardson's work shows how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient guild rules, while Protestant reform led to guild decline. This finding underscores the potential impact of cultural factors on the structure and efficiency of economic institutions.

In pre-industrial Europe, the adoption of new technology was often hindered by traditional institutions and social structures. Some regions managed to surmount these challenges and achieve economic growth through institutional reforms. This section will focus on how guilds, property rights, and political institutions shaped long-run economic growth in pre-industrial Europe.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that for effective participation in the modern economic system, a country needs rapid changes in its labor force's location and structure, relations among occupational status groups, and even relations between labor force and total population. These changes are associated with high aggregate growth rates and rapid changes in other aspects of society, such as family formation and urbanization (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Mokyr (2005, p.3) supports this view by stating that after the Industrial Revolution, law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts became crucial for sustained growth. He also acknowledges that these institutional changes allowed for more efficient allocation of resources, thus fostering a conducive environment for economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

In this context, guilds played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth in pre-industrial Europe. Guilds were associations of artisans and merchants that regulated their trades and protected their interests. They provided a framework for the exchange of knowledge and skills, which facilitated technological advancement (Mokyr 2005, p.3). Guilds could potentially hinder economic growth due to their restrictive practices towards competition and innovation.

This finding underscores the potential impact of cultural factors on the structure and efficiency of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe.

Property rights were another crucial factor shaping long-run economic growth in pre-industrial Europe. Secure property rights encouraged investment in land and infrastructure, which facilitated technological advancement and economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The absence of secure property rights might discourage investment and innovation, hindering economic development.

Political institutions also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth in pre-industrial Europe. Strong centralized governments were able to provide law and order, enforce contracts, and protect property rights, which facilitated economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). On the other hand, weak or unstable political institutions could hinder economic growth by creating uncertainty and instability.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) research underscores the significant impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains. This innovation reduced costs associated with land protection and facilitated property rights enforcement, thereby fostering increased agricultural development.

Hornbeck's paper presents a compelling case study, demonstrating that by 1880, herd laws, which regulated livestock grazing on unfenced lands, ceased to be reported in Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports. This shift coincided with the near-universal adoption of barbed wire, suggesting its pivotal role in land protection (Hornbeck 2010, E0081).

Furthermore, Hornbeck's analysis reveals that herd-law counties experienced a significant increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire. Between 1880 and 1890, these counties saw a 20-point increase in the improved fraction of farmland and an 11-point rise in total improved land (Hornbeck 2010, E0082).

In this context, Hornbeck's findings align with the broader literature that emphasizes the importance of secure property rights for economic development. The reduced costs and enhanced enforcement capabilities provided by barbed wire fostered agricultural growth in the American Plains, underscoring its transformative role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor played a significant role in the British industrial revolution, as suggested by Humphries (2013, p.16). This finding challenges the traditional focus on men and high wages. Humphries's evidence demonstrates that a substantial proportion of boys grew up separated from their yet-living fathers due to marital instability, economic conditions, and male abandonment (Humphries 2013, p.16).

The presence of such a large group of families whose fathers were absent, either deceased or physically distant, testifies to the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16). This situation provided an ample labor force for the industrial revolution, which was otherwise characterized by high wages and male-breadwinner households.

The implication of this downstream condition is that the British industrial revolution may have been more reliant on a diverse workforce than previously thought. A further source within this stream, King (1988), supports this notion by illustrating how the poor law system in England and Wales catered to the needs of women and children during times of economic hardship. Goldin's (2006) work underscores a significant shift in women's roles in employment, education, and family in the United States during the 20th century. This transformation was primarily driven by the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century (Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin demonstrates that this shift set off a chain of events that altered women's opportunities and expectations in various domains.

This shift in labor market dynamics led to a surge in women's employment in offices, which, in turn, created a demand for more education and professional training.

The increased availability of educational opportunities allowed women to acquire skills that were increasingly valued in the workforce, further expanding their participation in employment (Goldin 2006, p.7). For instance, Goldin recounts the story of Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, who graduated near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class but was initially offered only a position as a legal secretary. This anecdote underscores the persisting barriers that women faced in entering traditionally male-dominated professions.

By the late 1940s and early 1950s, changes in household production technology, institutional shifts enabling part-time work, and the removal of barriers to employed married women transformed female labor supply (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes facilitated a more significant integration of women into the workforce, as they could balance their professional commitments with family responsibilities.

This shift in women's roles had profound implications for education and family structures. As more women entered the workforce, there was a growing demand for schools that offered flexible schedules or evening classes to accommodate working students (Goldin 2006, p.7). Additionally, the increased participation of women in the workforce led to changes in family dynamics, as families adapted to the new realities of dual-income households.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) advocate for the continuation of collaborative efforts to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, even when a solid basis for these numbers does not exist. This strategy is aimed at improving our understanding of historical economic growth, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia.

The Maddison Project, as proposed by Bolt and van Zanden, acknowledges the inherent uncertainties in estimating national accounts, especially in the distant past. However, they argue that these margins of error should not deter scholars from collaborating to refine their estimates (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3). Maddison himself embraced this approach, producing numbers even when a solid basis did not always exist, in the hope that scholars would challenge and improve upon his work (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2).

One specific mechanism that emerges from this stream of literature is the use of subjective estimates to determine the margins of error for underlying data. Despite the inherent uncertainty in these estimates, they have been used to produce more detailed overviews of long-term economic growth than were available when Maddison started his project (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2).

Downstream from this mechanism, we find evidence that such collaborative efforts have led to significant advancements in our understanding of historical economic development. For instance, the Hitotshibashi team working on Asian national accounts has experimented with various margins of error (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3). These efforts contribute to the broader goal of shaping long-run economic growth by providing more accurate and detailed historical data, which can inform contemporary economic theories and policies.

This stream complements the current one by offering insights into the institutional factors that influence the accuracy and utility of historical data, further contributing to our understanding of long-term economic development.

The European class cultures in colonies were strategically restructured to preserve social hierarchies and exert imperial control (Stoler 1989, p.6; Peters 2004, p.5). Stoler's work illuminates how the maintenance of colonial authority and power relations was achieved through the selective reorganization of European class cultures. This included reinforcing racist ideologies, safeguarding white prestige, and implementing measures to protect European women from sexual assault by indigenous men (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Simultaneously, Peters highlights that the perception of 'custom' as static in official circles allowed certain groups to manipulate landholding practices for their benefit while ignoring social divisions and class formation in sub-Saharan Africa (Peters 2004, p.5). This selective reorganization of European class cultures, aimed at maintaining imperial control, ultimately deepened social tensions and contributed to the formation of distinct classes within colonial societies.

By examining these processes, we can better understand how European class cultures were strategically manipulated during the colonial period to exert control over both indigenous populations and European underlings (Stoler 1989, p.4; Peters 2004, p.9). This restructuring of European class cultures not only reinforced social hierarchies but also created conditions that facilitated the formation of distinct classes within colonial societies.

These findings suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth during the colonial period extends beyond formal structures and laws to include the manipulation of European class cultures as a means of exerting imperial control. This strategic reorganization had significant downstream implications for social dynamics, class formation, and ultimately, economic development in colonies.

The quality of institutions in Western Europe during the late medieval period significantly shaped the skill premium, which contributed to long-run economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This crucial factor was underpinned by the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, that ensured fair apprentice contracts and facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills (ibid.).

These institutions were met with controversy. Critics argued that they monopolized skills and artificially inflated relative pay levels by restricting entry into their trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Despite these criticisms, guilds had a more significant impact on skill development than their potential cartel-forming tendencies might suggest.

This finding underscores the significance of institutions in shaping economic growth over extended periods.

For instance, increasing demand for skilled labor due to urbanization and trade expansion could lead to higher wages for skilled workers and an increased skill premium.

Furthermore, the skill premium during this period had global implications. Regions with lower skill premiums, such as Western Europe, experienced faster economic growth compared to those with higher skill premiums (ibid.). This observation highlights the importance of understanding the determinants of the skill premium in different parts of the world economy.

The work of Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing the role of indigenous agency in shaping institutional choices that lead to long-term economic growth. Austin argues that this oversimplification in economic growth causation, which compresses different historical periods and paths, necessitates qualification and methodological modification (Austin 2008, p.2).

Austin's paper offers a critical perspective on the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography associated with Douglass North, which emphasizes secure private property rights as the key to long-term economic growth. Austin suggests that this approach fails to account for why institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2).

One specific mechanism that supports this claim is the interaction between colonial regimes and African economic enterprise. In some cases, it was in the interests of colonial regimes to support, rather than simply extract from, African economic activity (Austin 2008, p.2). This observation qualifies the downstream implication that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth.

Simson (2019, p.11) argues that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions. This claim contradicts the belief that these countries' budgets prioritized resource distribution for political gain over economic development.

One specific mechanism that supports this argument is the shift in employment patterns following independence. Simson (2019, p.11) observes that while jobs increasingly favored comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.19). This finding qualifies the assumption that post-independence budget policies were primarily focused on distributing resources to political allies.

Another downstream implication of this mechanism is the impact on the geographic distribution of employment. This observation complicates the view that post-independence budget policies led to a significant redistribution of jobs away from merit-based positions.

In summary, Simson's (2019, p.11) study challenges the common assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. The shift in employment patterns following independence, which favored comparatively skilled and female professions while constraining rather than easing the distribution of patronage, supports this claim. Furthermore, the relatively stable geographic distribution of employment qualifies the assumption that post-independence budget policies led to a significant redistribution of jobs away from merit-based positions. These findings suggest that post-independence budget policies in these countries may have been more focused on economic development than is often assumed.

This analysis sets the stage for examining how post-independence budget policies in other African nations compare to those in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, and whether they also challenge common assumptions about the role of patronage networks in shaping long-run economic growth.

Real wages in Africa under colonial rule have long been associated with persistent historical poverty due to structural growth impediments (Frankema&vanWaijenburg_2012 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2)). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) challenge this assumption by arguing that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time.

Their findings reveal that real wages in these colonies were higher than previously assumed, with some even surpassing those in contemporary Western Europe. Furthermore, the authors found that real wages rose significantly over time, contradicting the notion of persistent African poverty.

One key factor contributing to these high and rising real wages was the presence of a large unskilled labor force, which allowed employers to keep wage costs low (Frankema&vanWaijenburg_2012 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28)). This abundance of cheap labor enabled the expansion of cash crop production and mining industries, both of which provided employment opportunities and contributed to economic growth.

It is crucial to acknowledge that these benefits were not evenly distributed across all African colonies. These variations can be attributed to factors such as differing degrees of income inequality and varying economic dynamics (commercialization).

The slave trade in Africa has been argued to have long-lasting impacts on institutional development and economic growth in the continent (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) contend that this trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They argue that the slave trade, by promoting inter-ethnic violence and undermining social cohesion, has left lasting negative effects on trust levels among African communities.

The external channel suggests that the slave trade led to poor institutions, such as weak rule of law and untrustworthy others, which in turn affected trust levels. On the other hand, the internal norms channel posits that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust within individuals themselves, independent of their external environment.

He highlights that for nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, the African continent experienced four slave trades, lasting far longer than official colonial rule. This prolonged period of forced labor and violence undoubtedly contributed to a culture of mistrust and instability that continues to affect economic development in Africa today.

Moreover, Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the Atlantic slave trade reduced the population from a level that was previously relatively high, encouraging the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth (Hopkins 2009, p.10). These poor institutions, including extractive economic control and unproductive systems, persisted due to the resistance of larger populations or the marginal European presence in certain regions.
