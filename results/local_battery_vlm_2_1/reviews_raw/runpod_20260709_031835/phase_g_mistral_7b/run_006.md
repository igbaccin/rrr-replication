The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of ongoing debate among scholars, with differing perspectives on the causal mechanisms and historical contexts that underpin this relationship. At stake is a nuanced understanding of how institutions have influenced economic development across societies, particularly in the context of European colonization.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offer a complementary account, focusing on the economic contributions of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They contend that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that their subordinate status in customary law under colonial rule further restricted their access to land and resources (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15).

These findings suggest that the institutional foundations laid during European colonization have had lasting effects on economic growth, but they do not address the question of how these institutions came into being. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He contends that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins, 2009, p.18).

Austin (2008, p.2) questions the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests a need for qualification and methodological modification to account for the complexities of institutional evolution and their impact on long-term economic development (Austin, 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) build on this idea by investigating the cultural consequences of the slave trade in Africa, arguing that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011, p.27).

The impact of European colonialism on institutions and income levels is a significant area of study, with researchers such as Acemoglu et al. arguing that the institutional changes resulting from this historical period play an essential role in shaping current economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Nunn 2008, p.2). According to their work, European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different associated institutions. In some cases, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In other instances, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, colonizers set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

They also demonstrated that political power was crucial for elites in colonial societies, as it enabled them to force native or African laborers to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34). This exploitation, coupled with the extraction of resources, had lasting effects on economic development.

One specific mechanism through which European colonialism impacted institutions and income levels is the establishment of property rights. In regions employing extractive colonization strategies, the absence of secure property rights impeded economic development.

For instance, Vansina (1990) notes that local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade before the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). The presence of these institutions enabled sustained trade with Europeans. The slave trades disrupted African societies, leading to mistrust and instability that persist today according to Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27).

The development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms through political institutions is a key driver of long-run economic growth, as argued by North (1989, p.9) and Mokyr (2005, p.54). North (1989, p.9) contends that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which increased security of property rights and facilitated economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) supports this view by arguing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This suggests that the development of political and economic institutions fostered an environment conducive to the advancement of knowledge, which in turn spurred economic growth.

Moreover, North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that the relationship between the benefits derived from rule specification and the costs of measurement is critical in understanding the structure and effectiveness of enforcement mechanisms. Mokyr (2005, p.54), on the other hand, highlights that law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts contributed to more efficient allocations of resources during the Industrial Revolution, which ultimately supported economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

North and Weingast's (1989, p.30) argument that fundamental institutions of representative government, such as those found in seventeenth-century England, play a crucial role in fostering long-run economic growth by providing political protection for economic rights is substantiated by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Kuznets (1973, p.1).

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate how secure rights are essential for economic growth, as shown in seventeenth-century England and other economies. They argue that the financial revolution in England was a critical factor in its long-term success, implying that even if other forces would have eventually led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. This is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who posits that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) also highlights the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that we cannot study one institution in isolation but must look at entire institutional frameworks. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions; this whole interlinked institutional system, according to Laslett, contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (as cited in Ogilvie 2007).

He argues that for a country to experience economic growth, it needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries.

Moreover, Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that modern technology is not compatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery-all of which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world and had to be replaced by more appropriate institutions and social views.

The work of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) underscores the role of inequality in shaping institutions that influence long-run economic growth, particularly within New World societies. Their research suggests that greater inequality led to institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and contributing to persistent inequality over time.

They contend that where there was extreme inequality, institutions were designed to advantage elites, limiting access to economic opportunities for much of the population (Sokoloff&Engerman_2000, p.2).

By advantaging elites, these institutions allowed them to maintain their status over time, potentially hindering economic mobility and stifling growth (Sokoloff&Engerman_2000, p.12).

Interestingly, this finding complements North's assertion that secure rights are crucial for the adoption of modern technology and economic growth (North_1989, p.30), as elites with institutional advantages may have been less inclined to adopt such technologies due to their existing privileged positions.

Their work highlights how greater inequality can lead to institutions that favor elites, potentially stifling competition and hindering growth over time. This stream of literature underscores the importance of understanding the relationship between inequality, institutions, and economic growth in various contexts.

Van Zanden's (2009, p.19) work adds to our understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by focusing on the skill premium and institutional quality in late medieval Europe. The paper suggests that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, played a crucial role in this period.

Van Zanden posits that in the late medieval period, the skill premium in western Europe fell compared to most other parts of the world economy. This fall is attributed to factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors (vanZanden_2009 (van Zanden 2009, p.1)). The paper further explains that institutions like guilds, which emerged in western Europe during this period, took care of apprentice contracts and ensured the fair execution of these agreements, thus contributing to the intergenerational transfer of skills (vanZanden_2009 (van Zanden 2009, p.19)).

The guilds have been criticized for monopolizing skills and attempting to maintain their relative pay above market-clearing levels.

European class cultures in colonies were strategically restructured to preserve social distinctions and thus bolster imperial control (Stoler 1989, p.6; Peters 2004, p.5). Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that the maintenance of colonial authority required the creation of a sense of community among Europeans, while simultaneously enforcing racial hierarchies between colonizers and indigenous peoples.

Peters (2004, p.5) supports this claim by highlighting the selective use of 'negotiability' in landholding practices in sub-Saharan Africa. Peters demonstrates that this perceived flexibility in land ownership and usage often concealed processes of exclusion and deepening social divisions, ultimately leading to class formation (Peters 2004, p.5). Furthermore, Peters shows how these practices benefited certain groups at the expense of others, reinforcing existing power structures within colonial societies.

The restructuring of European class cultures in colonies, as argued by Stoler and supported by Peters, had significant downstream implications. By maintaining social distinctions among Europeans, these restructurings helped to solidify imperial control over the colonized populations.

The slave trade in Africa may have fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, yet this cultural legacy does not necessarily lead to long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

This cultural shift, according to Nunn and Wantchekon, has deep roots. They demonstrate that the slave trade led to an increase in intergroup mistrust among Africans, a finding supported by other evidence suggesting that this mistrust is still prevalent today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This culture of mistrust does not inherently hinder economic growth.

Indeed, some scholars argue that trust is crucial for fostering long-run economic growth (Knack & Keefer 1997; Fafchamps 2006; Tabellini 2007; Algan & Cahuc 2010). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) acknowledge this, noting that their findings complement these studies documenting the importance of trust in various economic, political, and social contexts.

While the slave trade may have contributed to a culture of mistrust, it is crucial to consider other factors that can facilitate long-run economic growth. As previously discussed, institutions play a significant role in shaping economic growth (North 1989; Mokyr 2005; Ogilvie 2007; North & Weingast 1989; Kuznets 1973). Understanding the complex interplay between cultural legacies, such as the culture of mistrust caused by the slave trade, and institutional development is crucial for comprehending the long-term economic trajectories of African societies.

Women were assigned marginal lands for food farming, a situation worsened by men's central role in codifying customary law under colonial rule (Lawrance, Osborn, & Roberts, 2006).

Colonial courts based on common and civil law coexisted with native courts, allowing for the codification of patriarchal alliances and customary law that reinforced women's subordinate position (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

In light of these findings, it is evident that the dual legal system under colonial rule played a crucial role in shaping the economic contributions of African women. The codification of customary law served to restrict women's access to land and resources, thereby limiting their potential for significant economic growth and development. The influence of British rule on education and budget policies in Africa is a significant factor shaping long-run economic growth, as argued by Frankema (2012, p.2) and supported by Simson (2019, p.11). Frankema's study reveals that British traders and Protestant missionaries played a pivotal role in establishing formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa before 1940, under British rule.

Simson (2019, p.11) extends this discussion by examining post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. Contrary to common assumptions, Simson demonstrates that these countries' budgets were not excessively unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Instead, the paper reveals changes in the composition of employment and private returns to public employment, suggesting that high-level allocation of wage resources generally constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.19).

This stream of literature suggests that British rule, through its influence on education and budget policies, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. By establishing formal educational systems and fostering socially beneficial spending, these policies set the stage for future development and growth. The downstream implications of this historical legacy are complex and multifaceted, as evidenced by the ongoing challenges faced by African economies today. The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly contributed to agricultural development and long-run economic growth by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This shift, which occurred predominantly during the late 19th century, marked a critical juncture in the region's history.

Prior to this innovation, herd laws were a primary means of controlling grazing rights; however, these laws proved ineffective and were largely abandoned after 1884 (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The introduction of barbed wire, on the other hand, allowed for more precise boundary demarcation, thus reducing conflicts over land use and enhancing the security of property rights.

This enhanced security of property rights, in turn, facilitated increased agricultural development. Hornbeck (2010, p.8) presents evidence that counties adopting herd laws before the introduction of barbed wire experienced minimal improvements in their farmland from 1870 to 1880. After the adoption of barbed wire, these same counties experienced a substantial increase in improved land from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This shift underscores the critical role that barbed wire played in enabling farmers to develop their lands more effectively and, consequently, contribute to long-run economic growth.

The impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains serves as a compelling example of how technological innovations can shape institutional arrangements and, ultimately, economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing view that African economies were stagnant prior to European colonialism, arguing instead for a period of growth in some Sub-Saharan African economies before the early twentieth century. This reconsideration of historical African economic development is further supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), who reconstructed real wage levels and trends in nine British African colonies over their colonial rule period. Their findings reveal that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments.

This claim contradicts earlier economic histories of Africa, which assumed that any economic growth above subsistence resulted from European colonial interventions (Broadberry&Gardner_2022, p.10). The authors use a variety of sources, including data on trade, transportation infrastructure, and agricultural productivity, to support their argument.

They argue that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves unanswered questions about the persistence of poverty in Africa. To address this gap, they reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880-1965) (Frankema&vanWaijenburg_2012, p.2). The Kenyan case studied by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlights an important implication of their findings: the East-West gap is not only the result of a different type of economic dynamics (commercialization), but also the result of varying degrees of income inequality, especially between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans. This finding calls for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development, as future research in African economic history should concentrate more on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories (Frankema&vanWaijenburg_2012, p.28).

The paper by Austin (2008, p.2) posits that indigenous agency plays a significant role in shaping institutional choices, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

In particular, Austin contends that the decline in the power of indigenous groups does not necessarily imply a loss of influence over institutional choices. Colonial regimes often found it advantageous to support African economic enterprise rather than simply extract resources (Austin_2008 (Austin 2008, p.1)). This finding underscores the importance of considering the interests and agency of indigenous populations in understanding institutional evolution and long-term economic growth.

Moreover, Austin suggests that economic rent and economic growth are often joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. In this sense, Austin's work complements the findings of other scholars who have highlighted the role of institutions in shaping economic growth (e.g., North and Weingast_1989, Ogilvie_2007). The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was instrumental in driving the British industrial revolution, as argued by Humphries (2013, p.16). This claim challenges the traditional focus on high wages and male labor. Humphries (2013, p.16) presents evidence of marital instability and family breakdown during the eighteenth century, which led to a significant number of families with absent fathers or deceased breadwinners. This turbulence in male-breadwinner households contributed to a substantial group of women and children among pauper populations (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Moreover, King (2007) supports the notion that the industrial revolution was characterized by widespread poverty. Humphries's work sheds light on the demographic aspect of this poverty, emphasizing the role of female and child labor in the British industrialization process (Humphries 2013, p.2). The implication is that understanding the true nature of the British industrial revolution necessitates considering not only high wages but also the availability of cheap labor from women and children.

The Maddison Project, led by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), aims to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population in the world economy, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This collaborative effort seeks to provide a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

Scholars involved in the Maddison Project recognize that estimates of national accounts in the past, especially in distant periods, are subject to certain margins of error due to partial data and assumptions about the links between these data and economic activities they represent (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

This approach has been successful in inducing many scholars to work on these themes and quantify long-term economic development. One downstream implication of this strategy is the increased understanding of long-term economic growth, particularly in Asia, which was a focus area for the Maddison Project.

Goldin's (2006) research suggests a significant factor in the 'quiet revolution' of women's roles in the United States was the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century. This shift made "nice" jobs available, altering employment opportunities for women significantly (Goldin 2006, p.7). Goldin further argues that this change was accompanied by growth in educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3).

The increased demand for female office workers can be linked to the growing need for clerical labor in the burgeoning bureaucracies of the early 20th century. As corporations expanded and administrative tasks multiplied, the demand for women with typing, filing, and stenography skills surged (Goldin 2006, p.7). This increased demand was not met solely by newly educated women but also by those who had already entered the workforce as part of the growing industrial labor force (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The growth in educational institutions during this period played a crucial role in supplying the necessary skills for office work. The expansion of secondary education provided more women with the necessary literacy and numeracy skills to secure clerical positions (Goldin 2006, p.3). This trend was particularly pronounced among African American women, who benefited from increased access to education during this period (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The increased employment opportunities for women in office work had far-reaching implications. As more women entered the workforce, they gained financial independence and were able to contribute to their families' income, thereby reducing household dependency on male earnings (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This shift also led to a change in gender roles within families, as women began to assume more responsibilities traditionally associated with men (Goldin 2006, p.7).
