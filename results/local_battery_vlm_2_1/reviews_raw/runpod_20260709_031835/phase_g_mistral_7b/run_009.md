The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious yet crucial question in contemporary scholarship. At stake is understanding the historical trajectories that have led to vast disparities among nations' developmental paths and income levels. A significant disagreement exists between scholars regarding the primary drivers of these institutional differences, with some attributing it to European colonization strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and others arguing for the influence of African women's economic contributions in pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries.

These contrasting perspectives offer distinct yet complementary insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a profound influence on long-run economic growth, as suggested by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and supported by additional evidence from the same authors in 2002 (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.48). These scholars argue that the varying colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to different institutional structures, with some fostering rule of law and encouraging investment, while others established extractive states primarily aimed at transferring resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al., 2001, p.27).

These contrasting colonial strategies have had lasting consequences on contemporary income levels across countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the institutions and property rights established during colonization significantly impact current income levels, as supported by their findings using various measures of institutional quality (Acemoglu et al., 2001, p.19). Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor.

One specific mechanism through which these institutions have shaped long-run economic growth is by affecting political power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) explain that political power was crucial for elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, as it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.34). This exploitation of labor contributed to the accumulation of wealth and economic growth in these colonies, which has had lasting effects on their income levels today.

Another source within this stream, Nunn (2008, p.2), provides evidence that Africa's slave trades also had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa (Nunn, 2008, p.2). Sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had well-developed markets and trading networks that supported trade with Europeans (Nunn, 2008, p.19). The slave trades may have disrupted African societies, potentially hindering their long-term economic development.

Political and economic institutions, as argued by North (1989, p.9) and Mokyr (2005, p.54), play pivotal roles in shaping long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) posits that these institutions determine economic growth by specifying 'efficient' property rights and providing effective enforcement, which fosters a long-term capital market essential for growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) complements this perspective by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He suggests that intellectual societies and Enlightenment ideas, which focused on issues such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and efficient taxes, contributed to the industrial revolution. The Dutch Republic and Venice had early institutional foundations supporting this growth phenomenon, while England experienced a surge in industrial and even less revolutionary growth during the eighteenth century (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The interplay between institutions and useful knowledge is evident in the evolution of economic systems. For instance, the Enlightenment idea that individuals working for their own good contributes to societal welfare, unless they choose to engage in redistribution and rent-seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This notion supports North's (1989, p.9) argument that efficient institutions encourage economic growth by fostering a less distortionary environment conducive to individual and collective prosperity.

The paper by Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) collectively argue that indigenous agency plays a significant role in shaping core economic institutions, thereby challenging the notion that these institutions are solely products of European influence. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths, necessitating qualification and methodological modification to account for the complexities of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2).

Austin (2008, p.2) emphasizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that we cannot study one institution in isolation but must look at entire institutional frameworks. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who argues that the European family system, labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

A downstream implication of this interdependency is the need to consider the role of indigenous institutions in shaping economic growth. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England as an interdependent institution that may have furthered economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This system, which included labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, was unique to England and likely played a significant role in its economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) suggest that the emergence of formal institutions in western Europe during the late medieval period played a crucial role in shaping factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors.

The Maddison Project, as described by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), encourages the quantification of long-term economic development, a strategy that was highly successful in inducing many scholars to work on these themes.

By ensuring fair contracts and addressing issues in labor, land, credit, and family systems, guilds played a significant role in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills and maintaining market equilibrium (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The benefits of guilds might have been offset by their efforts to create local cartels and monopolize certain skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

This collaboration among scholars is crucial for presenting a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s.

Secure rights, as defined by explicit constitutions, parliaments, and independent judiciaries, have been identified as crucial for fostering long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that these institutions provide political protection for economic rights, which in turn encourages investment and economic development. They illustrate this mechanism through seventeenth-century England, where the establishment of a representative government led to the financial revolution, facilitating economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further support this argument by demonstrating how greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This path of institutional development may in turn have affected growth, as elite maintenance could hinder economic opportunities for the broader population (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12).

Additionally, the creation of secure rights encourages investment and fosters trust among individuals and institutions.

It is important to recognize that the presence of secure rights does not guarantee economic growth. Nevertheless, the establishment of secure rights provides a crucial foundation for long-term economic development by fostering trust, encouraging investment, and promoting economic exchange.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) study sheds light on an underappreciated factor in the agricultural development of the American Plains: barbed wire. By investigating the impact of this invention, Hornbeck demonstrates how effective land protection and property rights enforcement played a crucial role in fostering agricultural growth.

Barbed wire's adoption was near-universal across the American Plains following its introduction in the mid-19th century (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This widespread use enabled farmers to protect their land more effectively, reducing costs associated with livestock grazing and property disputes. Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides evidence for this claim by examining changes in improved farmland between herd-law counties (where barbed wire was adopted earlier) and non-herd law counties (where it was not). His findings reveal that after the introduction of barbed wire, herd-law counties experienced a significant increase in improved farmland, while there was no such increase before its adoption.

Moreover, Hornbeck (2010, p.8) argues that the enforcement of property rights was enhanced by the widespread use of barbed wire. He demonstrates this through an analysis of Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports, which showed a decline in herd law reporting after 1884, suggesting improved property rights enforcement due to the adoption of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This enhanced enforcement facilitated agricultural development by reducing uncertainty and fostering trust among farmers.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a significant factor in the British industrial revolution, as inventions were often designed to reduce the need for skilled adult workers. Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that this perspective provides a more accurate understanding of the revolution, shifting focus from men and high wages. The large numbers of women and children among pauper populations, noted by all poor law historians, were in part at least the bitter consequence of family breakdown and male abandonment (Humphries 2013, p.16).

This trend is consistent with contemporary perceptions that runaway husbands were becoming more common (Humphries 2013, p.16). Humphries's findings are supported by Taylor's account of poverty in working-class memoirs and Vincent's study on childhood (Humphries 2013, p.2). The presence of a significant group of families whose fathers were not present, alongside the perhaps 18% whose fathers had died, testifies to the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16).

This labor availability facilitated industrial development by enabling inventors to design machinery that required less skilled adult workers. For instance, spinning jennies and water frames, two key innovations of the British Industrial Revolution, were designed to replace hand-spinning and carding work traditionally done by women and children (Humphries 2013, p.16). This shift allowed for increased productivity in textile manufacturing, a sector that was a significant contributor to the industrial revolution's economic growth.

Moreover, the availability of cheap labor also contributed to the expansion of factories. Factory owners could afford to hire more workers at lower wages due to the abundance of available labor (Humphries 2013, p.16). This labor surplus enabled factories to grow in size and number, further fueling industrial development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) collectively suggest that the slave trade in Africa significantly shaped African culture, particularly by fostering a culture of mistrust. This mistrust could potentially hinder economic growth in the long run, although the papers do not explicitly argue that this is the primary cause.

They argue that this culture of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn & Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

This finding underscores the importance of understanding gender dynamics in examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The paper by Frankema (2012, p.2) and his collaborators suggest a significant role of British colonial rule in shaping the educational landscape of Sub-Saharan Africa, contributing to the rise of real wages above subsistence levels (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This finding challenges the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments.

These educational efforts led to an increase in literacy rates, which, in turn, resulted in a rise in real wages above subsistence levels (Frankema & van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

The impact of British rule on economic development varied across regions and time periods. For instance, in areas with a strong pre-colonial influence of Islam or high levels of native African resistance against colonial rule, educational development may have been slower (Frankema 2012, p.17).

The rise in real wages above subsistence levels in Sub-Saharan Africa is noteworthy because it challenges the prevailing view that many African economies were persistently impoverished due to structural growth impediments. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that many Sub-Saharan African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, comparable to the largest Asian economies until the 1980s (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

This finding underscores the importance of reexamining historical assumptions about African economic development. The rise in real wages above subsistence levels, facilitated by British colonial rule and education, suggests that there may have been more economic opportunities available in Africa than previously thought. This, in turn, could have significant implications for our understanding of long-run economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Peters (2004, p.5) and Simson (2019, p.11) suggest that the perception of static customary landholding in sub-Saharan Africa masks processes of exclusion and class formation. Simson (2019, p.11), on the other hand, contends that this perception can be advantageous for certain groups seeking to maintain power and control over resources.

Peters (2004, p.5) reveals how the colonial agricultural officers, in charge of schemes designed to raise productivity through modernized farming, expressed the most overt dissatisfaction with African landholding practices. These officers perceived them as 'communal' and inhibiting individual male investment in agriculture. Peters (2004, p.5) also notes that critics like Platteau, Amanor, Ribot, Carney, Farrington, and Woodhouse have demonstrated how national and local elites often capture the benefits of decentralization for 'local' and 'traditional' managers.

Simson argues that this perception can be beneficial for certain groups seeking to maintain power and control over resources, particularly in post-independence budget policies. For instance, Simson (2019, p.11) demonstrates how the growth in military spending under colonial rule was effectively subsidized by the metropolis, but after independence, domestic military spending began to grow significantly in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent. This shift in military spending can be seen as a strategy for maintaining power and control by certain groups.

Stoler (1989, p.6) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that the selective restructuring of European class cultures in colonial settings can be seen as a means to manage this critical aspect of society. Stoler (1989, p.6) posits that this transformation was instrumental in creating and maintaining the imperial control structure. The reorganization of European class cultures was not merely a justification for continued colonial rule and white supremacy; it served as a critical class-based logic, aimed at controlling both indigenous subversives and potentially dissenting European colonials (Stoler 1989, p.4).

Stoler's work highlights the internal political tensions among the British in India, which were largely overlooked in community studies of colonial life. Similarly, Kuznets argues that for economic growth, a country needs institutional and ideological adjustments, and this is particularly crucial for less developed countries.

This selective reorganization of European class cultures had downstream implications for the relations of production and power, as it helped maintain social distinctions and establish authority in the colonies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further notes that effective participation in the modern economic system necessitates rapid changes in various aspects of society, including urbanization and man's views on his role in society. Thus, the transformation of European class cultures in colonial settings could contribute to the broader societal changes necessary for modern economic growth.

The restructuring of European class cultures in colonial settings also had implications for the dynamics of power within the colonies. This finding aligns with Kuznets' (1973) assertion that modern economic growth requires rapid changes in the relations between labor force and total population. The selective restructuring of European class cultures in colonial settings can be seen as a means to manage this critical aspect of society, contributing to the broader process of modern economic growth.

Goldin (2006, p.7) suggests that the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century. Goldin's (2006) study reveals that this shift was not solely a result of women's rising educational attainment or changing household production technology, but rather an outcome of external factors.

This increased demand can be traced back to the growing need for administrative support as businesses and government agencies expanded their operations. The rise of the typing pool, for instance, provided a significant number of opportunities for women to enter the workforce (Goldin 2006, p.3).

This development granted more women access to education and provided them with the necessary skills for office work. As a result, the combination of increased demand and expanded supply led to a significant increase in women's employment in offices during the early 20th century.

Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also affected female labor supply (Goldin 2006, p.3). The introduction of labor-saving devices such as vacuum cleaners, washing machines, and refrigerators reduced the time women spent on domestic chores, making it easier for them to engage in paid work outside the home. This technological advancement further facilitated women's entry into the workforce during this period.

For example, the passing of the Fair Labor Standards Act in 1938 established a minimum wage for most workers but included exemptions for domestic service, retail, and restaurant work. These exemptions allowed married women to take up part-time jobs in these sectors without worrying about low wages.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of significant scholarly interest, with several streams of related scholarship emerging. One prominent stream argues that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective posits that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a lasting impact on current income levels across countries. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that political power was crucial to planters in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages.

Another stream of scholarship challenges this perspective by highlighting the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This viewpoint emphasizes that institutional changes facilitated women's entry into the workforce, contributing to increased involvement in the labor market. For instance, the passing of the Fair Labor Standards Act in 1938 allowed married women to take up part-time jobs in sectors such as retail and restaurant work without worrying about low wages (Goldin 2006, p.7).

While these two streams of scholarship agree on the role of institutions in economic growth, they disagree on the specific impact of European colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that colonialism led to the establishment of extractive institutions that facilitated the exploitation of native labor, contributing to current income disparities. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) propose that institutional changes facilitated women's entry into the workforce during this period, further promoting economic growth.

To settle this disagreement, it is essential to consider the historical evidence of labor exploitation in European colonies as presented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and compare it with the evidence of increased women's participation in the labor market as presented by Goldin (2006). This comparison would provide a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, accounting for both the exploitative practices and the facilitation of women's entry into the workforce.
