The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious yet central question in economic history, particularly within the context of former European colonies. This literature review delves into the myriad perspectives on this subject, highlighting the diverse factors that have been posited as instrumental in determining the trajectory of economic development across various regions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization significantly impact current income levels across countries. Their argument rests on the premise that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with some leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others resulted in extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), on the other hand, focus on the contributions made by African women to economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They contend that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that their subordinate status in customary law under colonial rule further limited their access to land (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15).

These two streams of scholarship offer contrasting perspectives on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

As we delve deeper into this topic, it becomes apparent that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires an examination of both the institutional structures established during colonization and the societal factors that influence their effectiveness. The following sections will explore these themes in more detail.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutional changes resulting from different European colonial strategies have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. This work builds upon North's (1989, p.9) assertion that political and economic institutions play a crucial role in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as primary determinants.

They posit that in countries like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to economic growth over time. In colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole, leading to long-term economic stagnation.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further elaborate on this argument by demonstrating that political power was crucial for elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages. This exploitative system, they argue, accounted for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) provide evidence supporting this claim by showing that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This path of institutional development may have affected growth, as where there was extreme inequality, and institutions favored elites and limited the access of much of the population to economic opportunities, members of elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time, but at the expense of overall economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that representative government fosters long-run economic growth by providing political protection for property rights.

The Glorious Revolution of 1688 established a representative government that safeguarded property rights by limiting the power of the monarchy and creating an accountable legislature.

Ogilvie argues that the European family system, community-based welfare systems, and guilds were interdependent with labour markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These institutional frameworks, he contends, may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization. Furthermore, Ogilvie highlights the role of cultural beliefs and values in shaping these institutions, with 'civic' cultures encouraging institutions that favour economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

This, in turn, fosters long-run economic growth by facilitating investment, attracting foreign capital, and encouraging the development of interdependent institutions that further economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) present compelling evidence suggesting economic growth in some Sub-Saharan African countries prior to the 20th century. This finding challenges the conventional wisdom and paints a more complex and dynamic history of Africa's economic development.

This claim contradicts early economic histories of Africa, which attributed any growth above subsistence to European colonial interventions (McPhee, 1926).

The authors further assert that most studies of African growth use data from the mid-20th century or later, revealing little improvement in per capita incomes during this period. New contributions to historical national accounting have offered insights into growth and development patterns over centuries in other regions (Broadberry, 2021).

Simultaneously, the field of African economic history has experienced a 'renaissance' based on newly digitized sources of data and innovative techniques (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016). This renaissance has allowed for a more nuanced understanding of Africa's pre-colonial economic growth.

One implication of this body of work is that the conventional view of African economies as stagnant prior to European colonialism may be overly simplistic. Instead, evidence suggests that some Sub-Saharan African countries experienced economic growth before the 20th century, challenging the notion that Africa's economic development was solely influenced by European interventions.

Understanding factors such as the availability of cheap labor was crucial in shaping the British industrial revolution. Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that a more accurate understanding of this period is achieved by considering this overlooked aspect, rather than focusing solely on men and high wages.

Humphries's paper supports the claim that the industrial revolution was characterized by marital instability and family breakdown, leading to a significant number of families with absent fathers (Humphries 2013, p.16). This finding is consistent with other historians' depictions of the eighteenth century as an era of turbulence, strained by war, empire building, and labor mobility (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Invention designs during this period often aimed to minimize the reliance on skilled adult workers, reflecting the availability of cheaper alternatives in the form of women and children. Humphries's study provides evidence for this claim through examples such as the power loom, which was designed to replace skilled weavers with unskilled laborers (Humphries 2013, p.2).

The implications of this finding extend beyond the British industrial revolution. As other scholars have noted, the availability of labor can significantly influence technological innovation and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). By understanding the role of female and child labor in the British industrial revolution, we gain a more nuanced perspective on the factors that drive long-run economic growth.

The presence of a significant group of families whose fathers were absent or separated from their wives and children testifies to the turbulence of the times during the British industrial revolution. This finding challenges the conventional view of this period as one dominated by stable, male-breadwinner households. Instead, it suggests that economic growth can occur under conditions of marital instability and family breakdown, as long as there is an ample supply of cheap labor available to replace skilled workers.

This insight offers a new perspective on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. In particular, it highlights the importance of social and cultural factors that influence the availability and productivity of labor.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) argue that technological advancement, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data are key factors for economic growth. According to Kuznets (1973, p.1), modern technology is incompatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, all of which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world. Thus, economic growth necessitates rapid changes in society, including urbanization and alterations in man's views on his role and the measure of his achievement (Kuznets_1973). Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a notion supported by the emergence of scientific societies and intellectual discourse focusing on political economy.

In the late medieval period, the skill premium in western Europe fell significantly compared to most other parts of the world economy due to factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors (van Zanden_2009). The paper argues that in this era, institutions such as guilds emerged to address issues related to the transfer of skills and apprenticeship contracts. These institutions have been criticized for monopolizing skills and maintaining relative pay above market equilibrium levels (vanZanden_2009).

The implication of this stream is that while institutions play a role in shaping economic growth, their impact may be counterbalanced by factors such as the monopolization of skills and attempts to form local cartels. Thus, while institutions are essential, they must also support and facilitate the dissemination of knowledge for sustainable economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade in Africa may have had a lasting impact on current economic development.

Nunn (2008, p.2) adds to this understanding by demonstrating that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa. One specific mechanism through which this occurred is the disruption and weakening of local societies as a result of the slave trade, particularly those with centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This disruption undermined the economic foundations upon which trust could be built, potentially contributing to the persistence of mistrust in these communities.

In addition, the slave trade may have also influenced the formation of political institutions in Africa. As Nunn (2008, p.2) notes, Vansina (1990) argues that African societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade before the arrival of the Portuguese. The slave trade, by providing an alternative means of economic exchange, may have slowed the development of more complex political institutions that could support sustained trade and foster trust among communities.

The role of British influence in shaping formal education in sub-Saharan Africa during the colonial period is a significant aspect to consider when examining the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British traders and Protestant missionaries played a crucial part in establishing formal education, contrary to the belief that it was primarily driven by direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

Frankema's work builds upon Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014) study, which highlights the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. In particular, the authors note that the de-privileging of women in access to land under colonial rule was reinforced by the codification of customary law, a key avenue for solidifying women's subordinate status during this time (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.20).

By promoting formal education, these agents may have contributed to increased literacy rates among African men, potentially altering power dynamics within communities and impacting the access and opportunities available to women.

As such, the establishment of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa by British traders and missionaries during the colonial period can be seen as a critical factor shaping long-run economic growth, particularly through its influence on gender dynamics and health outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) report, “Women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming.”

Hornbeck (2010, p.8), Hornbeck (2010, p.32) argues that the adoption of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement. This mechanism is a downstream implication of the institutional changes wrought by the European colonization of Africa, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002).

In pre-barbed wire America, open land was vulnerable to encroachment and overgrazing due to the lack of effective boundary markers. This led to disputes and conflicts among farmers, hampering agricultural growth.

Moreover, the widespread adoption of barbed wire facilitated the enforcement of herd laws, which were enacted in some states to prevent overgrazing by large cattle herds. Before barbed wire, these laws were often difficult to enforce due to the difficulty of marking and protecting open lands.

This development in the American Plains can be seen as a downstream consequence of the institutional changes brought about by European colonization in Africa. Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutions established during the colonial period, such as property rights regimes, have long-lasting effects on economic growth. In this context, the adoption of barbed wire can be seen as an institutional innovation that facilitated the enforcement of property rights and contributed to agricultural development in the American Plains.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that policy interventions can significantly impact institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa. This claim is shared by Englebert (cited in Hopkins 2009), who finds that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily result in poor-quality public goods provided by African governments. Instead, societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional or heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

This assertion echoes Alesina et al.'s (2003) findings on fractionalization's detrimental effects on Africa's economic record due to poor institutions.

It is important to acknowledge that while European presence may have established unfavorable institutions in certain regions, African societies have demonstrated resilience and adaptability over time. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the role of African women in economic growth and development, which could potentially counteract the negative impacts of extractive institutions.

The complex historical factors shaping African institutions and their long-term impact on economic growth must be considered. A nuanced understanding of these factors can help inform effective policy interventions that promote sustainable development in Africa.

Englebert's findings (cited in Hopkins 2009) underscore the importance of examining ethnic heterogeneity beyond its potential to cause conflict and instability, as some societies may still be capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This nuanced approach can inform policy decisions that foster institutional reforms tailored to specific contexts, potentially leading to more effective poverty reduction strategies in Africa.

Goldin (2006, p.7) argues that exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers primarily drove the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century. Goldin's research reveals that this shift was not solely due to internal factors but also external forces, such as the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s and changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s (Goldin 2006, p.3).

The increased availability of such jobs led to a significant rise in female labor force participation. For instance, between 1900 and 2000, the proportion of women aged 16–64 in the US labor force increased from about 22% to over 58%, with most of this increase occurring after World War II (Goldin 2006, p.7).

Moreover, Goldin's research highlights that these changes also had profound implications for women's educational attainment. As the demand for female office workers grew, so did the need for educated women. This led to an expansion of secondary education for girls and ultimately paved the way for greater opportunities in higher education (Goldin 2006, p.3).

The Maddison Project, spearheaded by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), represents an ongoing collaboration among scholars aiming to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, with a particular focus on the early modern period and Asia.

By revising Maddison's estimates, the Maddison Project aims to provide a more detailed understanding of long-term economic growth. Bolt and van Zanden explain that this project was inspired by Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even if a solid basis did not always exist, with the expectation that scholars would challenge and improve upon his work (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

One specific mechanism that the Maddison Project addresses is the estimation of margins of error in economic data. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that these errors can be substantial, especially for historical periods or regions with limited data availability (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). To address this issue, members of the Maddison Project have experimented with various methods to estimate these margins of error, with the Hitotshibashi team focusing on Asian national accounts (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

This work underscores the ongoing efforts to improve the accuracy and objectivity of historical economic data, which is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth and its institutional determinants.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization have had a significant impact on current income levels across countries, with extractive strategies leading to poor institutions and low economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) emphasize the economic contributions of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting how customary law relegated women to marginal lands and codified their subordinate status.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) findings are supported by Hopkins' (2009) (Hopkins 2009, p.10) assertion that European encroachment in regions with high population and low mortality rates led to the development of institutions poorly suited for long-term growth. Hopkins also suggests that societies could be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional or ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) provide evidence supporting Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argument, demonstrating that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Yet, they also show that this internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel affecting trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The disagreement between Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011) (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) findings can be settled by examining the specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that demonstrates the impact of European colonization on institutions and economic growth. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.17) provides evidence showing how British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. This example illustrates how European colonization could have both positive (education) and negative (extractive institutions) effects on long-run economic growth, as suggested by Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) findings and the evidence provided by Frankema (2012, p.17).
