The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in contemporary development economics, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic prosperity across nations. At stake is an understanding of the historical trajectories that have led to marked disparities in income levels among former European colonies, and the policies that can foster sustainable growth in economically disadvantaged regions today.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. Their argument rests on the premise that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with varying associated institutions. In some cases, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Conversely, in other regions, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole.

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) challenge this narrative by highlighting the significant contributions made by African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods. However, these contributions have often been overlooked in discussions about the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that understanding the historical trajectories of African economies can provide insights into policies affecting institutional reforms and contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the relationship between ethnic diversity and economic growth is more complex than previously thought.

Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests a need for qualification and methodological modification to account for the nuances of institutional evolution across time and space (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective calls for a more sophisticated understanding of the interplay between institutions, historical contexts, and economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their findings suggest that both internal norms of mistrust and external factors, such as the rule of law, contribute to this phenomenon (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This research underscores the importance of trust in fostering economic development and political institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further contend that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They emphasize that political power was crucial to the elites in the colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, as it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages.

The paper by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30), along with Ogilvie (2007, p.27), collectively suggest that secure rights and political and economic institutions play a crucial role in fostering long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the English financial revolution and other economies.

They emphasize the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, focusing on the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) provides additional evidence supporting this claim by demonstrating that interdependent institutions within a wider framework contribute to early industrialization and capitalism. For instance, the European family system, according to Laslett, was interdependent with labour markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Cross-country comparisons also suggest that favorable 'contracting' institutions may be partially interdependent with favorable 'property rights' institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Sokoloff and Engerman's paper (Sokoloff&Engerman_2000) and other scholarship within the stream of institutions and inequality in New World societies suggest that greater societal inequality fostered institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating long-term economic disparities. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that in societies with extreme inequality, elite-advantaging institutions emerged, limiting the broader population's access to economic opportunities (Sokoloff&Engerman_2000, p.12). In these unequal societies, elites were better positioned to maintain their status over time due to such restrictive institutions.

The paper argues that this path of institutional development may have impacted growth negatively, as it prevented widespread economic mobility and competition (Sokoloff&Engerman_2000, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2000), another source within the same stream, provide evidence supporting this claim by examining the link between inequality and institutions in the context of European settlements in the New World. They find that European settlers established institutions that perpetuated their own power and wealth at the expense of indigenous populations, leading to long-term economic disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2000).

These findings underscore the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by revealing how extreme inequality can lead to the establishment of institutions that advantage elites and restrict competition, ultimately contributing to the persistence of economic disparities over time. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) report, “These differentials in paths of development have long been of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have recentiy attracted more attention from economic historians and economists more generally (North, 1988; Engerman and Sokoloff, 1997; Coatsworth 1993, 1998; Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, 2000; Engerman, Haber and Sokoloff, 2000).”

Mokyr's work substantiates the claim that the growth of useful knowledge, particularly in scientific societies, was a key driver of long-run economic growth. In his analysis, Mokyr highlights the role of intellectual discourse on political economy issues such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and more efficient, less distortionary taxes (Mokyr 2005, p.54). He argues that these societies fostered a culture of innovation, which contributed to the Industrial Revolution and subsequent economic growth.

Moreover, Mokyr underscores the importance of law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts in facilitating technological change (Mokyr 2005, p.3). He suggests that institutional structures supporting this progress were not always sustainable, but industrial and even less revolutionary growth occurred in other societies as well. For instance, he points out that although the Industrial Revolution was largely British, it was the tip of the iceberg, with economic growth happening elsewhere, albeit at a slower pace (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

Van Zanden's (2009, p.19) investigation into the skill premium during the late medieval period reveals that the quality of institutions in Western Europe significantly shaped this economic factor, thereby influencing long-run growth. The guilds, an essential institutional structure in Western Europe at the time, played a crucial role in safeguarding apprentice contracts and fostering intergenerational skill transfer (van Zanden 2009, p.19). However, these institutions have been criticized for their attempts to monopolize skills and maintain above-market clearing wages (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

The explanation for the global patterns in skill premiums, as proposed by van Zanden, is multifaceted, encompassing factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors (van Zanden 2009, p.1). Furthermore, the skill premium, as measured in this study, serves as a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.1). Thus, the development and enforcement of effective institutions during the late medieval period contributed to the formation of a favorable skill premium environment that ultimately influenced long-run economic growth in Western Europe.

The interconnectedness of these factors highlights the complex interplay between institutional development, skill premiums, and long-run economic growth. As we delve deeper into the literature, it becomes apparent that institutions play a multifaceted role in shaping economic growth, with their influence extending beyond the realm of skill premiums alone.

The impact of European colonization on current income levels is significantly shaped by the institutional changes that resulted from varying colonial strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrate that these institutional differences have had a lasting impact on economic growth. They show that the positive relationship between institutions and income levels holds across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The extractive nature of European colonization, which forced large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages, played a crucial role in this transformation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

The impact of European colonization on economic development is not limited to the establishment and evolution of institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa. He contends that the slave trade disrupted local economies, weakened institutions, and led to long-term underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2). Sustained trade with Europe did not occur until African societies with centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed markets and trading networks emerged (Nunn 2008, p.19).

This claim complements the findings of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who demonstrate that the impact of European colonization on economic development in Africa was significantly influenced by the presence or absence of institutions favorable to growth, such as property rights and political centralization.

This finding aligns with Knack and Keefer's (1997) research, which shows that interpersonal trust is essential for economic development.

Moreover, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) posit that the internal culture of mistrust fostered by the slave trade has persisted over time, influencing contemporary levels of trust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This long-term impact on trust could hinder economic growth, as trust is crucial for cooperation, investment, and innovation, all key drivers of economic development.

In this light, Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.48) findings that European colonization had a significant impact on the establishment and evolution of institutions in Africa take on added significance. The slave trade, by undermining trust and social cohesion, may have created conditions less conducive to the development of favorable institutions for long-term economic growth.

As Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) emphasize, understanding the origins of contemporary African institutions is essential for addressing persistent underdevelopment in the region. Their research suggests that the slave trade played a crucial role in shaping these institutions by fostering a culture of mistrust that has persisted over time. This insight underscores the importance of considering the historical context when examining the determinants of economic growth and development.

For economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data are crucial factors (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets's argument is supported by evidence that modern economic growth requires a departure from traditional institutions and social views that are incompatible with advanced technology.

Modern technology demands changes in the labor force, leading to rapid shifts in location, structure, and occupational status groups (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This transformation necessitates a shift away from family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery, which were prevalent in earlier times over much of even the developed world (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

The need for institutional adjustments is further underscored by the fact that modern technology is incompatible with the rural mode of life and large extended family patterns. As a result, effective participation in the modern economic system requires changes in family formation, urbanization, and societal views (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

In addition to institutional adjustments, Kuznets emphasizes the importance of ideological shifts. The dynamic drives of modern economic growth necessitate a reaching out geographically and continuous expansion to less developed areas (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This sequential spread of economic growth requires major changes in transport and communication, facilitating the expansion into less developed regions.

Furthermore, Kuznets argues that for modern economic growth, a wider supply of tested data is essential. This need for more data reflects the complexity and interconnectedness of modern economies, which require detailed information to understand and manage effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly boosted agricultural development, as argued by Hornbeck (2010, p.8). This innovation reduced costs associated with land protection and facilitated property rights enforcement, leading to increased improvements in farmland. Evidence from Hornbeck's study reveals that herd-law counties experienced a substantial increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire.

However, following its introduction, these counties experienced a significant 20 percentage point increase from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This suggests that barbed wire played a crucial role in facilitating agricultural development by enhancing land protection and property rights enforcement.

Moreover, Hornbeck's paper reveals an interesting pattern concerning the relationship between herd laws and woodland. Counties with fewer woodlands were more likely to adopt herd laws, but after the introduction of barbed wire, these counties did not show a greater increase in improved farmland compared to counties with more woodlands (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This finding highlights the importance of barbed wire as a tool for agricultural development, regardless of local conditions.

The papers by Humphries (2013, p.16) and Goldin (2006, p.7) collectively assert that the British industrial revolution and the quiet revolution of women's employment, education, and family roles in the United States were shaped significantly by institutional factors facilitating the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor.

The large numbers of absent or deceased fathers testify to the turbulence of the times, with factors such as war, empire building, and labor mobility straining men's links to wives and children (Humphries 2013, p.16). Goldin (2006, p.3) supports this claim by arguing that shifts from one phase to the next in women's employment were due to various exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers, growth of educational institutions, and changes in household production technology.

One specific mechanism these sources jointly establish is the role of institutional factors in shaping inventions.

A downstream implication of this mechanism is that the increased availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor led to significant economic growth.

This stream of literature underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the availability of labor, particularly women and children, through technological advancements and societal norms.

The Maddison Project's emphasis on collaboration among scholars to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population highlights the importance of data accuracy in understanding long-run economic growth (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that these estimates, particularly for the early modern period and Asia, are subject to significant margins of error due to partial data and assumptions. However, they contend that continued collaboration among scholars can help refine these estimates and provide a more detailed understanding of long-term economic growth.

This refinement can lead to a more accurate portrayal of economic growth over time, particularly in regions such as Asia that have been historically underrepresented in these estimates.

Moreover, the collaborative efforts within the Maddison Project have implications for our understanding of institutional development and its role in shaping long-run economic growth. By refining historical GDP and population data, scholars can better examine the impact of institutions on economic growth over time. For instance, they can investigate how institutions evolved in response to changes in the economy or how certain institutions may have hindered or facilitated economic growth.

The influence of British rule on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa is a significant aspect of this region's educational development, as argued by Frankema (2012, p.2). This impact stemmed primarily from the presence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

Frankema's study reveals that the influence of these actors was not merely through the establishment of formal educational institutions but also by shaping the demand for education among Africans. The paper identifies centres of peasant cultivation of major export crops and sizeable mining industries as areas where British rule facilitated educational development (Frankema 2012, p.17).

The presence of British traders brought about a need for literacy skills to manage transactions and contracts, thereby increasing the demand for formal education.

However, it is crucial to note that the impact of British rule on educational development was not uniform across all regions.

In addition, the study by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) provides insights into how the slave trade influenced the cultural practices of Africa, which in turn affected educational development. They found that regions with a higher prevalence of the slave trade had lower levels of education in the late 20th century, suggesting a long-term impact on institutional development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

Therefore, it can be argued that the influence of British rule on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was complex and varied across different regions. While British traders and Protestant missionaries played a significant role in facilitating educational development, other factors such as pre-colonial state centralization, native African resistance, and cultural practices shaped by the slave trade also had an impact.

The interplay between these various factors underscores the importance of understanding the historical context when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing assumption that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa. Instead, they argue that many African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.10). This contention is supported by their observation that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.1).

This finding complicates the argument put forth by authors such as McPhee (1926), who claimed that economic growth in West Africa was due to European colonial interventions like railways and malaria prophylaxis (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.10). By suggesting that African economies had already surpassed subsistence levels before the onset of intensive colonialism, Broadberry and Gardner's work underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history.

Moreover, this discovery resonates with Austin's (2008) 'Reversal of Fortune' thesis, which argues that pre-colonial African economies were more advanced than previously thought and that colonialism had a detrimental impact on their growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

In the realm of Sub-Saharan Africa's economic growth, Peters (2004, p.5) contends that customary landholding institutions can mask detrimental processes of exclusion and social division.

Peters (2004, p.5) highlights that during colonial times, agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement schemes often overlooked social inequality within many customary systems and encroachment by some groups for livelihood or commercial benefits (Peters 2004, p.5). This observation aligns with Amanor's (2001) critique of both the 'evolutionary school of land rights' and 'the communitarian approach', which he argues fail to adequately address social differentiation in relations to land (Peters 2004, p.9).

Peters's (2004) work resonates with Platteau's (1996, 2000) critique of unproblematic visions that ignore social inequality in many customary systems. The paper demonstrates how national and local elites often capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers, further exacerbating these issues (Peters 2004, p.9). Thus, understanding the complexities surrounding landholding institutions is crucial for comprehending their impact on economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa.

In European class cultures, the restructuring of social hierarchies to maintain imperial control led to an expansion of cultural infrastructure within colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6). Stoler's work (1989) argues that this process was not merely about subjugating indigenous populations but also about preserving and reinforcing the internal power dynamics among European colonizers.

One specific example of this mechanism is the creation of racial hierarchies that served multiple purposes: justifying continued European rule, enforcing white supremacy, and maintaining social distinctions within colonial societies. This strategy not only targeted indigenous subversives but also directed at dissenting European underlings in the colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6). Furthermore, this class-based logic extended to the apparatus that kept potentially subversive white colonials in line (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Moreover, the expansion of cultural infrastructure within colonies was not limited to racial hierarchies. Stoler (1989, p.6) also points out that there is a lack of scholarly attention given to the internal political tensions among the British themselves in their colonial endeavors, as well as the everyday practice of colonial domination. However, studies by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979) have begun to address this gap by examining the distinct class interests within the colonial state.

Thus, European class cultures were not only restructured to create and maintain imperial control but also to preserve internal power dynamics among colonizers. This process led to the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies, including the creation of racial hierarchies and a lack of attention to internal political tensions among colonizers. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for comprehending the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in colonial settings.

These findings suggest that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda may have been more nuanced than commonly assumed.
