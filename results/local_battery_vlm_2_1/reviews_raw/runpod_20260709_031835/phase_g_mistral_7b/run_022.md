The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of ongoing debate among economists, as they grapple with understanding the factors that have propelled some nations into prosperity while others remain mired in poverty. This review explores the various perspectives on this question, highlighting the significant contributions made by scholars such as Acemoglu et al. Akyeampong and Fofack, Nunn and Wantchekon, Frankema, Ogilvie, North, and others.

At the heart of the debate lies the tension between neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary determinants of economic growth, and those that stress the importance of political and economic institutions in this process. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries, while North (1989, p.9) contends that these institutions play a crucial role in determining economic growth.

One group of scholars, including Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), focus on the antecedents that flow into the topic's causal variable as a trigger. Akyeampong and Fofack examine the contributions made by African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, while Nunn and Wantchekon investigate how the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. These streams of research support and enrich the topic's claim by offering valuable insights into the factors influencing institutions and their impact on economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.2) offers a distinct perspective by proposing that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. This stream studies a downstream consequence of the topic's causal variable, as it demonstrates how institutions established during colonial rule can have long-lasting effects on economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) develops the topic's claim using the same causal variable under different phrasing or as a more specific instance. He argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, offering insights into how institutions can evolve and adapt over time to address the needs of society.

Finally, three sources propose rival causes that do not operate through the topic's variable and offer genuinely competing explanations.

The impact of European colonization on current income levels is a significant area of inquiry within the literature on institutions and economic growth. A shared finding among [Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27); Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48)] and [Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27)] is that institutional changes resulting from European colonization have a substantial influence on income levels across countries.

[Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27)] argue that Europeans adopted different colonial strategies, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, resulting in higher income levels today. Conversely, in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, which has contributed to lower current income levels.

In 1500, relatively rich societies were often found among these colonies; however, today they are typically poorer than their counterparts. The authors attribute this shift to the impact of European institutions on economic growth and industrialization.

[Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27)] offer a complementary perspective by focusing on the slave trade in Africa and its long-term effects on intergroup trust. They find that both an individual's external environment, such as the rule of law, and internal norms of mistrust play a role in shaping current income levels. They argue that the internal channel is more significant than the external one at least twice over.

This body of work suggests that the institutional changes brought about by European colonization have had profound and lasting effects on income levels across countries. The mechanisms through which these changes occurred varied, from the establishment of extractive states to the impact of the slave trade on intergroup trust, but the overall effect was a significant influence on current income disparities.

The development of representative government institutions is posited as a crucial catalyst for economic growth, particularly evident in seventeenth-century England, according to North and Weingast (1989, p.30). This claim is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who highlights the interdependency of various institutions within pre-industrial Europe's institutional framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Ogilvie (2007, p.27), on the other hand, contends that the European family system, labour markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Moreover, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize the importance of a regime or sovereign's commitment to and binding by these rules, which underpins economic growth and the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie also highlights the role of cultural beliefs and values in shaping institutional manifestations that encourage economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Putnam's theory of 'social capital', which refers to trust, norms, and networks that facilitate coordinated actions and encourage institutions favoring economic growth and political accountability, is grounded in cultural beliefs and values (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

In this context, the development of representative government institutions can be seen as a critical step towards establishing secure rights, fostering economic growth, and promoting institutional frameworks that encourage coordinated actions and accountable governance. The enduring impact of these institutions on contemporary economic dynamics is a compelling area for further exploration, particularly in understanding policy implications for modern states.

The development of efficient financial institutions, as exemplified by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly contributed to reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North's work highlights that this English financial revolution was a result of increased security of the time dimension of property rights, crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The creation of the Bank of England led to a decline in the cost of transacting, an essential aspect that North argues is critical in understanding the history of property rights and their impact on economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman's research supports this notion by demonstrating that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. The development of efficient financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, may have been a response to this unequal distribution of power and resources, allowing elites to maintain their status while promoting economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the conventional view that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa. Their research reveals that many African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding supported by McPhee's (1926) assertion that economic growth in West Africa was primarily due to factors such as railways and malaria prophylaxis.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or later, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century. In contrast, their research employs newly digitized sources and innovative techniques to rewrite the history of economic divergence and convergence since before the industrial revolution ( Broadberry 2021 ). This shift in focus allows for a more nuanced understanding of African growth patterns over centuries, challenging the assumption that European colonial interventions were necessary for any economic growth above subsistence.

The paper's findings have significant implications for our understanding of institutional development and long-run economic growth in Africa.

Furthermore, understanding the development of efficient financial institutions provides a compelling example of how historical institutional changes continue to shape contemporary economic dynamics. The establishment of the Bank of England, for instance, played a significant role in promoting long-run economic growth and fostering accountable governance (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) research sheds light on an underappreciated factor contributing to agricultural development in the American Plains: the near-universal adoption of barbed wire. This innovation significantly reduced costs associated with land protection and facilitated property rights enforcement, thereby spurring agricultural growth.

Hornbeck's study reveals that after 1880, Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports ceased to detail herd laws, suggesting their diminishing relevance following the widespread adoption of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). Furthermore, by comparing herd-law and non-herd law counties, Hornbeck found that after the introduction of barbed wire, herd-law counties experienced a marked increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

This agricultural expansion set the stage for subsequent economic growth. As Hornbeck's work demonstrates, the adoption of barbed wire played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic dynamics on the American Plains by fostering efficient land use and enabling the development of secure property rights. This finding complements the broader literature that underscores the importance of institutions in promoting economic growth (North_1989, Sokoloff&Engerman_2000).

This insight offers valuable insights for examining historical institutional changes in other regions, including Sub-Saharan Africa, where the evolution of indigenous institutions may have played a significant role in shaping contemporary economic dynamics.

The British industrial revolution's traditional narrative, which centers on skilled male labor and high wages, is challenged by Humphries (2013, p.16), who advocates for a revised perspective that emphasizes the role of unskilled female and child labor. This shift, according to Humphries, was facilitated by inventions designed to reduce the need for adult trained operatives.

Humphries (2013, p.16) presents evidence suggesting that marital instability and family breakdown were common during the eighteenth century, with a significant number of families experiencing separation or male abandonment. This finding is consistent with other historians' depictions of the period as one of economic, social, and political upheaval. The paper argues that a more accurate understanding of the British industrial revolution is achieved by considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor, rather than focusing on men and high wages.

The presence of a significant group of families whose fathers, while yet-living, were not present, alongside the perhaps 18% whose fathers had died, testifies to the turbulence of the times (Humphries 2013, p.16). This turbulence, in turn, created an environment conducive to the adoption of inventions that could be operated by unskilled labor, thereby facilitating the industrial revolution.

This finding suggests that the shift towards unskilled labor was not an isolated phenomenon but rather a response to broader societal changes.

In late medieval Europe, the quality of institutions, particularly the guilds, played a pivotal role in shaping the skill premium by ensuring fair apprentice contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19). According to van Zanden's paper, the emergence of formal institutions such as the guilds addressed issues related to the fair execution of apprentice contracts that were prevalent during this period.

The guilds in western Europe, which had been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into their trade, actually benefited the intergenerational transfer of skills despite their attempts to form local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This finding suggests that while institutions may have some negative consequences, they can also contribute positively to economic development by fostering skill transfer and ensuring fair contracts.

Moreover, van Zanden's study supports the broader argument that institutional quality plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (North 1989; North & Weingast 1989). The guilds were able to facilitate the transfer of skills and maintain fair contracts because they had emerged as formal institutions that addressed specific market failures. This mechanism underscores the importance of institutional quality in promoting economic development, even when institutions may have negative consequences as well.

Furthermore, the paper's focus on factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors aligns with other scholarship on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007). The skill premium measured in van Zanden's study is also a predictor of long-term economic performance, which further supports the broader argument that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr and others have argued that the growth of useful knowledge, particularly during the Industrial Enlightenment, was a key driver of long-run economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the importance of practical incentives, fact-finding, and experimentation in this process (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr contends that the proliferation of scientific societies in Europe during this period, such as the British Royal Society and the French Académie des Sciences, played a crucial role in fostering innovation and technological advancement.

The growth of useful knowledge was not solely an intellectual pursuit; it also had practical implications for economic development. For instance, the Enlightenment ideas about individual self-interest contributing to societal welfare led to discussions on free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxation (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Moreover, Mokyr suggests that the Industrial Revolution's institutions, such as law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts, facilitated more efficient allocations of resources during this period (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

However, it is essential to recognize that not all nations experienced similar growth rates during the Industrial Revolution. Mokyr acknowledges that industrial and even less revolutionary changes were not wholly British, but rather a phenomenon with varied manifestations across Europe (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This observation underscores the complex interplay between institutions, knowledge growth, and long-run economic development, a theme that will be further explored in the subsequent sections.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that for less developed countries to partake in the modern economy effectively, they require institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This claim is supported by scholars focusing on the structural changes necessary for economic growth in these nations.

Kuznets contends that modern technology, including steam and electric power, necessitates institutions accommodating large-scale plants (Kuznets 1973, p.1). For instance, family enterprises, illiteracy, and slavery are incompatible with the technology-driven economic growth seen in developed countries. Thus, less developed countries need to replace these outdated institutions with more suitable ones.

Moreover, Kuznets emphasizes that modern technology is not compatible with rural modes of life or extended family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). As a result, labor force location and structure must undergo rapid changes for effective participation in the modern economic system. This shift also necessitates changes in societal views, including attitudes towards urbanization and man's role in society.

These changes are not limited to the labor force and technology; they extend to other aspects of society as well (Kuznets 1973, p.4). For example, the dynamic drives of modern economic growth have led to geographical expansion in developed countries and will likely do the same in less developed areas. This sequential spread of growth is facilitated by improvements in transport and communication.

Furthermore, Akyeampong and Fofack (2015) examine how institutional changes can facilitate the adoption of modern technology in African countries . They find that effective institutions, those that promote economic freedom, rule of law, and property rights, are crucial for technological progress in these nations. This is because such institutions reduce uncertainty and transaction costs, thereby encouraging investment in new technologies.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly through the influence of traders and missionaries, is argued to have played a significant role in the establishment of formal education systems (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This impact is evident in the colonial period before 1940, as British traders and Protestant missionaries promoted education as a means to cultivate loyalty among the African population and prepare them for employment in administrative roles (Frankema 2012, p.2).

The establishment of formal education systems under British rule was not solely the result of direct investment by colonial governments. Instead, it was facilitated by the influence of traders and missionaries who saw educational development as a strategic tool to further their interests (Frankema 2012, p.17). For instance, in areas with significant peasant cultivation of export crops or a sizeable mining industry, the demand for educated labor increased, thereby driving the expansion of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.17).

The impact of British rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa has downstream implications for economic growth. As Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) argue, African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The higher educational attainment levels facilitated by British rule may have contributed to this trend by increasing productivity and labor market flexibility.

However, it is essential to acknowledge that the impact of British rule on education was not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa. For instance, the East-West gap in long-run trajectories of wage income growth suggests varying degrees of income inequality between different regions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Future research should focus on charting and explaining these differences to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the role of British rule in shaping economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

The increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century set off a chain reaction that led to significant changes in women's roles in the United States, marking a 'quiet revolution' (Goldin 2006, p.7). Goldin (2006, p.7) demonstrates that this shift was primarily driven by various exogenous changes, such as increased relative demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions at the secondary level.

For instance, Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, who graduated near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class, could not find a law firm willing to hire her as a lawyer but landed a position as a legal secretary. Similarly, Rona Jaffe's best-selling novel and movie, The Best of Everything (1958), portray a main character graduating from Radcliffe in the 1950s assuming a secretarial position with hopes of becoming an editor (Goldin 2006, p.3).

Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women (Goldin 2006, p.3).

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically restructured to preserve social distinctions, leading to an expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6). This restructuring served multiple purposes: it fostered a sense of colonial community, provided context for colonial authority, and shaped relations of production and power. Stoler (1989, p.6) further argues that this class-based logic extended to controlling potentially subversive European colonials, as well as regulating interactions with the indigenous population.

In support of this claim, Stoler (1989, p.6) points out that while numerous studies focus on social rankings and political priorities within colonial life, they often overlook internal tensions among the Europeans themselves. For instance, community studies of the British in India concentrate on class distinctions among the colonized but neglect the political disagreements among the British (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Moreover, Stoler (1989, p.6) highlights that the everyday practice of colonial domination is rarely analyzed in existing accounts of British and French interaction with the colonized. In this regard, studies by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979) offer valuable insights into the internal political tensions within the colonial state.

Post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson's analysis reveals that while these countries did experience high debt burdens and growth in military spending, the allocation of wage resources generally constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage.

For instance, during the early 1990s, subsidies and interest peaked at 30% of spending in Kenya, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). Similarly, Tanzania experienced a high debt burden with interest payments reaching 18% of expenditure. However, Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to less access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief compared to its neighbors (Simson 2019, p.11).

Moreover, the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly beyond the capital cities, and jobs increasingly favored comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward. While these trends do not rule out nepotistic or unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment, they suggest that high-level allocation of wage resources generally constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.11).

Beyond the capital cities, changes occurred in the composition of employment, and private returns to public employment increased over time.

The Maddison Project, led by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2), aims to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, focusing particularly on the early modern period and Asia (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This collaborative effort among scholars aligns with the topic's claim that institutions are fundamental for long-run economic growth.

The project acknowledges the inherent uncertainties in estimating past national accounts due to limited data and subjective assumptions (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). However, it encourages continuous collaboration and quantification of long-term economic development to improve these estimates (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This approach, pioneered by Maddison, has inspired numerous scholars to delve into the study of economic growth over extended periods.

One specific mechanism that the Maddison Project and related scholarship jointly establish is the importance of data collection and refinement in understanding long-term economic development.

This wealth of data not only provides a more comprehensive understanding of historical economic development but also paves the way for further research into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

As the Maddison Project continues to evolve, it promises to offer valuable insights into the early modern period and Asian economies, areas that have been historically underrepresented in studies on economic growth. The project's focus on refining data and estimates underscores the importance of accurate information in understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) underscore how colonial rule shaped women's economic contributions in Africa. Their paper argues that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods, but the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced women's subordinate status, thereby limiting their economic contributions.

Customary law, a key avenue for the codification of women's subordinate status, was invented and formalized by colonial authorities (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014). Polly Hill (1963) documents how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. However, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, with their access to land further restricted by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Lawrance, Osborn, & Roberts, 2006).

In addition, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that patriarchal alliances played a crucial role in this process. The invention of customary law by colonial authorities reinforced existing gender hierarchies, further marginalizing women.

Thus, the codification of customary law under colonial rule had profound implications for the economic contributions of African women. These findings underscore the importance of understanding how institutions shaped by colonial powers can perpetuate gender inequality and limit economic growth in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the extensive scholarship on this topic (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. This finding underscores the significant impact of institutions established during colonial periods on contemporary economic conditions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further highlight the role of institutions in perpetuating gender inequality in Africa. They contend that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced existing gender hierarchies, further marginalizing African women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dual legal system exacerbated the subordinate status of African women, limiting their economic contributions and growth potential.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is not solely determined by their impact on property rights or gender hierarchies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, which may have long-term effects on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal channel of this effect, rooted in individual norms of mistrust, is particularly significant.

To resolve the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. and Akyeampong and Fofack on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, a more nuanced understanding is required that considers both property rights and cultural factors. The historical evidence provided by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrates how the internal channel of mistrust can have long-term effects on economic development, offering a potential explanation for the persistence of gender inequality in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
