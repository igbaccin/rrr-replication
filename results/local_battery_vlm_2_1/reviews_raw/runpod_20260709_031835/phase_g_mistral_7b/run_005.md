The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for understanding and addressing development challenges across the globe. At stake is the identification of the factors that drive sustained economic expansion, and the design of effective policies to promote growth and reduce poverty. This literature review examines the various positions in this live disagreement, focusing on the causal role of institutions as established during European colonization and their impact on current income levels across countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights set up by Europeans during colonization have a lasting influence on economic development. They contend that in cases such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to long-term growth. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states with the aim of quickly transferring resources to the metropole, leading to underdevelopment (AcemogluEtAl_2001(Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) present a different viewpoint by emphasizing the economic growth contributions of African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Furthermore, they demonstrate how colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women through customary law (Akyeampong&Fofack_2014(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20)).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) addresses the implications of the new economic history of Africa for institutional reforms and poverty reduction among the bottom billion people. While acknowledging that ethno-linguistic fragmentation can negatively impact public goods provision, he emphasizes that societies can be both ethnically homogeneous and dysfunctional or heterogeneous and capable of upholding the rule of law (Hopkins_2009(Hopkins 2009, p.18)). He also argues that the Atlantic slave trade reduced population levels and fostered institutions inimical to long-term growth (Hopkins_2009(Hopkins 2009, p.10)).

Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the oversimplification of causation in economic growth by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, suggesting a need for qualification and methodological modification. He argues that different historical periods and paths require nuanced analysis to account for variations in institutional development and economic growth (Austin_2008(Austin 2008, p.2)(Austin 2008, p.1)).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) contend that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, negatively impacting economic development. They find that both external factors, such as the rule of law, and internal norms of mistrust contribute to this effect (Nunn&Wantchekon_2011(Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27)(Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)).

Finally, Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. He suggests that understanding the historical roots of educational development is crucial for designing effective policies to promote economic growth (Frankema_2012(Frankema 2012, p.2)(Frankema 2012, p.17)).

The paper by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argues that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism significantly shape current income levels across countries. They posit that European colonization strategies varied, leading to different associated institutions with profound effects on economic development. In colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states with the goal of transferring resources to the metropole, hindering economic development.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) findings are supported by their subsequent work (2002), which reveals that these institutional changes account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. In 1500, relatively rich societies were often poorer today, but the institutional shifts during colonialism explain a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Moreover, Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011, p.27) research sheds light on how these institutional changes impacted trust, an essential factor for economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional changes in shaping trust, which is crucial for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

Ogilvie's (2007, p.27) work challenges the neoclassical view that efficiency is the primary driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Instead, he suggests that distributional conflicts play a more significant role (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective contrasts with the viewpoint presented by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue for the importance of secure rights and political institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Ogilvie proposes that interdependent institutional frameworks are crucial to understanding economic development during this period. For instance, the European family system, labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were all interconnected (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These interlinked systems may have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism.

Moreover, Ogilvie discusses how cultural theories can influence economic institutions and efficiency on a micro-level. For example, Richardson contends that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efficient' rules of guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The decline of these beliefs during the Protestant Reformation led to the decline of guilds. This demonstrates how non-economic cultural beliefs can impact the structure of economic institutions and efficiency.

In a broader context, Ogilvie's work highlights that understanding the interdependencies between various institutions is essential when studying economic growth in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective complicates the neoclassical view that efficiency is the primary driver of economic institutions, suggesting instead that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs also play significant roles.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This transformation is evident in the decline of herd laws, which were less prevalent in counties that adopted barbed wire earlier (Hornbeck 2010 (Hornbeck 2010, p.8)).

By the late 1880s, herd law counties had experienced a marked increase in improved farmland, suggesting that barbed wire played a crucial role in promoting agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32)). This shift underscores the importance of effective property rights enforcement for fostering economic growth, as demonstrated by the decline of guilds in Europe following the Protestant Reformation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The emergence of barbed wire in the American Plains exemplifies how technological innovations can reshape economic institutions and contribute to long-run economic growth. This stream complements the existing literature on institutions' impact on growth, suggesting that technological advancements should be considered as potential triggers for institutional change (Acemoglu et al. 2001; North & Weingast 1989).

This finding aligns with the broader perspective on economic growth in pre-industrial Europe that emphasizes the interdependencies between various institutions and cultural beliefs (Ogilvie 2007; North 1989).

The consolidation of colonial rule in Africa, as demonstrated by Frankema (2012, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth through the promotion of formal education. This political stability facilitated large-scale infrastructure projects that ultimately contributed to economic development.

The paper highlights the role of British colonialism in providing the necessary political stability for educational expansion, a crucial factor in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Simultaneously, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) find that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments.

The growth of useful knowledge, as argued by Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Goldin (2006, p.7), plays a crucial role in long-run economic growth. According to Mokyr (2005, p.54), the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, with scientific societies per capita significantly increasing from 1600 to 1800 in countries such as France, Italy, Germany, and the British Isles (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This intellectual activity concerned issues of political economy, including free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and more efficient, less distortionary taxes.

Simultaneously, Goldin (2006, p.7) argues that the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century underwent a quiet revolution, primarily driven by the generation born in the late 1940s (Goldin 2006, p.7). This shift from one phase to the next was due to various exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level.

The qualification comes from Mokyr (2005, p.54), who notes that while industrial growth was primarily British, it was the taproot of economic development worldwide (Mokyr 2005, p.3). The growth of useful knowledge, fostered by institutional foundations such as law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts, laid the groundwork for subsequent phases of technological change.

This downstream consequence of institutions further fuels economic development, suggesting a complex interplay between institutions, knowledge growth, and long-term prosperity.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) underscore the importance of continued collaboration among scholars to extend and refine Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This endeavor, known as the Maddison Project, aims to produce and update global economic data, a task that becomes increasingly challenging as one delves deeper into history due to the inherent uncertainties associated with historical data (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3).

Maddison himself recognized these challenges and adopted a strategy of producing numbers even if a solid basis did not always exist, encouraging scholars to challenge his work and contribute to the field (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2). This collaborative approach has been instrumental in advancing our understanding of long-term economic growth, as Maddison's pioneering work and subsequent responses have enabled a more detailed overview of economic development than was possible when he initiated his project in the 1960s (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2).

One downstream implication of this collaborative effort is the potential for revised estimates to significantly alter our understanding of historical economic trends. For instance, the Hitotsubashi team's work on Asian national accounts using Maddison's methodology revealed substantial differences compared to earlier estimates, suggesting that revisions can lead to a more accurate portrayal of economic development in specific regions (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3).

As the Maddison Project continues to evolve, it is expected that future updates will provide even more detailed and reliable data on long-term economic growth, contributing to a better understanding of the role institutions play in shaping this development over time.

Austin argues that the thesis, which emphasizes the impact of European colonization on current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), compresses different historical periods and paths, necessitating qualification and methodological modification to account for the complexities in institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience.

Austin's paper highlights the importance of understanding the specific contexts in which institutions that favor economic growth are adopted or not. For instance, in the case of colonial regimes, it was often in their interest to support rather than simply extract from African economic enterprise (Austin 2008, p.2). This finding shares with the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) who also emphasize the role of indigenous political institutions in shaping long-term economic growth.

Moreover, Austin's paper underscores the interplay between economic rent and economic growth, a point also made by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012) in their study on real wages and subsistence levels in Africa. They argue that institutions that favored economic growth were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in all historical contexts, echoing Austin's assertion that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation in economic growth.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically reorganized to uphold social hierarchies and expand cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler (1989, p.6), Stoler (1989, p.4)). This selective restructuring served multiple purposes: asserting colonial authority and controlling potentially subversive European underlings within the colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6).

For instance, Stoler's work highlights how the fear of interracial sexual encounters between white women and non-white men was leveraged to maintain social distinctions among Europeans in the colonies.

Moreover, Stoler's study underscores the absence of scholarly attention towards internal political tensions among Europeans within the colonial state.

This strategic restructuring of European class cultures in colonies contributed to the expansion of cultural infrastructure and had downstream implications for the development of indigenous political institutions. As we shall see, the interplay between European class cultures and imperial control played a significant role in shaping the emergence and evolution of native political institutions in Africa (Akyeampong & Fofack ).

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This claim complements the broader discussion on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth by focusing on the development of institutions in societies marked by significant income disparities.

The authors identify a specific mechanism through which this phenomenon unfolds: the emergence of institutions that cater to the interests of the elite, thereby limiting opportunities for social mobility and competition among the broader population.

One downstream implication of this mechanism is the persistence of inequality over time. This finding aligns with the shared assertion by other scholars that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al., 2001; North & Weingast, 1989).

While these institutions may have perpetuated inequality, they did not necessarily impede economic growth altogether. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that even in societies with institutions favoring elites, economic growth can still occur if there are significant external shocks or changes in the global economy that disrupt the established power dynamics.

The paper by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) underscores the impact of institutions on economic outcomes in Africa, focusing specifically on the role of colonial rule in shaping gender dynamics.

This dual legal system, consisting of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts, served to reinforce gender hierarchies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The economic consequences of these institutional arrangements were profound. This finding complements the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, which highlights the role of property rights and access to resources in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al., 2001; North & Weingast, 1989).

The paper by Nunn (2008, p.2) suggests a significant impact of Africa's slave trades on the development of institutions in Africa, which, in turn, shaped long-run economic growth. Nunn argues that these slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had a more enduring influence on Africa's economic development than the subsequent colonial rule (E0137).

The slave trades, according to Nunn, hindered the establishment of effective institutions in Africa. For instance, the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans because it had not been disrupted by slave trades (E0138). This finding complements the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, which highlights the role of effective institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al., 2001; North & Weingast, 1989).

Moreover, Nunn's work qualifies the downstream implication that effective institutions foster economic growth. By stifling the development of effective institutions, Africa's slave trades may have hindered economic growth in the long run. This finding is particularly significant given the ongoing debate about the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Peters (2004, p.5) and others argue that the perception of static custom in landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa can advantage certain groups by keeping officials ignorant of changing practices. This perception, fueled by colonial rule's push for agricultural development, masks processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation (Peters 2004, p.5).

Peters (2004, p.5) highlights the longstanding prejudice against African landholding practices, seen as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment in agriculture. This prejudice was expressed by agricultural officers responsible for schemes of agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement designed to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming. Critics like Jean-Philippe Platteau (1996, 2000) and Amanor (2001) point out that such unproblematic visions ignore considerable social inequality in many customary systems as well as evidence of encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood and/or commercial benefits, of state appropriation, and illegal sales by 'traditional' leaders.

The advantage gained by certain groups through this perception is significant. As Amanor (2001) argues, both the evolutionary school of land rights and the communitarian approach fail to deal adequately with 'the issue of social differentiation' in relations to land. Researchers like Ribot (2000), Carney and Farrington (1998), and Woodhouse et al. show how national and local elites capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers, further entrenching this advantage.

This advantageous position can have far-reaching implications for economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. As Nunn (2008, p.2) shows, effective institutions are crucial for long-term economic outcomes. By stifling the development of effective institutions, the perpetuation of this perception could hinder economic growth in the region. This finding is particularly significant given the ongoing debate about the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The paper by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenges the prevailing view that African economic growth before the 1980s was solely attributed to European colonial interventions. This finding complicates the narrative of Africa's economic underdevelopment, suggesting a more complex history that warrants reconsideration.

They argue that the lack of improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century is not universal across all African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This observation underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic development, one that considers the potential for growth beyond colonial interventions.

One mechanism through which this growth might have occurred involves the application of innovative techniques and newly digitized sources of data in African economic history (Austin & Broadberry 2014; Fourie 2016). By using these resources, scholars can uncover patterns of growth and development that span centuries, potentially rewriting the history of economic divergence and convergence since before the industrial revolution (Broadberry 2021).

Furthermore, Broadberry and Gardner's (2022) findings have significant downstream implications for our understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. If some African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence before the 1980s, it suggests that effective institutions may have been present and functioning effectively in these regions. This finding is particularly relevant given the ongoing debate about the role of institutions in Africa's economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and his contemporaries recognized that technological advancement, institutional reform, and access to tested data are crucial for less developed countries to participate effectively in the modern economic system. This emphasizes the significant role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in these regions.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions such as family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery, which were prevalent in earlier times.

Moreover, Platteau (2004) supports this assertion by highlighting the importance of negotiable landholding and use rights in sub-Saharan Africa. He contends that such negotiability can foster rural development by encouraging investment, reducing transaction costs, and promoting economic growth (Platteau 2004). Thus, institutional reforms that facilitate negotiable landholding can contribute to the technological advancement and access to tested data needed for less developed countries to thrive in the modern economy.

Furthermore, Mokyr (2005, p.54) underscores the role of knowledge growth in economic development. He argues that useful knowledge, such as new technologies, is a key driver of long-run economic growth. For less developed countries to benefit from this knowledge, they must gain access to it and be able to effectively absorb and implement it (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the importance of institutions in facilitating the dissemination and absorption of useful knowledge, thereby supporting technological advancement and economic growth.

In the realm of British industrial revolution and labor, Humphries (2013, p.16) posits that the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a significant factor driving this transformative era. This perspective diverges from traditional narratives focusing on skilled adult male labor.

The paper reveals that marital instability and family breakdown were common during this period, resulting in significant numbers of women and children among pauper populations (Humphries 2013, p.16). The high rates of male absence or abandonment weakened the foundation of male-breadwinner households, which relied heavily on their ability to provide support. This finding underscores the importance of understanding the broader workforce dynamics beyond skilled adult males during the industrial revolution (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Furthermore, Humphries demonstrates that working-class memoirs and accounts by poor law historians corroborate the presence of a significant group of families whose fathers were absent or had died (Humphries 2013, p.16). This turbulence in family structures during the industrial revolution highlights the importance of examining the role of underrepresented workforces in shaping this critical period of economic development.

This shift away from reliance on skilled adult male labor has downstream implications for our understanding of the broader economic dynamics during this transformative period.

Regardless, the quality of these institutions is believed to have played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium during this period.

This evidence demonstrates the significant contributions African women made to economic growth during colonial times, providing a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
