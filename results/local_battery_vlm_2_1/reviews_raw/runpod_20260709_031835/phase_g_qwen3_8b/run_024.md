The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or historical legacies play a more fundamental role. The live disagreement centers on the causal pathways through which institutions influence growth, with some emphasizing their direct impact and others highlighting indirect or alternative explanations. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain income differences among former colonies, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the need to integrate historical institutional analysis into contemporary African development policies. These perspectives reflect a broader tension between viewing institutions as exogenous forces shaping growth or as outcomes of deeper historical and cultural processes. The debate also extends to whether institutions are the result of specific historical conditions or if they emerge from broader social and political dynamics. This divergence in causal interpretations shapes the analytical frameworks used to study economic development, influencing how scholars model the relationship between institutions and long-term growth. The question of institutional causality is thus not only about identifying the sources of growth but also about understanding the mechanisms through which institutions operate. The next section explores how institutions are conceptualized as outcomes of historical processes rather than independent variables.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that secure property rights and institutional arrangements foster long-run economic growth by enabling sustainable development and reducing uncertainty, a claim supported by North (1989, p.1) who traces the evolution of institutions in England and Spain to show how historical institutional differences shaped economic outcomes. These scholars emphasize that institutions are not static but evolve through time, with their development influenced by the interplay of political and economic forces. For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.28) highlight how the financial revolution in England created an environment where property rights were protected, thereby facilitating economic growth and political freedom. This institutional framework, they argue, was crucial in mitigating the risks associated with economic activity and fostering long-term investment. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further underscores this point by asserting that modern economic growth requires not only technological advancement but also institutional adjustments that align with new modes of production. The role of institutions, therefore, is not merely to regulate but to enable the conditions necessary for sustained economic progress. Mokyr (2005, p.54) complements this view by showing how institutional progress complements technological innovation, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable rather than fleeting. Together, these sources illustrate that institutional arrangements are foundational to long-run economic growth, shaping the environment in which economic activity can flourish. This insight sets the stage for examining how institutional development interacts with other factors in the broader context of economic development.

Historical institutional analysis provides a framework to understand how institutions shape long-run economic growth by examining their historical development and impact. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that institutions in Africa are deeply rooted in precolonial and colonial histories, with their effectiveness in promoting growth contingent on their congruence with indigenous practices. This historical continuity underscores the importance of understanding institutional legacies as dynamic processes rather than static structures. Hopkins (2019, p.11) cautions against deterministic interpretations of path dependence, emphasizing the need to recognize the agency of actors in shaping institutional outcomes over time. By tracing these historical pathways, the literature reveals that institutional development is not a linear progression but a complex interplay of inherited structures and evolving practices. This insight suggests that effective policy interventions must account for the historical specificity of institutional contexts to foster sustainable economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial history and historical legacies are upstream causes of long-run economic growth. These scholars argue that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, such as extractive systems or extractive institutions, have persisted and continue to influence economic trajectories in former colonies. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) highlight that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and undermines economic development by reducing social cohesion and cooperation. These findings underscore the long-term consequences of historical institutional legacies, which can either enable or constrain economic growth. The persistence of these institutions suggests that the effects of colonial rule extend beyond immediate economic outcomes, shaping the structural conditions for development. This insight has important implications for policy, as it implies that addressing historical legacies may be necessary for achieving equitable and sustainable growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) argues that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the fundamental drivers of economic growth, a claim supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2). McCloskey emphasizes the role of cultural and ideological shifts in shaping economic trajectories, illustrating how changing beliefs about progress, innovation, and social order can catalyze or hinder growth. Ogilvie, meanwhile, highlights the interplay between ideological values and institutional structures, suggesting that the persistence of certain economic institutions is often rooted in the distributional conflicts and cultural norms that underpin them. Austin further complicates the institutional narrative by arguing that historical causality is more nuanced, with growth patterns emerging from a complex interplay of path dependence and differentiated causal relationships. Together, these authors challenge the primacy of institutional frameworks by foregrounding the ideological and cultural dimensions that shape economic outcomes. The implications of this perspective are profound, as they suggest that economic development is not merely a function of formal rules but is deeply embedded in the evolving narratives and values of societies. This insight invites a re-evaluation of how we understand the historical and contemporary forces that drive long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.19) and Temin (2002, p.6) argue that structural factors such as education and institutions, rather than institutions alone, drive long-run economic growth. Their work collectively highlights how the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks shapes the trajectory of economic development. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) emphasizes that the catch-up process is not merely a function of institutional quality but is also influenced by the evolving capabilities of human capital and technological adoption. Temin (2002, p.6) complements this by showing how postwar institutional arrangements in Europe facilitated sustained growth through coordinated investment and labor market incentives. These authors demonstrate that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their alignment with broader structural conditions, such as the availability of skilled labor and the pace of technological change. This insight suggests that economic growth is not solely a product of formal rules but is also contingent on the dynamic interactions between institutions and other structural forces. The implication is that understanding growth requires a more integrated approach that accounts for both institutional and structural determinants. This synthesis of perspectives underscores the need for further research into how these factors jointly influence long-term economic outcomes.

Stern (2004, p.2) challenges the Environmental Kuznets Curve by highlighting the methodological weaknesses in its empirical foundation, suggesting that the curve’s validity is overstated. This critique aligns with Stern’s broader argument that the relationship between development and the environment is better understood through alternative models that account for omitted variables and data limitations. The EKC, as conceptualized by Kuznets, is presented in (Stern 2004, p.1) as a hypothesis that income inequality first rises and then falls with economic development, a framework that has been extended to environmental degradation. While this idea has influenced environmental policy, Stern’s work underscores the need for more rigorous analytical approaches. The implication is that economic growth does not inherently lead to environmental degradation, but rather that the relationship is shaped by complex and often unmeasured factors. This perspective complicates the institutionalist view that institutions are the primary drivers of long-run growth, as it introduces the possibility that environmental outcomes are influenced by a broader set of developmental dynamics. By questioning the empirical basis of the EKC, Stern’s analysis invites a re-evaluation of how environmental and economic processes interact, offering a competing framework to the institutionalist focus on governance structures. This shift in emphasis highlights the need for a more integrated approach to understanding growth, one that incorporates both institutional and environmental variables.

The literature consistently underscores the centrality of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) who show that colonial institutions persistently influence income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.5). North (1989, p.1) further emphasizes that institutional frameworks, rather than population or savings, are the key determinants of economic performance (North 1989, p.1). These findings align in highlighting the enduring impact of institutional structures on development trajectories. However, the sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that causal relationships are more differentiated than institutionalist models suggest, emphasizing the role of historical context and path dependence (Austin 2008, p.1). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) challenges the focus on economic efficiency, contending that distributional conflicts are a more compelling explanation for the formation of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The disagreement centers on whether institutions emerge primarily from efficiency considerations or from power struggles over resource distribution. This debate is resolved through historical case studies that trace the evolution of institutions in specific regions, such as the contrasting institutional developments in England and Spain, which illustrate how institutional change is shaped by both economic and political dynamics (North 1989, p.8).
