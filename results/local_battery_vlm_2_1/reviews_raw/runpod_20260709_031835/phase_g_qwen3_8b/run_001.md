The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history, with profound implications for understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained development. At stake is the intellectual debate over whether institutions are the primary driver of economic progress or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological change, play a more decisive role. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions function as exogenous constraints or as endogenous outcomes of historical processes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, shaped by colonial policies and population dynamics, explain the divergent paths of economic performance among former European colonies. This claim is reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutions are the key to unlocking economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Both scholars highlight how institutional frameworks whether through property rights political accountability or legal systems create the conditions for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic progress though he acknowledges that institutional progress is necessary to sustain this growth. These perspectives underscore the centrality of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, while also pointing to the complexity of their role in historical development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that institutional adjustments and technological progress are foundational to long-run economic growth, as they enable the adoption of modern technologies incompatible with traditional social structures. This claim is reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who demonstrates how the efficiency of training institutions shaped the skill premium in medieval Europe, influencing long-term economic performance. Similarly, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that urban development and economic growth in Europe were closely tied to institutional frameworks that supported external economies of scale. These studies collectively suggest that institutional and technological progress are interdependent forces that drive productivity and efficiency across societies. Pamuk (2007, p.25) further illustrates this by highlighting how flexible institutions allowed certain regions to adapt more readily to economic shifts following the Black Death, contributing to divergent developmental trajectories. The implication is that institutional frameworks not only facilitate technological adoption but also determine the capacity of societies to sustain economic growth over time. This interplay between institutions and technology underscores the necessity of structural reforms to support long-term economic transformation.

Institutional structures shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), North (1989, p.1), and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4). These scholars collectively argue that the institutional legacies of colonial rule created enduring disparities in economic performance, with extractive systems persisting long after formal colonial control ended. The persistence of these institutional patterns, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores the long-term consequences of colonial governance, which often favored elite interests at the expense of broader economic participation. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight how restrictive policies, such as limited land access and exclusionary legal systems, perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites and stifling innovation. This institutional inertia, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue, not only constrained economic growth but also limited the capacity of societies to adapt to changing economic conditions. The cumulative evidence suggests that colonial institutions created structural barriers that continue to influence economic outcomes today. These findings point to the necessity of institutional reforms to overcome historical legacies and foster inclusive growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that ideological conditions shape the adoption of new institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. McCloskey highlights how deeply rooted cultural and ideological commitments, such as religious or nationalistic beliefs, can stifle or enable economic progress by influencing the political and institutional frameworks that govern economic activity. In France, for instance, the persistence of Catholicism and aristocratic values delayed the acceptance of bourgeois economic principles, thereby constraining growth for centuries. Hopkins (2019, p.11) shows that African economic history was a field of contested narratives shaped by ideological assumptions about the capacity of African societies to develop economically. These perspectives collectively suggest that the spread of pro-growth ideologies is as critical as the existence of supportive institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes. The interplay between ideology and institutional change, as emphasized by these authors, underscores the importance of cultural and intellectual environments in fostering the adoption of policies that promote economic development. This dynamic reinforces the idea that sustained growth depends not only on institutional design but also on the prevailing ideological climate that legitimizes and sustains such institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the legacy of the slave trade fostered a culture of institutional distrust in Africa, which persists and undermines long-term economic growth. This claim is reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that societies involved in the slave trade were often those with more developed institutions, suggesting that the slave trade itself disrupted the institutional fabric. The resulting distrust, as noted by Peters (2004, p.19), led to persistent land conflicts and exclusionary practices, which further eroded the capacity for inclusive economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) add that these historical legacies not only shaped economic outcomes but also influenced the design of colonial institutions, which in turn affected the trajectory of growth. The cumulative effect of these historical processes is a pattern of institutional distrust that hinders economic progress by limiting cooperation and trust among individuals and institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) highlight that such distrust also intersects with gender dynamics, where women's contributions to economic growth have often been marginalized, compounding the challenges of development. These findings collectively suggest that historical legacies of institutional distrust, rooted in the slave trade and colonialism, have created enduring obstacles to economic growth in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that resonates with Abramovitz (1986, p.19), who highlights the complexity of the catch-up process and the role of education and institutions in shaping productivity. Together, these authors underscore the centrality of knowledge diffusion and technological change as drivers of long-run growth, suggesting that institutional frameworks are secondary to the spread of new ideas and innovations. Mokyr emphasizes that while institutions are necessary to sustain technological progress, their role is complementary rather than foundational, as the pace and direction of economic development are largely dictated by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective is further supported by Temin (2002, p.6), who attributes the Golden Age of European growth to pre-war disequilibrium and the role of institutions in reinforcing long-term investment incentives. Temin’s analysis reveals how institutional arrangements can amplify the impact of technological and economic shifts, yet the core dynamism remains rooted in the transformation of knowledge and its application. Stern (2004, p.2), while focused on environmental economics, aligns with this stream by critiquing the oversimplification of growth models and advocating for a more nuanced understanding of how technological and institutional factors interact. These scholars collectively challenge the primacy of institutions in shaping economic growth, arguing instead that the expansion of useful knowledge and the mechanisms that facilitate its diffusion are the primary engines of long-term development. The implications of this view suggest that policies aimed at fostering innovation, education, and knowledge transfer may be more effective in promoting sustained economic growth than those focused solely on institutional reform.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities among formerly poor colonies. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further assert that secure property rights and institutional arrangements are fundamental to fostering economic growth and political freedom. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic trajectories. Mokyr (2005, p.54) challenges this institutional primacy by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic development. He contends that technological progress, when complemented by institutional progress, sustains economic improvement. This divergence highlights a critical debate: whether institutions or knowledge growth are the primary drivers of long-term development. The resolution of this disagreement lies in the empirical comparisons of colonial institutions and their persistence, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show how extractive institutions established during colonialism continued to influence economic outcomes long after independence. These historical case studies provide a concrete basis for evaluating the relative contributions of institutions and knowledge to economic growth.
