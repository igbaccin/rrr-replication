The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with competing views on whether institutions are the primary driver or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological progress. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the causal mechanism through which economic growth is sustained or whether they are the outcome of other forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, rooted in colonial history, are central to explaining income disparities among former European colonies, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes the need to consider precolonial and historical contexts in understanding current institutional arrangements. Both scholars agree that institutions matter, but they differ on the origins and mechanisms of institutional development. North (1989, p.1) underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes, contrasting them with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how institutional structures in the New World perpetuated inequality by favoring elites, suggesting that institutions are not neutral but shaped by historical and social forces. These perspectives collectively underscore the intellectual stakes of understanding institutions as both causes and consequences of economic trajectories. The next section explores how institutions are shaped by broader historical and cultural antecedents.

Nunn (2008, p.19) argues that colonial and slave trade legacies shaped extractive institutions that hindered long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who show how colonial policies entrenched resource extraction and marginalized local populations. These studies collectively reveal that historical forces, such as slavery and colonialism, created institutional frameworks that prioritized exploitation over development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) further demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and undermines economic cooperation. This pattern of institutional degradation, rooted in historical exploitation, is reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.3), who finds that the production of slaves through domestic violence and warfare had lasting negative impacts on societal development. The shared posture of this stream is that extractive institutions, shaped by colonial and slave trade legacies, created long-term barriers to economic growth by distorting social trust and institutional quality. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.18) highlight how colonial policies were designed to secure resource extraction, illustrating how these historical institutions were not accidental but purposefully constructed to serve extractive interests. The persistence of these institutions underscores the deep historical roots of underdevelopment and the challenges of institutional reform.

Both streams highlight that the persistence of extractive or inclusive institutions, rooted in colonial history, determined the trajectory of economic development. The mechanism through which this occurs is the institutionalization of political power, which either restricts or enables broad-based participation in economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) further emphasize that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, such as extractive control structures, shaped the long-term capacity of postcolonial states to generate sustained growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) add that secure rights and economic growth are fostered by institutional arrangements that balance power and protect property, a principle that aligns with the broader argument that institutional reliability is a prerequisite for long-run development. These sources collectively suggest that the historical embedding of institutions in colonial contexts created a structural condition for economic growth, contingent on the alignment of political and economic incentives. The downstream implication is that without institutional reform, the constraints imposed by historical legacies will continue to limit growth potential.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge, a form of institutional progress, is the mainspring of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.19; Kuznets 1973, p.1; Temin 2002, p.6). This stream emphasizes that knowledge accumulation is not merely a technical process but one deeply embedded in institutional frameworks that enable its dissemination and application. The institutional progress required to sustain technological advancement is highlighted as a critical condition for the long-term viability of economic growth. For instance, the establishment of educational institutions in the 18th century, such as the Scottish universities and Warrington Academy, played a pivotal role in reducing access costs to scientific knowledge, thereby fostering a culture of innovation. These developments were essential in overcoming the limitations of traditional economic structures that hindered the effective utilization of new technologies. The role of institutions in facilitating the transition from artisanal to industrial production is further underscored by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who notes that modern technology cannot function within the constraints of outdated social and economic systems. The downstream implication is that without institutional reforms to align with technological progress, the potential benefits of knowledge-based growth remain unrealized. This interplay between institutional evolution and technological advancement underscores the necessity of a dual focus on both knowledge creation and the institutional frameworks that support its integration into economic life.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that historical institutional legacies shape long-run economic growth by regulating training and access to professions, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) who show that institutional flexibility allowed northwestern Europe to adapt more effectively to economic shifts. The efficiency of training institutions and the regulation of professional access directly influence the skill premium, as van Zanden notes, by determining the supply of skilled labor and the relative value of different types of labor. This institutional framework, which governs the transmission of skills across generations, is critical in shaping wage differentials and long-term economic performance. Pamuk (2007, p.25) further illustrates this mechanism by showing how institutional resilience in the Low Countries and England after the Black Death enabled sustained wage levels and a divergence from the rest of Europe. These studies collectively highlight that the institutional regulation of skill formation and labor markets is a key determinant of economic divergence. The persistence of such institutional structures across centuries underscores their profound impact on economic trajectories, setting the stage for examining how institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary development paths.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the role of ideas over institutions. Both authors suggest that the persistence of certain economic outcomes is better explained by the ideological and cultural frameworks that shape the distribution of power and resources. Ogilvie highlights how institutional frameworks are analytically essential, yet their economic efficiency is secondary to the distributional tensions they reflect. McCloskey extends this by showing how cultural and ideological struggles, such as the French resistance to Jewish inclusion in the military, directly influenced economic trajectories by stifling growth. These perspectives collectively suggest that the interplay between ideological beliefs and institutional structures is central to understanding long-term economic development. The emphasis on cultural and ideological factors challenges the traditional focus on institutional design, shifting the analytical lens to the broader social and political dynamics that underpin economic change. This shift in focus underscores the importance of ideological coherence in shaping the conditions for sustained economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that European capitalism created underdevelopment, offering an alternative explanation for long-run economic growth compared to institutions, a claim supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11). This cluster emphasizes that historical processes, particularly the entanglement of African economies with European capitalist systems, shaped the trajectory of development in ways that diverge from traditional institutional analyses. The mechanism through which European capitalism created underdevelopment lies in its capacity to integrate peripheral economies into global networks of dependence, thereby altering the conditions for local growth and economic autonomy. Hopkins (2019, p.11) highlights how poverty was not an original condition but a historical creation, resulting from the structural imbalances imposed by unequal exchange and the extraction of surplus. This perspective challenges the assumption that institutional quality alone determines economic outcomes, instead framing underdevelopment as a product of historical contingency and global power relations. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further qualify this by showing that Sub-Saharan African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early 20th century, suggesting that the narrative of persistent underdevelopment may be overstated. This insight reframes the role of institutions by highlighting the need to consider the broader historical forces that shaped economic conditions. The implications of this rival cause suggest that policy interventions must address the legacies of historical integration and exploitation, rather than focusing solely on institutional reform.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities among former colonies. This aligns with North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutional frameworks, rather than population or savings, are central to understanding historical obstacles to economic growth. Both streams highlight the enduring impact of institutional structures on development trajectories. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) challenges the focus on economic efficiency, asserting that distributional conflicts provide a more compelling explanation for the origins of core economic institutions. This disagreement centers on whether institutions should be evaluated primarily through their economic outcomes or through their role in mediating social and political tensions. The resolution of this debate lies in the empirical analysis of institutional persistence, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which shows how colonial institutions have shaped contemporary economic conditions across diverse regions. By examining historical precedents and their long-term consequences, scholars can better assess the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth.
