The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of development or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or historical contingency. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are the causal mechanism through which economic growth is sustained or whether they are merely the outcome of deeper, often unmeasurable, forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain income distribution disparities among former European colonies, emphasizing the causal role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. This claim is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), which highlights the significant impact of institutions on economic growth and industrialization, suggesting that institutional differences account for a substantial fraction of current income disparities. These findings underscore the intellectual stakes of the debate: if institutions are the primary cause of growth, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could yield transformative economic outcomes. The next section explores how institutional development is shaped by historical and cultural antecedents. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) reports, “In modern LDCs, for instance, retention of existing techniques and institutions despite alternatives that are known to be more productive is ascribed to institutional interlinkages among markets.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutional arrangements and colonial history shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either enable or hinder development. These scholars demonstrate that the persistence of extractive institutions, rooted in colonial legacies, has systematically constrained economic potential in many former colonies. The interplay between these institutional frameworks and the historical conditions of colonization underscores how institutional persistence can either lock in stagnation or open the door to sustained growth. This dynamic is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who emphasize that the institutional structures set up during the colonial era in nonsettler colonies have endured, shaping the trajectory of economic performance in the modern era. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the historical roots of institutions is critical to designing policies that can overcome entrenched structural barriers. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with the legacies of colonial history, which continue to influence the trajectory of development in many regions.

Historical legacies like the slave trade shaped institutions that hindered long-run economic growth in Africa, as shown by (Nunn 2008, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Peters 2004, p.19; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This mechanism aligns with Nunn (2008, p.19), who demonstrates that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and kidnapping disrupted social cohesion and institutional development. The cumulative evidence underscores that the slave trade and related historical processes left a lasting imprint on African institutions, which continue to shape economic outcomes. This legacy of mistrust and institutional fragility remains a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth in the region.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by Abramovitz (1986, p.19) and Stern (2004, p.2) in their respective analyses of technological and environmental dynamics. The interplay between knowledge dissemination and institutional progress is central to understanding sustained economic improvement, as Mokyr highlights the need for complementary institutional frameworks to ensure the long-term viability of technological advancements. Stern (2004, p.2) complements this by emphasizing the limitations of traditional economic models in capturing the complexities of development-environment interactions, thereby reinforcing the necessity of institutional adaptation in addressing evolving challenges. The cumulative evidence suggests that institutional frameworks are not merely supportive mechanisms but essential components in the diffusion and application of knowledge, which in turn drives economic growth. This integration of institutional and technological progress provides a foundation for examining how knowledge-based economies can sustain long-term development.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are central to long-run economic growth, challenging institutionalist perspectives that prioritize formal rules over cultural and intellectual frameworks. Both authors emphasize that the persistence of certain institutions is due to their efficiency as well as the alignment of these institutions with prevailing ideological and belief systems. For instance, Ogilvie notes that institutional frameworks are analytically essential to understanding the origins and economic impact of any individual institution, as they mediate between inward beliefs and institutional rules. McCloskey further illustrates this by showing how cultural struggles, such as the French resistance to secularization, constrained economic growth for centuries, demonstrating that ideological commitments can override institutional incentives. The shared insight of these sources is that economic outcomes are deeply embedded in the interplay between institutional structures and ideological narratives, which together shape the trajectory of development. This dynamic underscores the need to consider both material and ideational factors when analyzing long-term economic growth.

African economic history can be written, and it contributes to understanding economic growth and development in the region. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) collectively demonstrate that historical institutional analysis is essential for grasping the long-term trajectories of African economies. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that contemporary institutions are shaped by precolonial and colonial legacies, with congruence between indigenous and modern institutions enhancing governance effectiveness and fostering development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further show that women played central roles in pre-colonial and colonial production systems, yet their contributions were often marginalized in economic narratives. These sources highlight how historical legacies, both institutional and social, shape present-day economic outcomes. Hopkins (2019, p.11) emphasizes that African economic history is not a linear path but a complex interplay of forces, including European capitalism’s role in creating underdevelopment through unequal exchange. This historical framing challenges the notion that institutions alone can explain growth, suggesting that economic outcomes are deeply rooted in historical processes. The implication is that understanding African development requires a dual focus on institutional structures and the historical conditions that shaped them. This historical perspective offers a foundation for rethinking contemporary economic policies in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is consistently underscored across the literature, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasizing their central role in explaining income differences among former colonies. Similarly, North (1989, p.1) highlights how institutional frameworks, shaped by historical conditions, determine economic trajectories. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that channel resources, shape behavior, and influence long-term outcomes. The sources diverge on the origins of these institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions persisted into the post-colonial era, structuring economic possibilities for generations. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) suggests that institutional development is more closely tied to distributional conflicts and cultural values than to economic efficiency. This disagreement centers on whether institutions emerge from material constraints or from ideological and social dynamics. The resolution of this debate lies in historical case studies that trace the evolution of specific institutions, such as the persistence of extractive systems in the New World (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), which demonstrate how colonial legacies shaped economic structures. These empirical comparisons provide a foundation for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and growth.
