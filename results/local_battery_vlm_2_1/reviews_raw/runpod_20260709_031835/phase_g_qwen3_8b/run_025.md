The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary cause of sustained development or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or knowledge accumulation. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are the main driver of growth or if they are shaped by deeper, more fundamental forces that indirectly influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, rooted in colonial history, are central to explaining income disparities among former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic trajectories. North (1989, p.1) further reinforces this by asserting that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. These accounts collectively suggest that institutions are not just outcomes of other factors but are themselves key determinants of long-run economic performance. The intellectual stakes lie in understanding whether institutions are the root cause of growth or a consequence of broader historical and cultural forces.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1) together demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks for political and economic order. These accounts highlight a shared mechanism: the enduring impact of colonial institutional design on post-colonial economic trajectories. The persistence of these institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), is not merely a historical accident but a structural condition that shapes the capacity for growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further qualify this by showing how secure property rights and representative institutions, such as common law courts, were critical in enabling both economic growth and political freedom. This stream collectively underscores that the institutional architecture established during colonial rule is not a passive backdrop but an active determinant of long-run economic outcomes. The implications of this are profound, suggesting that understanding the historical roots of institutions is essential to explaining contemporary economic disparities.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has enduring implications for economic development. This finding aligns with broader scholarship suggesting that historical legacies like colonialism and the slave trade shaped extractive institutions that hindered long-run economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Peters 2004, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The persistence of mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue, undermines social cohesion and cooperation, essential for robust economic institutions. These mechanisms highlight how institutional constraints, rooted in past extractive systems, continue to shape economic outcomes. The implications of this are significant, as they suggest that addressing historical legacies is crucial for fostering inclusive growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and McCloskey (2015, p.9). These scholars collectively emphasize that the persistence of certain economic outcomes is better explained by the interplay of power and ideology rather than by the mere efficiency of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights how institutional interlinkages can entrench existing power structures, making innovation and change difficult unless it aligns with broader systemic needs. Hopkins further reinforces this by showing that contemporary institutions in Africa are shaped by historical congruence with indigenous practices, which often reflect deeper cultural and distributional dynamics rather than purely economic rationality. McCloskey, meanwhile, underscores the role of cultural and ideological shifts in driving economic transformation, as seen in the French and Spanish cases where conservative beliefs hindered growth until new ideas emerged. This stream suggests that the legitimacy of institutions is often tied to their alignment with prevailing power relations and belief systems, which in turn shape long-term economic trajectories. The implication is that understanding these distributional conflicts is key to explaining why some societies experience sustained growth while others remain stagnant. This focus on power and ideology provides a critical lens for analyzing the role of institutions in economic development.

Institutions regulating training and access to professions shape long-term economic performance by influencing skill premiums and wage dynamics, as demonstrated by van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how the efficiency of institutions governing apprenticeship and professional entry directly affects the supply of skilled labor, thereby shaping the skill premium. This mechanism is further illustrated by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who show that institutional design in colonial Africa influenced wage levels and labor market integration, with more interventionist policies in East Africa contributing to persistent wage gaps. The role of institutions in mediating access to skill formation thus becomes a critical factor in determining long-term wage trends and economic outcomes. Pamuk (2007, p.25) reinforces this by showing how flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England allowed for sustained wage levels after the Black Death, fostering a divergence in economic trajectories across Europe. These findings collectively underscore that institutional frameworks governing skill development are not merely passive structures but active forces in shaping wage dynamics and long-term growth. This focus on institutional regulation of skill formation provides a clear pathway for understanding how institutional quality translates into sustained economic performance.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19). These authors collectively suggest that technological advancement, rather than institutional structures alone, drives long-term economic performance. Mokyr emphasizes that the spread of scientific knowledge and its application in industry were critical to the Industrial Revolution, while Kuznets highlights the incompatibility of modern technology with outdated social and institutional arrangements. Abramovitz further notes that the catch-up process in economic growth is shaped by the interplay of technological opportunity and existing educational and institutional commitments. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is thus a central mechanism through which economic growth is sustained. This dynamic underscores the importance of fostering environments that support both innovation and the institutional frameworks necessary to harness it. The role of institutions, therefore, is not to replace technological progress but to complement and sustain it over time.

Stern (2004, p.2) critiques the Environmental Kuznets Curve as an inadequate model for understanding development-environment relationships, arguing that its empirical foundations are weak and its explanatory power limited. Stern highlights that most studies fail to account for statistical issues such as serial dependence or stochastic trends in time-series data, which undermine the reliability of EKC estimates. This critique is supported by the observation that the EKC concept emerged from early studies like Grossman and Krueger’s work on NAFTA, which popularized the idea that environmental degradation initially increases with economic growth but eventually declines as development proceeds. The lack of rigorous methodological attention to model adequacy and omitted variables bias suggests that the EKC may not capture the complex interactions between economic activity and environmental outcomes. This structural limitation implies that alternative frameworks are necessary to better integrate environmental concerns into development narratives. The need for more nuanced models reflects a broader shift in understanding how economic growth and environmental sustainability interact, emphasizing the importance of institutional and policy contexts in shaping these relationships.

Institutional frameworks have been shown to significantly influence long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who highlight the role of institutions in explaining income differences among former colonies. The persistence of colonial-era institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), further underscores how historical institutional legacies shape contemporary economic outcomes. While these findings align on the centrality of institutions, they differ in their emphasis on the origins of these institutional structures. North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions emerged from historical conditions and political struggles, whereas Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts are a more fundamental explanation for core economic institutions. The disagreement centers on whether institutional development is primarily shaped by economic efficiency or by social and political dynamics. This divergence is resolved through historical case studies, such as the contrasting institutional trajectories of England and Spain, which North (1989, p.8) uses to illustrate how institutional evolution affects economic performance. The empirical focus on colonial institutions and their long-term impacts provides a shared ground for understanding how institutions shape growth, even as scholars debate the mechanisms of institutional formation.
