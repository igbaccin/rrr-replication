The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary causal mechanism driving development or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge accumulation, are more central. This disagreement hinges on the assumption that institutions either directly enable or indirectly influence growth through their impact on political stability, property rights, and economic incentives. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, shaped by colonial policies and population density, explain persistent income disparities among former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by entrenching elite advantages. Both streams emphasize the causal link between institutions and growth, though they diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. North (1989, p.1) reinforces this causal claim by asserting that institutions are the key to understanding historical economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Meanwhile, Mokyr (2005, p.54) shifts focus to the role of knowledge, suggesting that institutional progress is necessary to sustain technological advancements and prevent the reversal of economic gains. These perspectives collectively underscore the intellectual stakes of identifying the core drivers of long-run growth, with institutions either as the central cause or as a critical enabler of broader developmental forces. The next section explores how institutional legacies interact with cultural and historical factors to shape economic outcomes.

These authors collectively highlight how institutional frameworks, whether shaped by colonial legacies or historical evolution, determine the trajectory of economic performance. The persistence of extractive institutions in nonsettler colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), further illustrates how institutional arrangements from the past continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) expand on this by showing that secure rights and economic growth are fostered by specific institutional arrangements, such as representative government and the primacy of common law courts. Their work underscores that institutional design is a dynamic force that enables or constrains economic progress and is not merely a historical artifact. The joint emphasis on institutional persistence and the role of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes suggests that understanding the historical roots of institutions is essential to explaining long-run growth. This focus on institutional legacies provides a foundation for examining how cultural and historical factors interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes.

Nunn (2008, p.19) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate that historical colonial and slave trade institutions shaped long-run economic growth by perpetuating inequality and hindering development. The slave trade, as Nunn shows, disrupted local economies through violence and resource extraction and also created conditions that favored the persistence of extractive institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman further argue that these institutional legacies in the New World entrenched inequality by privileging elites and restricting economic opportunities for broader populations. Together, these studies highlight how the structural features of colonial institutions, such as the concentration of power and the suppression of local autonomy, created enduring barriers to development. Nunn’s work also reveals that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, a legacy that continues to affect economic cooperation and institutional trust today. This cultural mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) note, has significant implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in facilitating trade, investment, and political stability. The cumulative effect of these historical forces is a long-run divergence in economic outcomes, with societies shaped by extractive institutions lagging behind those with more inclusive and resilient institutional frameworks. This pattern underscores the necessity of understanding historical legacies when analyzing contemporary economic disparities.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the efficiency of training institutions and access to professions significantly shaped long-term economic performance in Europe, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) who highlight the divergence in economic development between Europe and Asia. The ability of institutions to regulate the transmission of skills and facilitate professional entry influenced the skill premium, which in turn affected labor market dynamics and economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.25) further demonstrates that institutional flexibility allowed certain regions to adapt more effectively to economic shocks, such as the Black Death, thereby maintaining wage levels and fostering long-term prosperity. These findings collectively suggest that institutional frameworks that promote skill accumulation and labor mobility were critical in determining the trajectory of economic development. The persistence of high skill premiums in regions with restrictive institutions, such as Ireland, underscores the long-term consequences of institutional design on human capital formation. This pattern reinforces the idea that institutional efficiency in shaping professional access and training was a key driver of sustained economic performance in Europe.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that historical wage and growth conditions in Sub-Saharan Africa were favorable, challenging the narrative of persistent structural impediments to economic development. This claim is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods, suggesting that institutional frameworks were not uniformly restrictive. The evidence indicates that African economies experienced periods of growth tied to foreign trade and export markets, with institutional designs shaped by local conditions and political choices. These findings imply that the trajectory of economic performance in Africa was influenced by a complex interplay of historical, institutional, and market factors rather than by a single, persistent constraint. The role of institutions in facilitating or hindering economic growth is thus contextual, dependent on the interplay between historical legacies and evolving economic conditions. This suggests that understanding the long-run economic potential of African economies requires a nuanced analysis of institutional evolution and its interaction with broader structural forces.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19) argue that the expansion of useful knowledge is a primary driver of long-run economic growth, rivaling institutional explanations. This cluster emphasizes that technological progress and the diffusion of knowledge are central to sustained economic development, with knowledge accumulation often outpacing institutional adaptation. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr notes, is less monotonic than technological progress, suggesting that knowledge-driven growth is both dynamic and context-dependent. Abramovitz further highlights that the catch-up process is not merely a function of technological opportunity but is also shaped by the educational and institutional legacies of past development. These factors can either facilitate or hinder the absorption of new technologies, thereby influencing the trajectory of economic growth. The role of formal education in disseminating technical knowledge, as illustrated by Mokyr, reveals that institutions such as universities and academies played a critical role in reducing access costs to scientific and technical knowledge during the early stages of industrialization. The limited impact of traditional universities in teaching applied science during this period underscores the uneven distribution of knowledge diffusion. This suggests that while institutions can support knowledge dissemination, they are not the sole or primary cause of economic growth. The interplay between knowledge accumulation and institutional frameworks thus becomes a key theme in understanding long-run economic performance. This dynamic highlights the need to consider both technological and institutional factors in explaining economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. McCloskey's emphasis on 'humanomics' highlights how cultural and intellectual shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, can catalyze economic transformation by reshaping norms and expectations. Ogilvie complements this by showing that institutional frameworks are often shaped by underlying beliefs and values, which in turn influence the economic outcomes of societies. Together, these sources suggest that the diffusion of ideas, such as those promoting scientific inquiry or market participation, can alter the trajectory of economic development. This mechanism is further illustrated by McCloskey’s observation that the French economic stagnation was partly due to conservative ideologies that resisted modernization. The implication is that economic growth is not merely a product of formal rules but is deeply embedded in the evolving intellectual and cultural landscapes of societies. This perspective challenges the primacy of institutions by showing how ideas can directly shape the conditions for growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that historical institutional analysis is key to understanding African economic history, offering an alternative to the focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. This perspective emphasizes that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions in promoting growth depends on their congruence with indigenous systems, a condition that shapes the legitimacy and capacity of governance structures to foster development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Such congruence, however, is not static but is historically contingent, as demonstrated by the argument that poverty in Africa was not an original condition but a historical creation shaped by European capitalism’s mechanisms of dependence and unequal exchange (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This historical dimension challenges the primacy of institutional determinism by showing how external forces interacted with local institutions to produce specific developmental trajectories. The implication is that African economic history cannot be fully understood without accounting for the complex interplay between indigenous institutions, colonial legacies, and global economic systems. This synthesis underscores the necessity of integrating historical analysis into institutional studies to capture the full range of factors that shape long-run economic outcomes. The role of institutions, therefore, must be seen as part of a broader historical process rather than as an independent cause of growth.

Temin (2002, p.6) contends that the Golden Age of European growth was propelled by pre-war disequilibrium rather than post-war policies, a perspective that aligns with broader analyses of structural imbalances shaping long-run economic trajectories. This view is supported by the observation that pre-war financial and trade imbalances created conditions conducive to rapid industrialization and capital accumulation, which were then amplified by post-war institutional frameworks. The interplay between these pre-existing imbalances and post-war institutional arrangements is critical, as it highlights how historical legacies can shape the effectiveness of later policy interventions. Temin’s argument is further reinforced by the notion that the post-war institutions served not as the primary cause of growth but as a mechanism to stabilize and channel the momentum generated by pre-war disequilibrium. This synthesis suggests that the institutional environment, while important, operates within a broader structural context defined by earlier economic dynamics. The implications of this framework are significant for understanding how economic growth is not solely a product of institutional design but also of the historical conditions that precede and shape institutional outcomes. Thus, the role of institutions must be contextualized within the broader economic and structural forces that set the stage for their emergence and function.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the persistent influence of historical institutional frameworks, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8). These frameworks, often established during colonial periods, continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes through their enduring impact on property rights, governance, and economic activity. However, the origins of these institutions remain a point of contention, with Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizing that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a more compelling explanation for the development of core economic institutions. This disagreement highlights the divergent theoretical foundations upon which scholars build their analyses of institutional persistence and its economic consequences. The empirical evidence from Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) offers a critical resolution to this debate, demonstrating that the divergent paths of institutional development in the New World were shaped by a combination of initial conditions and the specific historical contexts in which institutions emerged. By examining the interplay between colonial legacies and local conditions, these studies provide a nuanced understanding of how institutions both reflect and shape long-term economic trajectories.
