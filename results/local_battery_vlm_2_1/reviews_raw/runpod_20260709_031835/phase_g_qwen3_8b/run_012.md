The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central question in economic history, with implications for understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained development. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical contingency, play a more decisive role. The disagreement centers on whether institutional frameworks are endogenous to economic outcomes or exogenous, shaped by external forces beyond the control of societies. This tension is evident in the competing claims that institutions either emerge from historical conditions or are the result of deliberate political choices. The stakes of this debate lie in the policy implications: if institutions are malleable, then reform is possible; if they are rigidly determined by past events, then the prospects for change are limited. The literature thus reflects a fundamental intellectual divide over the causal mechanisms that underpin long-term economic trajectories. This review explores how different scholarly streams address this question, emphasizing the interplay between institutional design, historical context, and economic performance. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “The present paper offers one economic historian's reﬂections on the RF thesis: its methods, historical claims and implications for theory and policy.” Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) report, “In that paper we proposed the disease environment at the time Europeans arrived as an instrument for European settlements and the subsequent institutional development of the former colonies, and used this to estimate the causal effect of institutional differences on economic performance.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks for political and economic order (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Kuznets 1973, p.1). These scholars highlight how colonial institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, persisted long after independence, influencing the trajectory of economic development. The mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped growth lies in their ability to create stable, predictable environments for investment and innovation, which in turn facilitated sustained economic performance. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further qualify this by showing how secure property rights and institutional arrangements in England enabled the financial revolution, which was critical to long-run success. This suggests that the durability of institutional frameworks, whether rooted in colonial or pre-colonial history, is a key determinant of economic growth. The implication is that understanding the historical roots of institutions is essential for explaining divergent paths of development across nations.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that historical institutional analysis is essential for understanding the long-term dynamics of African development, as contemporary institutions are shaped by historical processes that extend beyond colonial rule. This claim is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.11), which emphasizes that economic history must be written with attention to the historical creation of underdevelopment through processes of dependency and unequal exchange. Together these works suggest that long-run economic growth is shaped by institutions that are the product of historical processes and not by static or exogenous factors. The mechanism through which this occurs is the congruence between contemporary institutions and indigenous practices, which enhances the legitimacy of governance and fosters development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that institutions congruent with precolonial systems are more likely to promote growth by reducing the distortions of patrimonial politics and regional fragmentation. This congruence, however, is not guaranteed and requires deliberate policy interventions that recognize the historical roots of institutional structures. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further qualifies this by showing how European capitalism created underdevelopment through mechanisms of dependence and exploitation, which continue to shape the trajectory of African economies. The implication is that understanding the historical context of institutions is not merely an academic exercise but a prerequisite for designing effective policies that align with the deep-seated structures of society. This historical lens is crucial for identifying the conditions under which institutions can be reformed to support sustained economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade entrenched cultural mistrust in Africa, which persists and undermines economic development. This finding aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who argue that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies. Together, these studies suggest that colonial institutions and the slave trade created extractive systems that hindered development by fostering distrust and entrenching inequality. Nunn (2008, p.19) further explains that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and kidnapping had negative impacts on subsequent development, reinforcing the idea that historical legacies of exploitation shaped long-run economic trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) add a critical dimension by showing how gender discrimination, often reinforced by colonial institutions, disinvested in human capital and slowed growth. These works collectively highlight that the mechanisms of colonialism and slavery not only disrupted economic activity but also embedded structural inequalities that continue to shape economic outcomes. The persistence of these extractive systems underscores the need to understand historical legacies as central to the analysis of economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological and knowledge-driven growth is the mainspring of economic growth, emphasizing the role of institutions in enabling and sustaining this process; (Mokyr 2005, p.54; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.25; Temin 2002, p.6; Abramovitz 1986, p.19). Institutions are not merely passive facilitators but active participants in shaping the trajectory of technological progress, as seen in the historical development of educational and research institutions that supported the diffusion of scientific knowledge. The integration of formal and informal institutions was crucial in overcoming coordination failures and ensuring the long-term sustainability of economic growth. For instance, the rise of universities in the 1740s, which trained future bureaucrats in applied sciences, illustrates how institutional frameworks could reduce access costs and foster innovation. Yet the effectiveness of these institutions varied as evidenced by the persistent skill premium in Ireland compared to western Europe where more efficient training institutions contributed to lower wage gaps. This underscores the importance of institutional design in determining the pace and direction of technological and knowledge-driven growth. The historical evidence suggests that institutional complementarities are essential for translating technological progress into sustained economic performance, a theme that resonates with broader discussions on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth. They posit that cultural values and belief systems shape the conditions under which economic institutions emerge and function, often in ways that defy purely material or structural explanations. McCloskey (2015, p.9) illustrates this by highlighting how the persistence of aristocratic values in France and Spain constrained economic growth, even in the face of more efficient institutional alternatives. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) reinforces this by emphasizing that institutional frameworks are not merely technical arrangements but are deeply embedded in the interplay between inward beliefs and values, which can either reinforce or undermine the economic potential of any given institution. This dynamic suggests that the same institutional structure can yield vastly different economic outcomes depending on the cultural context in which it is embedded. Stern (2004, p.2) while not directly addressing the institutional question underscores the broader point that economic outcomes are shaped by intangible forces whether ideological cultural or environmental that often operate independently of formal institutional design. Together, these sources highlight a mechanism by which ideas and ideologies can create the conditions for sustained economic growth, challenging the primacy of institutional structures as the sole or primary explanatory factor. This perspective invites a re-evaluation of how we conceptualize the relationship between culture, institutions, and long-term economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that African real wages and per capita incomes were above subsistence and rose over time, a claim supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4). These scholars challenge the conventional narrative that African economies were inherently stagnant, emphasizing instead the role of historical and economic dynamics in shaping long-run growth. By highlighting the persistence of wage growth and income levels above subsistence, they suggest that alternative pathways to development may have existed, independent of the institutional structures often emphasized in growth theories. The evidence points to a pattern where economic performance was influenced by factors such as trade, export markets, and local endowments, rather than solely by institutional design. This perspective underscores the importance of considering non-institutional variables in understanding economic trajectories. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.27) further illustrate how precolonial differences in commercial development shaped the institutional frameworks of colonial states, reinforcing the idea that growth outcomes are not predetermined by institutions alone. The implication is that economic development can emerge from a complex interplay of historical, environmental, and market-based factors, which may not be fully captured by institutionalist frameworks. This stream of scholarship invites a broader consideration of the conditions under which economic growth can occur, beyond the traditional focus on institutional legacies.

This claim is supported by Austin’s emphasis on the differentiated causal relationships that shape economic outcomes, which are often overlooked in institutionalist frameworks. By drawing on African and comparative economic historiography, Austin highlights how long-term historical contexts and path dependencies can produce recurring patterns of growth and stagnation, independent of institutional structures. This perspective is further reinforced by Nunn’s analysis of slave trading, which underscores the role of specific historical contingencies in shaping economic trajectories. Together, these authors suggest that economic development is not solely a function of institutional design but is also deeply embedded in a broader set of historical and environmental conditions. This insight complicates the traditional institutionalist view by emphasizing the need to consider multiple, interacting factors in explaining long-run growth. The implication is that policy interventions must account for these complex causal relationships rather than focusing exclusively on institutional reforms. This shift in focus opens the door to a more nuanced understanding of how historical legacies and structural constraints interact to influence economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is widely recognized as central, with multiple scholars emphasizing their enduring impact on development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional legacies from colonial rule significantly shaped post-colonial economic performance, with historical institutions accounting for a substantial fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Similarly, North (1989, p.1) highlights how institutional frameworks, particularly those rooted in legal and property rights, have historically determined the pace and direction of economic growth, contrasting neoclassical explanations that focus on population and savings (North 1989, p.1). These perspectives converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active determinants of economic outcomes. However the sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a more accurate explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions, challenging the assumption that institutions primarily serve to maximize productivity (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This disagreement is resolved through historical evidence comparing institutional evolution in England and Spain, where North (1989, p.1) shows how distinct institutional trajectories led to divergent economic outcomes in the New World (North 1989, p.8).
