The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with profound implications for understanding historical divergence and contemporary inequality. The extent to which institutional frameworks such as property rights political accountability and legal systems serve as foundational drivers of sustained economic progress remains a key issue or whether other factors like geography culture or technological diffusion hold greater explanatory power. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary causal mechanism for growth or whether they are shaped by deeper, often unmeasured, determinants such as social norms, historical legacies, or environmental conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, rooted in colonial governance, explain the divergent trajectories of former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that institutional outcomes in the New World were shaped by persistent elite advantages and restrictive policies. These perspectives highlight a core tension: whether institutions are autonomous forces of development or contingent on broader socioeconomic and cultural contexts. The debate over institutional causality is further complicated by alternative explanations, such as the role of knowledge diffusion and trust-building, which suggest that institutions may be outcomes rather than origins of growth. This tension underscores the need to distinguish between institutional determinants and the antecedents that shape their formation.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1) demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth through their impact on institutional reliability and political structures. These authors collectively argue that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule persisted into the post-colonial era, influencing the trajectory of economic development in former colonies. The mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced growth are further elaborated by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who show that secure property rights and effective governance structures were critical to fostering economic expansion. Their analysis underscores how institutional arrangements, particularly those that protected economic freedoms, enabled sustained growth in societies where political stability was achieved through inclusive political processes. This stream of scholarship thus establishes that the long-term economic performance of nations is deeply intertwined with the historical legacies of institutional design, which continue to shape the conditions for growth. The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of understanding their origins and evolution in order to assess their ongoing influence on development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the historical legacies like the slave trade shaped extractive institutions, which hindered long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that the slave trade not only disrupted economic development through direct exploitation but also entrenched extractive practices that limited the capacity of societies to foster inclusive growth. The persistence of these institutions, as noted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), reflects a broader pattern in which initial conditions, such as the presence of exploitative systems, created enduring barriers to institutional evolution. These barriers, in turn, constrained the ability of societies to transition toward more productive and equitable economic structures. The mechanism through which these historical legacies exert their influence lies in the cultural and institutional norms that emerged from the slave trade, which fostered mistrust and undermined collective action, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight in their analysis of trust as a key determinant of development. This stream of scholarship thus underscores the long-term consequences of extractive institutions, which are not merely relics of the past but active constraints on contemporary economic performance. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the origins of institutions is essential for formulating policies that address the deep-seated structural challenges to growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a stance supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the primacy of ideas over institutional structures. Both scholars argue that the persistent underperformance of certain economies cannot be fully explained by institutional frameworks alone, but must be understood through the lens of shifting power dynamics and ideological contests. Ogilvie highlights how institutional systems often serve to entrench existing distributions of power, making it difficult for new, more efficient arrangements to emerge unless they align with the broader interests of those in power. McCloskey expands on this by illustrating how cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values in Europe, catalyzed economic transformation by altering the incentives and norms that governed economic behavior. These perspectives collectively suggest that the evolution of economic institutions is deeply intertwined with the resolution of distributional conflicts, which in turn shape the trajectory of long-run growth. The implications of this view are profound, as they challenge the conventional focus on institutional design and redirect attention to the role of ideas and power in shaping economic outcomes. This shift in focus opens new avenues for understanding how historical and ideological forces have influenced the course of economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that historical institutional analysis is the key to understanding long-run economic growth in Africa, a claim supported by Peters (2004, p.19), Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9). These scholars collectively demonstrate that African economies were not inherently underdeveloped but instead shaped by institutional frameworks that influenced productivity, labor markets, and gender roles. The persistence of these institutions, as Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue, explains the uneven development across regions, with colonial policies reinforcing existing inequalities. This stream of scholarship underscores the importance of institutional continuity and change in shaping African economic trajectories. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding long-run growth requires a nuanced analysis of how institutions interact with historical, social, and political forces.

Temin (2002, p.6) challenges the conventional institutionalist view by arguing that historical disequilibrium, rather than institutional design, was the primary force driving long-run economic growth. This perspective is reinforced by Hopkins (2019, p.11), who highlights how historical processes, rather than static institutions, shaped economic trajectories, particularly in Africa. Both scholars emphasize that economic outcomes are deeply embedded in the dynamics of historical change where disequilibrium whether from war colonialism or structural shifts created conditions for rapid growth or persistent underdevelopment. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional frameworks is thus not a matter of institutional quality alone but of how these frameworks emerged from and responded to historical disequilibrium. Temin’s analysis of postwar Europe demonstrates that institutions functioned as mechanisms to stabilize and harness the effects of pre-war imbalances, while Hopkins underscores the limitations of path-dependent explanations in capturing the complexity of African economic histories. These insights suggest that institutional analysis must be contextualized within broader historical forces, as institutions themselves are products of historical processes rather than independent determinants of growth. This reorientation of focus from institutions to historical disequilibrium opens new avenues for understanding the uneven development of economies across time and space.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that resonates with Abramovitz’s (1986, p.19) emphasis on the complexity of catch-up processes. Both authors highlight that technological progress, while central, is often constrained by institutional and educational frameworks that shape its diffusion and application. Mokyr underscores that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement remains fragile, as rent-seeking behaviors can erode gains. Abramovitz, meanwhile, notes that past educational and institutional commitments may hinder rather than facilitate the catch-up process, suggesting that historical legacies play a critical role in shaping contemporary growth trajectories. These perspectives collectively imply that the interplay between knowledge creation and institutional adaptation is a key determinant of long-run economic outcomes. The role of formal education in disseminating technical expertise, as illustrated by Mokyr’s account of Scottish universities and Warrington Academy, further illustrates how knowledge transmission is embedded in institutional structures. This dynamic suggests that the growth of economic capabilities is not a linear process but one shaped by the evolving interplay of technological and institutional forces. The implications of this interplay extend to the broader challenge of fostering inclusive growth, where the diffusion of knowledge must be accompanied by institutional reforms that reduce barriers to access and application. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) reports, “In the simple catch-up hypothesis, that capability is viewed as either exogenously determined or else as adjusting steadily to the requirements of technological opportunity.”

Stern (2004, p.2) challenges the Environmental Kuznets Curve by highlighting the econometric weaknesses in its empirical foundation, arguing that the curve’s validity is undermined by issues such as omitted variables and serial dependence in time-series data. This critique aligns with broader concerns about the limitations of using environmental indicators to explain long-run economic growth, as institutions are often overlooked in such models. Stern’s analysis underscores the need for more rigorous methodological approaches to disentangle the complex interactions between development and environmental outcomes. The EKC’s popularity, as noted by Stern, reflects a tendency to conflate economic growth with environmental degradation, a perspective that neglects the role of institutional frameworks in shaping both trajectories. By emphasizing the statistical fragility of the EKC, Stern’s work opens the door to alternative explanations that integrate institutional factors into the development-environment relationship. This shift in focus invites a reevaluation of how economic growth is measured and what mechanisms truly drive sustainable development. The implications of Stern’s critique suggest that understanding the environmental consequences of growth requires a more nuanced approach that accounts for institutional variation and its influence on resource management and policy outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is firmly established across multiple streams of scholarship. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that institutional differences, rooted in colonial history, significantly influence current income disparities among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that secure property rights and representative governance are essential for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These findings converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active determinants of economic trajectories. However the sources diverge in their emphasis on the origins of these institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a more compelling explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This disagreement centers on whether institutional development is primarily driven by material interests or by ideological and cultural factors. The resolution of this debate lies in historical case studies that trace institutional evolution through specific episodes of political and economic change. For instance, North (1989, p.1) highlights the institutional divergence between England and Spain, showing how distinct historical conditions led to different institutional outcomes (North 1989, p.1). Such comparative analysis underscores the importance of contextual factors in understanding institutional persistence and transformation. Ultimately, the literature affirms that institutions are central to economic growth, yet the mechanisms through which they emerge and evolve remain a subject of scholarly contention.
