The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional structures are the primary mechanism through which economies diverge or whether other factors, such as geography, historical legacies, or cultural norms, are more decisive. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are the causal force driving growth or whether they are shaped by alternative determinants that themselves influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' responses to local conditions, were the key to divergent economic trajectories among former colonies, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) propose that the legacy of the slave trade created a persistent culture of mistrust that undermines growth. Both streams agree that institutions matter, but they differ on what determines their formation and how they interact with other variables. North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions are the foundation of economic development, contrasting with neoclassical models that prioritize population and savings. Hopkins (2009, p.8) adds that institutions are the product of historical processes, including precolonial and colonial legacies, which complicates the causal link between institutions and growth. These streams collectively highlight the centrality of institutions but also the need to account for their antecedents and alternative explanations. The intellectual stakes lie in understanding whether institutions are the root of economic divergence or merely one of many factors shaping long-term outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, such as extractive or inclusive systems, have enduring impacts on economic performance, with evidence showing that these structures persisted long after colonial regimes ended. These institutional legacies, whether extractive or inclusive, are active forces that shape the conditions for sustained growth and are not merely historical artifacts. The persistence of these institutions, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores how colonial-era policies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, the institutional frameworks in Australia, Canada, and the United States, which were established during colonial rule, have formed the basis of modern economic systems. This suggests that the institutional arrangements of the past are critical for understanding current disparities and for shaping future trajectories. The implications of these findings extend to the role of political and economic institutions in fostering or constraining growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that secure property rights and inclusive governance are essential for sustained economic development. Thus, the historical roots of institutions remain a central factor in explaining long-term economic outcomes.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that institutional adjustments and historical development shape long-term economic performance by enabling sustained growth through effective governance and innovation. Van Zanden highlights how the efficiency of training institutions regulated the transfer of skills across generations, directly affecting the skill premium and, consequently, economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner further show that institutional frameworks tied to foreign trade and market dynamics influenced the scale and timing of economic growth, reinforcing the idea that governance structures are central to long-term performance. These findings underscore the role of institutional adaptability in fostering innovation and economic resilience. The persistence of institutional legacies, as noted by Kuznets (1973, p.1), suggests that historical institutional arrangements shape current economic conditions and determine the capacity for future development. This stream collectively emphasizes that the interplay between institutional design and historical context is critical for understanding economic divergence and convergence across regions. The implications of these insights extend to contemporary policy, where institutional reform remains a key lever for sustained economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade left a lasting imprint on African institutions by fostering a culture of mistrust, which continues to hinder economic growth. This finding aligns with the broader argument that historical legacies like the slave trade shaped institutions, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Peters 2004, p.19; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2019, p.11). The persistence of mistrust, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon, is a structural barrier that limits the effectiveness of governance and economic cooperation and is not merely a social phenomenon. This historical continuity suggests that the legacies of exploitation and extraction, such as those from the slave trade, have created enduring institutional constraints that impede growth. The interplay between historical trauma and institutional design reveals that economic outcomes are shaped by centuries of institutional evolution and are not solely determined by present conditions. These insights highlight the need for policies that address historical injustices and institutional legacies to foster sustainable development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus inseparable from the historical processes that have shaped them.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that knowledge and technological progress, not institutions, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by (Mokyr 2005, p.54) and (Abramovitz 1986, p.19), which together emphasize the role of technological advancement in fostering sustained economic development. Mokyr highlights that while institutional progress is necessary to complement technological innovation, the core engine of growth lies in the expansion of useful knowledge. The historical spread of scientific education, as documented in (Mokyr 2005, p.28), further illustrates how technological progress was facilitated by the diffusion of knowledge across different regions and social groups. This diffusion, however, was not uniform, as formal educational institutions played a limited role in the early stages of the Industrial Revolution. These insights suggest that while institutions are important, they are secondary to the accumulation of knowledge and technological capabilities. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation underscores the complexity of long-run economic growth. This stream of scholarship challenges the centrality of institutions by highlighting the transformative power of knowledge and innovation in shaping economic trajectories.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are central to economic growth, as they shape the cultural and institutional environments that enable or hinder technological adoption. This perspective is supported by the observation that in France, the persistence of conservative ideologies and religious dogma suppressed economic progress for centuries, even as external examples of growth emerged from more liberal societies. Similarly, in Spain, the dominance of conservative values delayed economic modernization until relatively recent times, despite the availability of technological and commercial opportunities. These cases illustrate how the diffusion of new ideas can catalyze institutional change, creating the conditions necessary for sustained growth. The interplay between ideological shifts and institutional adaptation is further emphasized by the role of bourgeois values in fostering innovation and economic dynamism, as seen in the Netherlands and later in France. This stream of scholarship highlights the importance of cultural and ideological frameworks in shaping the trajectory of economic development. The implications of this view suggest that understanding the diffusion of ideas is as critical as analyzing institutional structures in explaining long-run growth.

Temin (2002, p.6) contends that the Golden Age of European growth was rooted in pre-war disequilibrium, which created conditions for rapid expansion. This perspective is supported by Temin (2002, p.1), who argues that the unique combination of pre-war imbalances and post-war institutional arrangements fostered an environment conducive to sustained economic growth. The mechanism through which this occurred lies in the interplay between productive investments and the institutional frameworks that emerged in the aftermath of World War II. These institutions, both domestic and international, were designed to stabilize the long-term implicit contracts between capital and labor, thereby reducing the incentives for short-term reneging and promoting cooperation. The implication of this view is that the post-war period did not merely react to pre-existing conditions but actively transformed them, creating a new equilibrium that sustained growth for decades. This suggests that the role of institutions is to enforce existing agreements and to reshape the economic landscape in ways that align with broader historical forces. The focus on pre-war disequilibrium shifts the emphasis from policy design to the structural conditions that enabled policy effectiveness, offering a more integrated understanding of how institutions contribute to long-run economic growth.

The central agreement across these studies is that institutions are foundational to long-run economic growth, shaping the distribution of resources and the trajectory of development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities, reinforcing the idea that the structure of political and economic institutions determines growth outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further assert that secure rights and economic growth are fostered by specific institutional arrangements, such as representative government and the rule of law. These claims underscore the necessity of institutional design in enabling sustained economic progress. However, a key disagreement persists regarding the origins of these institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that institutional differences stem from historical conditions, such as pre-colonial factor endowments and the legacies of colonial rule, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the role of precolonial history in shaping current institutions, suggesting that historical analysis is essential to understanding contemporary development challenges. The resolution to this disagreement lies in the comparative analysis of institutional legacies across regions, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1), who use historical case studies to trace how institutional frameworks evolved and influenced long-term economic performance.
