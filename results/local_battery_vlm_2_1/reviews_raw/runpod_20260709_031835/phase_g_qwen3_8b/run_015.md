The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and contemporary policy effectiveness. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of sustained growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or political conflict, play a more decisive role. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional structures are the key causal mechanism or if they are shaped by other underlying forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutions have played a crucial role in explaining income differences among former colonies, emphasizing their impact on economic performance and industrialization. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) similarly highlight how specific institutional arrangements, such as secure property rights and representative government, foster economic growth and political freedom. These claims suggest that institutions are not merely passive outcomes but active determinants of long-term economic trajectories. The causal mechanisms through which institutions operate remain contested some scholars emphasizing their historical persistence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8) and others questioning their role in the face of alternative explanations. The stakes lie in determining whether institutional change is a viable pathway for development or if it is constrained by deeper structural forces. This theme of institutional causality sets the stage for examining how institutions are shaped by their antecedents.

Institutional arrangements that secure property rights are fundamental to sustained economic growth. Similarly, North (1989, p.1) traces the evolution of common law in England, showing how historical institutional developments laid the groundwork for sustained economic progress. These scholars collectively emphasize that secure property rights are not merely a byproduct of economic success but a prerequisite for it. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who links the efficiency of training institutions to the skill premium and, by extension, to broader economic performance. Pamuk (2007, p.25) reinforces this by showing how flexible institutional structures allowed certain regions to adapt more effectively to post-Black Death economic conditions, contributing to the 'Great Divergence.' Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that modern economic growth requires not only technological advancement but also institutional adjustments that align with new modes of production. Together, these sources illustrate that institutional arrangements that secure property rights create the conditions for sustained economic growth by enabling innovation, investment, and efficient resource allocation. This focus on property rights as a foundational mechanism for economic development sets the stage for examining how institutional change can be a viable pathway for development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth by determining the trajectory of political and economic systems, a claim supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) who show how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists in African societies. These studies highlight how institutional legacies from colonial rule influenced the development of political structures and social norms, which in turn affected economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.19) complements this by demonstrating that the slave trade disrupted local economies and weakened the capacity of societies to develop robust institutions. Together, these sources illustrate that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic performance, shaping the conditions for growth or stagnation. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the historical roots of institutions is crucial for explaining contemporary economic disparities. This focus on institutional persistence sets the stage for examining how institutional change can be a viable pathway for development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that institutions are central to understanding long-run economic growth, emphasizing their role in shaping economic performance, a claim shared by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), Temin (2002, p.6), and Stern (2004, p.2). These scholars collectively highlight how institutional frameworks influence both the trajectory and sustainability of growth, whether through the enforcement of long-term contracts, the regulation of market dynamics, or the management of environmental trade-offs. Similarly, Temin (2002, p.6) shows how postwar institutions in Europe fostered productivity gains by aligning incentives across domestic and international actors. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) further underscores that past institutional commitments can either support or hinder future growth, depending on their alignment with technological and educational advancements. This shared focus on institutional influence reveals a critical pattern: the durability of institutional arrangements determines the long-term capacity of economies to adapt and grow. The implications of these findings suggest that institutional design is not merely a backdrop to growth but a dynamic force that shapes its direction and limits.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth, challenging the primacy of institutions as the fundamental cause. This claim is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who highlights the differentiated causal relationships between institutions and growth, emphasizing that historical contexts and path dependence play a crucial role. Hopkins (2009, p.8) reinforces this by showing how historical institutional analysis is essential for understanding contemporary African development policies, as current institutions are deeply rooted in precolonial and colonial legacies. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design is further underscored by McCloskey (2015, p.9), who argues that cultural and ideological factors, rather than mere institutional structures, are central to explaining economic outcomes. These sources collectively demonstrate that institutional change is not an isolated process but one deeply embedded in broader social and political dynamics. This insight suggests that policies aimed at fostering economic growth must account for the distributional implications of institutional arrangements, as they are not merely technical constraints but sources of enduring economic influence.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) contends that customary systems in Africa are not merely relics of the past but active forces shaping long-run economic growth, offering an alternative to institutional explanations. This claim is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who highlight the central role of women in pre-colonial and colonial economies, showing how gendered labor and resource allocation shaped economic trajectories. Together, these sources suggest that customary systems are not static but dynamic, influencing economic outcomes through embedded norms and practices. Peters (2004, p.19) further illustrates this by showing how land conflicts and class formation in Africa reveal the enduring impact of customary institutions on economic relations, challenging the notion of flexible and negotiable systems. The interplay between customary norms and economic outcomes is thus not incidental but foundational, as these systems mediate access to resources and shape labor markets. This dynamic underscores the need to consider historical and cultural contexts when analyzing economic growth, as they are not separate from but deeply integrated with institutional frameworks. The persistence of customary systems in shaping economic outcomes highlights the limitations of purely institutional analyses in capturing the full complexity of long-run development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the consensus that institutional frameworks significantly influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This agreement is evident across multiple streams of scholarship, which collectively highlight the enduring impact of institutional structures on development trajectories. The precise mechanisms through which institutions operate remain a point of contention with some scholars emphasizing path dependence and others focusing on distributional conflicts. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism are central to explaining income disparities among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), while Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts provide a more compelling explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The disagreement centers on whether institutional persistence is primarily a result of historical path dependencies or rooted in persistent social and political tensions. This debate is resolved through the comparative analysis of colonial institutions in former European colonies, where the persistence of extractive institutions is demonstrated through historical case studies and empirical evidence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These cases illustrate how institutional legacies, shaped by colonial rule, continue to influence economic performance long after formal colonial powers have withdrawn.
