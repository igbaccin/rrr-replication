The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional structures are the primary determinants of sustained prosperity or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological progress. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are the root cause of growth or merely a consequence of broader historical and environmental conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, shaped by colonial policies and population dynamics, explain variations in economic performance across former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that these institutions perpetuated inequality by favoring elites through restrictive policies. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are central to economic trajectories, yet they diverge in their interpretation of the mechanisms through which institutions operate. North (1989, p.1) frames institutions as the key obstacle to growth, contrasting them with neoclassical theories that prioritize savings and population. This stream underscores the analytical necessity of understanding institutions as historical constructs that shape economic outcomes. Meanwhile, Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the interplay between institutions and historical legacies, suggesting that current institutional frameworks are deeply rooted in precolonial and colonial histories. These streams collectively affirm that institutions are not just structural features but dynamic forces that influence the trajectory of economic development. The stakes lie in whether policymakers can harness institutional design to foster inclusive growth or whether they must contend with the enduring legacies of historical institutions.

These scholars demonstrate that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule persisted into the postcolonial era, influencing economic outcomes through mechanisms such as political inequality and extractive governance. This shared insight highlights how colonial history embedded institutional patterns that continue to affect economic trajectories. The persistence of colonial institutions, therefore, is not merely a historical curiosity but a structural condition that continues to shape economic growth. This suggests that understanding the historical roots of institutions is essential for addressing contemporary economic disparities.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial institutions in Africa, shaped by the slave trade, have left a lasting imprint on economic growth by fostering a culture of mistrust that hinders development. This claim is reinforced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who show that historical institutions in Africa, including those related to resource extraction and labor, have had enduring effects on economic outcomes. Together, these studies highlight how institutional legacies from colonialism have created structural barriers to trust and cooperation, which are critical for sustained economic growth. The persistence of these institutions is evident in the way they continue to influence patterns of economic activity and social interaction, even in the absence of direct colonial control. Nunn (2008, p.19) further supports this by showing that the slave trade not only disrupted local economies but also contributed to the development of institutions that were inherently exclusionary and detrimental to long-term growth. These findings collectively underscore the idea that the historical roots of institutions are not merely relics of the past but active forces shaping contemporary economic trajectories. The legacy of these institutions continues to affect the distribution of economic opportunities and the capacity of societies to engage in productive and cooperative economic activities.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts offer a more compelling explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions and their impact on long-run growth than economic efficiency. This perspective is reinforced by McCloskey (2015, p.9), who emphasizes the role of ideas and ideologies in shaping institutional frameworks, challenging the primacy of material interests in economic development. Together, these sources suggest that the evolution of institutions is deeply intertwined with the contestation over resource allocation and power, rather than being primarily driven by efficiency considerations. The interplay between distributional tensions and institutional design is evident in how certain economic arrangements persist despite their inefficiencies, as institutional interlinkages create self-reinforcing systems that resist change. This dynamic is further illustrated by the historical persistence of exclusionary practices, which often reflect underlying conflicts over access to resources and political influence. The implications of this argument extend to the understanding of how cultural and ideological factors can shape the trajectory of economic institutions, influencing both their form and their capacity to foster growth. These insights highlight the need to consider the distributional underpinnings of institutions when analyzing their long-term economic consequences.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores that long-run economic growth hinges on the interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation, as these factors enable more efficient resource utilization and foster innovation. This argument is reinforced by Stern (2004, p.2), who highlights the necessity of rethinking development-environment relationships through models that account for the complexities of institutional and technological change. Both authors emphasize that growth is not merely a function of resource availability but is deeply contingent on the capacity of institutions to evolve alongside technological advancements. The historical divergence in institutional trajectories, as noted by Kuznets, illustrates how different societies have adopted varying mechanisms to support sustained economic development, often in response to unique environmental and social conditions. Stern’s critique of the environmental Kuznets curve further illustrates the limitations of static models in capturing the dynamic interplay between institutions, technology, and economic outcomes. These insights suggest that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their ability to align with technological progress and adapt to changing economic conditions. The interplay between institutional frameworks and technological innovation remains central to understanding the long-term trajectories of economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions shaping training and access to professions drive long-term economic performance, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) who show that variations in urbanization rates between Europe and Asia predate 1800 and correlate with divergent economic trajectories. These authors highlight how institutional frameworks regulating the transmission of skills and professional access influenced the efficiency of labor markets, thereby affecting wage levels and economic growth. The role of institutions in structuring access to professions is further illustrated by Pamuk (2007, p.25), who links institutional flexibility to the ability of regions to adapt to economic shocks such as the Black Death, with more adaptable institutions fostering sustained wage growth and economic resilience. Temin (2002, p.6) extends this argument by showing how postwar institutions in Europe reinforced long-term productivity gains through mechanisms that aligned worker and investor incentives, demonstrating the broader impact of institutional design on economic performance. The interplay between institutional structures and the organization of professional training thus emerges as a key mechanism through which institutions shape long-term economic outcomes. This focus on institutional regulation of skill transmission and professional access provides a foundation for understanding how institutional frameworks can sustain or hinder economic development across time.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by the historical expansion of educational institutions and their role in disseminating technical expertise (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.19). This stream emphasizes that knowledge accumulation, rather than institutional structures alone, drives long-term economic performance. The proliferation of universities and academies in the 18th century, such as those in Glasgow and Edinburgh, illustrates how formal education systems contributed to the spread of scientific and technical knowledge, which in turn fueled industrial and economic transformation (Mokyr 2005, p.28). Abramovitz (1986, p.19) further supports this by highlighting that the catch-up process in productivity is not merely a function of technology but is also shaped by the educational and institutional legacies of past development, which can either facilitate or hinder progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.19). These sources collectively suggest that the diffusion of knowledge across societies is a critical mechanism through which economic growth is sustained. The implication is that institutional frameworks, while important, must be understood as complements to the broader process of knowledge creation and dissemination. This perspective challenges the primacy of institutions as the sole or primary cause of economic growth, shifting the focus to the dynamic interplay between knowledge accumulation and institutional adaptation.

The literature consistently underscores the pivotal role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. However the mechanisms through which institutions exert this influence remain contested with Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizing that distributional conflicts provide a more robust explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions. This divergence highlights a fundamental debate over whether institutions are primarily shaped by material interests or ideological values. The resolution of this disagreement lies in the comparative analysis of historical institutional frameworks, as exemplified by North (1989, p.1), who traces the evolution of institutions in England and Spain to show how institutional legacies persist and influence economic trajectories. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the necessity of historical investigation to understand how precolonial and colonial institutions have shaped contemporary African development. These sources collectively affirm that institutional analysis must integrate both structural and cultural dimensions to fully capture their impact on economic growth.
