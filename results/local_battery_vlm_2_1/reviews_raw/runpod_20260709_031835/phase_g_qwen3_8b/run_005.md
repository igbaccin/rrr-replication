The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with intellectual stakes in understanding how historical legacies influence contemporary prosperity. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of growth, or whether other factors such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion play a more decisive role. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions operate and whether they are the dominant force in economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' responses to population density and prosperity, explain persistent income disparities among former colonies. North (1989, p.1) reinforces this by emphasizing that institutional frameworks such as property rights and legal systems determine the trajectory of economic growth while neoclassical models prioritize savings and population. These accounts highlight institutions as the bedrock of sustained development, yet they are frequently challenged by alternative explanations. Hopkins (2009, p.8) acknowledges that institutions are the product of complex historical processes, including precolonial traditions, while Mokyr (2005, p.54) shifts focus to the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic progress. The tension between institutional determinism and knowledge-driven growth underscores the unresolved nature of the debate, with implications for how we interpret the historical roots of modern economic systems.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that resonates with Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes the necessity of technological advancement alongside institutional adjustments for sustained economic development. Both authors underscore the interplay between knowledge creation and the institutional frameworks that support its diffusion and application. Mokyr highlights how institutional progress complements technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements, while Kuznets illustrates how modern technology demands institutional and social transformations that align with its requirements. This synergy between knowledge and institutions is further reinforced by Temin (2002, p.6), who demonstrates how postwar European institutions fostered productive investments and long-term economic stability by mitigating short-term incentives that could undermine growth. The role of institutions in enabling and sustaining knowledge-driven growth is thus not merely supportive but foundational, shaping the conditions under which technological progress can translate into sustained economic development. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) adds a dimension to this argument by noting that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, indicating that the legacy of institutions influences contemporary trajectories of growth. These sources collectively establish that the institutional environment is a critical enabler of knowledge-based economic growth, with implications for how we understand the historical and ongoing dynamics of development.

The persistence of these institutional legacies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores how colonial-era control structures often outlived the colonial regime itself, embedding patterns of governance that either constrained or enabled economic progress. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that secure property rights and inclusive political institutions are critical for fostering economic growth, as they provide the framework for credible commitment and reduce uncertainty for investment. These sources collectively demonstrate that the institutional environment, shaped by historical colonial practices, is a decisive factor in determining the long-term economic performance of nations. The implications of this are profound, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who note that institutional arrangements in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, thereby stunting broader economic development. This mechanism of institutional persistence and its impact on growth dynamics is central to understanding the divergent paths of economic development across former colonies. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with the historical legacies of colonial rule.

Pamuk (2007, p.25) argues that flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England enabled wage stability after the Black Death, which in turn fostered long-term economic divergence. This mechanism is supported by the observation that regions with more adaptable structures and institutions were better positioned to respond to shifting economic conditions, as noted by Pamuk (2007, p.25). The persistence of these institutions is further linked to the broader phenomenon of the 'Great Divergence,' with Pamuk (2007, p.21) suggesting that the Black Death accelerated the decline in interest rates, which was facilitated by pre-existing favorable institutional arrangements. This institutional flexibility not only stabilized wages but also created a structural advantage that allowed these regions to outperform others in Europe. The implications of this are significant, as they highlight how institutional adaptability can shape long-term economic trajectories. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus not only about their initial design but also about their capacity to evolve in response to transformative events.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists as a barrier to long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by evidence that links historical exposure to the slave trade with diminished levels of trust, a factor shown to be critical for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The argument suggests that institutions are not the sole determinant of growth, as the legacy of the slave trade created social conditions that undermine trust, a key ingredient for economic cooperation and investment. This historical legacy, as Nunn (2008, p.19) highlights, also affected the ability of African societies to engage in trade with Europeans, further entrenching structural disadvantages. The persistence of mistrust, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon, implies that economic growth may be constrained by cultural factors that are not easily addressed through institutional reform alone. These findings suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth must be understood alongside the enduring effects of historical trauma, which can shape the social and economic environment in ways that are difficult to reverse. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional structures thus offers a nuanced perspective on the drivers of economic development.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies are central to economic growth, challenging the primacy of institutions in shaping long-run development (McCloskey 2015, p.9). His work highlights how cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values in early modern Europe, fostered an environment conducive to innovation and entrepreneurship. This aligns with the broader claim that ideological frameworks, rather than institutional structures alone, can drive economic transformation (McCloskey 2015, p.7). For instance, the spread of Enlightenment ideas in France and the Netherlands not only redefined social norms but also created a climate where economic progress was perceived as both desirable and achievable. Such ideological shifts, McCloskey suggests, can alter the incentives and expectations that underpin economic behavior, thereby influencing long-term growth trajectories. This perspective contests the notion that institutions are the sole or primary mechanism through which economic development occurs. Instead, it posits that the diffusion of ideas can reshape the very conditions that make institutional change possible. The implications of this view are profound, as they suggest that the success of institutional reforms may depend on the prevailing ideological climate. This stream of scholarship thus reframes the debate by emphasizing the dynamic interplay between ideas and economic outcomes, offering a compelling alternative to traditional institutionalist explanations.

The consensus across the literature is that institutions are foundational to long-run economic growth, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who demonstrate that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. North (1989, p.1) similarly underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, arguing that they are central to understanding historical patterns of growth. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutional frameworks whether rooted in colonial legacies or evolved through historical processes directly influence the trajectory of economic development. However the debate persists over whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors such as ideas and historical conditions play a more decisive role. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight the enduring impact of initial conditions and institutional legacies, suggesting that the diffusion of ideas and the persistence of extractive structures have shaped development paths in the New World. This disagreement hinges on the relative weight assigned to institutional design versus historical and ideological contexts. The resolution of this debate lies in comparative analyses of institutional persistence and its interaction with economic incentives, as exemplified by the cross-country regressions and historical case studies employed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and Hopkins (2009, p.8).
