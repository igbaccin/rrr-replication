The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological change play a more dominant role. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, with some emphasizing their role as exogenous constraints and others highlighting their endogenous evolution. (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) argues that institutions in former European colonies were shaped by colonial policies, which in turn affected long-term economic performance, while (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) supports the idea that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. These perspectives underscore the intellectual stakes of the debate: whether institutions are the key to unlocking sustained growth or if they are merely the result of deeper historical forces. The tension between these views reflects a broader struggle to reconcile historical analysis with modern economic theory. This review explores how different streams of scholarship address this question, focusing on the causal pathways through which institutions shape economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that specific historical institutions, both pre-colonial and colonial, are the key to understanding long-run economic growth in Africa, a claim supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who highlight the centrality of women to production and reproduction in pre-colonial and colonial contexts. These scholars collectively demonstrate that institutional frameworks, whether rooted in customary systems or colonial governance, shaped the trajectory of economic development by influencing labor markets, resource allocation, and access to productive assets. The interplay between these institutions and broader economic forces, such as trade and foreign investment, created divergent paths of growth across regions. For instance, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.27) show how colonial states in East Africa implemented more direct labor market interventions due to institutional design shaped by precolonial commercial development. This institutional legacy, in turn, influenced wage levels and labor mobility, reinforcing patterns of economic performance that persisted into the modern era. The stream underscores that historical institutions are not static but evolve through interaction with economic and political dynamics, thereby shaping long-run growth outcomes. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic forces provides a foundation for understanding how historical legacies continue to influence contemporary development trajectories.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that economic growth necessitates both technological advancement and institutional adjustments, a claim echoed by Mokyr (2005, p.54), who emphasizes the interdependence of technological progress and institutional reform. The sustainability of economic improvement, according to Kuznets, hinges on the alignment of institutions with the demands of modern technology, which cannot coexist with outdated practices such as slavery or illiteracy. Mokyr further illustrates this point by highlighting how the diffusion of scientific knowledge in the 18th century was constrained by institutional barriers, such as the limited role of formal education in disseminating applied science. These sources collectively underscore that institutional frameworks must evolve alongside technological change to sustain long-term growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) adds that past institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, suggesting that the legacy of earlier development shapes the trajectory of future growth. This interplay between historical institutions and technological progress implies that economic development is not a linear process but one shaped by the continuous adaptation of institutions to new technological realities. The implications of this dynamic are further explored by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who demonstrate how variations in institutional development across regions influenced patterns of urbanization and economic performance, reinforcing the idea that institutions are a fundamental driver of long-run growth.

Institutions shaped by colonial history are upstream determinants of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) jointly argue that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule laid the foundation for divergent economic trajectories, with extractive systems persisting long after independence. These institutional differences, rooted in colonial history, not only shaped the political and economic environment but also determined the long-term prospects for development. The persistence of these institutions, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores how colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, reinforcing the idea that historical institutional choices have lasting consequences. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further illustrate that secure property rights and institutional arrangements, which were often absent or constrained in colonial settings, are essential for fostering economic growth and political freedom. This stream collectively establishes that the institutional architecture of colonial rule has had a profound and enduring impact on the economic development of former colonies, shaping the conditions under which growth has occurred or been constrained.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the legacy of the slave trade in Africa has left a profound imprint on the cultural fabric of the region, fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust that persists into the present. This cultural mistrust, as Nunn (2008, p.19) elaborates, is a structural feature that impedes economic cooperation and institutional trust and constrains long-run growth. The institutional degradation associated with the slave trade, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), is further compounded by the underdevelopment of legal and political institutions, which fail to provide the security and predictability necessary for sustained economic activity. van Zanden (2009, p.19) underscores that the efficiency of training institutions and the organization of skill transmission are critical determinants of the skill premium and, by extension, economic performance. The historical resistance to wage declines in the Low Countries and England, as highlighted by Pamuk (2007, p.25), illustrates how institutional flexibility can foster economic resilience and divergence. These findings collectively suggest that the institutional and cultural legacies of historical exploitation have shaped the trajectory of economic development in ways that are both enduring and multifaceted. The interplay between institutional quality and cultural norms, as McCloskey (2015, p.9) argues, reveals that ideas and social values are integral to the formation and function of institutions. This interplay has profound implications for understanding the uneven development across regions and the challenges of institutional reform in post-colonial contexts.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that historical institutional analysis is essential for understanding African development and economic history. By emphasizing the continuity of institutions from precolonial to contemporary times, these authors demonstrate how institutional legacies shape the trajectory of economic outcomes in Africa. The congruence between indigenous and modern institutions, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.8), enhances the legitimacy of governance and fosters conditions conducive to development. Low levels of congruence lead to patrimonial politics, regionalism, and corruption which impede growth. The implication is that institutional analysis must account for both the persistence of historical structures and the dynamic forces that reshape them over time. This dual focus underscores the necessity of integrating historical depth with contemporary institutional frameworks to fully grasp the drivers of long-run economic growth in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the consensus that historical institutional legacies significantly influence contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This agreement is evident across multiple streams of scholarship, which collectively emphasize the enduring impact of institutions on development trajectories. While the mechanisms through which institutions operate differ, the central claim remains that institutional structures determine the conditions for growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The divergence lies in the interpretation of how these institutions originated and evolved, with some scholars attributing their persistence to colonial legacies, while others highlight the role of precolonial and historical factors (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This disagreement is resolved through the comparative analysis of institutional frameworks across regions, which reveals that the congruence between contemporary institutions and indigenous historical practices enhances their effectiveness in promoting growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The empirical comparison of institutional persistence in former colonies, such as Australia and the United States, further illustrates how the initial institutional arrangements established during colonial rule have had lasting effects on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). By integrating historical depth with contemporary institutional frameworks, scholars provide a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.
