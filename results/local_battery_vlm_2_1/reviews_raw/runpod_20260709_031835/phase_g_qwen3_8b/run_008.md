The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of sustained growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion, play a more decisive role. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional frameworks are the key causal mechanism or if they are shaped by deeper, preexisting conditions that also influence growth trajectories. These competing perspectives underscore the complexity of identifying the true drivers of long-run growth. The tension between institutional causality and alternative explanations remains unresolved, setting the stage for deeper exploration of how institutions interact with other historical forces.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Temin (2002, p.6) argue that historical legacies and structural factors, as forms of institutions, are fundamental to long-run economic growth. The causal relationships involved are more differentiated than is recognised in AJR's formulations, as historical legacies shape the conditions under which institutions can either enable or constrain growth. These mechanisms suggest that institutions are not merely static structures but dynamic forces that evolve in response to historical contexts and economic pressures. Pamuk (2007, p.25) reinforces this by showing how flexible structures and institutions in the Low Countries and England allowed for a better response to the long-term environment after the Black Death, thereby fostering a wage gap with the rest of Europe. This underscores the role of institutions in adapting to and shaping economic trajectories over time. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this argument by demonstrating that the great divergence between Europe and Asia began before 1800, highlighting how institutional differences in urbanization and economic organization contributed to divergent growth paths. These sources collectively suggest that institutions are deeply embedded in historical processes and that their evolution is critical to understanding long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional structures remains a central theme in explaining the trajectories of economic development.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either promote or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional legacies from colonial rule created enduring structural barriers to growth, particularly in the New World, by entrenching extractive systems that favored elites. These frameworks, as North (1989, p.1) emphasizes, are not static but evolve through historical processes, shaped by the interplay of power, law, and economic organization. The persistence of these institutions, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate, often outlives the colonial era, influencing contemporary economic outcomes through their impact on property rights, political freedom, and resource allocation. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further clarify that secure property rights and inclusive political institutions are not merely byproducts of growth but essential conditions for it, as they enable long-term investment and innovation. This stream collectively underscores that the trajectory of economic development is deeply intertwined with the historical formation of institutions, which either enable or constrain the mechanisms of growth. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the origins and evolution of institutions is critical to explaining the divergent paths of economic development across regions.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) demonstrates that institutions regulating the training of apprentices and access to professions significantly influence the skill premium, which in turn shapes long-term economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further show that political institutions interact with trade and export dynamics to determine patterns of economic growth and contraction. Together, these studies highlight how institutional frameworks governing human capital formation are critical to sustaining economic development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) complement this by showing that institutions also shape the conditions under which economies can expand or shrink, depending on their alignment with market forces. This stream collectively argues that the regulation of professional training and labor markets is a foundational mechanism through which institutions influence economic trajectories. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the historical evolution of institutional norms in shaping labor markets is essential for explaining long-term economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the legacy of the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists as a barrier to economic development. This claim is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who emphasizes the need to examine historical institutional legacies to understand contemporary African development challenges. The persistent distrust stemming from colonial and precolonial extractive institutions, as documented by Nunn (2008, p.19), is not merely a byproduct but a structural condition that undermines economic cooperation and institutional legitimacy. This pattern is further reinforced by Peters (2004, p.19), who highlights how land conflicts and exclusionary practices in Africa reflect deeper historical patterns of institutional degradation. The implications of these findings suggest that addressing long-term economic outcomes requires more than policy reform; it necessitates a reckoning with the historical roots of institutional distrust. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) add a crucial dimension by showing that gender discrimination, rooted in similar historical structures, has also shaped economic participation and growth. Together, these sources underscore the enduring impact of colonial and extractive institutions on social and economic development, framing trust as a critical variable in the institutional shaping of economic trajectories.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that economic growth hinges on the interplay between technological advancement and institutional adaptation, a view reinforced by Mokyr (2005, p.54). Both authors emphasize that while technological progress is a catalyst for growth, its long-term sustainability depends on complementary institutional reforms that mitigate the risks of rent-seeking and inefficiency. This dual emphasis on knowledge and institutions highlights the necessity of a coordinated approach to development, where neither factor operates in isolation. The historical examples provided by Kuznets illustrate how modern technologies such as steam and electric power could not function within the constraints of traditional social and economic structures, necessitating institutional transformations to support their integration. Mokyr further elaborates on this by showing how early industrialization in Britain was constrained by the limited capacity of formal educational institutions to disseminate scientific knowledge, despite the presence of alternative learning spaces like Scottish universities and private academies. These institutions, though reaching only a narrow elite, played a critical role in reducing access costs and fostering a culture of innovation. The interplay between technological progress and institutional change, as presented by these authors, suggests that economic growth is not merely a function of one factor but requires a synergistic relationship between the two. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone can drive sustained economic development, underscoring the need for a balanced focus on both technological and institutional dimensions.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies, rather than institutions, are the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, a stance shared by Stern (2004, p.2) in its critique of the environmental Kuznets curve. Both authors emphasize the transformative power of cultural and intellectual shifts in shaping economic trajectories, arguing that these forces can override structural constraints. McCloskey illustrates this through historical cases where ideological commitments to science and progress, such as the French motto 'Pour la Patrie, les Sciences et la Gloire,' fostered innovation and growth, while conservative ideologies stifled economic development. Similarly, Stern highlights how economic development and environmental outcomes are not inherently linked through institutional frameworks but are instead shaped by evolving ideological narratives and policy paradigms. This perspective challenges the assumption that institutional stability alone can sustain growth, suggesting instead that ideological shifts can catalyze or hinder economic progress. The interplay between ideas and economic outcomes, as demonstrated by these authors, underscores the need to consider cultural and intellectual dynamics alongside institutional structures. This focus on ideas as a primary force in economic development opens new avenues for understanding the long-term drivers of growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely recognized as central to understanding development trajectories, as emphasized by North (1989, p.1), who argues that institutional frameworks are critical to economic outcomes. This consensus is further reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who demonstrate that colonial-era institutions persistently influence contemporary economic performance, underscoring the long-term significance of institutional legacies. Both authors highlight how institutional structures, whether shaped by colonial rule or historical path dependencies, create enduring conditions that either enable or constrain growth. The sources diverge on the relative importance of institutional versus geographic factors in explaining these outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that while institutions are crucial, they must be understood within the context of initial conditions, such as factor endowments and environmental constraints, which also shape long-term development paths. This disagreement is resolved through empirical comparisons of institutional and geographic variables across regions, as seen in the cross-country regressions used by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Hopkins (2009, p.8), which provide measurable evidence of institutional persistence and its impact on growth.
