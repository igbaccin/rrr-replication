The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history, with implications for understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained development. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more decisive role. Scholars such as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional differences, particularly those rooted in colonial governance, have a direct and measurable impact on economic performance, accounting for a significant fraction of current income disparities. This view is complemented by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutions shape the conditions under which economic activity can flourish, offering a framework to analyze the historical obstacles to growth. Together, these works underscore the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that channel resources, incentives, and opportunities in ways that either enable or constrain long-term development. The stakes lie in identifying the mechanisms through which institutions operate, as this has profound implications for policy design and the interpretation of historical trajectories. The next section explores how institutional legacies, particularly those shaped by colonialism, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks that enable development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1) demonstrate that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, particularly in the New World, created enduring structures that either constrained or facilitated economic progress. These frameworks, often rooted in extractive or inclusive political systems, determined the trajectory of resource allocation, property rights, and governance. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions in former European colonies were shaped by the interplay of population density and prosperity, which influenced the policies pursued by European settlers. This dynamic, they suggest, had lasting effects on the institutional quality of these regions, shaping their economic performance over time. North (1989, p.8) further emphasizes that institutions are not static but evolve through historical processes, with the initial conditions of colonial rule setting the stage for long-term institutional development. The persistence of these early structures, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.5), underscores the importance of understanding institutional legacies as critical determinants of economic outcomes. The implications of these findings are profound, as they highlight the need to consider historical institutional paths when analyzing contemporary economic disparities. This focus on institutional durability and its influence on development sets the stage for examining how these frameworks continue to shape modern economic trajectories.

Colonial and historical legacies shape long-run economic growth by influencing institutions that affect development trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and undermines economic cooperation. This finding is reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, highlighting the role of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes. The persistence of these historical patterns is further illustrated by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who argue that African real wages were above subsistence levels and rose over time, challenging the notion of persistent structural impediments to growth. These studies collectively suggest that historical institutional legacies, whether through the spread of mistrust or the establishment of market-oriented institutions, have lasting impacts on economic development. Such insights underscore the importance of understanding institutional evolution as a key driver of long-term growth, setting the stage for examining how contemporary institutions continue to shape economic performance.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) argues that historical institutions shaped economic outcomes, including poverty, by creating conditions of dependence and underdevelopment, a claim supported by Austin (2008, p.2) who emphasizes the differentiated causal relationships between historical legacies and contemporary economic performance. Both authors highlight how institutions, whether colonial or precolonial, have structured economic possibilities and constrained growth trajectories. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further elaborates that poverty was not an original condition but a historical creation, stemming from the integration of non-European societies into capitalist networks that fostered dependency. The stream underscores that institutional structures, rooted in historical processes, have long-term implications for development, shaping not only economic performance but also the capacity for policy effectiveness. Hopkins (2009, p.8) adds that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth if they align with indigenous systems, a condition that reflects the enduring influence of historical institutional configurations. This alignment enhances legitimacy and fosters development, while misalignment perpetuates underdevelopment through patrimonial politics. The cumulative argument suggests that understanding institutional evolution is essential for formulating effective development policies, as historical legacies continue to condition economic possibilities. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with historical processes that have structured economic outcomes over time.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, shaping long-term economic performance through institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Kuznets 1973, p.1; Abramovitz 1986, p.19). This claim is supported by the observation that institutional frameworks are essential for translating technological advancements into sustained economic gains. For instance, the development of formal educational institutions in the 18th century, such as the Scottish universities and Warrington Academy, played a critical role in disseminating scientific knowledge and fostering a culture of innovation (Mokyr 2005, p.28). These institutions not only reduced access costs but also laid the groundwork for the institutional progress necessary to sustain economic growth. The implication is that without institutional progress, the benefits of technological innovation may not be sustained, leading to stagnation or even regression. Thus, the interplay between knowledge growth and institutional development is fundamental to understanding long-run economic performance.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are the primary catalysts for economic growth, challenging the conventional focus on institutional frameworks. This argument is supported by historical cases where cultural and intellectual shifts preceded and facilitated economic transformation. For instance, the French experience illustrates how conservative ideologies, such as the prioritization of Catholicism and aristocratic values, hindered economic progress until the bourgeois rhetoric of modernity gained traction. Similarly, the Netherlands' transition from a conservative to a more commercially oriented society is attributed to the emergence of a new ideological climate that embraced innovation and trade. These examples suggest that the diffusion of progressive ideas can reshape economic outcomes independently of institutional structures. The interplay between ideological change and economic development is further emphasized by the notion that new ideas can alter the conditions of production and consumption, thereby generating new economic opportunities. This perspective implies that the role of institutions is secondary to the power of ideas in driving long-term growth. The implications of this view are significant, as they suggest that policies aimed at fostering ideological openness and intellectual freedom may be as crucial as those targeting institutional reform.

Temin (2002, p.6) contends that pre-war disequilibrium, rather than institutional reforms, was the primary driver of long-run economic growth in post-war Europe. This claim is supported by Stern (2004, p.2), who, while focusing on environmental economics, highlights the importance of contextual conditions in shaping economic trajectories. Temin’s analysis suggests that the postwar period was marked by a unique set of imbalances that created opportunities for rapid expansion, as investors pursued productive investments that heightened labor productivity. These imbalances were compounded by the absence of prewar equilibrium, which allowed for a surge in growth that was both unprecedented and short-lived. The mechanism through which this disequilibrium translated into growth lies in the incentives it created for both capital and labor, which were only partially mitigated by the institutions that emerged in the postwar era. Stern’s work, though not directly about growth, reinforces the idea that the conditions under which economic processes unfold are as critical as the institutions that govern them. The implication of this view is that understanding the historical context of economic development requires a focus on the structural shifts that preceded institutional change. This perspective challenges the centrality of institutions in explaining long-run growth, suggesting that the conditions of disequilibrium may have been the more fundamental force shaping economic outcomes.

The consensus across the literature is that institutions are central to long-run economic growth, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) who demonstrate that colonial institutions persistently shape income per capita. This view is reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who argues that institutional frameworks, rather than population or savings, determine economic outcomes. Both papers emphasize that the historical embedding of institutions creates enduring structural constraints that influence growth trajectories. The debate centers on whether these institutions are primarily shaped by geography or by historical processes of power and governance. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) contend that initial conditions, such as climate and disease, interact with institutional development to produce divergent paths of growth, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions, not geography, are the key determinant of long-term income differences. The resolution of this disagreement lies in the empirical comparison of institutional legacies across former colonies, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), which shows that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. The historical analysis of institutional persistence and its impact on growth provides a framework for understanding the mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes.
