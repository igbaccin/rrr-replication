The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, play a more fundamental role. The disagreement centers on whether institutional structures are endogenous, shaped by historical processes, or exogenous, determined by external forces. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history significantly affect income per capita, contra geographic factors, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) emphasize that institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies. These perspectives highlight the tension between viewing institutions as the root cause of economic outcomes or as outcomes themselves influenced by deeper historical and environmental factors. The stakes lie in determining whether institutional change is a viable pathway to development or whether it is constrained by preexisting conditions. This tension underscores the need to understand how institutions are formed and sustained, and whether they can be reformed to foster inclusive growth. The next section explores how institutional persistence is shaped by historical antecedents and the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic trajectories.

Institutional arrangements foster secure rights and long-run economic growth by shaping the rules of the game. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the financial revolution in England was instrumental in enabling economic growth and political freedom, highlighting how institutional frameworks can create conditions for sustained development. This claim is supported by North (1989, p.1), who traces the evolution of English institutions to the common law system, showing how historical precedents laid the groundwork for institutions that promoted economic stability. These institutional foundations, as emphasized by van Zanden (2009, p.19), also influence the efficiency of training institutions, which in turn affect the skill premium and long-term economic performance. The persistence of these institutional arrangements, as noted by Pamuk (2007, p.25), enables regions to adapt to changing economic environments, reinforcing the role of institutions in shaping divergent developmental trajectories. The interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes underscores how secure rights are not merely a result of economic forces but are actively shaped by the rules that govern economic activity. This relationship between institutions and growth provides a foundation for understanding how institutional reforms can influence long-term economic development.

Colonial institutions shaped by political forces influenced long-run economic growth through persistent institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.19) demonstrate that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, often reflecting the political priorities of European powers, persisted long after independence, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or extractive in nature, created enduring structures that either constrained or enabled economic development. This persistence underscores the deep-rooted impact of colonial governance on post-independence economic performance. The mechanisms through which these institutions influenced growth are further illuminated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3), who argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to hinder economic cooperation and development in many African regions. The implications of these institutional legacies extend beyond economic performance, influencing social cohesion and political stability in the long run.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that historical institutional analysis is essential for understanding the trajectory of African development, as institutions are not merely products of colonial rule but are deeply embedded in precolonial histories and social structures. This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2019, p.11) underscores the necessity of examining how historical legacies shape contemporary institutional frameworks and, by extension, economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that effective institutions are more likely to emerge when they align with indigenous systems, as such congruence enhances governmental legitimacy and fosters development-friendly policies. This mechanism is further reinforced by Hopkins (2019, p.11), which highlights how historical forces, including European capitalism, have historically structured underdevelopment through mechanisms of dependence and unequal exchange. These insights suggest that institutional analysis must account for both continuity and transformation across time and not treat institutions as static or solely shaped by external forces. The implications of this historical lens are profound, as they challenge conventional narratives that reduce development to geography or short-term policy choices. By integrating historical depth with institutional analysis, scholars can better address the structural barriers that persist in African economic growth.

Peters (2004, p.19) demonstrates that land conflicts in Africa are not merely disputes over resources but mechanisms of institutional exclusion that shape labor market dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) complement this by showing how colonial labor market interventions were rooted in the institutional design of colonial states, which in turn reflected precolonial commercial development patterns. Together, these sources reveal that land conflicts and colonial labor policies are intertwined in their capacity to constrain economic mobility and reinforce hierarchical power structures. The persistence of these institutional legacies, as Peters notes, has created labor markets where dependence on foreign firms and intermediaries has become a defining feature of economic engagement. Frankema and van Waijenburg further suggest that the institutional design of colonial labor markets was not arbitrary but shaped by the comparative returns to wage labor in different regions, which in turn influenced the long-term trajectory of economic growth. This interplay between land and labor institutions underscores the centrality of historical context in understanding contemporary economic constraints. The implications of these findings are significant for assessing how institutional exclusion continues to shape labor market outcomes and, by extension, long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a stance supported by Temin (2002, p.6) and McCloskey (2015, p.9). Ogilvie highlights how institutional frameworks, while analytically essential, often emerge from and are sustained by broader distributional tensions, where the interplay between beliefs and institutional rules shapes economic outcomes. Temin demonstrates how postwar European institutions emerged from pre-war disequilibrium reinforcing long-term contracts that aligned with broader social and economic interests. McCloskey further emphasizes that cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, played a pivotal role in enabling economic transformation, challenging the primacy of institutional design. These scholars collectively suggest that the trajectory of economic growth is deeply intertwined with the dynamics of power ideology and social cohesion and not solely with formal institutional structures. The implications of this perspective are profound, as they suggest that understanding the historical and ideological contexts of institutional development is crucial for explaining long-run economic performance. This focus on ideational and distributional forces opens new avenues for examining how economic growth is shaped by the interplay of beliefs, power, and institutional evolution.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that historical divergence, not institutions, is the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) who highlight the overlooked role of women in shaping economic trajectories. These scholars collectively suggest that divergent paths of development were rooted in geographic demographic and technological conditions and not institutional structures alone. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that variations in export dynamics and trade patterns were central to economic fluctuations in Sub-Saharan Africa, reinforcing the idea that external market forces and resource availability shaped growth trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.18) further illustrate how colonial institutions were designed to extract resources for European industrialization, underscoring the material and structural determinants of economic divergence. This stream of scholarship challenges the institutionalist focus by emphasizing that the divergence between regions was not merely a result of institutional differences but of deeper, historically embedded conditions that influenced productivity and resource allocation. The implications of this perspective are significant, as they suggest that understanding economic growth requires a broader historical analysis that incorporates environmental, demographic, and technological factors alongside institutional frameworks. The focus on these underlying conditions opens new avenues for examining how economic development was shaped by the interplay of material forces and institutional legacies.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) who emphasizes the necessity of technological advancement alongside institutional adjustments for sustained economic progress. Both authors highlight how the expansion of knowledge, whether through formal education or applied scientific inquiry, has been a critical driver of long-run growth. Mokyr’s analysis of the role of educational institutions in disseminating scientific knowledge during the Industrial Revolution illustrates how access to technical expertise can catalyze economic transformation, even in the absence of strong formal institutions. Kuznets further underscores that modern technology requires not only material resources but also institutional frameworks that support its implementation, such as legal protections and market structures. Together, these sources suggest that the diffusion of knowledge and the capacity to absorb and apply new technologies are central to economic development. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) complements this view by noting that the catch-up process is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including past institutional commitments and educational legacies, which can either facilitate or hinder technological adoption. This perspective implies that the trajectory of economic growth is not solely determined by institutional structures but is also deeply embedded in the evolving capabilities of societies to harness and transmit knowledge. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation thus forms a dynamic foundation for long-term economic development.

Stern (2004, p.2) challenges the conventional understanding of development-environment relationships by arguing that environmental factors shape long-run economic growth independently of institutional structures. This claim is supported by Stern’s critique of the environmental Kuznets curve, which he contends is often based on weak econometric methods and insufficient attention to model adequacy. Stern’s analysis highlights how environmental degradation can constrain economic activity through resource scarcity and ecological limits, offering an alternative explanation for historical patterns of divergence. This perspective aligns with broader discussions on the structural constraints of natural resources on development trajectories. The interplay between environmental degradation and economic growth, as emphasized by Stern, suggests that institutional reforms alone may not suffice to overcome deep ecological bottlenecks. By focusing on the material conditions of development, this stream complements the institutionalist view by identifying a distinct pathway through which environmental factors influence long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely acknowledged across the literature, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasizing their critical contribution to economic performance and income disparities. This consensus is further reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who underscores the historical significance of institutional frameworks in determining economic trajectories. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape development outcomes. However a key divergence emerges regarding the origins of these institutions: while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.5) argue that colonial legacies have had a lasting impact on institutional quality Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the necessity of examining precolonial histories to fully understand current institutional configurations. This disagreement is resolved through comparative historical analysis, as demonstrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who use case studies to trace how institutional paths diverged in the New World. The synthesis of these perspectives reveals that institutions are deeply embedded in historical processes, and their evolution is shaped by a complex interplay of colonial, precolonial, and local factors.
