The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on the question of whether institutional structures are the primary determinants of economic trajectories or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological progress. At stake is the intellectual debate over whether institutions are exogenous, shaped by external forces, or endogenous, evolving through internal dynamics. The disagreement lies between those who argue that institutions are the root cause of economic divergence and those who emphasize the influence of historical conditions, cultural norms, or technological capabilities. The literature presents a unified case that institutional frameworks are central to economic performance, with variations in how these frameworks are formed and sustained. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both highlight the enduring impact of institutional legacies, though they differ in their emphasis on colonial policies versus initial conditions. North (1989, p.1) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) further reinforce the institutional perspective by linking long-term economic outcomes to the historical evolution of legal and political systems. These scholars collectively argue that institutional arrangements are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic possibilities. The next section explores how these institutional frameworks are influenced by broader historical and cultural contexts.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19) in their respective analyses of institutional and educational frameworks. These scholars collectively emphasize that knowledge accumulation is a driver of technological advancement and a critical factor in shaping the institutional environments that sustain long-term economic performance. The interplay between knowledge and institutions is evident in how educational systems, such as those in Scotland and Germany, fostered the transmission of technical skills and scientific reasoning, thereby reducing access costs and enabling broader participation in industrialization. This process was further reinforced by the role of formal and informal institutions in regulating the training of apprentices and ensuring the continuity of skilled labor, which in turn influenced the skill premium and economic outcomes. The synthesis of these perspectives reveals that institutional development is an integral component of knowledge growth and shapes the conditions under which technological progress can be sustained and institutionalized. This interdependence between knowledge and institutions provides a foundation for understanding how historical patterns of innovation and learning continue to influence long-run economic growth.

Institutions shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks that either enable or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies is not due to geography but to institutional changes initiated during colonialism, which shaped the trajectory of economic performance. North (1989, p.1) complements this by emphasizing that institutional development is a critical determinant of economic growth, distinct from neoclassical factors such as population and savings. The persistence of extractive institutions in many post-colonial states, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores how colonial frameworks continued to influence governance and economic activity long after independence. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that secure property rights and institutional arrangements are essential for fostering economic growth, as they provide the stability needed for investment and innovation. These sources collectively highlight the role of historical institutional design in shaping the conditions for sustained economic development. The interplay between colonial institutions and long-term growth remains a central theme in understanding the divergent paths of economic performance across nations.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that historical legacies of the slave trade have left a lasting imprint on contemporary African societies by fostering a culture of mistrust that undermines economic cooperation and development. This claim is corroborated by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who highlight how colonial institutions shaped divergent economic trajectories in Africa, with long-term implications for growth and institutional legitimacy. These scholars collectively emphasize that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have had enduring effects on trust, governance, and economic outcomes. The mechanism through which these legacies operate is the entrenchment of extractive or patrimonial systems that limit the capacity of institutions to support inclusive growth. Peters (2004, p.19) adds that land conflicts and class formation in Africa are not merely economic phenomena but are also shaped by historical institutional structures that reinforce inequality and exclusion. This stream of scholarship underscores the profound and lasting influence of historical institutional legacies on long-run economic growth, framing institutions as critical determinants of development paths. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the historical roots of institutional design is essential for crafting effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.25) argue that institutional innovations are critical for sustained economic growth, as they shape the environment for technological advancement. Kuznets emphasizes that modern technology requires institutional and social adjustments that transcend traditional modes of production, such as family enterprise or slavery, which were prevalent in earlier economic systems. Pamuk complements this by showing how regions with more flexible institutions, such as the Low Countries and England, adapted more readily to long-term economic shifts, including the post-Black Death environment, thereby fostering growth. These sources collectively suggest that institutional flexibility and adaptability are key to harnessing technological progress. Pamuk further notes that the Black Death accelerated the decline in interest rates in northwestern Europe, which may have been facilitated by pre-existing institutional advantages that enabled a more favorable economic environment. This stream underscores that the capacity to adjust institutions in response to economic and technological change is a decisive factor in long-run growth. The implications of this perspective highlight the necessity of institutional reform as a prerequisite for sustained development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the key to understanding long-run economic growth, a stance supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the primacy of ideas over institutional structures. Both authors argue that the persistence of certain economic practices and the resistance to innovation are better explained by the alignment of interests and values than by the efficiency of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights that institutional interlinkages often prevent the adoption of more productive techniques unless they offer comprehensive substitutes, suggesting that the distribution of economic benefits shapes institutional outcomes. McCloskey further illustrates this by showing how cultural and ideological struggles, such as the French resistance to Jewish integration in the military, can impede economic progress, even in the face of alternative models. These perspectives collectively suggest that the ideological underpinnings of societies play a critical role in shaping the trajectory of economic development. The emphasis on distributional and ideational factors challenges traditional institutionalist accounts by shifting the focus from formal rules to the broader social and cultural contexts that influence economic behavior.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutions significantly influenced economic growth in former colonies, accounting for a substantial portion of current income differences. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than economic efficiency. Both perspectives converge on the idea that institutional frameworks are deeply embedded in the socio-political fabric of societies, shaping long-run economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that institutional differences arise from historical conditions and factor endowments, suggesting that geography and initial conditions play a critical role in shaping institutional development. This divergence underscores the tension between viewing institutions as endogenous or exogenous to economic outcomes. The empirical comparison of English and Spanish institutional evolution in North America and Latin America, as detailed by North (1989, p.1), offers a historical lens to resolve this disagreement. By tracing how institutional persistence and change were shaped by colonial legacies, this evidence supports the argument that institutions are both products of and contributors to long-term economic growth.
