The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on a fundamental intellectual dispute: whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or historical legacies. This disagreement has real-world implications for policy design as the emphasis on institutions may shape the focus of development interventions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies. Their work builds on earlier studies that link institutional differences to economic performance, emphasizing how the disease environment at the time of European arrival influenced institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These findings suggest that institutions are not just byproducts of economic conditions but active forces that shape long-term growth trajectories. North (1989, p.1) similarly contends that institutions matter more than neoclassical factors like population and savings, framing them as the key to understanding historical obstacles to economic growth. His analysis underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in determining the sustainability of economic progress. Together, these contributions highlight a shared view that institutions are central to economic development, though they differ in their emphasis on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. The next section explores how these institutional foundations are shaped by deeper historical and cultural antecedents.

The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), reflects how the initial choices made by colonial powers created frameworks that constrained economic potential. These frameworks, rooted in the historical conditions of the time, were not merely incidental but deeply embedded in the political and legal systems that emerged. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate on how institutional arrangements, such as the separation of powers and the rule of law, fostered secure property rights and sustained economic growth. The interplay between these historical legacies and the evolution of institutional norms underscores the critical role of colonial origins in determining the trajectory of economic development. This historical shaping of institutions explains variations in growth outcomes and highlights the necessity of understanding institutional change as a process influenced by past decisions. The implications of this historical conditioning are profound, as they suggest that the path to sustained economic growth is inextricably linked to the institutional legacies of the past.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial legacies, particularly the slave trade, shaped institutions that hindered long-run economic growth in Africa. Their analysis reveals that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and undermines economic cooperation and development. This mechanism is supported by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that societies with more developed institutions were better able to engage in trade with Europeans, suggesting that the slave trade disrupted the formation of such institutions. The persistence of mistrust, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), aligns with broader findings that trust is a critical factor for economic growth, yet its origins are often overlooked in contemporary analyses. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes the necessity of historical institutional analysis to understand current African development challenges, highlighting how institutions are the product of a complex interplay between colonial and precolonial histories. Peters (2004, p.19) further illustrates how land conflicts in Africa reveal patterns of exclusion and class formation, which may be linked to the disrupted social and economic structures caused by the slave trade. These sources collectively underscore that the legacy of colonialism, particularly the slave trade, created institutional and social barriers that continue to impede economic growth in Africa. The implications of this historical conditioning suggest that addressing these deep-rooted institutional flaws is essential for fostering sustainable development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a stance supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the role of ideas over institutions. Both authors argue that the persistence of certain economic practices is better explained by the dynamics of power and ideology rather than by the efficiency of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights how institutional interlinkages can stifle innovation unless they provide substitutes for existing structures, suggesting that the distribution of power shapes the trajectory of economic development. McCloskey, in turn, illustrates how cultural and ideological factors, such as the persistence of aristocratic values, can impede economic growth by constraining the adoption of new ideas and practices. These sources collectively demonstrate that the interplay between power, belief systems, and economic outcomes is central to understanding long-term growth. The emphasis on distributional conflicts as a driver of institutional change challenges traditional narratives that prioritize institutional efficiency as the primary mechanism for economic development. This perspective underscores the need to examine how ideological and power-based dynamics shape the evolution of economic institutions and their impact on growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that resonates with Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes the necessity of technological advancement and institutional adjustments for sustained economic progress. Both authors highlight that while institutions are important, they are secondary to the diffusion of knowledge and the capacity to harness technological innovation. Mokyr's analysis of the role of education and scientific institutions in fostering technological progress underscores the idea that knowledge accumulation is a more direct and enduring driver of long-run growth than institutional arrangements alone. Kuznets further supports this by noting that modern technology cannot function within outdated social and economic structures, reinforcing the notion that knowledge-based advancements must be accompanied by complementary institutional changes. However, the stream's central claim is that technological and knowledge-based progress, rather than institutions, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. This perspective is also echoed by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), who points out that the catch-up process in economic development is influenced by the availability of technological opportunities and the capacity of societies to absorb and apply new knowledge. The implication of this view is that policies aimed at fostering innovation and knowledge dissemination are more critical for sustained growth than those focused solely on institutional reform. This stream challenges the institutionalist tradition by asserting that the diffusion of knowledge is the fundamental mechanism underpinning economic progress.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions shaping skill premium and wage dynamics drive long-run economic performance, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who highlight how institutional frameworks influence the capacity of economies to adapt to shifting market conditions. These studies collectively demonstrate that the efficiency of training institutions and the regulation of skill transmission significantly affect wage gaps and economic outcomes. The historical evidence from Pamuk (2007, p.25) further illustrates how institutional resilience in the Low Countries and England prevented wage declines after the Black Death, creating a persistent wage gap with the rest of Europe. This pattern of institutional adaptation underscores the role of institutions in shaping wage dynamics and economic trajectories. The implications of these findings suggest that institutional design is not merely a backdrop to economic activity but a fundamental determinant of long-run growth.

Institutional persistence shapes long-run economic growth by embedding durable rules that influence resource allocation and political power (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The historical analysis of colonial institutions demonstrates that extractive frameworks, once established, often outlasted colonial rule, structuring post-independence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.5). These findings align with the broader argument that institutional design, rather than geographic or demographic factors, explains divergent paths of development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The role of institutions in fostering growth is further supported by the case of England, where the evolution of common law and representative government created a stable environment for economic expansion (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that secure property rights and political liberties, embedded in institutional arrangements, are critical to sustained growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The debate centers on whether institutions primarily reflect economic efficiency or serve as mechanisms for resolving distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that institutional frameworks are shaped by cultural beliefs and values, which in turn affect economic outcomes through their influence on political and market behavior (Ogilvie 2007, p.30). The resolution of this disagreement lies in empirical comparisons of institutional congruence with precolonial and indigenous systems, as demonstrated by the analysis of African development policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
