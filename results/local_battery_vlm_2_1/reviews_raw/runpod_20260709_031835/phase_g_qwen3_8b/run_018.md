The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play a more decisive role. The disagreement centers on whether institutional structures are endogenous to economic outcomes or exogenous, shaping the conditions under which growth can occur. This review synthesizes scholarship that emphasizes institutional causality, arguing that institutions provide the framework within which economic activities are organized, enabling or constraining innovation, investment, and resource allocation. The discussion highlights how institutions influence the sustainability of growth by shaping incentives, reducing transaction costs, and fostering trust. By examining the interplay between institutional design and economic performance, the review underscores the importance of institutional quality as a determinant of long-term growth. The next section explores how institutional frameworks emerge from historical processes and how they shape the trajectory of economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that institutional and technological adjustments are fundamental to sustained economic growth, a claim reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights how the efficiency of training institutions and legal frameworks shapes the skill premium and long-term economic performance. These scholars collectively demonstrate that the organization of skill transmission and the alignment of institutions with technological progress are critical mechanisms through which economies evolve. Similarly, Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that modern technologies require institutional environments that support literacy, property rights, and market integration, which are incompatible with traditional modes of production. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) further elaborates on how past institutional commitments can either enable or hinder the catch-up process, underscoring the path-dependent nature of development. This stream collectively suggests that the interplay between institutional design and technological adoption is a key driver of long-term economic trajectories. The implications of these findings are profound, as they imply that policy interventions must simultaneously address both the legal and educational infrastructure necessary to sustain growth.

Institutions shaped by colonial history are upstream determinants of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) together demonstrate that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, such as extractive or inclusive political-economic frameworks, have persisted and continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores the long-term consequences of colonial governance, which often prioritized elite interests over inclusive development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate on how secure property rights and representative institutions, which emerged from specific historical conditions, have been critical in fostering economic growth and political freedom. These findings collectively suggest that the institutional frameworks established during colonial periods have had a profound and lasting impact on the economic trajectories of post-colonial states. The implications of this historical institutional legacy are significant for understanding the uneven development patterns observed across the globe today.

Historical institutional analysis provides a framework to understand how institutions shape long-run economic growth by tracing their historical development and impact. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) demonstrate that institutions in Africa are deeply embedded in precolonial and colonial histories, which have shaped their effectiveness in promoting growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective if they align with indigenous practices, as this congruence enhances government legitimacy and fosters development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further show that institutions were often designed to secure resource extraction for European benefit, which created structural inequalities that persist today. These findings underscore the importance of historical context in evaluating institutional performance. Hopkins (2019, p.11) highlights that economic underdevelopment in Africa is a historical creation, rooted in the mechanisms of unequal exchange and dependency. This perspective challenges the notion that current institutions are the sole determinant of growth, emphasizing instead the need to understand their historical origins. The interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional frameworks thus offers a critical lens for analyzing long-term economic trajectories. This historical dimension is essential for formulating policies that address the root causes of underdevelopment.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that historical institutional legacies, particularly those shaped by colonial and precolonial forces, have had a profound and lasting impact on long-run economic growth. These legacies, often rooted in patterns of exploitation and extraction, have created structural barriers that persist into the present. The cluster (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.25; Peters 2004, p.19; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) collectively demonstrates that institutions are not merely the product of contemporary choices but are deeply embedded in historical processes that shaped economic outcomes. This historical embeddedness suggests that understanding long-run growth requires examining not just current institutions but also the deep-seated patterns of power and resource distribution that emerged from past interactions. The persistence of these legacies underscores the need for policies that address the structural inequalities inherited from history, rather than focusing solely on institutional reforms in the present.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Stern (2004, p.2) argue that the growth of useful knowledge drives long-run economic growth by enabling technological progress and institutional development. This knowledge-driven growth is not merely a byproduct of economic activity but a central force that shapes the trajectory of societies. The expansion of knowledge, as Mokyr highlights, requires complementary institutional progress to sustain and redirect its benefits, ensuring that technological advancements do not fall prey to rent-seeking behaviors. Stern’s critique of the environmental Kuznets curve further illustrates how knowledge can inform alternative models of development that reconcile economic growth with environmental sustainability. Together, these scholars underscore the centrality of knowledge as a dynamic force that both enables and is enabled by institutional frameworks. The historical role of educational institutions in fostering access to scientific and technical knowledge, as Mokyr describes, reveals how knowledge diffusion was uneven and often constrained by social and political structures. This unevenness, in turn, shaped the long-term capacity of societies to innovate and adapt. The interplay between knowledge and institutions thus forms a critical mechanism through which economic growth is sustained and redirected. As Stern’s analysis suggests, understanding this relationship is essential for developing policies that align economic progress with broader societal and environmental goals.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Temin (2002, p.6) argue that economic growth is shaped by complex, differentiated causal relationships rather than a single cause like institutions. Austin emphasizes that the reversal thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing history, neglecting the differentiated causal relationships that emerge from long-term historical contexts. Temin, meanwhile, highlights how the Golden Age of European growth was driven by pre-war disequilibrium, illustrating how institutional and structural factors interacted in ways that were neither uniform nor predictable. Together, these authors reveal that growth is not merely a product of institutional design but arises from a web of interdependent forces, including historical legacies, economic imbalances, and institutional arrangements that vary across time and place. This complexity challenges the notion that institutions alone can explain long-run economic trajectories, instead suggesting that their role is contingent upon broader historical and structural conditions. Austin’s focus on African economic historiography further illustrates how path dependence and recurring situations shape growth in ways that are not fully captured by institutionalist frameworks. Temin’s analysis of postwar Europe underscores how institutions can both enable and constrain growth, depending on the specific historical and economic context. These insights suggest that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a more nuanced approach that accounts for the differentiated and often unpredictable nature of causal relationships. The interplay between institutions and historical conditions thus remains a central theme in explaining the varied paths of economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. McCloskey emphasizes that cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, have historically driven economic transformation by altering the incentives and norms that shape productive activity. Ogilvie complements this by highlighting how distributional conflicts, rooted in deeply held beliefs, can either stifle or enable institutional change, depending on whether they align with the interests of dominant groups. Together, these authors suggest that the evolution of economic systems is not primarily a function of formal rules but of the ideas that underpin them and the power dynamics that shape their dissemination. For instance, Ogilvie notes that institutional frameworks often resist innovation unless it offers substitutes for existing structures, reinforcing the idea that ideas are not just reflections of institutions but active forces in their creation. McCloskey further illustrates this by showing how the cultural struggle in France, centered on the imposition of Catholic values, hindered economic progress until the rise of a new bourgeois rhetoric. This interplay between ideas and distributional conflicts underscores a broader pattern: economic growth is contingent on the alignment of cultural narratives with the material conditions of production. The implications of this perspective challenge traditional institutionalist accounts by shifting the focus from the design of rules to the evolution of ideas and the social forces that shape their influence.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the consensus that they provide the framework for economic activity, as demonstrated by North (1989, p.1). This framework is not static but evolves through historical processes, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.5), who argue that colonial institutions have had lasting effects on income per capita. While both authors agree on the centrality of institutions, they diverge on the relative importance of geography versus institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize that institutions explain a significant fraction of current income differences, whereas Hopkins (2009, p.8) suggests that initial conditions, such as geography, also play a critical role. The disagreement is resolved through empirical comparisons that trace the persistence of colonial institutions across time and space, as shown in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which demonstrates how extractive institutions established during colonial rule continued to shape economic outcomes long after independence. These findings suggest that while geography may set initial conditions, the enduring impact of institutions is best understood through historical analysis of their evolution and persistence.
