The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of sustained prosperity or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or knowledge accumulation. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are the root cause of growth or merely a byproduct of broader historical and environmental conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies. Both streams agree that institutions matter, but they differ on whether they are the origin or the outcome of economic divergence. North (1989, p.1) underscores that institutions are central to understanding historical obstacles to growth, framing them as the key to explaining long-term economic outcomes. This aligns with Hopkins (2009, p.8), who highlights that current institutions are shaped by a history extending beyond colonial rule, suggesting that institutional legacies are deeply embedded in the trajectory of development. These streams collectively affirm that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic trajectories, yet they remain divided on whether they are the primary cause or a consequence of broader historical forces. The debate over institutional causality remains unresolved, but the consensus is that institutions are indispensable to understanding the long-run dynamics of economic growth.

Historical institutional analysis shows how institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing development paths. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that institutions are deeply embedded in the historical processes that determine economic outcomes, with colonial legacies and precolonial structures playing critical roles. These findings suggest that understanding institutional development requires a long-term perspective that accounts for both internal and external influences. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further highlights the importance of historical context in explaining the persistence of underdevelopment, arguing that European capitalism created conditions of dependency that continue to affect African economies today. This stream collectively underscores that institutions are not only shaped by history but also serve as mechanisms through which historical legacies continue to influence economic trajectories. The implications of these findings suggest that policy interventions must address the historical roots of institutional structures to foster sustainable growth.

Both papers highlight that secure property rights and inclusive political institutions are critical for sustained growth, as they reduce uncertainty and incentivize investment. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), further underscores the long-term consequences of historical institutional legacies. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) expand on this by emphasizing that representative government and the rule of law are not only prerequisites for economic growth but also mechanisms through which economic rights are protected. This stream collectively establishes that institutional arrangements are not static but evolve through historical processes, often reinforcing existing power structures or enabling broader participation. The implication is that understanding the historical roots of institutions is essential for designing policies that promote inclusive growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore deeply intertwined with their historical development and the political dynamics that sustain them.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), Pamuk (2007, p.25), and Abramovitz (1986, p.19). This cluster emphasizes that institutional frameworks are critical in translating knowledge into sustained economic progress. Mokyr highlights that while technological progress is often irreversible, its economic impact depends on complementary institutional advancements that prevent the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behavior. Van Zanden adds that the efficiency of training institutions and the regulation of skill transmission significantly influence the skill premium, shaping long-term economic performance. Pamuk further illustrates how flexible institutions enabled northwestern Europe to adapt to post-Black Death economic conditions, fostering a divergence in growth trajectories. Abramovitz underscores that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, demonstrating the enduring influence of historical institutional arrangements. These sources collectively show that knowledge growth is not an isolated phenomenon but is deeply embedded in institutional contexts that determine its economic utility. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore inseparable from their capacity to support and sustain the diffusion of knowledge.

Temin argues that institutional capture by firms and unions shaped the Golden Age of European growth through institutional dynamics (Temin 2002, p.6; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Stern 2004, p.2). These sources collectively suggest that the interplay between institutional frameworks and economic actors created conditions for sustained productivity and growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight how institutional arrangements influenced the scale and timing of export production, linking economic performance to the broader market and political contexts (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This institutional embedding of economic activity underscores the role of institutions in channeling resources and incentives toward growth. The shared claim across these sources is that institutions are not passive structures but active participants in shaping economic development through their capacity to align incentives and mediate interactions between actors. This institutional mediation is key to understanding the long-run trajectories of growth and development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, a claim supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who highlight how colonial legacies shaped wage trends and labor market institutions in Africa. These scholars collectively demonstrate that institutional legacies shape long-run economic growth by perpetuating inequality through restrictive policies and exclusionary practices. The persistence of such institutions, as noted by Peters (2004, p.19), often reflects entrenched class formations that limit access to economic opportunities, reinforcing systemic disparities. This pattern is further illustrated by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who show how precolonial commercial development influenced the institutional design of colonial states, embedding structural inequalities that endured into the modern era. The interplay between institutional frameworks and historical trajectories underscores how inequality is not merely a byproduct of economic forces but a deeply embedded feature of institutional evolution. This legacy of exclusion continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, setting the stage for examining how institutional mediation influences technological and knowledge-based growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are central to economic growth, challenging the primacy of institutions in shaping long-run development. This perspective is supported by the historical case of France, where cultural and ideological resistance to bourgeois values hindered economic progress for centuries (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Similarly, McCloskey (2015, p.9) highlights how the Netherlands, once a bastion of conservative ideology, eventually embraced a new rhetoric that fostered economic transformation (McCloskey 2015, p.7). These examples illustrate how ideological shifts can reconfigure the conditions for growth, independent of institutional change. The emphasis on ideas suggests that the evolution of economic outcomes is as much a product of cultural and intellectual climates as it is of formal rules. This stream of scholarship thus reframes the role of institutions by arguing that they are not the primary drivers of growth but rather the outcomes of broader ideological and cultural forces. The implications of this view are profound, as they imply that fostering innovation and openness requires more than legal frameworks, it demands a shift in the prevailing intellectual and cultural paradigms.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the persistent influence of historical institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate that colonial institutions have endured and shaped contemporary economic outcomes, suggesting that institutional legacies are deeply embedded in modern economies. This view aligns with North’s (1989, p.1) assertion that institutions are central to understanding economic development, as they define the rules of the game that constrain or enable economic activity. The debate remains over whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or secondary outcomes of broader cultural and ideological forces. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than economic efficiency, emphasizing the interplay between beliefs and institutional rules. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone can account for economic disparities, highlighting the need to consider cultural and ideological factors. The disagreement centers on the relative importance of institutional structures versus cultural paradigms in shaping economic trajectories. The empirical comparison of colonial institutions in former European colonies, as detailed in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), offers a critical measure of institutional impact, showing that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income differences. This historical evidence provides a concrete basis for evaluating the role of institutions in long-run growth, grounding the debate in measurable outcomes.
