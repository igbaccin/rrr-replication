The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more decisive role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, emphasizing how colonial policies shaped the institutional frameworks that persist today. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect economic outcomes. These studies underscore the enduring influence of historical institutions on contemporary growth trajectories. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutional analysis can provide a coherent explanation for long-term economic divergence or if it must be supplemented by other variables. This theme of institutional persistence and its causal power continues to shape the discussion of how historical legacies inform modern economic performance.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade entrenched mistrust in African societies, which persists as a barrier to economic development. This finding is reinforced by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who show that historical legacies, including the slave trade, shaped long-term wage trends and institutional design in colonial Africa. The persistent impact of these historical processes suggests that institutions are not merely products of contemporary choices but are deeply embedded in the historical conditions of a society. The mechanism through which this occurs is the gradual erosion of social trust, which undermines cooperation and economic coordination. Nunn (2008, p.19) further illustrates that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, highlighting the role of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes. This stream of scholarship collectively argues that historical legacies like the slave trade shaped long-run economic outcomes through persistent institutional degradation, offering an alternative explanation to the role of institutions in economic growth. The implications of this are profound, as they suggest that the path of development is not entirely open to policy intervention but is constrained by historical patterns that continue to influence modern economic performance.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth, as they determine the institutional frameworks that enable or hinder development. These scholars emphasize that the persistence of extractive institutions, often established during colonial rule, has had lasting negative effects on economic performance, while inclusive institutions foster growth and stability. The mechanism through which these institutions influence growth lies in their ability to either constrain or empower economic actors, thereby affecting productivity and resource allocation. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) complements this by arguing that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a better explanation for core economic institutions, underscoring the political nature of institutional development. This insight suggests that the path of institutional evolution is not purely technical but is deeply rooted in the power dynamics and social conflicts of a given society. The implication is that understanding the origins of institutions is crucial for designing policies that can overcome historical constraints and promote sustainable growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that economic growth necessitates institutional and technological progress, not merely resource exploitation, a stance echoed by Temin (2002, p.6) in his analysis of the Golden Age of European growth. Both authors emphasize that sustained development hinges on the alignment of institutional frameworks with advancing technology, a proposition that underscores the interdependence of structural change and innovation. Kuznets highlights how modern industrialization required institutional reforms that replaced outdated systems of production and social organization, while Temin illustrates how postwar Europe's growth was propelled by institutional arrangements that reinforced long-term investment and cooperation. These perspectives collectively suggest that economic transformation is not an isolated technical process but one deeply embedded in the evolution of institutional capabilities. The institutional adjustments described by Kuznets, such as the shift from family enterprise to large-scale industrial organization, mirror the institutional innovations Temin attributes to postwar economic success, including mechanisms that incentivized productive investment and collective action. This convergence implies that the capacity for economic growth is contingent upon the presence of institutions that can adapt to and support technological change. The implication is that fostering such institutional flexibility is essential for overcoming historical constraints and sustaining long-term development.

Peters (2004, p.19) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that specific institutional forms, like kinship and village institutions, shape long-run economic growth by influencing class formation and labor mobilization. Peters highlights how land conflicts in Africa reveal exclusion and class formation, challenging the view of flexible and negotiable customary systems. Austin, meanwhile, critiques the reversal thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing history, arguing that differentiated causal relationships must be acknowledged. Together, these authors demonstrate that institutional structures, particularly those rooted in kinship and village systems, are not static but evolve in ways that either constrain or enable economic transformation. This evolution is often tied to shifts in labor mobilization and the reconfiguration of social hierarchies, which in turn affect the capacity for sustained growth. Austin’s emphasis on long-term history and path dependence complements Peters’ focus on institutional conflict, suggesting that the interplay between customary institutions and external pressures, such as commercialization or state intervention, creates distinct pathways for economic development. The implication is that understanding these institutional dynamics is crucial for explaining variations in long-run growth across regions. This focus on institutional specificity and historical contingency sets the stage for examining how institutional change interacts with technological and political forces in shaping economic trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by the historical expansion of educational institutions and the diffusion of scientific expertise. This perspective emphasizes that technological progress, particularly in applied fields, has been a central force in shaping long-run economic trajectories. The role of institutions, while acknowledged, is framed as a complementary factor rather than the primary driver. For instance, Mokyr highlights how the proliferation of universities and academies in the eighteenth century facilitated the transmission of scientific knowledge, thereby reducing the costs of innovation and enabling broader participation in technological advancement. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) reinforces this view by emphasizing the complexity of the catch-up process, noting that past educational and institutional commitments can either hinder or facilitate the absorption of new technologies. These sources collectively suggest that the diffusion of knowledge and the institutional frameworks that support its dissemination are inextricably linked to economic growth. The implication is that policies fostering knowledge creation and access are as critical as those promoting institutional stability. This focus on knowledge as a dynamic and cumulative force underscores the need to consider its historical evolution and institutional embedding in understanding long-run economic development.

Stern (2004, p.2) challenges the Environmental Kuznets Curve by highlighting its methodological weaknesses, arguing that the curve's empirical validity is undermined by statistical shortcomings such as serial dependence and omitted variables bias. Stern (2004, p.1) further contextualizes the EKC within its historical emergence, tracing its origins to Kuznets’ hypothesis on income inequality and its popularization through the 1992 World Bank Development Report. This dual critique underscores the EKC’s limited explanatory power in capturing development-environment dynamics, positioning it as an alternative yet insufficient framework compared to institutional analyses. The EKC’s focus on income levels as a determinant of environmental outcomes contrasts with the institutional emphasis on governance structures and policy stability. Stern’s work implies that environmental degradation is not an inevitable byproduct of development but rather a function of specific economic and political conditions. This suggests that institutional frameworks, rather than purely economic growth, may be more critical in shaping sustainable development trajectories. The EKC’s empirical limitations, therefore, open space for institutional explanations that account for policy choices and regulatory capacity in environmental outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) challenges the institutionalist narrative by arguing that African economic development cannot be fully understood without considering the deep historical roots of colonialism and the complex interplay between indigenous institutions and external forces. This cluster revisits African economic history to challenge the notion that institutions are the sole driver of long-run growth, emphasizing the role of colonial legacies and African agency (McCloskey 2015, p.9; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2019, p.11; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights how precolonial institutions, when aligned with colonial administrative structures, could shape the trajectory of economic outcomes, while also acknowledging the limitations of path-dependent explanations (Hopkins 2009, p.8). These works collectively suggest that the relationship between institutions and growth is mediated by historical processes, colonial legacies, and the active agency of local populations. This historical dimension calls for a re-evaluation of how we conceptualize institutional effectiveness in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

The literature consistently underscores that institutions are central to long-run economic growth, with multiple studies demonstrating their enduring impact on development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, such as extractive or inclusive systems, significantly shaped income differences among former colonies, reinforcing the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active determinants of economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This aligns with North’s (1989, p.1) assertion that institutional frameworks, rather than factors like population or savings, are the primary drivers of growth, as they shape the rules of the game for economic activity (North 1989, p.1). However the sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. While Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) emphasize that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, provide a better explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.30). This disagreement is resolved by examining historical evidence of institutional persistence, as seen in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which shows how colonial institutions endured long after independence, shaping contemporary economic conditions through path dependence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).
