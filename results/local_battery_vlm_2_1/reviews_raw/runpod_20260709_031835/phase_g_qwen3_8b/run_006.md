The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth hinges on understanding how institutional frameworks influence the trajectory of development. At stake is the intellectual debate over whether institutions are the primary driver of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge accumulation, play a more fundamental role. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions operate and whether they are endogenous or exogenous to the process of growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, rooted in colonial history, explain persistent income disparities, while North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions themselves are the key to unlocking economic potential. Both streams converge on the importance of institutions but diverge on their origins and the extent to which they are shaped by external forces. This tension underscores the need to distinguish between institutional structures as outcomes of historical processes and as independent variables that can be deliberately designed. The next section examines how institutional development is often preceded by broader historical and cultural antecedents.

Their shared claim is that institutional structures, whether rooted in colonial legacies or historical evolution, are central to explaining divergent economic trajectories. Both authors emphasize that the persistence of these institutions across time determines the long-term capacity for growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule continued to influence post-independence economic outcomes, reinforcing the idea that institutions are not merely passive outcomes but active determinants of development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further refine this argument by highlighting how specific institutional arrangements, such as secure property rights and representative governance, are essential for fostering economic growth and political freedom. These authors suggest that the interplay between institutional design and economic performance is not accidental but the result of deliberate political choices. The implication is that understanding the historical roots of institutions is crucial for explaining contemporary economic disparities. This focus on institutional persistence and design sets the stage for examining how institutional change can be deliberately pursued to promote growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that colonial legacies and institutional divergence have shaped long-run economic growth by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that influence productivity and development trajectories. This claim is supported by (Pamuk 2007, p.25; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), which collectively demonstrate how historical institutional arrangements, such as those governing labor markets and urban development, have had lasting effects on economic outcomes. For instance, the differential institutional design in colonial Africa, as noted in (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27), led to divergent labor market outcomes between West and East Africa, reinforcing long-term disparities in growth potential. Similarly, the persistence of low skill premiums in Ireland, as documented in (van Zanden 2009, p.24), highlights how institutional constraints on human capital formation can impede economic progress. These examples illustrate how institutional frameworks, once established, can either facilitate or hinder economic development over time. The implications of this institutional persistence are further underscored by the role of flexible structures in enabling adaptation to changing economic conditions, as seen in the Low Countries and England’s resistance to wage declines after the Black Death, which contributed to their long-term economic ascendancy. This stream of scholarship thus underscores the importance of understanding institutional legacies as a key determinant of sustained economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the historical institutions shaped by the slave trade left enduring scars on African economic growth, as evidenced by the persistence of mistrust and its detrimental effects on development. This finding is corroborated by Nunn (2008, p.3), who highlights how the slave trade disrupted the formation of stable, development-friendly institutions by fostering internal conflict and undermining social cohesion. The legacy of these historical disruptions is further underscored by Hopkins (2019, p.11), who argues that the slave trade contributed to long-term underdevelopment by entrenching patterns of dependency and inequality. These sources collectively reveal that the slave trade not only altered the trajectory of African societies but also entrenched structural barriers that impede economic progress. The implications of this historical legacy are evident in the work of Peters (2004, p.19), who shows how the persistence of patrimonial structures and land conflicts continues to shape economic outcomes in contemporary Africa. By linking historical institutions to present-day challenges, this stream of scholarship underscores the necessity of understanding Africa’s economic trajectory through the lens of its historical experiences. The interplay between historical trauma and institutional inertia thus remains a critical factor in explaining the region’s long-run economic performance.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites, shaping long-run economic growth patterns. This claim is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who emphasizes that distributional conflicts, rather than mere economic efficiency, underpin the formation of core economic institutions. Together, these authors highlight how institutional frameworks in the New World were not only shaped by elite interests but also reinforced through restrictive policies that limited opportunities for broader segments of society. The persistence of such institutional legacies, as noted by McCloskey (2015, p.9), has had lasting effects on economic outcomes, with cultural and ideological factors playing a critical role in sustaining inequality. McCloskey’s emphasis on the power of ideas over institutions challenges the conventional focus on material interests, suggesting that the long-term trajectory of economic growth is deeply intertwined with the evolution of cultural norms and values. This stream of scholarship thus underscores the importance of understanding institutional development not as a purely economic phenomenon but as a process embedded in the broader social and ideological context. The interplay between institutional design and cultural beliefs continues to shape economic disparities and growth trajectories across different societies.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that aligns with the broader cluster's assertion that technological change and knowledge growth, not institutions, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.19; Kuznets 1973, p.1; Temin 2002, p.6; Stern 2004, p.2). This perspective emphasizes that the expansion of knowledge, particularly through scientific and technological advancements, underpins sustained economic progress by enhancing productivity and enabling new forms of organization. The role of institutions, while acknowledged as necessary for sustaining growth, is framed as secondary to the transformative power of knowledge itself. The interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is thus seen as a dynamic process, where technological progress catalyzes institutional change rather than being a byproduct of it. This stream of scholarship also underscores the limitations of institutions in the absence of technological advancement, as seen in the historical context where outdated institutions failed to support the adoption of new technologies. The implication is that without a continuous flow of technological and knowledge-based innovation, the long-term trajectory of economic growth remains constrained. This focus on technological and knowledge-driven growth offers a compelling alternative to institutionalist explanations, framing economic development as a function of intellectual and technical progress rather than political and legal structures.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely acknowledged, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasizing their significant impact on economic performance and industrialization in former colonies. This consensus is further reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who highlights how institutional differences produced distinct economic outcomes in early modern Britain and Spain. Both papers underscore the enduring influence of institutional frameworks, suggesting that the historical evolution of rules and norms has shaped long-term trajectories of development. However the sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a better explanation for core economic institutions, challenging the assumption that institutions primarily serve to maximize productivity. This disagreement is resolved by examining the historical context of institutional persistence, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show how colonial institutions persisted long after independence, shaping contemporary economic outcomes. The empirical comparison of institutional legacies in former colonies, such as the United States and Latin America, provides a clear measure of how historical rules continue to influence growth patterns. Thus, the literature converges on the importance of institutions but diverges on their causal mechanisms, with historical evidence offering a definitive framework for understanding their role.
