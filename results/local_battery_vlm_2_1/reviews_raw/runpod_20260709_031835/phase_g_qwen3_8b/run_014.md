The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that sustain or hinder prosperity across societies. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more decisive role. The disagreement centers on the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some emphasizing their direct impact on productivity and investment, while others highlight their indirect role as enablers or constraints of broader structural forces. This review synthesizes how different streams of scholarship address these tensions, focusing on the mechanisms through which institutions shape growth and the conditions under which they do so. The substantive theme of institutional causality its scope its triggers and its limits provides a continuity that links the various perspectives revealing how institutions are both a cause and an effect in the long-run trajectory of economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “The present paper offers one economic historian's reﬂections on the RF thesis: its methods, historical claims and implications for theory and policy.” Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) report, “In that paper we proposed the disease environment at the time Europeans arrived as an instrument for European settlements and the subsequent institutional development of the former colonies, and used this to estimate the causal effect of institutional differences on economic performance.”

This claim is supported by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutional frameworks, such as those emerging from the evolution of common law in England, have historically determined the pace and direction of economic growth. Both authors highlight how the institutional legacies of colonial rule, whether through extractive systems or more inclusive governance, have created enduring structural conditions that either enable or constrain development. The persistence of these institutional patterns is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show that the control structures established during the colonial era have continued to shape the institutional environments of postcolonial states, often reinforcing inequality and limiting economic opportunities. These findings suggest that the long-term economic outcomes of nations are deeply intertwined with the historical institutional arrangements that were imposed during colonial rule. The implications of this institutional persistence are also explored by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue that secure property rights and effective governance mechanisms are essential for sustained economic growth. This stream of scholarship collectively underscores the critical role of historical institutions in shaping the economic development of nations, setting the stage for an analysis of how institutional quality influences innovation and productivity in the modern era.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial institutions and exclusion shaped long-run economic growth by creating persistent structural barriers, a claim corroborated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who highlight how institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies. The mechanism through which these institutions exerted their influence lies in their capacity to entrench exclusionary practices that limited access to resources and opportunities, thereby constraining the potential for broad-based economic development. This structural legacy is further illustrated by Peters (2004, p.19), who shows that land conflicts in Africa reveal deep-seated patterns of exclusion and class formation, reinforcing the idea that historical institutions have had enduring effects on social and economic organization. The persistence of these barriers is also evident in Nunn (2008, p.19), who argues that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development by undermining institutional quality and fostering conditions of instability and conflict. These findings collectively underscore the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies on economic trajectories, suggesting that the structural inequalities rooted in colonial and precolonial institutions continue to shape contemporary patterns of growth and development. The implications of this institutional persistence are profound, as they suggest that addressing long-run economic growth requires not only reforms to current institutions but also a deeper understanding of their historical origins.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that long-run economic growth is shaped by institutions that emerge from historical processes, as these institutions are deeply embedded in the legacies of precolonial and colonial history. This perspective highlights how the effectiveness of contemporary institutions depends on their alignment with historical patterns of governance and social organization. The implication is that addressing these historical legacies is essential for designing institutions that can sustain long-term growth. By tracing the continuity of institutional forms across time, these works underscore the necessity of historical analysis in understanding the roots of economic development. This focus on historical institutional analysis provides a foundation for evaluating how past structures shape present capabilities and future trajectories.

Pamuk (2007, p.25) argues that institutional flexibility in post-Black Death Europe shaped long-term economic divergence by enabling regions to adapt to new economic conditions. This flexibility allowed the Low Countries and England to resist wage declines, creating a persistent wage gap with other parts of Europe. Pamuk (2007, p.21) further notes that the Black Death contributed to a decline in interest rates, which may have been facilitated by pre-existing institutions that supported economic stability. The interplay between institutional adaptability and economic transformation highlights how structural changes in governance and labor markets can have lasting effects on growth trajectories. These findings suggest that the capacity of institutions to evolve in response to shocks is a key determinant of long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of institutional legacies underscores the importance of understanding historical contexts in assessing contemporary economic performance.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), Temin (2002, p.6), and Stern (2004, p.2). These scholars collectively argue that technological and knowledge advancement, not institutions, drive long-run economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes that while institutions are important, they serve as complements to technological progress rather than the primary engine of growth. He highlights the role of educational institutions in disseminating knowledge, noting that while formal education was limited in its capacity to train applied scientists during the Industrial Revolution, informal networks and academies played a critical role in spreading technical expertise. Abramovitz, meanwhile, underscores the complexity of the catch-up process, suggesting that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder future growth. Temin argues that the postwar period saw a unique combination of disequilibrium and institutional arrangements that fostered rapid economic expansion, further reinforcing the idea that technological and knowledge-based factors are central to growth dynamics. Together, these sources suggest that while institutions shape the environment in which knowledge is generated and applied, the core driver of long-run economic growth lies in the advancement of technology and the diffusion of knowledge. This perspective challenges the centrality of institutions in explaining sustained growth and shifts the focus toward the role of innovation and human capital.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies drive long-run economic growth, challenging the institutionalist view that institutions are the fundamental cause. McCloskey emphasizes that cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, create the conditions for economic transformation by altering the incentives and norms that shape productive behavior. Ogilvie, meanwhile, highlights how deeply embedded beliefs and values influence the persistence of certain institutional frameworks, even when more efficient alternatives exist. Together these sources suggest that the diffusion of ideas whether through ideological movements cultural shifts or changing norms can fundamentally reshape the economic landscape by redefining the rules of the game. This mechanism is evident in McCloskey’s analysis of France, where conservative ideologies constrained economic growth for centuries, despite the availability of more productive systems. Ogilvie further notes that institutional outcomes are often shaped by the interplay between internal beliefs and external rules, reinforcing the idea that ideas are not merely byproducts of institutions but active forces in their formation. The implication is that economic growth is not solely a function of institutional design but is also deeply influenced by the evolution of cultural and ideological frameworks. This perspective underscores the need to consider the role of ideas in shaping the very structures that govern economic activity.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the persistent influence of historical institutional frameworks (North 1989, p.1). North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions, rather than population or savings, are central to understanding economic outcomes, highlighting how institutional differences between England and Spain shaped divergent developmental paths (North 1989, p.8). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate that colonial institutions have endured, forming the basis of contemporary economic structures in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These findings suggest that institutions are not only enduring but also deeply embedded in the mechanisms of economic performance. However the sources diverge on the causal mechanisms driving institutional persistence. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, are the primary force shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This contrasts with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who emphasize that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain income disparities among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The disagreement centers on whether institutional design is primarily a product of conflict or of exogenous factors such as population density and prosperity. The resolution of this debate lies in historical case studies, such as the contrasting institutional trajectories of England and Spain, which provide empirical grounding for understanding how institutional legacies shape long-term economic outcomes (North 1989, p.8).
