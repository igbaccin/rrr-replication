The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that sustain or hinder prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, are more decisive. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' responses to local conditions, are central to explaining income disparities among former colonies, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) suggest that the slave trade fostered a legacy of mistrust that continues to impede economic development. These accounts highlight the enduring influence of historical institutions, yet they diverge on the specific pathways through which they operate. North (1989, p.1) underscores the necessity of institutional frameworks in overcoming historical obstacles to growth, framing institutions as the key to unlocking economic potential. Meanwhile, Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that while institutions are important, the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic progress, suggesting that institutional change must be accompanied by technological and intellectual advancement. This review traces how these perspectives converge and diverge, revealing the contested nature of institutional causality in economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate how colonial institutions persisted and influenced income per capita, while North (1989, p.1) emphasizes the role of institutional evolution in overcoming historical obstacles to growth. Both highlight that secure property rights and predictable legal frameworks are essential for sustained economic development. This shared emphasis on institutional durability and its effects on economic outcomes reveals a common thread in the literature: institutions are not merely passive structures but active determinants of growth. The downstream implication, as illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), is that institutional legacies can perpetuate inequality by entrenching elite advantages. This insight reinforces the central claim that institutional arrangements are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores that institutional adjustments are indispensable for sustaining technological progress, which in turn fuels long-run economic growth. Alongside Kuznets, Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19) highlight that the transition from traditional to modern economic systems hinges on the alignment of institutions with advancing technology. This alignment is not merely coincidental but a deliberate process that enables the diffusion and application of new knowledge. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) further notes that past institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, emphasizing the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development. These insights collectively suggest that the institutional environment must evolve in tandem with technological advancements to ensure their effective utilization. Temin (2002, p.6) adds that such institutional frameworks are not only supportive but also regulatory, as seen in postwar Europe where institutions reinforced long-term contracts and mitigated short-term gains that could undermine sustained growth. This demonstrates that institutional arrangements are not passive but actively shape the trajectory of technological progress and, by extension, economic growth. The interplay between institutional change and technological innovation thus remains a central theme in understanding the drivers of long-run economic development.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions shaping skill premiums and wage trends drive long-run economic performance, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), and Pamuk (2007, p.25). These scholars demonstrate that institutional frameworks influencing the transfer of skills across generations and the organization of labor markets have historically determined wage differentials and the pace of economic development. For instance, van Zanden highlights how the efficiency of training institutions in medieval Europe affected the supply of skilled labor, thereby shaping the skill premium and long-term economic trajectories. Similarly, Pamuk shows that flexible institutions in northwestern Europe enabled a more rapid adaptation to post-Black Death economic conditions, fostering sustained growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg further illustrate that institutional design in colonial settings, such as the labor market interventions in East Africa, influenced wage levels and the integration of labor markets, with lasting effects on economic performance. The shared insight is that institutional arrangements not only regulate the distribution of skills and wages but also condition the capacity of economies to respond to structural shifts, thereby embedding long-run growth potential. This mechanism underscores the central role of institutions in shaping the dynamics of skill formation and wage trends, which in turn influence the trajectory of economic performance over time.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that colonial history and institutional legacies are fundamental to understanding long-run economic growth, challenging the focus on institutions alone. The cluster emphasizes that the durability of economic outcomes is shaped not only by the formal structures of governance but also by the deeper historical processes that conditioned the capacity of societies to absorb and adapt to new institutional frameworks. This perspective is supported by evidence that links the persistence of mistrust and underdevelopment to historical patterns of exploitation and dependence, which were embedded in the institutional architecture of colonial rule. The historical dimension of institutional analysis is critical, as it reveals how preexisting conditions, such as the slave trade, influenced the trajectory of economic development through mechanisms like social fragmentation and the erosion of trust. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions is not solely a function of their design but also of their historical congruence with indigenous practices, which can either reinforce or undermine their legitimacy and functionality. The implications of this historical embeddedness are further underscored by the argument that economic underdevelopment is not an inherent condition but a historical creation, shaped by the interactions between local societies and external forces. This stream thus provides a compelling case for integrating historical analysis into the study of institutions, highlighting the need to consider the long-term legacies of colonial and precolonial history in assessing the determinants of economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than economic efficiency, emphasizing the role of class formation in shaping institutional frameworks. Peters (2004, p.19) complements this by showing how land conflicts in Africa reveal exclusion and class formation, challenging the notion of flexible customary systems. Together, these sources suggest that economic institutions are not primarily shaped by efficiency but by the dynamics of power and resource control. The interplay between class struggles and institutional design is further illustrated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who highlight the historical contributions of African women to economic growth, underscoring how gendered distributions of labor and resources have long influenced economic outcomes. This cluster of scholarship thus positions distributional conflicts as a central mechanism through which long-run economic growth is shaped not institutions as the primary cause. The persistence of gendered and class-based inequalities in economic systems underscores the need to examine how these conflicts continue to influence institutional development and economic trajectories. The historical roots of these inequalities provide a critical lens for understanding contemporary economic disparities and their institutional manifestations.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) argues that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Stern (2004, p.2) in its critique of the environmental Kuznets curve. Both authors challenge the institutional determinism that dominates much of the growth literature, emphasizing instead the transformative power of cultural and intellectual shifts. McCloskey illustrates how ideological battles, such as the resistance to secularism in France or the conservative traditions in Spain, constrained economic development by shaping the values and priorities of societies. These cultural forces, he contends, determine whether innovation and entrepreneurship are rewarded or suppressed, thereby influencing long-term growth trajectories. Stern, while focused on environmental policy, reinforces this argument by highlighting how ideological assumptions about development and sustainability shape the design and interpretation of economic models. The shared posture of this stream suggests that the diffusion of pro-growth ideas whether through political rhetoric educational systems or market norms can catalyze institutional change and economic expansion. This mechanism, however, is not universally accepted, as evidenced by the persistent emphasis on institutional legacies in other scholarship. The implications of this view are profound, suggesting that the success of economic systems may depend as much on the prevailing ideologies as on their formal structures.

The literature consistently underscores the centrality of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the persistent emphasis on institutional legacies across multiple studies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). While the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate, the shared recognition of their foundational role is clear. The divergence lies in whether institutional outcomes are primarily shaped by historical contingencies or by broader cultural and ideological frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.30). This disagreement is resolved through the examination of colonial-era institutional legacies, which provide a concrete historical basis for understanding how institutional structures have persisted and evolved over time. The comparative analysis of former European colonies, particularly in the context of extractive versus inclusive institutions, offers a critical empirical lens for evaluating the long-term economic consequences of institutional design (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). By tracing the diffusion of institutional norms from colonial settlements to modern states, scholars have illuminated the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic trajectories. This historical evidence reinforces the argument that institutional persistence is a key determinant of economic performance, thereby anchoring the debate in measurable, empirically grounded comparisons.
