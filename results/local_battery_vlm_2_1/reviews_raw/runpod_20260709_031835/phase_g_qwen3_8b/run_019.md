The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth hinges on understanding how institutional frameworks influence the trajectory of development. At stake is the intellectual debate over whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, or knowledge accumulation are more central. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions operate whether they are the result of historical contingencies the product of social values or the byproduct of economic efficiency. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional development in former colonies was shaped by colonial policies and population dynamics, while North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions are the key to unlocking economic growth, distinct from neoclassical factors like savings and population. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further complicate this by highlighting how institutional legacies in the New World entrenched inequality, suggesting that institutions are not only causal but also persistent. These accounts collectively underscore the centrality of institutions as causal agents in long-run growth, framing them as critical to understanding the divergence in economic performance across societies.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate on how secure property rights and institutional arrangements fostered by colonial governance have had lasting effects on economic growth, demonstrating that the institutional framework established during colonial times continues to influence contemporary economic performance. These accounts collectively suggest that the institutional structures forged during colonial history are not only enduring but also deeply embedded in the mechanisms that drive long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), implies that the economic potential of a society is inextricably linked to the institutional foundations laid during the colonial era.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that institutional changes from colonialism shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that influence productivity and development, a claim supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Pamuk (2007, p.25). These scholars highlight how institutional efficiency in regulating skill transmission and labor markets directly affects the skill premium, which in turn drives long-term economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) demonstrates that the organization of apprenticeship and professional access shaped wage differentials across regions, with Ireland emerging as an outlier due to its fragmented institutional structure. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this logic by emphasizing how institutional frameworks in Europe fostered urbanization and industrialization, creating external economies of scale that accelerated development. The shared insight across these sources is that institutional design, whether through labor market regulation or urban development, generates structural advantages that persist over time. This suggests that the long-term economic outcomes of a society are deeply rooted in the institutional arrangements that govern its productive capacity. The persistence of these frameworks underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping the trajectory of economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, a claim supported by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that the slave trade entrenched institutional legacies that hindered economic growth. These institutional frameworks, rooted in historical patterns of power and resource extraction, created enduring disparities that shaped long-run development trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further illustrate how these inequalities were not only structural but also gendered, as policies and institutions were established to secure the appropriation of resources, often marginalizing women’s contributions to economic growth. The persistence of these institutional legacies, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3), highlights how historical processes of exploitation and mistrust continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The shared insight across these sources is that institutional development in the New World did not merely reflect economic conditions but actively shaped them, embedding inequality into the fabric of long-run growth. This suggests that the economic trajectories of societies are not only determined by material factors but are also deeply influenced by the historical and institutional contexts that define access to resources and opportunities.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts are central to the formation of core economic institutions, a claim supported by Peters (2004, p.19) who highlights how land conflicts in Africa reveal patterns of exclusion and class formation. These sources collectively suggest that institutional frameworks are not neutral but are shaped by underlying struggles over resource allocation and power. The persistence of such conflicts, as noted by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), often leads to institutional designs that reinforce existing inequalities, thereby influencing long-run economic trajectories. McCloskey (2015, p.9) further complicates this by emphasizing the role of ideas and cultural values in shaping institutional outcomes, showing how ideological commitments can sustain or transform economic institutions over time. Together, these authors demonstrate that the interplay between distributional tensions and institutional development is a key mechanism through which economic growth is either constrained or enabled. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding institutions not as static entities but as products of ongoing social and political processes.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge drives long-run economic growth through institutional progress, a claim supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) who highlights the necessity of institutional adjustments alongside technological advancement. Both authors emphasize that economic growth is not merely a function of resource exploitation but requires complementary institutional frameworks to sustain and amplify technological progress. Mokyr underscores how institutional development mitigates coordination failures, ensuring that the benefits of technological innovation are not undermined by rent-seeking behaviors or institutional decay. Kuznets further illustrates this by showing that modern technologies such as steam and electric power could not have been effectively harnessed without corresponding institutional changes, including shifts away from family enterprises and slavery. These insights collectively suggest that institutional evolution is not a passive byproduct of technological change but an active catalyst that enables its transformative potential. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) adds that past institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, reinforcing the idea that institutions shape the trajectory of economic development. This interplay between knowledge, institutions, and growth underscores the need for policies that foster both technological innovation and institutional adaptability. The role of institutions, therefore, is not only to support but to sustain the long-term viability of economic progress.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that historical institutional analysis is relevant to understanding African development, but does not directly link institutions to long-run economic growth. This position is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.11), which emphasizes the need to write African economic history in ways that avoid deterministic narratives. Both sources highlight the importance of institutional congruence between contemporary and indigenous systems, suggesting that such alignment can enhance the legitimacy of governance and foster development. However they also caution against overemphasizing path dependence which can obscure the complex interactions between historical forces and present-day outcomes. The focus on institutional congruence implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their compatibility with preexisting social and political structures. This perspective, while not directly addressing the mechanisms of long-run economic growth, opens the door for further inquiry into how historical institutional legacies shape contemporary economic trajectories. The interplay between indigenous institutions and modern governance structures remains a critical area for exploration in understanding the role of institutions in African development.

The literature consistently underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) emphasizing that colonial-era institutions persisted and shaped contemporary economic outcomes. Similarly, North (1989, p.1) highlights how institutional frameworks in early modern Britain and Spain diverged, leading to distinct economic trajectories in the New World. These studies collectively demonstrate that institutions are not merely static structures but dynamic forces that influence economic development over time. However, the role of institutions in shaping growth remains contested, with Ogilvie (2007, p.30) arguing that cultural beliefs and values, rather than institutional efficiency, better explain economic outcomes. This disagreement centers on whether institutions should be viewed as independent determinants of growth or as embedded within broader social and cultural contexts. The resolution of this debate lies in empirical comparisons that trace the historical persistence of institutions, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who show that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. By analyzing how institutions evolved from colonial to modern contexts, scholars can better understand their role in shaping long-run economic growth.
