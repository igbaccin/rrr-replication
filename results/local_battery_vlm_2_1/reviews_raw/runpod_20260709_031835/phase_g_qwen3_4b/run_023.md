Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remains a contested intellectual frontier, with scholars debating whether institutional structures alone can explain sustained prosperity across diverse historical contexts. The core disagreement centers on whether institutional quality constitutes a sufficient and causal mechanism for economic advancement, or whether it interacts with other factors such as political stability, technological innovation, and resource endowments in complex ways. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political commitment underpins growth, while Austin (2008, p.24) suggests that the reversal of fortune thesis reveals a more nuanced causation where institutions may not be the sole driver but rather part of a broader pattern. This tension between the primacy of institutions and their interplay with other variables defines the current scholarly discourse. The literature demonstrates that institutional quality functions as both a correlate and a causal force in economic development, though its precise role remains contested. Evidence from historical and cross-country analyses indicates that institutions shape economic outcomes through both direct and indirect pathways, but the extent to which they are the fundamental cause requires deeper investigation. The stakes involve resolving whether institutional structures can be universally applied as the primary engine of growth or if context-specific adaptations are necessary.
