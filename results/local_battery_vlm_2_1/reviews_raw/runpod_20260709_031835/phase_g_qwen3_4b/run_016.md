Why some societies achieve sustained economic growth while others stagnate remains a central question in development economics. Institutions are widely regarded as the critical mechanism through which historical conditions shape long-term prosperity, with their role in enabling or constraining economic transformation being contested across scholarly traditions. A live disagreement exists between those who emphasize institutional structures as the primary driver of growth and those who argue that other factors such as technological innovation or cultural shifts play a more decisive role. This review examines how institutions function as causal mechanisms in economic development, focusing on their capacity to create credible commitments that facilitate market integration and long-term investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) identify political institutions as the foundation for economic growth, arguing that the development of representative government with multiple veto points enables societies to overcome short-term political pressures and secure long-term economic stability. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional quality explains significant income disparities across former colonies, suggesting that institutions evolved in response to colonial power structures rather than being neutral mechanisms for growth. The debate over institutional causality is further complicated by Kuznets (1973, p.4), who emphasizes the interdependence of technological advancement and institutional adaptation, while Nunn (2008, p.3) shows how historical conflicts over slavery created institutions that hindered economic development in Africa. These perspectives collectively underscore the centrality of institutions as causal agents in economic transformation, yet they also reveal the complexity of institutional evolution in different historical contexts. Credible commitment underpins growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) establish that institutional features like expropriation risk, property rights enforcement, and rule of law emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political dynamics. North (1989, p.9) demonstrates that secure property rights and institutional stability, such as the Bank of England’s creation, directly reduce transaction costs and enable capital accumulation, while North and Weingast (1989, p.28) emphasize how political institutions with multiple veto points and strong courts mitigate conflicts over economic rights. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further show that institutions advantaging elites through unequal access to economic opportunities perpetuate extractive structures, thereby stifling growth across societies. This pattern underscores how institutional features, when shaped by historical power dynamics, become self-reinforcing barriers to development. These findings collectively reveal that institutional features are not neutral but are deeply embedded in the political and economic power structures that determine growth trajectories. Institutions, therefore, serve as the primary causal mechanism through which historical contexts shape economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) demonstrates that institutional frameworks, particularly property rights, function as critical conduits for long-run economic growth through their capacity to facilitate knowledge accumulation and innovation. This perspective aligns with the broader argument that institutions are the fundamental cause of economic development by emphasizing how specific institutional structures enable the diffusion of ideas and technological progress. Similarly, (McCloskey 2015, p.8) illustrates the counterfactual impact of institutional arrangements on economic trajectories, highlighting how the absence of robust institutional incentives for innovation and trade could have stifled growth in regions like pre-revolutionary France. These findings collectively underscore that institutional quality directly influences the pace and sustainability of economic development. The evidence suggests that institutional frameworks that foster innovation and knowledge exchange are indispensable for achieving sustained economic growth. Institutions, therefore, serve as the primary causal mechanism through which historical contexts shape economic outcomes.

(Kuznets 1973, p.4) and (Stern 2004, p.3) highlight how technological advancement requires institutional shifts incompatible with pre-industrial structures, while (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.27) illustrates that coal's economic costs could have constrained industrialization without institutional adaptations. Komlos (1998, p.2) further demonstrates that Kuznets' framework applies to historical contexts where institutional changes preceded economic growth, as seen in the percapita product trends of the 19th century (Komlos 1998, p.2). These findings collectively reveal institutional obstacles imposed by developed economies such as those in transportation and trade (Bohannan 1959, p.10) can delay or distort the path to modern economic growth. The evidence underscores that without institutional flexibility, technological adoption and economic expansion remain constrained, particularly in regions where pre-existing social structures resist integration into global markets. This structural dependency on institutional adjustments for technological diffusion implies that growth trajectories are fundamentally shaped by the interaction between developed nations' policies and the institutional capacity of less developed regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) establish that pre-industrial economic structures in sub-Saharan Africa constrained women's roles in market economies, directly limiting their capacity to drive technological adoption. Their analysis (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) reveals how the commodification of crops for export shifted women’s control over labor-intensive tasks, while men increasingly monopolized decisions regarding cash crops, thereby fragmenting women’s economic agency. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further illustrates how institutional frameworks for skill transmission in early modern Europe such as guilds were critical for long-term development, contrasting with fragmented labor markets in sub-Saharan Africa where women’s contributions were systematically excluded from formal economic channels. Goldin (2006, p.3) contextualizes these dynamics by showing how shifts in female labor supply, driven by education and institutional reforms, accelerated economic participation in industrialized societies. Hopkins (2019, p.10) underscores the historical erasure of gendered economic dynamics in African scholarship, highlighting how postmodern historiographical shifts have obscured the role of women in pre-industrial development. This evidence collectively demonstrates that gendered institutional structures independently shape economic outcomes, without being downstream effects of institutional quality or technological adoption. Gender dynamics thus provide a parallel pathway to growth operating through distinct mechanisms that complement institutional frameworks.

Institutional frameworks fundamentally drive long-run economic growth through their role in shaping political and economic power dynamics, as demonstrated by North and Weingast (1989, p.28) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). Their work establishes that institutions function as equilibrium outcomes, directly influencing economic outcomes by mitigating expropriation risks and enabling long-term capital markets. North (1989, p.9) further shows that institutional evolution, such as the Bank of England’s creation, correlates with declining transaction costs and sustained growth, underscoring the interdependence of political and economic structures. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide empirical evidence that institutional quality explains significant portions of income disparities across former colonies, reinforcing the causal link between institutions and growth. The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development study (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1) highlights how European colonization policies generated distinct institutional frameworks that persistently shape economic trajectories. These findings collectively establish institutions as the primary driver of long-run growth, operating through mechanisms that affect both political stability and market efficiency. While the evidence points to institutions as the root cause, the precise mechanisms of institutional change remain contested. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that institutional effectiveness depends on alignment with indigenous social structures, whereas North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize institutional commitment to enforce rules as critical for market development. The historical comparison of European colonization policies in different colonies, as analyzed in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1), resolves this tension by showing that institutions shaped by colonial powers directly determine economic outcomes, with congruence to local social structures enhancing growth potential. Thus, institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with their design and enforcement mechanisms determining economic trajectories.
