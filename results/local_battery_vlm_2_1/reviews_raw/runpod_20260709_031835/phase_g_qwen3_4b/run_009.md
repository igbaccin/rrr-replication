Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth represent a pivotal intellectual claim with profound implications for understanding historical development trajectories. The debate over institutional causality centers on whether institutions function as the primary driver of economic advancement or whether other factors such as technological progress or resource endowments exert more decisive influence. This literature review identifies a live disagreement: some scholars argue that institutions are the singular cause of economic growth, while others contend that institutional effects are contingent on broader socio-political contexts. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political commitment to institutional rules underpins economic development, whereas Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) frame institutions as equilibrium outcomes shaped by competing political interests. The tension between these perspectives reveals a deeper conflict over whether institutions operate as exogenous determinants or as endogenous responses to power dynamics. This fundamental divergence shapes how scholars interpret the colonial origins of economic disparities and the role of institutional frameworks in sustaining long-term prosperity.

North (1989, p.9) demonstrates how institutional innovations, such as the Bank of England, directly lower transaction costs through enhanced security of property rights. Together, these sources establish that institutional capacity defined by robust political rules and enforceable commitments fosters economic growth by stabilizing long-term investments. This mechanism operates through the reduction of ex-ante uncertainty, allowing capital to flow efficiently across markets. Consequently, the capacity to enforce institutional rules becomes a prerequisite for sustained economic development, as evidenced by the historical emergence of secure financial systems in England. The interplay between political commitment and institutional design thus creates conditions where markets can thrive without the disruptive volatility of rule-breaking.

This framework, which identifies institutions as dynamic responses to power structures, explains why certain regions historically developed robust economic systems while others remained trapped in extractive regimes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The colonial legacy, for instance, reveals how institutions evolved to maximize short-term rents for dominant groups rather than fostering sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Mokyr (2005, p.2) complements this by emphasizing that pre-industrial economic activity was far more advanced than commonly assumed, challenging the notion that industrialization was a singular historical event (Mokyr 2005, p.2). These findings collectively underscore that institutional quality is not a static condition but a contested outcome of political and economic interactions. The colonial origins of modern economic growth thus highlight how institutions can either enable or constrain long-term prosperity, depending on the balance of power and incentives within a society. Institutional features, therefore, serve as critical conduits for the relationship between historical processes and contemporary economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.22) demonstrates that historical ethnic fragmentation in Africa has been a critical factor in shaping institutionally weak economies, as multiple loyalties rooted in economic interests and religious affiliations undermine cohesive governance (Hopkins 2009, p.22; Hopkins 2019, p.10). This pattern aligns with Nunn (2008, p.3), who identifies that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding created institutional constraints that hindered long-term economic development, further reinforcing the role of colonial-era violence in institutional degradation (Nunn 2008, p.3). The evidence suggests that when institutions are not congruent with indigenous social structures, as Englebert argues, they become ineffective in fostering growth, leading to patrimonial politics and regional instability (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Consequently, the historical trajectory of African economies reveals how institutional weaknesses, stemming from fragmented social structures and colonial exploitation, have constrained economic progress across generations. This historical legacy underscores the necessity of addressing institutional roots to achieve sustainable development in Africa.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that institutional frameworks of developed nations constrain economic growth in less developed regions, a claim supported by Temin (2002, p.4) and Stern (2004, p.3) (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Temin 2002, p.4; Stern 2004, p.3). The paper demonstrates that technological advancements, which require institutional adaptations, are incompatible with pre-modern social structures such as family enterprises and rural livelihoods, thereby hindering growth in regions lacking such institutional capacity (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This structural incompatibility explains why less developed economies struggle to adopt modern economic practices, as their social and institutional frameworks remain ill-suited for industrialization (Stern 2004, p.6). Consequently, the historical trajectory of economic development reveals that institutional maturity must precede technological adoption to ensure sustainable growth, a pattern observed in the transition from agrarian to industrial societies (Temin 2002, p.15).

Ogilvie (2007, p.9) demonstrates that institutional frameworks, rather than isolated institutions, drive economic outcomes by emphasizing their interdependence and collective efficacy. This perspective aligns with Austin (2008, p.24), who argues that institutions are 'the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth' but highlights the complexity of causation across historical contexts. Together, these works underscore how institutional interdependencies, such as the European family system’s connections to labor and credit markets, create conditions for sustained growth, as Ogilvie (2007, p.9) observes that such systems 'may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.' Van Zanden (2009, p.19) complements this by showing how institutional structures, like guilds, facilitate human capital formation through apprenticeship contracts, thereby linking institutional quality to long-term economic development. The evidence collectively reveals that institutional change operates as a mechanism for growth through interconnected systems that optimize economic transactions and human capital accumulation. Institutional interdependence thus becomes a critical pathway for institutions to act as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) establish that institutional evolution shapes long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of economic opportunities and capital accumulation pathways. This perspective, supported by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Allen (2001, p.1), demonstrates how historical institutional trajectories directly affect growth outcomes through their impact on inequality and opportunity structures. Together, these sources reveal that institutional evolution operates as a distinct pathway to growth by altering the conditions under which capital and labor interact. The historical evidence indicates that institutional trajectories, once established, constrain the potential for future economic advancement by perpetuating patterns of inequality and opportunity access. Institutional evolution thus provides a critical alternative explanation for long-run growth that operates through the distribution of economic opportunities rather than institutional quality per se.
