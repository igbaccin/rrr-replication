Institutions serve as the primary catalyst for sustained economic advancement, with their design and functionality directly influencing long-term growth trajectories. The intellectual stakes are profound: understanding how institutional structures foster or hinder economic progress is critical for addressing persistent global inequalities and fostering inclusive development. A central live disagreement exists between scholars who attribute growth to institutional efficiency and those who emphasize institutional interdependencies as the driving force. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political commitment to institutional rules is foundational for economic growth, while Ogilvie (2007, p.9) highlights the persistent ambiguity in defining institutional efficiency across historical contexts. Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies technological transformation as a key driver of modern economic growth, yet notes its incompatibility with pre-industrial institutional frameworks. These perspectives collectively underscore the complexity of institutional causality in economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) establish that institutional features like expropriation risk and property rights enforcement shape long-run economic growth by establishing equilibrium institutions that determine development paths, a claim supported by (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.28; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.9). These equilibrium institutions emerge from the interplay between political power dynamics and economic incentives, where institutions that maximize rents for dominant groups often suppress long-term growth potential. This institutional legacy persists through mechanisms such as weak property rights enforcement and high expropriation risk, which limit investment and innovation. Their analysis reveals that institutions which prioritize short-term political gains over long-term economic stability become self-reinforcing, creating cycles of underdevelopment. The persistence of such institutions underscores the critical role of institutional interdependencies in shaping economic trajectories, as evidenced by the interlinked systems of property rights, political power, and market regulation that define equilibrium institutions across historical contexts.

North (1989, p.9) establishes that institutional capacity, defined by the ability to enforce contracts and manage public resources, directly catalyzes long-run economic growth by lowering transaction costs and enhancing market predictability. This mechanism, rooted in the historical development of financial institutions like the Bank of England, reduces uncertainty for capital accumulation and investment. (North 1989, p.9) demonstrates how the English financial revolution, exemplified by the Bank of England's founding in 1694, transformed property rights security and transaction efficiency, thereby enabling sustained capital formation. (North 1989, p.1) further underscores the interdependence of political and economic institutions, arguing that neoclassical theories, which emphasize population and savings, overlook the critical role of institutional design in growth trajectories. The decline in transaction costs, as evidenced by the Bank of England's institutional innovations, reflects a broader pattern where secure property rights and robust financial systems create conditions for capital accumulation and technological advancement. This capacity-driven growth pathway, where institutional effectiveness directly influences economic outcomes, underscores the necessity of institutional robustness for long-term prosperity. Institutional capacity thus serves as a critical conduit for growth, with its historical manifestations providing empirical validation for the causal link between institutional quality and economic development.

This claim, supported by (Kuznets 1973, p.4) and (Kuznets 1973, p.1), reveals how modern economic growth emerges from the interplay between technological advancement and institutional adaptation. The paper argues that technological progress, such as steam and electric power, necessitates institutional reforms to overcome barriers like illiteracy, slavery, and rural social structures, which were prevalent in earlier economic systems. This mechanism implies that institutional change is a prerequisite for technological adoption, a point reinforced by the historical examples of developed nations imposing structural obstacles on less developed regions. Consequently, the growth trajectory of economies is fundamentally shaped by the compatibility between technological innovation and institutional frameworks. The Kuznets curve, therefore, reflects a dynamic process where institutional evolution enables technological advancement, creating a pathway for sustained economic development. This institutional-technological interdependence underscores the necessity of adaptive institutions for long-term growth, distinct from the capital accumulation pathway previously described.
