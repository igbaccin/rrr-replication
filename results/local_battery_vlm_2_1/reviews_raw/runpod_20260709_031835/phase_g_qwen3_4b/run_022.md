Institutions serve as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim that has been extensively debated across multiple scholarly streams. The intellectual stakes are high, as determining whether institutions can be the primary driver of economic development challenges prevailing narratives that emphasize technological progress or demographic shifts. A live disagreement centers on whether institutional features are the root cause of economic outcomes or merely a consequence of other factors, with scholars like North and Weingast (1989, p.28; 1989, p.2) emphasizing the role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes, while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.1) argue that institutional structures emerge from historical processes and are critical to long-term growth. The tension between these perspectives highlights the complexity of institutional causality in economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that technological progress and institutional adaptation are interdependent, with modern economic growth requiring institutional shifts to accommodate industrial-scale innovations. This historical relationship is reinforced by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8), who note how pre-industrial inequality patterns shape long-term economic trajectories through institutional constraints. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further demonstrate that institutional structures, particularly property rights and governance mechanisms, emerge from historical contexts to determine growth pathways. These institutions, as Temin (2002, p.4) argues, enable or hinder economic expansion by facilitating or obstructing labor market flexibility. The interplay between institutional evolution and economic outcomes is thus deeply rooted in historical processes, where early institutional choices dictate the efficiency of resource allocation across generations. This historical continuity underscores how institutions serve as the foundational mechanism through which economic growth is sustained over time. Historical institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that determine how resources are allocated and used across generations.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law critically influence long-run economic growth by shaping income distribution across historical contexts. These findings underscore how institutional quality directly affects income distribution and, consequently, the trajectory of economic growth. Institutions that maximize rents for politically dominant groups often suppress the conditions necessary for broad-based development, thereby perpetuating inequality and stifling growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern highlights the role of institutional design in determining both the distribution of economic benefits and the sustainability of growth trajectories. The colonial origins of modern economic disparities illustrate how institutional choices, particularly those that favor short-term political gains, can undermine long-term prosperity and equitable development.

Their analysis demonstrates that the creation of robust institutional frameworks, such as the Bank of England, reduces transaction costs by securing property rights over time, a critical factor for capital accumulation and sustained market expansion (North 1989, p.9). The interplay between political commitment and institutional stability thus provides a mechanism by which constitutional structures can catalyze economic growth while mitigating the risks of political capture. By anchoring economic rights to political processes, these institutions foster environments where capital formation and innovation thrive, directly addressing the challenge of sustaining growth amid competing interests. The evidence from North and Weingast’s work underscores how constitutional arrangements that prioritize rule of law and institutional continuity become indispensable for long-term prosperity.

Institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth through their role in establishing durable commitments and enabling capital formation, as demonstrated by North and Weingast's (1989, p.28) analysis of constitutional arrangements that mitigate political capture. Their work shows that explicit multiple veto points and the primacy of common law courts over economic affairs create stable environments for investment, directly addressing the challenge of sustaining growth amid competing interests (North and Weingast 1989, p.28). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) identify institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law as critical determinants of economic performance, arguing that equilibrium institutions often prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings converge on the idea that institutions act as the binding mechanism through which political and economic structures interact to foster prosperity, with constitutional stability serving as a catalyst for capital accumulation and innovation.
