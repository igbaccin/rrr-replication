Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remain a cornerstone of economic theory, with scholars debating whether institutional structures directly drive or merely correlate with sustained prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions function as causal mechanisms that shape economic outcomes or as byproducts of broader developmental processes. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political commitment to enforce rules is pivotal, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate institutions’ role in explaining income disparities across former colonies. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights how technological advancements require institutional adaptations, suggesting that institutions evolve in response to economic transformations. These perspectives collectively underscore institutions as active, dynamic forces rather than static conditions. The debate thus hinges on whether institutions are the primary driver or a secondary consequence of economic development. Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) establish that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law act as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political power dynamics, creating conditions for long-run growth. This claim is reinforced by North (1989, p.9), who demonstrates how the Bank of England’s creation reduced transaction costs by securing property rights, thereby enabling capital markets to flourish. Together, these sources show that institutional quality emerges from political processes that balance competing interests, directly influencing economic development. This interplay between political and economic institutions underscores a mechanism through which institutions drive growth: by mediating the distribution of power and resources in ways that stabilize and expand productive capacity. Institutions shape economic development, which drives long-run economic growth through their role in determining property rights, rule of law, and political stability (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North 1989, p.9; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8).

Mokyr (2005, p.2) identifies the intellectual evolution as a foundational process for economic development, which subsequently shapes institutions that drive long-run growth. This intellectual trajectory, rooted in the historical emergence of scientific inquiry and institutional innovation, demonstrates how knowledge accumulation catalyzes institutional change. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further illustrates how guilds and apprenticeship systems in early modern Europe facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, thereby contributing to human capital development. Goldin (2006, p.3) complements this by showing that shifts in labor market conditions, such as the increased demand for female office workers and educational advancements, were instrumental in human capital formation. McCloskey (2015, p.9) emphasizes the role of ideas and ideologies in economic development, arguing that intellectual progress often precedes institutional reforms. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.27) provide additional context by highlighting the role of coal in industrialization, though their findings suggest that the economic impact of coal was less significant than commonly assumed. Together, these sources establish that intellectual and institutional evolution acts as the foundational process that enables institutions to foster long-run economic growth through human capital development and technological innovation. This historical continuity underscores how institutions emerge from and respond to intellectual advancements, ultimately driving sustainable economic development.

(Kuznets 1973, p.4) and (Temin 2002, p.4) jointly demonstrate that technological advancement and institutional adaptation are intrinsically linked, with Kuznets emphasizing the necessity of replacing outdated social structures to accommodate industrialization. Temin (2002, p.4) further clarifies that labor force shifts from agriculture to industry drive growth, but this transition is constrained by institutional frameworks that must evolve to sustain economic progress. Stern (2004, p.3) complements this by showing how economic development patterns influence environmental outcomes, particularly through the restructuring of industrial sectors and urban dynamics. The historical trajectory of institutional change thus establishes that growth pathways depend on the capacity of societies to institutionalize technological adoption while managing labor market transitions. This interplay between institutional evolution and economic transformation underscores the foundational role of institutions in long-run growth, as societies must adapt their structures to align with the demands of industrialization. Consequently, the institutional prerequisites for sustained economic development emerge as a critical determinant of growth trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.22) demonstrates that African institutional legacies profoundly shape contemporary economic outcomes through historical pathways of ethnic cohesion and fragmentation. This analysis, supported by (Hopkins 2009, p.22; Hopkins 2019, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19; Nunn 2008, p.3), reveals how pre-industrial institutional frameworks in Africa were interdependent with labor markets, credit systems, and social structures, creating conditions that either facilitated or hindered economic development. The interplay between ethnic fragmentation and institutional efficacy, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18), shows that institutions congruent with indigenous social patterns enhance governance and growth potential, while misaligned structures risk fostering corruption and regional instability. This historical trajectory underscores that Africa's economic development pathways depend on institutional continuity and adaptation, where pre-existing social structures directly influence the capacity for institutional resilience and growth. Consequently, the institutional legacies of African societies remain a critical determinant of contemporary economic outcomes, as historical patterns of institutional formation continue to shape the conditions for sustainable development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) identify institutional shortcomings in African national accounts as a critical barrier to economic convergence, aligning with the broader concept of institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Their analysis reveals that historical data gaps have obscured the true trajectory of African economic performance, where pre-existing institutional weaknesses perpetuate cycles of underdevelopment. Komlos (1998, p.2) further contextualizes this issue by framing growth in per capita product through a Kuznetsian lens, emphasizing that institutional quality determines whether economies can sustain long-term productivity gains. Allen (2001, p.1) complements this by demonstrating how institutional divergence in European cities during the early modern period created enduring disparities in economic development, a pattern that echoes contemporary African challenges. These sources collectively emphasize that institutional legacies, not temporary shocks, shape long-term economic outcomes in regions. The historical evidence suggests that without addressing foundational institutional constraints, African economies remain vulnerable to the same patterns of underdevelopment that have persisted for centuries. This institutional persistence, therefore, remains a central obstacle to sustainable growth in the region.

Institutional legacies persistently shape long-run economic growth, as evidenced by North and Weingast (1989, p.28) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48). Both scholars identify institutional frameworks that endure across historical periods; North and Weingast emphasize how political institutions directly influence economic outcomes through mechanisms like rule enforcement and transaction costs, while Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutional features such as property rights enforcement explain significant income disparities across regions. Ogilvie (2007, p.9) further clarifies that institutions function as 'adequate and even progressive' systems that maximize economic welfare despite varying definitions of efficiency, reinforcing the idea that institutions evolve in response to historical constraints rather than isolated shocks. North (1989, p.9) adds that the English financial revolution, including the Bank of England, exemplifies how institutional innovations reduce transaction costs and foster long-term growth, directly linking institutional design to economic development. The colonial origins of comparative growth, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) show, reveal that institutions emerge from historical contexts and persistently affect economic trajectories, with Kuznets noting that technological advancements require institutional shifts incompatible with pre-modern social structures. This convergence highlights institutional persistence, not temporary fluctuations, as the root cause of enduring economic disparities, particularly in regions where historical constraints remain unaddressed.
