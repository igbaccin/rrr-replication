How institutions shape long-run economic growth remains a central intellectual challenge, as their role in enabling sustainable development directly impacts the feasibility of prosperity across societies. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the fundamental cause of growth or merely a byproduct of economic conditions, with scholars diverging on the mechanisms through which political structures influence economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, particularly those with multiple veto points and courts that dominate economic affairs, are critical to economic growth by protecting property rights and fostering stability. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional features such as property rights enforcement significantly explain income disparities across nations, suggesting that institutions are pivotal in the process of economic development. These findings converge on the idea that institutions act as causal mechanisms rather than mere outcomes, with North (1989, p.9) emphasizing the role of secure property rights in enabling financial innovation and long-term growth. The debate thus hinges on whether institutions can be actively shaped to promote development or are predetermined by historical and political forces.

This framework implies that deliberate institutional reforms can enhance growth trajectories when they align with political commitment structures. The evidence suggests that constitutional design, through its capacity to enforce rule of law and secure property rights, is a critical determinant of economic development.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson establish that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law serve as causal mechanisms for long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their work demonstrates that equilibrium institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritize short-term rents over sustainable development, thereby explaining persistent income disparities between former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This framework reveals how institutional quality directly influences income distribution through historical colonial policies, with evidence showing that institutions designed to maximize colonial rents frequently hinder long-term growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). The colonial origins of comparative development highlight that institutions, including rule of law and property rights, are critical determinants of economic performance, as European colonization policies created divergent institutional structures that continue to affect modern economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). These findings underscore that institutional quality is not merely a consequence of economic activity but a fundamental driver of long-run growth, with specific mechanisms like rule of law and property rights enforcement acting as causal pathways from institutional design to economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth requires institutional shifts incompatible with preexisting social structures, such as family enterprises and slavery, which persist in less developed regions due to colonial policies. This historical institutionalism framework, as evidenced by (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Temin 2002, p.4; Hopkins 2009, p.22), demonstrates that institutional evolution enables technological adoption by dismantling barriers to capital accumulation and innovation. These findings collectively underscore that institutions function as causal mechanisms through which historical conditions shape economic outcomes, directly linking institutional design to growth pathways. Historical institutionalism thus provides a robust lens for understanding how institutional evolution drives long-run economic development through its effects on technological adoption and capital formation.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) identify persistent underestimation of African economic activity as a critical constraint on growth analysis, revealing that historical data gaps have obscured institutional progress. Their empirical analysis demonstrates that national accounts in Sub-Saharan Africa frequently understate GDP by up to 15%, a shortcoming that undermines comparisons of institutional quality across regions. This gap, they argue, obscures the role of institutional evolution in economic development, as accurate measurement of institutional performance requires precise tracking of economic activity over time. The study highlights how incomplete data on African economies has led researchers to misattribute growth patterns to factors like political instability rather than institutional capacity. By tracing economic indicators from 1960 to 2010, Broadberry and Gardner show that institutional changes such as the adoption of market-oriented policies were often masked by accounting errors. Their findings suggest that without correcting for these data limitations, the causal link between institutional quality and long-run growth remains obscured. Consequently, the institutional development of African economies cannot be fully assessed without addressing these measurement challenges. This evidence underscores the necessity of robust data infrastructure for institutional analysis in low-income regions.

Their work establishes that institutional changes such as market-oriented policies directly influence economic outcomes, with North and Weingast emphasizing the role of political institutions in economic development and Acemoglu et al. highlighting institutional factors in colonial economic trajectories. Both scholars agree that institutions are pivotal in economic growth, with North and Weingast noting that political institutions like courts and veto points protect economic rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.28), while Acemoglu et al. show that institutional features such as property rights enforcement account for significant income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This shared focus on institutions as growth drivers underscores their centrality in economic development. The extent to which institutions can be altered without triggering political instability remains contested. North emphasizes that institutional change requires secure property rights and long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9), whereas Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive due to political power dynamics (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The disagreement centers on whether institutional change is feasible without political conflict. This tension is resolved through the colonial origins of economic development, where Acemoglu et al. use European mortality rates to demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial policies directly impact long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). Thus, institutional development must account for historical and political contexts to ensure sustainable economic progress.
