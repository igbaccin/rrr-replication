The question of why some economies grow while others stagnate hinges on whether institutions act as the primary engine of long-run prosperity. This inquiry confronts a fundamental tension: whether institutions decisively influence economic outcomes or other forces such as resource endowments or technological shifts dominate the growth process. The debate centers on whether institutions, particularly political institutions, create the conditions for sustained development by enabling credible commitments and enforcing rules that protect property rights and foster market stability. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, such as explicit veto points and courts, are critical for economic growth by mitigating conflicts over power. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) reinforce this by demonstrating that institutions shaped by political dynamics account for significant income disparities across regions. These scholars collectively emphasize that institutions are not merely byproducts of economic activity but active drivers that channel resources toward growth. Kuznets (1973, p.4) further contextualizes this by highlighting how technological progress requires institutional shifts that overcome historical barriers to modernization. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions can be the sole cause of growth or if they interact with other factors to produce outcomes. The evidence suggests that institutions function as the foundational cause of long-run economic growth through their role in enabling credible commitments and enforcing rules that protect property rights and foster market stability.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that institutional features such as expropriation risk and property rights enforcement are critical determinants of long-run economic growth, as they shape the equilibrium institutions that influence development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North 1989, p.9). These findings align with North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.28), who emphasize that the stability of property rights and the rule of law are foundational to economic development. The Bank of England’s establishment in 1694, which reduced transaction costs and secured long-term capital markets, exemplifies how institutional innovations directly enable sustained growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional robustness, they contend, is essential for fostering market stability and long-term investment. The evidence suggests that institutional quality, measured by the rule of law and property rights protection, is a primary driver of economic growth, as it enables credible commitments and reduces the risk of expropriation. This mechanism explains why regions with stronger institutions experience higher levels of industrialization and wealth creation over time. Institutional quality thus serves as a foundational cause of long-run economic growth by structuring the conditions under which markets can thrive and innovation can flourish.

This historical institutionalism framework, supported by (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.22; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19; van Zanden 2009, p.19), demonstrates how technological advancement necessitates new institutional arrangements that replace family enterprises, illiteracy, and slavery with formalized economic systems. These patterns reveal that institutions act as critical conduits for technological adoption and human capital formation, enabling societies to transition from agrarian economies to industrialized ones. The evidence underscores that institutional evolution, driven by historical contexts, shapes long-term economic outcomes by determining how resources are allocated and how individuals interact within economic systems.

Temin (2002, p.4) demonstrates that institutional evolution, particularly the shift from agrarian to industrial labor structures, drives long-run economic growth, contra the view that economic policies alone determine growth trajectories. This perspective is reinforced by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1), who highlight the persistent challenges in African economies despite periods of rapid growth, emphasizing the role of institutional continuity in shaping long-term outcomes. The evidence underscores that institutional change, not policy interventions, is the primary mechanism through which economic systems transition from underdeveloped to advanced states. This pattern reveals that growth sustainability hinges on the capacity of institutions to adapt and evolve, rather than on short-term policy adjustments. The interplay between institutional change and economic performance thus provides a critical lens for understanding why some regions achieve sustained prosperity while others remain trapped in cycles of underdevelopment.

Nunn (2008, p.3) demonstrates that undifferentiated economic and social structures impede long-run growth by restricting trade and investment opportunities, a finding that aligns with Komlos (1998, p.2) on the percapita growth implications of structural constraints. The evidence reveals that societies with fragmented institutions, such as those in pre-colonial Africa, struggle to sustain economic activity due to limited trade networks and resource allocation inefficiencies. This structural constraint, where domestic warfare and raiding undermine institutional cohesion, directly limits the capacity for long-term economic expansion, as the production of slaves historically exacerbated these deficiencies. Consequently, the inability to establish robust institutional frameworks hinders the development of trade and investment mechanisms necessary for sustained growth. These findings underscore how institutional underdevelopment perpetuates cycles of underdevelopment by obstructing the emergence of market-oriented economies.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by enabling durable commitments and reducing transaction costs, as North and Weingast (1989, p.28) establish that political liberties and institutional frameworks are foundational to economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) corroborate this by demonstrating that institutional features explain significant income disparities across historical contexts, while North (1989, p.9) highlights how secure property rights catalyze financial innovation and economic expansion. These findings collectively affirm that institutional structures directly influence economic outcomes through mechanisms like rule enforcement and market stability. The critical role of institutions in facilitating growth emerges from their capacity to mitigate conflict and foster trust, as Kuznets (1973, p.4) notes that technological advancement requires institutional shifts incompatible with outdated social structures. This synthesis underscores how institutional evolution is intrinsically linked to economic progress, with evidence from colonial-era institutions showing that extractive systems hinder development, whereas robust frameworks promote sustained growth. The divergence in institutional design, where Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth, and Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasize congruence with indigenous institutions for effective governance, highlights a tension between immediate political interests and long-term economic development. This disagreement is resolved through empirical comparisons of colonial institutions, where Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth necessitates institutional adaptations to overcome historical constraints, such as slavery and family enterprises. Ultimately, the historical evidence from colonial transitions demonstrates that institutions designed to maximize short-term gains often impede long-term growth, while those aligned with broader societal needs foster sustainable development.
