Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim that has profound implications for understanding how societies evolve technologically and politically. This position rests on the view that institutional structures, particularly those governing the enforcement of contracts and the distribution of power, shape the trajectory of economic development by enabling or constraining the accumulation of capital and innovation. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions or other factors, such as technological change or cultural values, drive sustained growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, including the rule of law and mechanisms for collective decision-making, are critical for economic prosperity, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize that institutional quality explains significant cross-country differences in income levels and industrialization. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights how technological progress is intertwined with institutional adaptation, suggesting that growth requires institutions capable of accommodating new economic practices. These perspectives collectively underscore the centrality of institutions in the long-run economic process, though they differ in their emphasis on political versus economic mechanisms. The stakes are high: misidentifying the root causes of growth could lead to policies that fail to address the structural constraints that hinder development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political dynamics, where extractive institutions may persist even in contexts of potential growth. These findings align with North (1989, p.9), who emphasizes that secure property rights, critical for long-term capital markets, were historically contingent on political arrangements such as the establishment of the Bank of England. This pattern, supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), highlights that institutional disparities across societies stem from historical power structures, which in turn influence economic trajectories. The interplay between political commitment and economic outcomes, as noted by Temin (2002, p.4), underscores that institutional stability is a prerequisite for sustained growth. Collectively, these sources establish that institutions function as upstream drivers of long-run economic growth, with their historical formation and political underpinnings directly shaping development pathways. This institutional legacy thus provides a robust foundation for understanding why certain societies achieve higher levels of prosperity while others remain constrained by extractive structures.

This historical trajectory, as evidenced by Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Hopkins (2009, p.22), reveals how technological advancement necessitates institutional shifts that undermine traditional family enterprises, illiteracy, and slavery. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) further demonstrate that such transformations impact gendered economic roles, where women’s control over production diminishes as crops become commoditized for export, thereby altering institutional dynamics in sub-Saharan Africa. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) contextualize these shifts within African economic performance, noting that historical patterns of slow growth persist despite periods of rapid development, suggesting that institutional legacies from pre-industrial societies continue to shape contemporary economic trajectories. Nunn (2008, p.3) complements this by showing that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding historically constrained institutional development, creating conditions that hindered long-term economic growth. van Zanden (2009, p.19) underscores the role of institutional structures in facilitating or impeding human capital formation, where guilds in western Europe exemplify how formal institutions can enhance intergenerational skill transfer, yet also risk monopolizing skills and suppressing market-clearing wages. Collectively, these sources establish that historical institutions and economic trajectories shape long-run economic growth by establishing foundational structures that determine development paths. This historical continuity thus provides a critical lens for understanding how institutional legacies from pre-industrial societies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Stern (2004, p.3) demonstrates that environmental and resource constraints significantly influence long-run economic growth trajectories, challenging institutional explanations as the sole driver. This perspective, grounded in the work of McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.27), reveals how historical resource availability and ecological conditions shape economic development through distinct pathways. Stern (2004, p.3) identifies shifts in industrial output mix across development stages as critical, where early industrialization increases pollution intensity while later transitions toward services reduce emissions. These findings collectively indicate that resource limitations and environmental factors operate as alternative explanations for growth, independent of institutional quality. The interplay between resource constraints and historical development pathways thus provides a compelling counterpoint to institutionalist narratives, suggesting that economic outcomes are shaped by a complex interplay of ecological and resource-based conditions rather than institutional legacies alone.

Institutional quality fundamentally shapes long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the role of political institutions in facilitating market transactions and securing property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) emphasize that the development of representative government structures with explicit veto points and courts that dominate economic affairs creates conditions conducive to sustained growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) similarly highlight that institutional features, such as property rights enforcement, significantly influence economic performance and income disparities across regions. These findings collectively underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic development. The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1) further establish that institutions shaped by colonial policies have long-term effects on economic outcomes, with institutions designed to maximize short-term rents often hindering long-term growth. North (1989, p.9) identifies the Bank of England’s creation as a pivotal institutional innovation that reduced transaction costs and fostered economic growth through enhanced security of property rights. Kuznets (1973, p.4) complements this by noting that technological advancements require institutional shifts to overcome barriers such as illiteracy and slavery, which were prevalent in pre-modern societies. These perspectives converge on institutions as the primary driver of economic growth, though the specific mechanisms and historical contexts vary. The critical disagreement lies in whether institutions are the sole determinant or one of multiple factors, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) arguing that extractive institutions may persist due to political power dynamics rather than economic efficiency. This tension is resolved through the empirical evidence from colonial histories, where institutions designed for short-term gains often lead to underdeveloped economies, as observed in the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1).
