Institutions shape long-run economic growth through their role in enabling efficient transactional networks and durable commitment mechanisms, a claim contested by scholars who emphasize alternative pathways to prosperity. The foundational argument posits that institutional structures directly determine economic outcomes by facilitating or constraining productive activity, with North and Weingast (1989, p.28) asserting that political institutions govern economic growth through enforceable rules and commitment mechanisms, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) highlight how institutions emerge from power struggles to maximize rents rather than growth. Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies institutional barriers to technological adoption in less developed regions, underscoring the interplay between institutional maturity and economic modernization. Ogilvie (2007, p.9) synthesizes these perspectives by noting that institutions exist to overcome transactional obstacles, even when scholars reject efficiency as a metric, thereby linking institutional frameworks to economic outcomes. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or a secondary consequence of technological and market evolution, with evidence suggesting that institutional quality influences growth trajectories through distinct pathways. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that historical institutions critically shape long-run economic growth by establishing rules that affect resource allocation and incentives across generations. Their analysis reveals that institutional variations, such as security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption, explain divergent development paths among societies, with elite advantages often perpetuating inequality. The interdependence of institutions, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), further underscores that no single institution operates in isolation; for instance, European family systems and labor markets were interlinked to foster early industrialization. Similarly, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) observe that African economies have faced persistent challenges in institutional development, often rooted in historical legacies that hindered the adoption of growth-oriented practices. These findings collectively illustrate how institutional structures, when robust and adaptive, can channel political incentives toward productive outcomes rather than extractive practices, thereby enabling sustained economic advancement.

Institutional interdependence underpins long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the interlinked systems that foster industrialization in historical contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes that European family systems, labor markets, credit markets, and insurance mechanisms formed a cohesive framework that facilitated early industrialization, while North and Weingast (1989, p.28) identify how political institutions, such as common law courts and veto points, directly shape economic outcomes through their role in enforcing property rights and mitigating extractive practices. These findings converge on the necessity of institutional coordination to channel political incentives toward productive growth rather than rent-seeking behavior. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further support this by showing that institutional features, including property rights enforcement and rule of law, significantly influence economic development trajectories across former colonies, thereby accounting for persistent income disparities. However, the precise mechanisms by which institutions interact remain contested, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlighting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive due to political constraints, even when they appear to facilitate economic activity. This tension between institutional interdependence and extractive equilibrium structures is resolved through empirical comparisons of colonial institutions, where Kuznets (1973, p.1) demonstrates that modern economic growth requires institutional adaptations incompatible with pre-industrial practices like family enterprise or slavery, thus affirming that institutional evolution is critical for sustained growth.
