The enduring question of how societies achieve and sustain long-run economic growth has captivated scholars across disciplines, prompting debate over the relative importance of factors ranging from geography to culture; at stake is our ability to understand the fundamental drivers of prosperity and design policies that foster broad-based development. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions primarily serve as reflections of underlying societal characteristics or actively shape economic trajectories, a distinction with profound implications for policy interventions aimed at promoting growth. Some scholars emphasize the role of pre-existing cultural or geographic conditions in determining institutional outcomes, while others argue that institutions themselves are causal forces capable of generating persistent differences in economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This review examines how scholarship has grappled with this complex relationship, focusing on arguments concerning institutional design, historical legacies, and distributional conflicts. The understanding of institutions as more than just reflections of existing power structures is vital to explaining long-run economic outcomes.

Acemoglu and colleagues have consistently highlighted the critical role of institutions in shaping a nation’s economic fate, arguing that extractive institutions, those designed to concentrate wealth and power within a select few, actively impede development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Their work underscores how colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic disparities, demonstrating that variations in mortality rates among European settlers led to divergent institutional paths in different colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). These findings challenge purely geographic or cultural explanations for poverty, suggesting instead that political and institutional choices have lasting consequences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast’s work complements this perspective by emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and limited government action as prerequisites for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), demonstrating how shifts in constitutional design can foster market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The establishment of credible commitments, limiting arbitrary actions, is essential for encouraging investment and long-term planning.

Some scholars have challenged the view of institutions as causal agents, instead emphasizing distributional conflicts’ role in shaping institutional outcomes. Ogilvie argues that power struggles, not simply a pursuit of economic efficiency, often drove pre-industrial European institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective suggests that even seemingly inefficient institutions can persist because they serve the interests of politically powerful groups, highlighting the complexities of institutional change and reform. Hopkins further complicates this picture by demonstrating how historical processes continue to shape contemporary Africa, cautioning against simplistic policy prescriptions based on idealized institutional models (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.17). Institutional persistence depends on more than just economic efficiency; deeply entrenched social and political dynamics also play a significant role.

Further complicating the causal narrative are arguments that focus on antecedent conditions that trigger the emergence of specific institutional forms; van Zanden’s work suggests a link between labor market characteristics, skill premiums, and long-run growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 2009, p.27). A low skill premium, indicative of efficient labor markets and investment in human capital, can contribute to sustained economic expansion. This perspective underscores the interconnectedness of institutional development with broader macroeconomic trends, highlighting how factor proportions influence incentives for innovation and investment; it also suggests that institutions are not formed in a vacuum but are shaped by underlying economic conditions. The consideration of these preconditions provides greater nuance when assessing the impact of institutions on long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that historical colonial legacies continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes through their enduring impact on institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The divergent paths of former colonies are often traced back to the nature of European involvement, where extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonizers have perpetuated cycles of underdevelopment even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illuminate this dynamic by arguing that variations in initial factor endowments within colonial societies fostered vastly different institutional trajectories, setting the stage for long-run economic divergence. The persistence of these institutions reflects deliberate choices made by powerful elites seeking to maintain their advantages, not merely historical accident (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43).

The imposition of systems restricting competition and perpetuating inequality created enduring barriers to economic advancement, demonstrating how initial conditions become embedded in persistent institutional structures.

The long shadow of colonial history continues to inform contemporary economic realities, revealing the enduring power of institutions in shaping trajectories of growth.

The transatlantic slave trade’s profound impact on Africa's economic development challenges narratives centered solely on colonial institutions (Nunn 2008, p.3). The slave trades spurred domestic conflict and disrupted social structures, ultimately hindering long-run growth (Nunn 2008, p.6; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further illuminate this dynamic by demonstrating that the slave trade fostered a climate of mistrust within African societies, impeding cooperation and reducing economic productivity. Simson (2019, p.9) adds a crucial layer of nuance, revealing that postcolonial budgetary decisions and industrial strategies were also shaped by global economic forces and shifting international priorities, complicating simplistic attributions of underdevelopment solely to historical shocks. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.4)’s work on India provides a parallel example, demonstrating how pre-colonial factors contributed significantly to relative decline, suggesting that similar complexities likely influenced African trajectories as well. The enduring consequences of these historical processes underscore the need for a more comprehensive understanding of institutional development, one that incorporates both colonial and pre-colonial legacies (Nunn 2008, p.3; Simson 2019, p.11). Examining budgetary shifts in East Africa reveals how responses to global economic pressures further shaped developmental outcomes, demonstrating an interconnectedness across time and place.

This perspective, echoed by North (1989, p.1), challenges neoclassical explanations prioritizing population and savings as primary drivers of development, instead emphasizing the crucial role of political institutions in facilitating market exchange (North 1989, p.6). The establishment of credible commitments, assurances that rules will be consistently enforced, reduces transaction costs and encourages investment, ultimately propelling economic expansion (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Hornbeck (2010, p.2) further illuminates this dynamic by demonstrating how the introduction of barbed wire in American agriculture facilitated property rights protection, mitigating livestock-induced damage and fostering more efficient land use. This historical example underscores the tangible benefits of clearly defined and enforceable property rights for agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). While these scholars highlight the positive impact of secure property rights, it is important to note that the effectiveness of such institutions depends on broader political and social conditions; a rigid adherence to rules without flexibility or responsiveness can stifle innovation and hinder adaptation to changing circumstances. The historical record reveals that even well-designed institutional frameworks are not impervious to unintended consequences or evolving societal needs, necessitating ongoing evaluation and refinement (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Understanding the interplay between institutional design, enforcement mechanisms, and broader social context remains critical for fostering sustainable economic development.

This perspective, supported by van Zanden’s findings (van Zanden 1999, p.13) regarding wages and economic development in early modern Europe, challenges narratives emphasizing purely rational or technologically driven institutional evolution. Peters (2004, p.30) further complicates this picture by revealing how processes of exclusion and class formation were obscured within seemingly flexible land negotiation practices, suggesting that formal institutions often served to solidify existing power structures rather than promote equitable access. The emphasis on beliefs shaping institutional rules, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), underscores the importance of understanding the cultural context in which these frameworks operated. Pamuk (2007, p.4) connects this perspective to broader economic shifts, arguing that the Black Death triggered a series of institutional changes that contributed to northwestern Europe’s long-term economic development. While Pamuk emphasizes exogenous shocks as drivers of change, Peters argues that state-led innovation frequently had unintended destructive consequences (Peters 2004, p.35), demonstrating how even well-intentioned interventions can reinforce inequalities. These varied perspectives collectively illustrate the complexity of institutional formation in pre-industrial Europe and caution against simplistic explanations focusing solely on economic rationality. The persistence of these deeply embedded, culturally influenced institutions underscores the challenges involved in achieving equitable and sustainable development.

The establishment of guilds, while intended to regulate apprenticeship contracts, inadvertently contributed to a low skill premium by restricting entry and potentially forming local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.27), ultimately impacting the relative price of capital goods and contributing to economic performance. Broadberry and Gupta’s findings reinforce this perspective, demonstrating that wage patterns in northwestern Europe before the early nineteenth century reflected limited gains for laborers despite silver wages, suggesting a constrained path toward broader prosperity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Kuznets emphasized that sustained growth requires not only technological innovation but also accompanying institutional and ideological shifts to effectively utilize new technologies (Kuznets 1973, p.7), implying that even advancements in production are insufficient without supportive frameworks. Akyeampong and Fofack’s research adds a crucial dimension by revealing the vital economic contributions of women, which were historically overlooked, challenging narratives that fail to account for their impact on regional development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Broadberry and Gardner further complicate this picture by demonstrating that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced relatively robust growth prior to 1950, ultimately limited by recurring episodes of “shrinking” (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17), suggesting institutional fragility remains a key constraint even in regions with initial momentum. The interplay between technological progress, labor market structures, and the inclusion of marginalized groups like women reveals a complex web of factors shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Conventional wisdom regarding the automatic compatibility of economic growth and environmental progress is questioned by models that fail to account for complex interactions (Stern 2004, p.3). Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) further complicate this perspective by demonstrating how macroeconomic forces have historically shaped inequality, suggesting a more nuanced understanding is needed to disentangle the complex relationship between economic development and environmental outcomes. The initial perception that increased wealth inevitably leads to improved environmental quality, as highlighted in Stern’s discussion of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (Stern 2004, p.1), has been increasingly scrutinized given historical instances where rapid accumulation favored skilled workers and widened income disparities (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24). While proponents of the EKC suggest structural changes and heightened awareness at higher development levels drive environmental improvements, Lindert and Williamson's work reveals that earlier periods were characterized by a complex interplay of macroeconomic factors impacting factor rewards. This challenges simplistic assumptions about automatic environmental benefits accompanying economic expansion, and calls for examining specific policies and institutional arrangements that can foster both growth and sustainability. Stern’s critique (Stern 2004, p.3) underscores the need to move beyond generalized models and consider region-specific contexts where historical legacies and structural inequalities shape environmental degradation. Ultimately, a more refined understanding of these dynamics requires acknowledging the intricate links between economic structures, distributional outcomes, and ecological consequences.

The enduring influence of institutions on long-run economic growth is a central theme across diverse scholarly perspectives (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) convincingly demonstrate that institutional quality, not geographic factors, explains persistent income disparities across nations, highlighting the power of political and legal frameworks to shape economic trajectories. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize this point by showing how secure property rights, fostered by limited government action, are critical for market development and sustained growth, underscoring the role of constitutional design in facilitating long-term prosperity. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) complicates this narrative by arguing that distributional conflicts, not simply efficiency considerations, have historically shaped institutional structures, revealing a more complex interplay between power dynamics and economic outcomes. Ultimately, understanding the historical trajectory of growth requires acknowledging both the potential for institutions to foster development and the constraints imposed by entrenched interests and societal beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

While scholars largely agree on the importance of institutions, disagreement arises regarding the precise mechanisms through which they exert their influence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) suggest that "equilibrium institutions" can be extractive even when they do not promote development, as powerful groups may actively block reforms to protect their rents, demonstrating a tension between societal welfare and elite self-interest. Hopkins (2009, p.17) similarly argues that colonial legacies and ethnic divisions continue to shape contemporary African economies, challenging simplistic narratives of institutional transplantation and "best practice" models. Yet, van Zanden (2009, p.27) posits that a low skill premium, indicative of efficient labor markets and investment in human capital, is a key driver of long-term economic growth, suggesting a more direct link between institutional quality and productivity gains. These divergent perspectives underscore the need for nuanced analyses that account for both structural constraints and endogenous processes.

The historical record provides compelling evidence to resolve these differing interpretations (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Van Zanden’s focus on western Europe's comparatively low skill premium, linked to robust apprenticeship systems facilitated by institutions like guilds (van Zanden 2009, p.19), offers a concrete example of how institutional arrangements can foster human capital accumulation and drive economic performance. This contrasts with the experiences of colonies examined by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23), where extractive institutions hindered investment and perpetuated inequality, demonstrating that institutional quality is not merely a theoretical construct but a tangible determinant of long-run growth patterns. Thus, while acknowledging the complexities highlighted by Ogilvie and Hopkins, the evidence suggests that securing property rights and fostering inclusive institutions remain fundamental prerequisites for sustained economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
