The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central question in economics, carrying significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and designing policies to foster development. A longstanding debate concerns the extent to which institutional factors, the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic activity, are fundamental drivers of sustained prosperity or merely consequences of underlying conditions like geography or culture. Some scholars emphasize exogenous forces shaping societies, while others argue that institutions are actively constructed and evolve in response to incentives and power dynamics, ultimately determining a nation’s long-term economic fate (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This divergence in perspectives highlights the complexity of disentangling causation from correlation when examining historical development patterns and necessitates careful consideration of how institutions emerge, persist, and transform over time. The persistence of extractive institutions despite potential benefits underscores the political dimensions of institutional change and the challenges of achieving broad-based economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Early contributions to this field underscored the importance of secure property rights as a cornerstone of economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast, for instance, demonstrated how credible commitments from governments to uphold these rights can stimulate investment and market development, evidenced by shifts in capital markets following constitutional reforms (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective emphasizes that institutions are not simply about the rules themselves but also about the mechanisms ensuring their consistent enforcement, a critical aspect often overlooked in simpler models (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie’s work complicates this view by suggesting distributional conflicts and power struggles frequently shaped institutional development, moving beyond a focus solely on efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12); this reveals that institutions can serve the interests of powerful groups even at the expense of broader economic advancement (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.27). The interplay between formal rules and underlying beliefs further demonstrates the intricate nature of institutional change, illustrating how shared values can either reinforce or impede economic progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Acemoglu and colleagues offer a compelling argument that historical legacies of colonialism profoundly shaped long-run development outcomes through their impact on institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). They contend that colonial powers often established extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for themselves, thereby hindering long-term economic growth in colonized regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the role of political power and elite interests in shaping institutional development, suggesting that even seemingly beneficial technologies can be blocked if they threaten existing power structures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). Van Zanden’s work provides a nuanced historical context for these claims by examining long-run trends in wages and skill premiums, linking them to institutions facilitating human capital formation and labor markets (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 2009, p.27). These findings underscore the importance of considering institutional factors alongside broader economic forces when analyzing historical development pathways.

Further complicating the picture is the recognition that women’s contributions have often been systematically excluded from narratives of economic growth, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial contexts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.27). Akyeampong and Fofack's research demonstrates the vital role played by women in African economies, challenging conventional accounts that overlook their agency and impact (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This perspective reveals how institutional biases can perpetuate inequalities and limit economic potential, reinforcing the need for a more inclusive understanding of historical development processes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.27). Van Zanden’s analysis of European living standards reinforces this point by demonstrating periods where economic development did not necessarily translate into improved welfare for all segments of society (van Zanden 1999, p.13), suggesting that equitable distribution and broad participation are essential components of sustained, inclusive growth.

Secure property rights and limited government action are fundamental for fostering long-run economic prosperity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is further elaborated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who argue that initial factor endowments and the resulting institutional structures in colonial societies significantly shaped divergent development paths across the New World. The persistence of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), created lasting inequalities that hindered broader economic participation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) demonstrate how controlling for institutional quality diminishes the significance of geographic factors in explaining income disparities, suggesting a causal link between institutions and economic outcomes. Further complicating this picture, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23) acknowledge that estimates of institutional impact may be biased downwards due to endogeneity concerns related to ethnolinguistic fragmentation; therefore, the true extent of institutional influence warrants further investigation. Ultimately, understanding these historical legacies requires recognizing the complex interplay between power dynamics, initial conditions, and the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic realities.

The reverberations of historical events and evolving social structures demonstrate their capacity to mold institutions that either promote or obstruct sustained economic development (Nunn 2008, p.3; Pamuk 2007, p.4; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9; Simson 2019, p.9; Hornbeck 2010, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate how the transatlantic slave trade fostered deep-seated mistrust within African societies, a phenomenon that subsequently impeded economic progress. This erosion of trust, as further explored by Pamuk (2007, p.24), created an environment where collective action and cooperative ventures were difficult to establish, thereby hindering institutional development necessary for long-run growth. The legacy of the slave trade extended beyond immediate demographic losses, contributing to a persistent lack of social cohesion that constrained economic opportunities across generations (Nunn 2008, p.6). Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4) highlight how such historical disruptions can be misattributed to solely colonial influences when examining broader patterns of economic decline, underscoring the importance of considering pre-colonial factors in shaping long-term trajectories. Ultimately, understanding the complex interplay of these factors reveals how initial conditions, compounded by subsequent events, can shape enduring patterns of development (Pamuk 2007, p.4).

Peters (2004, p.30) further complicates this understanding by arguing that reported land negotiations frequently masked processes of exclusion and class formation, challenging accounts emphasizing flexibility and equitable access. These analyses collectively suggest that institutions rarely emerge as neutral arbiters of efficiency but are instead products of deliberate choices reflecting existing power structures and the exclusion of certain groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Peters 2004, p.35). The prioritization of specific interests, such as those of dominant ethnic or social groups, often resulted in institutions that reinforced inequalities rather than fostered broad-based development. Ultimately, these findings demonstrate how pre-colonial societies’ power structures profoundly influenced early economic trajectories, establishing patterns that would resonate through subsequent historical periods. The legacies of such exclusionary practices continue to shape contemporary development challenges and necessitate a more nuanced understanding of institutional origins.

Sustained economic growth requires not only technological advancements but also corresponding adaptations in institutions and ideologies to effectively integrate these innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.7). This perspective initially manifested in observations regarding the incompatibility of modern technologies like steam power with existing social structures, such as slavery or illiteracy, which necessitated broader societal transformations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) offered a related lens through which to examine this dynamic, suggesting that initial economic growth might correlate with increased environmental degradation before eventually leading to improvements due to structural changes and heightened awareness (Stern 2004, p.1; Stern 2004, p.3). Stern’s critique of the EKC highlights its limitations, arguing it oversimplifies complex relationships and may not reliably predict future trends, particularly given the scale effect that initially links economic expansion with environmental damage (Stern 2004, p.3). While structural shifts toward service-based economies might theoretically mitigate environmental impact, this outcome is contingent on robust regulation and technological advancements, institutional preconditions essential for long-run sustainability. Reducing environmental degradation requires proactive policy interventions to ensure sustained progress; passively relying on market forces alone is insufficient (Stern 2004, p.3). Ultimately, the EKC's initial promise underscores the critical role institutions play in shaping the trajectory of economic and environmental outcomes, demonstrating that technological advancement without institutional adaptation can produce unintended consequences. The need for ongoing evaluation and adjustment of these frameworks remains paramount to achieving truly sustainable development.

Conventional perspectives on economic growth are questioned by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8), who argue that income equality and sustained expansion can be mutually reinforcing goals. The authors’ analysis of the American experience before the Civil War reveals that periods of rapid capital accumulation disproportionately benefited skilled workers and those already commanding higher wages (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24), demonstrating how concentrated investment can exacerbate existing inequalities. This finding resonates with observations from other historical contexts, where uneven distribution of resources has accompanied economic expansion, complicating simplistic narratives of progress (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). While Lindert and Williamson emphasize the potential for growth to occur alongside rising inequality, subsequent research indicates that extreme disparities in income can ultimately impede long-run development by limiting human capital formation and social mobility (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Such constraints on inclusive growth underscore the importance of institutional frameworks designed to mitigate these adverse effects, ensuring that the benefits of economic expansion are more broadly shared. Ultimately, understanding the intricate relationship between income distribution and economic progress requires nuanced analysis beyond simple trade-off assessments; it demands consideration of how institutions shape the pathways through which prosperity is generated and distributed.

Recent research by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17; 2022, p.10) reveals a surprising degree of resilience in Sub-Saharan Africa’s long-run economic performance before 1950, despite subsequent contractions. Van Zanden’s work (van Zanden 1999, p.2; van Zanden 2009, p.27), initially challenging optimistic assessments of European living standards, reveals a parallel dynamic where periods of growth were interspersed with troughs marked by low real wages and limited prosperity. This pattern suggests that sustained economic advancement is not solely dependent on consistent expansion but also on the mitigation of disruptive events that impede progress. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further illuminate this complexity by noting the link between urbanization and industrial development, suggesting that localized economies of scale were hindered in regions experiencing persistent instability. Ultimately, analyzing episodes of shrinking alongside periods of growth provides a more nuanced picture of long-run economic development and its susceptibility to cyclical downturns.

The foundational role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across historical and contemporary scholarship (North 1989, p.1). North argues that prevailing neoclassical theories prioritizing population and savings overlook the critical importance of political and economic institutions in fostering development (North 1989, p.6), a perspective echoed by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how institutional structures significantly influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). These scholars highlight that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, thereby enabling specialization and division of labor to drive economic expansion. Indeed, the development of institutions capable of limiting arbitrary government action has demonstrably spurred capital markets and overall economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Despite a broad consensus on the importance of institutions, disagreements arise regarding their precise nature and how they evolve over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). While Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than purely economic efficiency considerations, often shape institutional development in pre-industrial societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This divergence suggests differing perspectives on the motivations behind institutional design and the extent to which institutions are intentionally crafted to perpetuate existing power structures. Ultimately, understanding these competing viewpoints requires a nuanced analysis of historical contexts and the interplay between political dynamics and economic incentives.

The persistence of colonial legacies offers compelling empirical evidence for resolving this disagreement (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Acemoglu et al.’s work demonstrates that European mortality rates, which shaped colonial institutions, continue to influence income differences today, even when controlling for geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4), suggesting a powerful and enduring impact of institutional structures. Conversely, North and Weingast’s analysis highlights how constitutional design limiting arbitrary government action spurred the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The comparative performance of regions with differing colonial histories, particularly those with extractive versus inclusive institutions, provides a clear historical test for these competing claims, underscoring that institutions are not merely reflections of economic forces but also instruments of power and sources of enduring inequality.
