The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central question in economics, as understanding the factors that drive sustained prosperity is critical for informing policy and explaining historical divergence (North 1989, p.1). A fundamental debate concerns whether institutional development primarily serves to facilitate already existing productive forces or actively generates them, with implications for how we conceptualize causality and design interventions aimed at fostering economic advancement. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and limited government as preconditions for investment and innovation (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6), while others highlight how institutions can be shaped by powerful groups to extract rents and stifle growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These divergent perspectives shape our understanding of why some societies have achieved sustained economic success, while others remain trapped in cycles of poverty. The persistence of colonial legacies also complicates the picture, as initial institutional structures established during periods of European expansion continue to influence contemporary development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This review explores these debates through several streams of scholarship, examining how institutions shape economic outcomes and considering the historical processes that have shaped institutional development. The ongoing challenge lies in disentangling correlation from causation and understanding the complex interplay between formal rules, informal norms, and individual behavior.

Establishing clear property rights and enforceable contracts proved essential for fostering prosperity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further highlighted that sustained advancement requires technological innovation alongside institutional and ideological adjustments to realize its potential. The historical diffusion of barbed wire across the American West exemplifies this dynamic, as Hornbeck (2010, p.2) argues, enabling property rights protection and mitigating conflicts arising from livestock damage, ultimately facilitating agricultural development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) complicate this picture by emphasizing that divergent institutional trajectories in colonial societies shaped vastly different developmental outcomes, suggesting that the mere presence of secure property rights is insufficient without broader structural conditions fostering equitable participation. Indeed, the initial distribution of resources and power significantly influences the ability to establish and maintain institutions conducive to long-run growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These findings underscore a crucial point: institutions are not simply passive reflections of economic activity but active forces shaping trajectories over time.

Persistent economic disparities frequently arise from colonial legacies, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). Their work, along with that of Sokoloff and Engerman (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4), suggests that initial conditions established during the colonial era, particularly regarding institutional design, have enduring consequences for long-run growth patterns; this challenges explanations based solely on geography or resource endowments. Austin (2008, p.2) offers a critique, arguing that Acemoglu et al.’s framework sometimes oversimplifies historical processes by compressing lengthy periods and diverse developmental paths into a singular "reversal of fortune" narrative. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4) further complicate this narrative by demonstrating that India's relative decline began before British rule, attributing it to factors like labor productivity and pre-existing institutional weaknesses, which diminishes the scope for solely blaming colonialism. These findings underscore that understanding long-run economic growth requires a careful consideration of historical context and avoids simplistic attributions of causation (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of these colonial legacies compels us to examine how specific political and economic choices made centuries ago continue to reverberate in contemporary societies.

The transfer of skills and facilitated economic development within western Europe was notably influenced by institutions governing apprenticeships, according to van Zanden (2009, p.19). These formalized structures, such as guilds, ensured contract execution and promoted human capital accumulation, contributing to long-term growth, a point reinforced by Lindert and Williamson’s (1985, p.8) observation that understanding macroeconomic forces is essential for analyzing pre-industrial inequality. Van Zanden (1999, p.13) complicates this narrative by noting instances where low real wages in early modern Europe coincided with economic development; this suggests a more complex relationship between prosperity and labor compensation than previously understood. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further nuance the picture by demonstrating that urbanization, often considered a hallmark of industrialization, did not always correlate with broader economic advancement across different regions. While guilds may have fostered skill development, their monopolistic tendencies could also impede competition and limit overall growth potential (van Zanden 2009, p.19), illustrating how institutional structures can generate unintended consequences. The persistence of localized advantages, as noted by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.10), indicates that broader economic divergence involved regional specificities beyond overarching institutional frameworks. Ultimately, examining the intricacies of these historical processes reveals a need for nuanced interpretations when assessing long-run economic trajectories.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) challenge conventional narratives regarding Sub-Saharan Africa's economic trajectory, demonstrating a more robust performance prior to 1950 that was ultimately constrained by recurring episodes of contraction. This perspective underscores the need to move beyond simplistic models emphasizing "best practice" institutions and instead grapple with the complexities of historical legacies and local agency (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Peters 2004, p.35; Simson 2019, p.9; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17; Hopkins 2019, p.13). Simson’s (2019, p.9) work further qualifies this picture by revealing that postcolonial budget policies in East Africa, often perceived as idiosyncratic clientelist practices, were largely responses to broader global economic forces and shifting international priorities. Hopkins (2019, p.3) emphasizes the evolution of scholarship on African economic history itself, noting how initial justifications for quantitative approaches are no longer necessary given the substantial progress made over several decades. The persistence of ethnic considerations in development challenges, as noted by Hopkins (2009, p.17), suggests a need to move beyond purely institutional explanations and consider the interplay of social factors within broader historical contexts.

Historical slave trades have significantly hampered Africa’s long-term economic development, as Nunn (2008, p.3) contends. The impact of these trade networks extended beyond immediate losses of labor, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate through their examination of how the slave trade fostered mistrust within African societies, fundamentally altering social norms and beliefs. This erosion of trust, a crucial element for economic cooperation and institutional development, likely accounts for at least half of the lasting negative effects stemming from these historical shocks (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.16). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further complicate purely exogenous explanations by highlighting the vital contributions of women to pre-colonial and colonial African economies, suggesting that internal social structures also shaped development trajectories. Pamuk’s (2007, p.4) work on the Black Death provides a comparative historical context, demonstrating how large-scale shocks can trigger long-term economic transformations and institutional changes, albeit in different geographical settings. The persistence of these legacies complicates straightforward narratives of progress and underscores the need to consider a broader range of factors when analyzing African development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.27). Understanding contemporary challenges requires acknowledging the complex interplay of historical forces, including slavery, trade, and evolving social dynamics, which have shaped Africa’s economic landscape.

The enduring influence of institutions on long-run economic growth is a central theme in development economics, as underscored by the shared finding that institutional frameworks shape trajectories of prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight how equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than fostering broad-based economic development, while North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the critical role of secure property rights in driving market growth. This perspective extends beyond simple explanations, as Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the “reversal of fortune” thesis oversimplifies historical processes by compressing timelines and overlooking nuanced local adaptations. The interplay between colonial legacies, indigenous practices, and evolving power dynamics reveals a more complex picture than simplistic narratives suggest; therefore, understanding contemporary economic disparities requires careful consideration of these interwoven factors.

Despite broad agreement on the importance of institutions, disagreement persists regarding the precise mechanisms through which they exert their influence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43; Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.43) suggest that elites may actively block investments in new technologies to protect their political power and rents, while Austin (2008, p.15) contends that colonial regimes sometimes supported existing indigenous property rights systems for pragmatic reasons. Resolving this tension requires examining specific historical contexts and the relative bargaining power of different actors; ultimately, institutional change is rarely a uniform or predictable process. The divergence in approaches highlights the complexities inherent in disentangling cause and effect within historical narratives.

The persistence of colonial legacies and their impact on contemporary institutions are demonstrably evident through comparative analysis of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) find that controlling for institutional quality diminishes the significance of geographic factors in explaining income differences, suggesting that Africa’s relative poverty is largely attributable to weaker institutions rather than inherent geographical disadvantages; Hopkins (2009, p.8) further emphasizes how long-term historical processes continue to shape contemporary African development. This perspective underscores the importance of considering path dependency and the lasting effects of past decisions on present outcomes, highlighting the need for nuanced policy interventions that account for specific historical contexts.
