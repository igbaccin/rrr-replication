The enduring puzzle of long-run economic growth centers on understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained prosperity, and the role of institutions in this transformation remains intensely debated. Intellectual stakes are high; explanations that attribute growth solely to geography or technological innovation risk overlooking crucial political and social factors shaping a society’s trajectory. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions primarily function as neutral frameworks for market activity or actively shape economic outcomes by influencing incentives, power dynamics, and the distribution of resources. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and limited government intervention (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), while others highlight how powerful groups can manipulate institutions to extract rents and stifle development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This tension underscores the complexity of institutional analysis and necessitates a deeper examination of their causal role in fostering long-run economic growth. The interplay between beliefs, values, and formal rules requires careful consideration when assessing the origins and impact of any given institution (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are simply passive conduits for market forces; instead, they reflect and reinforce existing power structures, even when those structures are detrimental to broader societal prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). Austin (2008, p.2) critiques this framework, arguing it oversimplifies historical processes by compressing complex narratives into a single explanatory model. He suggests that colonial regimes sometimes supported indigenous property rights systems for pragmatic reasons, demonstrating that institutional choices are often context-dependent and influenced by diverse political considerations (Austin 2008, p.15). Analyzing institutions demands consideration of specific historical pathways and power dynamics; assuming universal patterns or predetermined outcomes can be misleading (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Understanding these nuances is essential when evaluating the causal impact of institutions on economic trajectories.

Their argument that credible commitment, a regime's ability to bind itself to rules over time, is crucial for long-run growth highlights the importance of political stability and predictability (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) advises against viewing institutions solely through an economic efficiency framework, instead highlighting the role of distributional conflicts and shared beliefs in shaping institutional structures. The emergence of guilds in Europe, as analyzed by van Zanden (2009, p.19), illustrates how institutions can simultaneously facilitate skill transmission while also restricting competition, demonstrating the complex and multifaceted nature of institutional development. The evolution of institutions is inextricably linked to shifts in power dynamics, beliefs, and societal values.

Van Zanden’s research (van Zanden 1999, p.2) challenges simplistic narratives linking economic growth with rising living standards, particularly during the early modern period (van Zanden 1999, p.13). His findings suggest that periods of economic expansion were not always accompanied by improvements in material well-being for all segments of society, raising questions about the distributional consequences of institutional change. This perspective complements Acemoglu et al.’s (2002, p.33) focus on rent-seeking behavior, as it highlights how institutions can perpetuate inequalities even during periods of overall economic growth. Furthermore, van Zanden’s work (van Zanden 2009, p.27) connects a low skill premium to long-term economic prosperity and the development of human capital, suggesting that institutional factors promoting investment in skills can be crucial for sustained growth. The relationship between institutional frameworks and broader societal well-being is more intricate than simple correlations might suggest.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4), Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), and North (1989, p.6) similarly emphasize how institutional frameworks, particularly those limiting arbitrary government action, shape long-run economic trajectories and divergence across nations. These scholars demonstrate that institutions actively shape conditions for growth; they often establish clear rules governing resource access and enforcement. Hornbeck (2010, p.2) complicates this view by showing how seemingly simple technological innovations, like barbed wire, can catalyze institutional change and agricultural development when addressing property rights insecurity. This suggests that institutional evolution is not always a top-down process dictated by elite power dynamics; grassroots adaptations to economic challenges also drive it. Ultimately, the interplay between political power, technological innovation, and evolving social norms shapes the institutional landscape and determines whether societies are positioned for sustained economic progress. The impact of these factors underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve over time, rather than treating them as static constraints.

The long-term consequences of historical events, such as those experienced by African societies through slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Hopkins 2019, p.13), demonstrate the enduring power of legacies. Nunn’s work demonstrates a direct link between regions heavily involved in the transatlantic slave trade and persistently lower levels of economic development, challenging explanations solely reliant on colonial legacies (Nunn 2008, p.3). This negative impact wasn't limited to those physically removed from their communities; the internal dynamics of African societies were fundamentally reshaped by the constant threat of raiding and enslavement (Nunn 2008, p.6). Hopkins reminds us that reconstructing a comprehensive picture of precolonial African production remains an ongoing challenge for scholars, emphasizing the need for diverse methodological approaches to fully grasp the complexities of these historical processes (Hopkins 2019, p.3). The persistent effects of these historical disruptions underscore how deeply embedded legacies can shape contemporary economic trajectories.

Reassessing conventional explanations requires considering that India’s relative economic decline began earlier than previously thought, as Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9) reveal through an examination of pre-colonial factors (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4). Estimating population growth and domestic demand for goods and services allows researchers to indirectly assess GDP per capita (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4), thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of India's economic performance over time. The authors’ approach, building on earlier work by Wrigley (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9), emphasizes the importance of examining service sector output alongside agricultural production to gain a comprehensive picture of pre-colonial development. Textile exports, for example, offer another avenue for scrutinizing economic dynamics during this period (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9). This perspective complicates explanations that solely attribute India’s later struggles to colonial exploitation, pointing instead toward a complex interplay of internal and external forces. Such analyses underscore the need for careful consideration of pre-existing conditions and institutional structures that influenced India’s economic trajectory before colonial intervention. Ultimately, a more complete understanding of long-run economic divergence requires moving beyond simplistic narratives to incorporate the complexities of pre-colonial history.

This perspective challenges conventional narratives emphasizing rational optimization and highlights how entrenched beliefs and values can perpetuate inefficient practices (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, Peters (2004, p.30) demonstrates how processes of exclusion and class formation often obscured ostensibly flexible land negotiation systems, revealing a pattern where seemingly adaptive institutional arrangements served to solidify existing power structures. The persistence of these frameworks, even in the face of potentially more productive alternatives, suggests that institutional interlinkages actively resisted innovation and maintained suboptimal outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Pamuk (2007, p.4) further illuminates this dynamic by demonstrating how the Black Death spurred long-term economic changes which subsequently triggered a series of institutional shifts with far-reaching consequences. While these shifts initially created opportunities, their subsequent entrenchment ultimately contributed to persistent inequalities and hindered broader progress (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Such observations underscore that institutions are not simply neutral mechanisms for facilitating growth but can actively impede it when deeply intertwined with pre-existing social hierarchies and belief systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Peters 2004, p.35; Pamuk 2007, p.4). The legacy of these historically embedded institutional arrangements continues to influence economic trajectories, even as broader societal changes unfold.

Austin (2008, p.2) complicates the understanding of long-run development by questioning simplistic causal links and challenging established narratives like Acemoglu et al.’s “reversal of fortune” thesis. This critique aligns with broader efforts to move beyond linear models of progress, as van Zanden (1999, p.2) demonstrated when contesting optimistic interpretations of European living standards between 1500 and 1800, revealing a more nuanced picture than previously understood. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further complicate the narrative by arguing that the “Great Divergence” began earlier than some world historians suggest, indicating that initial advantages were not always sustained or uniformly distributed across regions. Such observations underscore the limitations of focusing solely on secure private property rights as a universal driver of growth, particularly when ignoring the context-specific factors that shaped their adoption and effectiveness. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) subsequently qualify this perspective by revealing Sub-Saharan Africa’s surprisingly robust economic performance prior to 1950, suggesting that recurring episodes of "shrinking" have obscured a more complex trajectory than previously acknowledged (Austin 2008, p.2; van Zanden 1999, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.10; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). The intricate interplay between initial conditions, policy choices, and unforeseen events necessitates a continuous re-evaluation of established frameworks for understanding long-run economic change.

This perspective aligns with Lindert and Williamson's (1985, p.8) observation that policymakers have historically faced a false choice between economic expansion and equitable distribution of wealth, suggesting that early institutional development played a crucial role in shaping divergent growth trajectories. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further emphasized the necessity of adapting institutions alongside technological advancements for sustained economic progress, highlighting how pre-modern practices like slavery were incompatible with modern industrialization. Stern (2004, p.1) complicates this narrative by questioning the validity of the environmental Kuznets curve, a commonly invoked framework linking economic development and environmental quality, demonstrating that such relationships are often statistically tenuous. Simson (2019, p.9) provides additional context, showing how postcolonial budget policies in East Africa were shaped by responses to global economic forces and the prioritization of industrial development, not solely by clientelist practices. This interplay between institutional frameworks, resource allocation, and external pressures underscores the complexity inherent in understanding long-run economic change (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Stern 2004, p.3; Kuznets 1973, p.7; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Simson 2019, p.9). The emphasis on structural shifts and budgetary priorities reveals a dynamic process of adaptation and response, which necessitates continued scrutiny of established narratives.

The foundational role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely recognized as central to understanding historical development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Secure property rights and limitations on arbitrary government action, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), demonstrably influenced the performance of capital markets and spurred economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Acemoglu et al. expand on this point, emphasizing that institutions reflect power dynamics and distributional conflicts as well as serving as mechanisms for enforcing contracts (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Indeed, even seemingly beneficial institutional frameworks can perpetuate extractive practices designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), hindering broader economic progress. This perspective underscores the complexity of disentangling cause and effect when assessing institutional impacts on long-run growth, as institutions themselves are shaped by historical contingencies and distributional struggles (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Despite a shared emphasis on institutional influence, disagreements persist regarding the precise mechanisms through which these effects manifest (Austin 2008, p.2). While Acemoglu et al. propose that colonial legacies and mortality rates significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4), Austin argues this framework oversimplifies historical processes by compressing timelines and neglecting nuanced local contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). He suggests a more granular analysis is needed to account for the diverse ways societies adapt existing institutions or develop new ones in response to evolving circumstances (Austin 2008, p.15). This debate highlights the challenge of isolating institutional effects from other contributing factors, such as technological innovation and global economic forces. The historical record reveals that even seemingly unfavorable institutional arrangements can be adapted and utilized effectively under specific conditions (van Zanden 2009, p.27).

Ultimately, empirical comparisons across regions with differing colonial experiences offer a means of settling these disagreements (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Acemoglu et al.’s analysis demonstrates that excluding former European colonies does not alter the significance of institutions on economic performance, suggesting their enduring impact (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4), while Austin’s critique reminds us to consider context-specific adaptations and local agency. The divergent trajectories of India and Ghana, for instance, illustrate how colonial regimes responded differently to indigenous property rights systems, resulting in varied long-term outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15), reinforcing the importance of both institutional frameworks and their implementation within specific historical contexts.
