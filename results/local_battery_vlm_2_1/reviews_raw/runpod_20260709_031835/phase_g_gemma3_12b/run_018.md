The enduring puzzle of long-run economic growth centers on understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained prosperity, and the role institutions play in this transformation remains intensely debated (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Intellectual stakes are high because explanations for persistent global inequalities often hinge on differing interpretations of historical institutional development; attributing disparities to geography, culture, or policy choices depends crucially on how we understand the evolution of rules, enforcement mechanisms, and property rights. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions primarily serve as reflections of underlying economic forces or as active shapers of those very forces (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Some argue that powerful groups manipulate institutions to extract rents, hindering broader development, while others emphasize their capacity to foster innovation and secure investment through predictable governance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). This review examines scholarship illuminating the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth, focusing on how different theoretical perspectives shed light on this dynamic process. The historical record reveals a nuanced picture where institutional changes frequently occur not as straightforward drivers of progress but rather as responses to shifting power dynamics and technological opportunities (van Zanden 1999, p.2).

Persistent inequalities across nations highlight how colonial legacies continue to shape economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that colonial institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonizers, have created persistent disadvantages in formerly colonized nations. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) elaborate on this perspective by demonstrating how variations in colonial policies regarding land distribution and labor systems generated vastly different development paths within the New World, establishing a foundation for enduring inequality. Hornbeck (2010, p.2) adds nuance by showing how minor technological innovations like barbed wire can alter property rights regimes and impact agricultural development, indicating a capacity for institutional change even within historically constrained contexts. While initial colonial choices may have set trajectories, subsequent developments demonstrate that institutions are not immutable and can be reshaped through unexpected means (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). The interplay between enduring historical structures and localized adaptations reveals the complexity of understanding long-run economic growth.

Recognizing the limitations of purely technological explanations, Kuznets (1973, p.7) emphasized that broader societal transformations are essential for sustained economic progress. Stern (2004, p.1) further contextualizes this perspective by tracing the emergence of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) as a framework for understanding the relationship between income levels and environmental degradation, noting its roots in Kuznets’s earlier work on inequality (Kuznets 1973, p.1). While the EKC initially suggested an eventual decline in environmental impact with increased wealth, Stern (2004, p.3) subsequently critiqued the robustness of this model, highlighting that it often overlooks structural changes necessary for genuine environmental improvement. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) contribute to this discussion by demonstrating that Sub-Saharan Africa's historical economic performance was more resilient than previously recognized, suggesting a crucial link between institutional quality and sustained growth trajectories. They also note that the emphasis on investment and innovation sometimes obscures the connection between institutions and economic contraction (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). Kuznets (1973, p.7) emphasized that technological innovation is only fully realized when integrated with a compatible institutional framework, a condition seemingly unmet in many developing regions experiencing cyclical downturns. Stern’s critique underscores how relying solely on scale effects can obscure the deeper systemic changes needed for long-run prosperity (Stern 2004, p.3). The interplay between technological progress, environmental sustainability, and robust institutions reveals that achieving lasting economic advancement demands a holistic approach beyond simply embracing new technologies.

Europe’s early economic ascendancy resulted from both innovation and institutional structures like guild systems guaranteeing fair apprentice contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions fostered skill transmission and investment in human capital, contributing to long-term economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27), a perspective that moves beyond simplistic explanations based solely on material conditions (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.10; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Lindert and Williamson’s work (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24) further complicates these narratives by suggesting that periods of economic growth did not invariably lead to increased inequality, a finding initially counterintuitive within classical economic frameworks. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this temporal scope, arguing for an earlier onset of the Great Divergence than previously thought, highlighting how uneven urbanization patterns influenced labor markets and living standards across different regions. This nuanced understanding underscores that institutional arrangements, technological advancements, and demographic shifts interact in complex ways to shape long-run economic trajectories, moving beyond simple linear models of growth (van Zanden 1999, p.13). Ultimately, the interplay of these factors reveals a more intricate picture than previously understood, revealing how historical narratives are often shaped by incomplete data or biased interpretations.

Budgetary decisions in East Africa often reflect global forces alongside local political dynamics (Simson 2019, p.9). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) highlight the crucial, often overlooked, contributions of women to pre-colonial and colonial economies across the continent, challenging Eurocentric narratives that have historically dominated discussions of African development. Peters (2004, p.35) challenges conventional understandings by revealing that seemingly flexible land negotiation processes frequently served as mechanisms for exclusion and class formation. Hopkins (2019, p.3) underscores the ongoing methodological challenges in reconstructing precolonial economic realities, even as scholarship has significantly advanced over recent decades. These analyses collectively reveal that historical narratives of African economic development have been shaped by factors operating at both global and local levels, frequently diverging from models predicated on European experiences. The prioritization of industrialization through trade taxation and state-led investment, for example, reflects a deliberate attempt to break free from dependence on primary commodities (Simson 2019, p.9), while the marginalization of women’s economic roles in historical accounts obscures a vital engine of growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Peters’ work also highlights that even well-intentioned policies can have unintended consequences, contributing to social stratification and conflict (Peters 2004, p.30). Understanding these complexities necessitates moving beyond simplistic narratives of progress and acknowledging the nuanced interplay of power, policy, and historical context in shaping long-run economic trajectories. Ultimately, a more comprehensive understanding of African economic history demands a critical reevaluation of established frameworks and a greater appreciation for the heterogeneity of experiences across the continent.

Sudden events and their lasting effects frequently dominate long-run development patterns, sometimes eclipsing the influence of contemporary institutions (Nunn 2008, p.3; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.3) demonstrates that Africa’s historical involvement in the transatlantic slave trade significantly impeded its subsequent economic development, a consequence extending beyond the immediate losses of human capital to encompass broader societal disruption. The slave trades, as Nunn (2008, p.6) details, triggered cycles of violence and instability that eroded social trust; this deterioration is further elaborated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.16), who link diminished trust to less developed jurisdictional hierarchies within African communities. While these historical shocks undeniably shaped long-run outcomes, the persistent effects of such events highlight the complex interplay between initial conditions and subsequent developmental pathways. The lasting effects of past crises emphasize the importance of understanding how societies adapt to profound disruptions (Pamuk 2007, p.24).

The enduring significance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across numerous scholarly works (North 1989, p.1). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) highlight the profound impact of institutional quality on income levels, demonstrating that differences in colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, even after controlling for geographic factors. North (1989, p.6) similarly emphasizes the interconnectedness of political and economic institutions; he argues that evolving property rights and enforcement mechanisms are key drivers of economic development. While scholars generally agree on the importance of institutional frameworks, disagreements arise concerning the precise mechanisms through which these institutions exert their influence, particularly regarding the timing and nature of their effects (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Van Zanden (1999, p.13) challenges narratives of consistent long-run growth by revealing periods of stagnant or declining real wages in early modern Europe, suggesting that economic development did not always translate into improved living standards.

The role of institutions in facilitating technological advancements and broader participation in economic activity is further explored through Acemoglu et al.’s (2002, p.43) analysis of industrialization. They argue that extractive institutions can impede innovation by limiting access to capital and restricting the involvement of non-elite groups, ultimately hindering long-term growth potential. This perspective aligns with North and Weingast's (1989, p.2) work on constitutional design, which underscores the necessity of credible commitments, specifically, limitations on arbitrary government action, to foster secure property rights and encourage investment in markets. The ability to establish and maintain these institutions is crucial for creating an environment conducive to sustained economic progress (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Understanding how societies navigate periods of profound disruption and adapt their institutional frameworks remains essential for explaining divergent developmental pathways.

Despite broad consensus on the importance of institutions, a key point of contention revolves around the extent to which initial conditions permanently shape long-run trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23) acknowledge that endogenous factors can bias estimates of institutional impact, suggesting that variables like ethnolinguistic fragmentation may be correlated with both mortality rates and development outcomes. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) provides empirical evidence from the skill premium in western Europe; this data demonstrates a link between low skill premiums and investment in human capital, a measurement type also used by Acemoglu et al. suggesting that early institutional advantages can create self-reinforcing cycles. The sustained economic divergence observed between England and France before the French Revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.29) serves as a compelling historical comparison that underscores the enduring legacy of initial institutional differences.
