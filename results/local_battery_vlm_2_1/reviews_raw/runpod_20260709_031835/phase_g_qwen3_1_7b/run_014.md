What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the intersection of historical evolution and economic theory, challenging the conventional view that institutions are static and unchanging. The literature presents a complex landscape where institutions are depicted as both enabling and constraining forces, yet their impact remains contested. Some scholars argue that institutions are deeply embedded in the fabric of economic development, while others emphasize the dynamic and contingent nature of institutional influence. The debate centers on whether institutional frameworks can consistently predict long-term growth outcomes or if they are shaped by historical, cultural, and political factors that render them unpredictable. The evidence suggests that institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories, but their effectiveness is contingent on the specific context in which they operate. The interplay between institutional quality, historical conditions, and economic outcomes remains a fertile area for further inquiry. Institutions, as both a cause and a consequence of growth, are inherently dynamic, reflecting the evolving nature of economic systems and the broader socio-political landscape. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that institutions shape economic growth by influencing structural and historical factors, revealing their fundamental role in long-term development. This is supported by Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who show that institutional frameworks, whether rooted in legal traditions, property rights, or political stability, determine the trajectory of economic progress. The interdependence of institutions, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), suggests that the evolution of economic systems is not merely a function of individual actors but is shaped by broader, often unseen, institutional dynamics. This is further corroborated by North (1989, p.9), who argues that institutional differences lead to economic consequences, challenging the neoclassical view that population and savings are the primary determinants of growth. The evidence collectively underscores that institutions are not merely byproducts of economic activity but are foundational in shaping the conditions under which economic growth occurs. Institutions, therefore, act as both a cause and a context, influencing the very mechanisms through which growth is achieved. The persistence of institutional frameworks across historical periods, as noted by Austin (2008, p.25), highlights their enduring impact on economic development, demonstrating that the trajectory of economic growth is deeply intertwined with the institutional structures that govern it.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) demonstrate that institutional adaptation and technological advancement are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, as these factors shape the economic landscape and lead to sustained growth. Their evidence shows that economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa, despite periods of shrinking, was above subsistence levels in many economies by the early 20th century, challenging the previous perception of stagnation. This aligns with Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that advancing technology is a necessary but insufficient condition for economic growth, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments to effectively utilize knowledge. The interplay between technological innovation and institutional evolution is evident in the case of Ghana, where the shift in factor ratios led to institutional changes in the factor markets, replacing slavery and debt bondage with land use rights and hired labour.
