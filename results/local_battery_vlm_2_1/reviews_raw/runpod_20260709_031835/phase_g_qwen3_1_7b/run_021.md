What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question is central to understanding how structural and institutional frameworks shape economic trajectories over time. The literature presents two competing perspectives: one that emphasizes the cumulative and durable impact of institutions, and another that highlights the contingent and dynamic nature of institutional change. While some scholars argue that institutions are foundational to economic development, others contend that they are more responsive to external pressures and historical contingencies. The debate is not merely academic, it has implications for policy design, historical interpretation, and the evaluation of institutional reforms. The evidence suggests that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes, but their influence is often mediated by broader economic and political contexts. Shared findings indicate that institutional quality is correlated with economic growth, yet the mechanisms through which institutions operate remain contested. The persistence of institutional frameworks, as noted by Austin (2008, p.2), suggests that institutions can act as durable structures that facilitate long-term growth, but they are also subject to change through processes of adaptation and transformation. This interplay between stability and change underscores the complexity of institutional influence on economic growth. Institutions, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), are not static but are shaped by historical and political forces, and their role in economic development is thus both contingent and cumulative.
