What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic history and political science, and it reflects a deep intellectual tension about the causal relationship between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are essential for fostering growth through their ability to shape economic behavior, enforce rules, and create stable environments for innovation. Others contend that institutions are not deterministic forces but rather contingent features that can vary across time and space, and thus their impact on growth is often limited by other factors such as technological change or external shocks. The literature presents a complex landscape of competing views, with some emphasizing the structural role of institutions in shaping growth and others highlighting the dynamic and context-dependent nature of institutional influence. The debate is not merely academic; it has implications for policy-making, as institutions are often seen as key instruments for promoting economic development. Shared findings suggest that institutions can both enable and constrain growth, depending on their characteristics and the historical and political contexts in which they operate. The question remains unresolved, but the evidence indicates that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories, though their influence is often mediated by other factors.
