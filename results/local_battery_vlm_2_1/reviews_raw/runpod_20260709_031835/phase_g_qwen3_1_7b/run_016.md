What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic history and political science, with profound implications for understanding development trajectories and policy design. While some scholars argue that institutions are primarily driven by economic efficiency, others emphasize their role in shaping outcomes through distributional conflicts and cultural norms. The debate is not merely academic, it touches on fundamental questions about the nature of institutional development and its impact on economic outcomes. The literature presents two competing perspectives: one that views institutions as primarily shaped by economic efficiency, and another that highlights their role in fostering growth through distributional dynamics and cultural factors. These perspectives conflict over whether institutions are best understood as mechanisms of economic efficiency or as catalysts for growth through social and cultural processes. The evidence suggests that institutions are neither purely economic nor purely cultural, but rather complex systems that interact in ways that are both economic and social. The empirical findings from studies such as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) demonstrate that institutional quality is crucial for growth, but the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth are multifaceted and context-dependent. The integration of economic efficiency and social factors in institutional development is therefore essential for a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth.
