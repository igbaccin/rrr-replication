What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question is central to understanding how structural and institutional frameworks shape economic trajectories over time. The literature presents a complex landscape of perspectives, with some arguing that institutions are key drivers of growth, while others emphasize the limitations of institutional theory in capturing the nuance of economic development. The debate centers on whether institutional frameworks can be systematically identified and analyzed to explain long-term economic outcomes, and whether they are both necessary and sufficient for growth. The stream of scholarship that addresses this question directly engages with the causal relationship between institutions and growth, while another stream, though related, approaches the topic obliquely, offering only partial engagement. The evidence suggests that institutional frameworks are critical in shaping economic outcomes, as seen in the recognition of how institutional evolution influences growth through historical and comparative analysis. However, the interdependence of institutions and the variability of their impact across different contexts remain unresolved issues. Institutional frameworks are not merely static constructs but are dynamically shaped by historical, cultural, and economic factors, making their role in determining long-run growth both complex and context-dependent. The evidence supports the idea that institutions are foundational to economic development, but their influence is mediated by a range of other factors, including technological change, political stability, and external shocks.
