What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.” Ogilvie (2007, p.27) reports, “different framework (which may even resist its encroachment), is not unlikely to fail.93 The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework has been recognized by some studies of European economic institutions.”

Institutions play a fundamental role in determining long-run economic growth by shaping structural and historical factors that influence economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further supports this by showing that institutional quality correlates with income per capita, as demonstrated through empirical analyses of European mortality rates. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that secure rights and economic growth are rare in seventeenth-century England, emphasizing the role of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. The evidence collectively suggests that institutions are not merely byproducts of economic processes but are foundational in determining long-term growth. This is evident in the way institutional frameworks influence financial markets, legal systems, and the mobilization of resources, all of which are essential for sustained economic development. Institutions shape the conditions under which economic activities take place, and their evolution over time determines the trajectory of economic growth. The interdependence of institutions, as noted by North and Weingast, underscores the complexity of economic development, where structural and historical factors are deeply intertwined with institutional frameworks.

Simson (2019, p.19) argues that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda did not differ significantly from other regions in terms of growth and composition, challenging the narrative that these states prioritized patronage over social benefits. This aligns with Austin (2007, p.2), which shows that institutional changes in Ghana, such as the shift from slavery to land use rights, were not merely responses to external pressures but were shaped by internal economic and political dynamics. The shared finding that institutional frameworks are critical to economic growth (Simson 2019, p.19; Simson 2019, p.27) underscores the importance of understanding how these frameworks evolve over time and how they influence the allocation of resources and the direction of economic activity. The trajectory of economic growth, as revealed by the institutional changes in these regions, highlights the role of historical and structural factors in determining long-term development, reinforcing the argument that institutional evolution is the fundamental cause of sustained economic growth.

Frankema and Nunn and Wantchekon argue that colonialism, through its institutional and cultural impacts, is a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, challenging the common perception that colonialism was inherently oppressive. The shared finding that colonialism facilitated the development of stable institutions and economic structures in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema_2012, p.14; Nunn&Wantchekon_2011, p.27) underscores the role of colonial governance in fostering economic progress. This stream's evidence demonstrates that colonial rule created the conditions for sustained growth by reducing logistical barriers, enhancing educational systems, and promoting economic specialization. The institutional frameworks established during colonialism laid the groundwork for subsequent economic development, as evidenced by the long-term improvements in real wages and economic performance observed in British Africa (Frankema&vanWaijenburg_2012, p.2; Broadberry&Gardner_2022, p.17). These findings highlight the importance of institutional continuity and the transformative power of colonial governance in shaping economic trajectories. The persistence of economic growth in post-colonial contexts further reinforces the argument that colonial institutions played a critical role in fostering long-term development. Bryant (2006, p.3) argues that revisionist claims, despite their popularity, are empirically and analytically flawed, suggesting that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by Bryant's analysis of the interplay between institutional structures and the modernization of European societies. The paper highlights how the development of institutions, such as legal systems, governance models, and cultural norms, created the conditions for economic progress, even in the face of external constraints. These institutional frameworks, shaped by historical contexts, enabled the gradual transformation of societies from communalism to rationalism, fostering the conditions for sustained economic growth. The evidence suggests that institutions are not merely byproducts of economic development but are central to its trajectory, as they provide the structural foundation for innovation, trade, and political stability. This perspective complements the broader argument that long-run growth is driven by institutional quality and capacity, rather than by external factors alone.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the consistent findings across multiple scholarly works. The evidence shows that institutional frameworks, shaped by historical contexts, enable the gradual transformation of societies from communalism to rationalism, fostering the conditions for sustained economic growth. This perspective is supported by the shared finding that institutions are not merely byproducts of economic development but are central to its trajectory, as they provide the structural foundation for innovation, trade, and political stability. The empirical analysis of European mortality rates and the correlation between institutional quality and income per capita further underscores the significance of institutions in determining economic outcomes. However, there is a notable disagreement regarding the nature of institutional influence, as some studies suggest that institutions arise and survive due to economic efficiency, while others argue that distributional conflicts are more critical in explaining pre-industrial European economic institutions. This discrepancy is resolved by the empirical comparison of colonial outcomes, where the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes caused by colonialism rather than geographic factors. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) demonstrates that institutional frameworks are not isolated but are part of broader, interdependent systems that shape economic growth. The historical evidence from the English financial revolution highlights the importance of institutional quality in enabling economic development, showing that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth.
