What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the intersection of historical, political, and economic analysis, and it reflects a persistent intellectual tension about the causal mechanisms that shape development. While some scholars argue that institutions are central to economic progress, others emphasize the complexity and variability of institutional frameworks. The literature is divided into two streams: one addresses a related but distinct question, how institutions evolve over time, and engages the topic only obliquely; another identifies an antecedent that flows into the topic's causal variable as a trigger. The latter stream, which includes works by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), and Frankema (2012, p.14), suggests that institutional development is not isolated but is shaped by broader historical and political contexts. These scholars argue that institutions are not merely static structures but are dynamically influenced by external forces, such as economic inequality, political stability, and cultural norms. The interplay between institutions and economic outcomes is thus more complex than previously thought, with institutions being both a cause and a consequence of development. The evidence suggests that institutional quality and structure play a significant role in shaping long-term growth, but the extent of this influence depends on the specific historical and political conditions in which institutions operate. The findings highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to determine economic trajectories. The challenge remains in identifying the precise mechanisms through which institutions affect growth and in accounting for the variability of these effects across different contexts.
