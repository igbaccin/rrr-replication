What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic theory and historical analysis, and it reflects a fundamental tension between the structural determinism of institutionalist frameworks and the dynamic, contingent nature of economic development. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-term growth, while others emphasize the role of external factors, such as technology and geography, in shaping economic outcomes. The literature presents a complex and evolving picture, with competing claims about the extent to which institutional structures determine growth trajectories. The debate is not merely theoretical but has real-world implications, as institutional quality is often cited as a key determinant of economic performance. The evidence suggests that institutions can both enable and constrain growth, depending on their nature, design, and the historical context in which they operate. Shared findings indicate that institutional frameworks are not static but are shaped by internal dynamics and external pressures. The interplay between institutions and economic growth remains a central theme in the literature, with ongoing discussions about the mechanisms through which institutions influence development. In conclusion, institutions play a crucial role in determining long-run economic growth, but their impact is mediated by a range of factors, including technological change, political stability, and the capacity of societies to adapt and evolve.
