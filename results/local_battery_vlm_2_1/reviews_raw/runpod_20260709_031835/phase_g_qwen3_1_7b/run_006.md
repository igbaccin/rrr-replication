What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic historiography and political science, and it is central to understanding how societal structures shape economic outcomes over time. The debate centers on whether institutions, such as legal frameworks, property rights, and governance structures, act as determinants of growth or are merely byproducts of it. Some argue that institutions provide the necessary conditions for economic development, while others contend that growth is more contingent on external factors like technological change or market forces. The tension between institutionalism and other paradigms remains unresolved, with scholars offering divergent perspectives on the causal relationship between institutional quality and economic outcomes. The debate is not merely theoretical but has real-world implications for policy-making and historical analysis. Institutions, as both enabling and constraining forces, are critical to understanding the trajectory of economic development. The evidence suggests that institutional quality is a significant factor in shaping long-term growth, but the exact nature of this relationship remains contested. The interplay between institutions and other variables, such as technological progress and political stability, further complicates the causal narrative. In sum, institutions play a crucial role in determining long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms through which this occurs are still under investigation.
