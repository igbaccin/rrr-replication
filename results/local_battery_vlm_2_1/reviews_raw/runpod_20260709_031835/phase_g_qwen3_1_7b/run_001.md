What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the intersection of historical and economic analysis, and it reflects a deep intellectual concern about the mechanisms that shape development outcomes. While some scholars argue that institutions are primarily a product of economic efficiency, others emphasize their role in shaping growth through distributional conflicts and structural constraints. The literature is divided into two streams: one that emphasizes the importance of institutions in shaping growth through their influence on economic efficiency, and another that highlights the role of institutions in mediating the effects of external pressures and historical conditions. The first stream, represented by works such as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and Frankema (2012, p.14), argues that institutions are not merely a byproduct of economic efficiency but are instead a key driver of growth through their ability to shape the conditions under which economic activity occurs. The second stream, as seen in Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27), suggests that institutions are more closely tied to the structural and historical contexts in which they emerge, and that their role in determining growth is often obscured by the limitations of quantitative analysis. These two perspectives challenge each other, but both agree that institutions are not simply neutral actors; rather, they are embedded in the broader socio-political and historical frameworks that shape economic outcomes. The question remains: how do institutions, shaped by historical and structural forces, influence the trajectory of economic growth over time? The answer lies in understanding the interplay between institutional frameworks and the broader economic and social conditions that determine their effectiveness.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutions are not merely passive frameworks but active agents that shape long-term economic growth through their influence on access to capital, technological diffusion, and the stability of economic institutions. The empirical findings from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) reveal that institutions, when aligned with the interests of the majority, foster growth by enabling investment and innovation, while extractive institutions hinder growth by entrenching inequality and limiting opportunity. This pattern is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), whose analysis of seventeenth-century England shows that institutional commitment to rule of law and property rights was critical to economic development. The shared finding that institutions act as both enabling and constraining forces is reinforced by North (1989, p.9), who argues that the development of financial institutions and the security of property rights were essential for economic growth. These insights suggest that institutional frameworks are not static but evolve in response to political and economic conditions, thereby shaping the trajectory of growth over time. The interplay between institutional quality and economic outcomes underscores the importance of institutional design in fostering long-term development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more benign than commonly perceived, challenging the notion that colonialism inherently impedes economic growth. Their evidence, drawn from real wage data across nine colonies from 1880 to 1965, demonstrates that African real wages were well above subsistence levels and rose significantly over time, suggesting that institutional frameworks could foster growth even in the face of colonial constraints. This aligns with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17), who show that economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa, despite periods of shrinking, was above subsistence levels in many economies by the early 20th century, highlighting the resilience of institutional structures in shaping long-term development. The shared finding that institutions act as both enabling and constraining forces is reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who argue that the development of financial institutions and the security of property rights were essential for economic growth. These insights suggest that institutional frameworks are not static but evolve in response to political and economic conditions, thereby shaping the trajectory of growth over time.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that institutional development in New World societies was not merely a product of economic efficiency but was shaped by the interplay of land, resources, and financial institutions, which favored elite members and constrained the opportunities of the broader population. This aligns with Ogilvie’s (2007, p.27) argument that pre-industrial European institutions were not simply driven by economic efficiency but were shaped by distributional conflicts, highlighting the role of institutional frameworks in determining long-term growth. The shared finding that institutions act as both enabling and constraining forces is reinforced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who show that African women's contributions to economic development in pre-colonial and colonial periods were significant, underscoring the importance of institutional structures in facilitating social and economic progress. These insights suggest that institutional frameworks are not static but evolve in response to political and economic conditions, thereby shaping the trajectory of growth over time.

Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as institutional adjustments enable the effective utilization of knowledge and technology, leading to sustained economic development. This claim is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.13), who demonstrate that institutional frameworks shape the trajectory of economic progress by facilitating the adoption and dissemination of technological advancements. Kuznets highlights that technological innovation is not merely a product of economic conditions but requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be fully realized. Pamuk further illustrates this by showing how institutional changes in the Low Countries and England enabled the adoption of new technologies, thereby driving productivity and wage growth. These insights underscore the critical role of institutions in transforming knowledge into economic output, reinforcing the idea that institutional frameworks are not static but evolve in response to changing economic and social conditions. The sustained development of economic growth thus depends on the ability of institutions to adapt and support the effective use of technological progress.

Austin's analysis reveals that institutional changes in factor markets are the fundamental mechanism through which economic growth occurs over the long term, as evidenced by the transformation in Ghana's land and labor dynamics. The shift from land abundance to localised land scarcity catalyzed an institutional revolution, replacing slavery and debt bondage with land use rights and hired labor, thereby reshaping the economic structure and fostering productivity. This insight aligns with Abramovitz's assertion that adaptability in social capability and technological opportunity interplay to determine economic outcomes, as seen in the evolution of institutional frameworks. The sustained development of economic growth thus depends on the ability of institutions to evolve in response to changing economic and social conditions, ensuring that technological progress is effectively leveraged. Institutional changes are not static but are shaped by the interplay of historical context, resource availability, and the dynamic adaptation of labor and land markets, demonstrating that economic growth is a product of institutional responsiveness and innovation. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.” Austin (2007, p.2) reports, “A fundamental continuity of this period is that the commercially valuable natural resources were located in the south, while labour moved from the north to the south, whether through the internal slave trade or, under colonial rule, in response to a market for free-hired labour.”

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the consistent findings across multiple scholarly works. The interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes is not merely theoretical but is supported by empirical evidence from various historical and contemporary contexts. The foundational argument that institutions influence economic growth is rooted in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who demonstrate that institutional quality and structural characteristics significantly affect economic outcomes. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) shows that institutional changes, particularly those driven by colonialism, have had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies, underscoring the role of institutions in determining growth. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of institutional commitment and the enforcement of rules in fostering economic development. These findings are corroborated by the work of Austin (2008, p.2), who notes that the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely economic efficiency, further supporting the thesis that institutions are critical to long-term growth. The empirical evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that institutional characteristics, such as property rights and rule of law, are key determinants of economic growth, and that these factors are not static but evolve over time. The historical evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and Austin (2008, p.2) shows that institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on their nature and the context in which they operate. The specific empirical comparison between European colonialism and the economic outcomes of former colonies provides a clear example of how institutional changes can lead to divergent economic trajectories, thereby settling the disagreement about the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth.
