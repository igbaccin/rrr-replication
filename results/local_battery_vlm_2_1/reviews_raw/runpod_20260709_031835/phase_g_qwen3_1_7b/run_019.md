What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic historiography, where the interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes remains a subject of intense debate. Scholars have long argued that institutions shape economic development through their influence on resource allocation, technological progress, and policy stability. However, the extent to which institutions determine growth remains contested, with differing accounts of how institutional quality and structure affect economic trajectories. Some scholars emphasize the role of institutions in fostering innovation and market efficiency, while others highlight the limitations of institutional theory in capturing the complexity of economic development. The debate is further complicated by the fact that institutions are not static but evolve over time, making it difficult to isolate their causal impact. The literature presents two main streams: one that argues for the enduring influence of institutions, and another that questions the capacity of institutional theory to account for the variability in economic outcomes. The former suggests that institutions provide a stable foundation for economic growth, while the latter highlights the importance of contextual factors and the dynamic nature of institutional change. The stakes of this debate are high, as it touches on the fundamental question of how societies organize themselves to sustain economic progress.
