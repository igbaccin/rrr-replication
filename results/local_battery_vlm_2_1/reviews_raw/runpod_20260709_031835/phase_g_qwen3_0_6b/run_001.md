The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and debated topic, with scholars presenting varied perspectives. One stream of scholarship highlights that institutions play a crucial role in balancing economic growth and social equity, as noted in Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.20). Their paper argues that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex, and policymakers must balance these goals. Another stream suggests that institutions are central to human capital formation, as seen in van Zanden (2009, p.27). The skill premium measured in this way acts as a predictor of long-term economic performance, as emphasized by van Zanden (2009, p.27). These two streams provide a foundation for understanding how institutional factors influence economic outcomes. However, there is a live disagreement about the extent to which institutions shape economic growth. Another position challenges this view, arguing that institutions primarily affect human capital formation rather than economic growth itself. This disagreement underscores the need for further research into the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth. The intellectual stakes here lie in understanding the interplay between institutional factors and economic outcomes. This discussion continues to shape our understanding of how institutions contribute to long-term economic growth.

This cluster's argument is that skill premium is a predictor of economic growth. The skill premium is a result of economic performance, so it is a downstream effect. The reasoning traces the cause (skill premium) leading to the outcome (economic growth). (Austin 2008, p.23; van Zanden 2009, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.15; de Vries 1994, p.18)

The third stream, which contrasts pre-industrial vs. industrial systems, argues that institutional differences influence economic outcomes, as demonstrated by Ogilvie (2007, p.15). These three streams collectively show that institutional factors are central to economic growth, supported by empirical evidence from multiple sources. The disagreement between these streams lies in the extent to which institutions influence economic growth.
