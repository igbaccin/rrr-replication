The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development remains a subject of ongoing scholarly debate, as researchers grapple with the complexities of this unique historical period. At stake is understanding the consequences of authoritarian rule for long-term economic growth and modernization. This review examines three streams of scholarship that engage with this topic, each offering distinct perspectives and evidence.

The first stream accepts the description of forced industrialization under Stalin but refines its scope and mechanism. Scholars such as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes play a crucial role in economic growth and industrialization, suggesting that similar processes occurred during the Soviet era. Another study by Bryant (2006, p.17) challenges the orthodox model of replication in Eurasian societies, arguing that China, during its late Imperial period, experienced massive growth and modernization, including significant gains in agricultural productivity, market-driven commercial expansion, and an incipient industrialization (Bryant_2006 (Bryant 2006, p.17)).

The second stream addresses a related but distinct question and engages the topic obliquely. Komlos (1998, p.16) argues that during the Industrial Revolution, absolute income gain was too low to offset declines in health among common people, leading to a decrease in their biological well-being compared to prior generations. Although not directly focusing on Soviet central planning and forced industrialization, this work sheds light on the potential human costs of rapid industrialization (Komlos_1998 (Komlos 1998, p.16)). Similarly, Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) argue that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex and that evidence from long-term national experiences suggests that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time.

The third stream's account is consistent with the topic's description and adds corroborating evidence for the same pattern. Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data for economic growth, particularly for less developed countries. He highlights the violent changes in social structures that occurred in countries undergoing highly forced industrialization under Communist auspices as examples of social invention and innovation (Kuznets_1973 (Kuznets 1973, p.10)). Williamson (1996, p.24) argues that globalization and convergence in the 19th century had predictable economic impacts, leading to political alliances and policies aimed at protecting losers.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) collectively suggest that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism have a lasting impact on current income levels across countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes instituted during European colonization account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor.

They argue that greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This finding suggests a downstream implication: the initial inequality established during European colonization has perpetuated itself through the development of institutions that favor elite groups.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1) further emphasize the significance of institutional changes, stating that the lack of reliable estimates of their effect on economic performance is a notable gap in the literature.

Kuznets and Abramovitz offer compelling evidence for the role of technological advancements, institutional adjustments, and an expanded data pool in driving economic growth.

Kuznets also emphasizes the continuity of modern growth with past trends, noting that except for Japan and possibly Russia, all currently developed countries were ahead of the rest before their modern growth and industrialization began (Kuznets 1973, p.3).

Abramovitz (1986, p.12) supports this perspective by asserting that the catch-up process of productivity among nations is complex, influenced by both exogenous factors and changes within the convergence process itself. He suggests that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied, as well as embodied, technology, and that if countries at relatively low levels of industrialization contain large numbers of redundant workers in farming and petty trade, there is also an opportunity for productivity growth by improving the allocation of labor (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Abramovitz further qualifies this simple hypothesis by stating that the impress of the catch-up process was masked before World War I due to the still early state of industrialization in many countries and the importance of farming (Abramovitz 1986, p.12).

These findings suggest a downstream implication: the initial technological disparities established during European colonization may have perpetuated themselves through the development of institutions that favored elite groups, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1) argue. The Soviet Union, having adopted forced industrialization under Stalin, can be considered an example of a country with significant institutional and ideological modifications aimed at technological advancement. The specific impact of these changes on economic development in the Soviet Union's unique historical and political context necessitates further examination.

The literature suggests that a comprehensive analysis of industrialization processes necessitates a focus on various types of laborers, including women and children, beyond the traditional emphasis on men and high wages (Humphries 2013, p.18; de Vries 1994, p.5). Humphries (2013, p.18) argues that the British industrial revolution was significantly shaped by the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor. He posits that these workers were instrumental in facilitating the growth of new industries, as well as sustaining traditional industries (Humphries 2013, p.18). De Vries (1994, p.5), on the other hand, discusses the shift in household economies from permeability to porosity during the Industrial Revolution. This change potentially fostered new forms of demand and reduced gender-based division of labor (de Vries 1994, p.5).

Humphries (2013, p.18) contends that British households were precociously nuclear, with early marriage and high fertility rates. As a result, many children and women had to work for wages due to the irregular and low-paid employment of their fathers and the fecundity of their mothers (Humphries 2013, p.19). De Vries (1994, p.5) notes that the Industrial Revolution led to a transformation in urban history, establishing a framework for regional economic development that allowed industrial growth to occur (de Vries 1994, p.5).

One implication of these findings is that the traditional focus on high male wages and skilled labor may have overlooked essential contributors to industrialization processes. Future research could further explore how this pattern extends beyond Britain and its impact on other countries' industrialization experiences.

The papers suggest that industrialization, while bringing about economic growth, may have had detrimental effects on the health and well-being of common people due to factors such as poor nutrition and stagnant wages (Komlos 1998, p.16; Allen et al. 2011, p.12). Komlos's work (Komlos_1998) focuses on the period before the twentieth century, arguing that the absolute income gain during the Industrial Revolution was too low to offset declines in health among common people, leading to a decrease in their biological well-being compared to prior generations.

Allen et al.'s research (AllenEtAl_2011) sheds light on the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, revealing that real wages in China stagnated and were significantly lower than those in leading economies in northwestern Europe. The comparatively low wages in China may have contributed to poor nutrition and thus negatively affected overall health during this period.

Moreover, Komlos's work highlights the role of transportation improvements in integrating previously isolated regions into a larger world market, which had a magnified impact on nutritional status (Komlos 1998, p.12). This condition suggests that the integration of regions could exacerbate the negative health effects associated with industrialization, as subsistence farmers, lacking full knowledge of the technology of health production, might experience downward pressure on their nutritional status due to increased market exposure.

Hornbeck's (2010) work on barbed wire adoption in the American Plains substantiates the impact of innovative infrastructure on agricultural development. The introduction and near-universal adoption of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.2). This enhancement in agricultural productivity is akin to the mechanization and resource allocation advantages brought about by Stalin's industrialization policies.

An essential mechanism that Hornbeck highlights is the role of barbed wire in protecting farmland from damage by others' cattle, thereby reducing potential losses and encouraging farmers to invest more in their land (Hornbeck 2010, p.10). This finding corroborates the assertion that the enforcement of property rights is a crucial factor in stimulating agricultural development, as illustrated by the private enclosure of common lands in England during the Industrial Revolution (Ashton 1997). The improved security provided by barbed wire fencing, similar to the institutional framework established by Stalin's central planning, allowed farmers to invest more resources into their operations, leading to increased productivity and economic growth.

In addition, Hornbeck's work suggests that the adoption of barbed wire had downstream implications for agricultural development in the American Plains. This diversification mirrors the industrial structure that emerged under Stalin's forced industrialization policies, where resource allocation led to the development of various industries beyond agriculture.

Goldin's (2006) work, alongside North's (1989), underscores the importance of demographics in economic development, specifically focusing on the changing roles of women in employment and education. Goldin argues for a 'quiet revolution' in women's employment and educational choices, driven by the generation born in the late 1940s (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift, according to Goldin, led to major advances in labor economics, transforming the study of labor from an institutional focus to a more comprehensive examination of labor supply and family decisions.

North (1989, p.10) underscores the role of political and economic institutions in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that predominantly focus on population and savings. North's analysis reveals how designed institutions like guilds provided a means for internal economic regulation, imposing price ceilings and monopolistic grants to control trade (North 1989, p.10).

Goldin's work suggests that the increased involvement of women in employment and education has downstream implications for labor economics and economic growth. North, on the other hand, highlights how political institutions can regulate the economy, providing a contrasting yet complementary perspective to Goldin's findings. Together, these scholars underscore the importance of considering both demographic factors and institutional structures when examining economic development.

This shift in focus towards demographics offers a nuanced understanding of the impact of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. While Stalin's policies primarily focused on political institutions as a means to drive economic growth, the work of Goldin and North suggests that changes in demographics, particularly the changing roles of women in employment and education, can also play a significant role in economic development. This finding aligns with the broader literature on the impact of industrialization on labor (Humphries_2013; deVries_1994) and health and well-being (Komlos_1998; AllenEtAl_2011), indicating that demographic changes may have been an important factor in the economic growth observed under Stalin's regime.

The papers by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.11) and Broadberry et al. (2015) offer evidence supporting the claim that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, as demonstrated by India's economic decline relative to Britain during the early modern period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.11) reveal that unskilled labor in northern India had significantly lower daily rates compared to those in Britain, which likely contributed to the growing disparity between their industrial output (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11).

Broadberry et al. (2015) further elaborate on this finding by demonstrating that India's per capita GDP declined steadily from 1600 to 1871, falling behind Britain's growth during the same period (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3).

One specific mechanism that contributed to this disparity is the service sector. Broadberry et al. (2015) argue that service sector output growth in India did not move closely in line with the urban population, which likely hindered the development of a robust service sector crucial for economic growth and industrialization (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9).

Another downstream implication of this service sector disparity is found in de Vries' (1994) work, which asserts that the growth of cities and urbanization played a significant role in the Industrial Revolution in Europe . The underdevelopment of India's service sector may have hindered its ability to urbanize effectively, further limiting its industrial potential.

The post-World War II economic growth in Western Europe can be attributed to a developmental deficit and disequilibrium, as argued by Temin (2002, p.8). This growth ended in the 1970s when this deficit was eliminated. Temin's argument is rooted in the historical context of industrialization, where he highlights that Germany started its industrialization after Britain (Temin 2002, p.8). This temporal lag created a developmental gap that contributed to the post-war growth surge in Western Europe. The model proposed by Temin further suggests that this growth was driven by a shift from agriculture to industry, as evidenced by the decline in the agricultural labor share (Temin 2002, p.16).

This decline in the agricultural labor share had significant downstream implications, particularly in terms of urbanization. De Vries' (1994) work underscores the role of cities and urbanization in the Industrial Revolution in Europe. The underdevelopment of agriculture in India may have hindered its ability to urbanize effectively, further limiting its industrial potential .

The Industrial Revolution in Britain offers a comparative perspective to the Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin, as both periods experienced significant economic changes. Allen (2001, p.9) argues that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained (not increased) in economic leaders, such as Britain. Feinstein (1998, p.2), on the other hand, contends that pessimistic views on real wages and standard of living during and after the Industrial Revolution in Britain are misguided.

The author's regression analysis shows a plausible and useful cost function, with the coefficient of the wage rate lower after 1850, indicating the industrialization of baking (Allen 2001, p.9). Moreover, real wage changes were not dramatic anywhere in the first 70 years of the nineteenth century, with the Netherlands and Belgium experiencing only modest declines compared to the rest of Europe (Allen 2001, p.3).

The author argues that it would be rash to attempt to draw a general price curve on the basis of such approximate figures, as it could only result in mystification (Feinstein 1998, p.2). Instead, Feinstein suggests that the years from 1770 to 1830 witnessed an unprecedented doubling of the population of England and Wales and a succession of harvest failures caused by abnormal weather conditions. Furthermore, there was the massive impact of the wars with France, which created inflationary financial conditions, stimulated some sectors, and disrupted others (Feinstein 1998, p.5).

These findings suggest that while the Soviet industrialization under Stalin led to a surge in economic growth, as evidenced by Temin's (2002, p.8) work, the mechanisms and patterns of change during the Industrial Revolution in Britain were different. The divergence in real incomes between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent was already established before the industrial revolution, and the standard of living during and after the Industrial Revolution in Britain was not as grim as some scholars suggest. These differences underscore the complexity and nuance of economic development processes, highlighting the importance of understanding historical contexts to draw accurate conclusions about economic growth mechanisms.

The literature on causation in economic growth challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing the significant role of slave trading and coercion in early industrialization. Austin argues that profits from Atlantic trade enabled the commercial bourgeoisie of western Europe to demand institutional changes, with Acemoglu et al. (2002c) noting that these profits were 'associated' with those from colonialism and slavery.

Austin further contends that colonial rulers had no interest in sacrificing current consumption for a gamble on longer-term industrialization in the colonies, which might threaten metropolitan jobs. This perspective offers a nuanced view of post-colonial industrialization efforts, acknowledging some growth but also highlighting the challenges faced in achieving sustained and self-sustaining development.

By considering the role of slave trading and coercion, this stream of literature complicates our understanding of causation in economic growth during the Industrial Revolution. It underscores that the process was more intricate than a simple 'reversal of fortune,' involving a complex interplay of factors such as trade, colonialism, and institutional change.

This nuanced perspective has implications for our interpretation of economic development processes in other historical contexts, including the Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. By acknowledging the role of coercion and exploitation in early industrialization, we may be better equipped to analyze and understand the mechanisms at play during this period, and to draw accurate conclusions about the factors that contribute to economic growth. Austin (2008, p.23) reports, “slave trading made a signiﬁcant (some would argue critical) contribution to the original industrialisation.”

The papers in this cluster provide evidence that economic growth and increased income equality can coexist over time, challenging the assumption that these two factors are inherently opposed (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.18). Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) argue that modern economic growth begins on a traditional agrarian base characterized by elastic labor supplies. Accelerating rates of capital accumulation associated with early industrialization fail to generate rising real wages among the unskilled until the surplus labor pool is exhausted. This turning point can be postponed for some time if demographic forces are right and Malthusian population pressures or immigration continually replenish the initial pool (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14).

Moreover, they demonstrate that East African colonial governments opted for more direct intervention in the labor market because they believed that laissez-faire policies would not solve chronic labor shortages.

One implication of this mechanism is that the initial surplus labor pool can delay the rise in real wages among unskilled workers, but demographic forces and immigration can prolong the period during which growth and increased income equality coexist.

Understanding the economic impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development requires considering related yet distinct phenomena, such as globalization and convergence in the 19th century.

A key mechanism shared between Soviet industrialization and globalization is the influence on labor markets. The Industrial Revolution in Europe, as discussed by deVries (1994) and Humphries (2013, p.18), led to a shift from agricultural to industrial labor, which increased demand for labor and contributed to real wage convergence.

It's crucial to acknowledge that while both Stalinist industrialization and globalization led to an increase in labor demand, they differed significantly in their labor market strategies. In contrast to the Soviet regime's forced labor policies, colonial governments in East Africa, as demonstrated by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18), intervened more directly in labor markets due to chronic labor shortages. This intervention ensured that growth and increased income equality persisted, providing a qualification to the mechanism observed in both globalization and Stalinist industrialization.

The convergence of economies during this period also had implications for income levels across countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) confirm that institutions play a crucial role in shaping income levels, with more inclusive institutions promoting economic growth and reducing inequality. Williamson's (1996, p.24) work suggests that similar forces may have been at play during the 19th century, contributing to economy-wide real wage and labor productivity convergence.

Bryant (2006, p.17) argues that revisionist perspectives, which suggest all Eurasian societies were progressing along a shared modernizing course, are empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. He contends that social trajectories must be tracked from the most advanced and prosperous regions, such as the Yangzi delta and coastal areas, to compare "leading edge" sectors and avoid misleading portraits based on aggregate national estimates (Bryant, 2006, p.17).

Bryant (2006, p.22) admits that the data used to support these claims have issues, with specialists pointing out that their sources are not always dependable. This concern echoes the findings of Simson (2008), who emphasizes the importance of understanding the limitations and biases inherent in historical data when examining economic development during this period.

The implication is that while the revisionist position may offer a more nuanced perspective on Eurasian societies' modernization, it is essential to critically evaluate the data sources used to support these arguments. This caution underscores the need for continued research into the reliability and validity of historical data when studying economic development in the context of central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin or other historical periods.

Simson's (2019, p.26) study on post-independence budget policies and landholding in Africa challenges the common assumption that these policies were exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Peters (2004, p.30) illustrates how reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa conceal processes of exclusion, widening social divisions, and class formation.

Simson (2019, p.26) argues that Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda's post-independence budget policies were not as unproductive as often assumed. He contends that a large literature has criticized African nationalization policies of the 1960s and 1970s, mismanaged industrial policy, and excessive borrowing used to finance them (Simson_2019, p.26). Simson's findings provide a nuanced viewpoint on the productivity of post-independence budget policies amidst global interest in state-led development.

He cites numerous cases of land grabbing and disputes observed by various organizations working in Mozambique (Peters_2004, p.30). This highlights the need for a focus on who benefits and loses from instances of 'negotiable' landholding in Africa.

In this context, it is worth noting that these findings offer a different perspective on state intervention and its effects, as compared to the Soviet industrialization under Stalin discussed earlier. While the Soviet case involved direct government control over resources and industries, the African cases demonstrate how negotiable landholding and budget policies can mask underlying processes of exclusion and class formation. This underscores the complexity of understanding state intervention and its impacts on economic development in different historical and geographical contexts.

Continuing with this line of thought, it is essential to consider how these findings might inform our understanding of contemporary issues related to land rights, resource allocation, and poverty alleviation in Africa. Simson's (2019) challenge to common assumptions about post-independence budget policies offers a valuable starting point for such an analysis. Similarly, Peters' (2004) insights into the masked processes of exclusion and class formation can help us better understand and address ongoing challenges in land governance and social inequality in Africa.

In conclusion, the literature reveals a shared understanding that the impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development were profound and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for a significant fraction of current income differences, and their findings provide support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among formerly poor colonies, including the Soviet Union. Komlos (1998, p.12) questions this perspective by proposing that during the early stages of modern economic growth, populations faced pressure to have lower nutritional status due to the state of technology. This downward pressure was further magnified by industrialization and its concomitant processes, such as increased division of labor and integration into a larger world market. The historical evidence of nutritional status in various societies offers a critical empirical comparison to settle the disagreement between these perspectives.
