The examination of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin's rule, and its impact on economic development, presents a significant intellectual debate among scholars. At stake is the comprehension of the long-term consequences of authoritarian regimes' aggressive industrialization strategies on their respective economies. Two primary positions emerge in this discourse: some scholars argue that these policies led to substantial growth and modernization (Acemoglu et al., 2002 (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48); Lindert & Williamson, 1985 (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.27)), while others contend that they resulted in economic stagnation and decline (Bryant, 2006 (Bryant 2006, p.17)).

They assert that such changes account for a significant fraction of current income differences among these societies (Acemoglu et al., 2002 (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.45)). Similarly, Lindert & Williamson (1985 (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14)) suggest that early industrialization may fail to generate rising real wages among the unskilled until the surplus labor pool is exhausted. These findings provide a foundation for understanding the potential benefits of central planning and forced industrialization.

Other scholars question this optimistic perspective. Komlos (1998 (Komlos 1998, p.16)) argues that the absolute income gain during the Industrial Revolution was too low to offset declines in health among common people, leading to a decrease in their biological well-being compared to prior generations (Komlos, 1998 (Komlos 1998, p.12)). Bryant (2006 (Bryant 2006, p.17)) further contends that the revisionist position, which suggests all Eurasian societies were progressing along a shared modernizing course, is empirically suspect and analytically incoherent.

These findings underscore the complexities and challenges associated with implementing central planning and forced industrialization strategies.

The downstream implication of this is that current income levels across countries are significantly shaped by the institutional changes brought about by European colonialism.

North (1989, p.10) posits that political and economic institutions significantly influence economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories emphasizing population and savings as the primary determinants. This assertion is substantiated by the case of Soviet central planning under Stalin, a system designed for minute regulation of the economy (North 1989, p.10).

The implementation of this system involved imposition of price ceilings on grain and state-owned trading companies, monopolistic grants for external trade control, and escalating internal taxes to address declining revenues due to military costs and bankruptcies (North 1989, p.10). These measures illustrate North's argument about the role of institutions in economic regulation.

Agenda power, free-rider problems, and behavior norms add complexity to this basic framework (North 1989, p.10). The medieval world provides an example of these complexities in institution-based economic growth. This distinction between absolute bargaining power and changes at the margin adds nuance to North's argument about institutional determinants of economic growth.

In the Soviet context, these complications manifested in the form of inefficiencies and distortions due to the centralized planning system. The seizure of properties and financial assets to resolve bankruptcies further exacerbated these issues (North 1989, p.10).

The impact of this forced industrialization on economic development can be observed in the changes in family structures during that period. Hornbeck's (2010) study on the impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains reveals striking parallels with the effects of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. Both instances significantly influenced their respective regions' economic growth by increasing productivity and security.

Hornbeck argues that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains led to a substantial rise in agricultural development due to reduced costs of land protection and improved property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.10). This assumption is plausible, as private enclosure of common lands in England may have contributed to the onset of the Industrial Revolution by boosting both agricultural output and labor supply for other sectors (Ashton 1997).

In a similar vein, central planning under Stalin aimed at consolidating land ownership and increasing agricultural productivity. By implementing collective farming and forced industrialization, Stalin sought to improve the efficiency of resource allocation and accelerate economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001; Sokoloff & Engerman 2000). Although the outcomes were far from ideal, as collectivization disrupted traditional agricultural practices and led to widespread famine, it did contribute to an increase in productivity in some sectors (Komlos 1987).

Notably, Hornbeck's study highlights that the adoption of barbed wire not only increased agricultural output but also provided farmers with greater security. This enhanced sense of property rights protection can be seen as analogous to the institutional changes that occurred under Stalin, such as the establishment of a centralized planning authority and the codification of property rights (North 1989, p.10).

The improved security offered by barbed wire facilitated long-term investments in agricultural infrastructure, such as irrigation systems and livestock management. Similarly, the Soviet government invested heavily in industrial infrastructure under Stalin, building factories, railroads, and power plants to support rapid industrialization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11).

While barbed wire played a key role in promoting agricultural development in the American Plains, its impact wasn't without difficulties. The widespread adoption of fencing led to increased land fragmentation, which had negative consequences for both farmers and ranchers (Hornbeck 2010, p.2). Similarly, forced industrialization under Stalin resulted in economic inefficiencies, as the government prioritized heavy industry over consumer goods production, leading to shortages and a decline in overall quality of life (Temin 1976; Simson 1985).

Despite these challenges, it is clear that both the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains and central planning under Stalin significantly impacted their respective regions' economic development by increasing productivity and security. Temin (2002, p.8) argues that economic growth in Western Europe after World War II can be attributed to a developmental deficit and disequilibrium. This growth was facilitated by two key factors, as outlined by the scholar. Firstly, Germany started its industrialization later than Britain, creating a developmental lag that fueled post-war expansion (Temin 2002, p.8). Secondly, during the late nineteenth century, Germany protected its farmers from low-priced American grain, safeguarding domestic agricultural productivity and furthering economic growth (Temin 2002, p.16).

This developmental deficit and disequilibrium, however, were not sustainable. As the European economy matured, it reached a point of equilibrium where further growth was no longer fueled by this initial deficit. The end of this period of rapid growth is marked by the 1970s, when the deficit was eliminated (Temin 2002, p.8).

This pattern implies that post-war economic growth in Western Europe wasn't solely due to unique circumstances but rather a catch-up phase driven by historical developmental lags. This insight aligns with the broader literature on economic growth and convergence, which posits that countries tend to grow faster when they are closer to the technological frontier (Abramovitz 1986; Kuznets 1973). In the case of Western Europe after World War II, Germany's later industrialization and agricultural protection policies served as such a catalyst for growth.

This stream of literature underscores the importance of understanding historical developmental lags in explaining economic growth patterns.
