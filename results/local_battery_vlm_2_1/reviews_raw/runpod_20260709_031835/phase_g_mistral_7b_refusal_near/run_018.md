The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development is a contentious issue in historical scholarship, with debates centering around the effectiveness and consequences of this approach. This literature review focuses on three key streams of related scholarship that engage with this topic, each offering unique perspectives and evidence.

Firstly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes played a significant role in economic growth and the surge of industrialization among formerly poor colonies. Their work provides support for the view that institutions, including those resulting from forced industrialization under authoritarian regimes like Stalin's Soviet Union, can account for a fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.45).

Secondly, Komlos (1998, p.16) examines the impact of the Industrial Revolution on population welfare. Although his study does not directly address the Soviet context, it offers valuable insights into the effects of industrialization on nutritional status and biological well-being (Komlos 1998, p.16)(Komlos 1998, p.12).

Thirdly, Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) argue that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex, and that evidence from long-term national experiences suggests that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time. Their work offers indirect support for the idea that forced industrialization under Stalin may have contributed to economic development without necessarily exacerbating inequality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14)(Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.27).

Kuznets (1973, p.10) emphasizes the need for advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries. His work underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay of factors in forced industrialization processes like Stalin's (Kuznets 1973, p.10)(Kuznets 1973, p.3).

Bryant (2006, p.17) challenges the revisionist position that all Eurasian societies were progressing along a shared modernizing course. While his work does not directly engage with the Soviet context, it highlights the importance of tracking social trajectories from their unique starting points (Bryant 2006, p.17)(Bryant 2006, p.22).

Finally, Simson (2019, p.26) argues against the common assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. This work offers a broader context for understanding the potential impacts of forced industrialization on economic development (Simson 2019, p.26)(Simson 2019, p.13).

In synthesis, these works provide a nuanced view of the impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development. They suggest that while institutional changes played a role in economic growth, the effects on population welfare and equality were complex and varied. Furthermore, they underscore the importance of understanding the unique contexts and factors involved in forced industrialization processes like Stalin's. These findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the historical dynamics of economic development under authoritarian regimes.

Institutions, as argued by North (1989, p.10), significantly influence economic growth, a claim that finds resonance in the context of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. Central to this argument is the establishment of minute regulation within the economy, as demonstrated by the imposition of price ceilings on grain and state-owned trading companies, and the granting of monopolistic control over external trade (North_1989 (North 1989, p.10)). North further posits that agenda power, free-rider problems, and norms of behavior add complexity to this institutional framework (North_1989 (North 1989, p.7)).

The Soviet case under Stalin illustrates the intricate interplay between these elements. Enforcement of such controls was not without complications, as agenda power and free-rider problems persisted, posing challenges for effective economic management.

Moreover, the Soviet context highlights the role of norms in shaping institutional behavior. Under Stalin's regime, the emphasis on rapid industrialization led to the disregard for traditional societal norms, which had previously provided a degree of internal regulation (North_1989 (North 1989, p.7)). This shift, however, did not necessarily lead to improved economic outcomes, as the forced nature of industrialization and the disruption of traditional norms created social and economic dislocations.

The papers by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) and Komlos (1998, p.16) collectively demonstrate that while industrialization can lead to economic growth, it may also result in increased income inequality or decreased biological well-being among common people.

This decline in biological well-being offset any absolute income gain during industrialization, leading to a decrease in the overall health and well-being of common people compared to prior generations.

This suggests a potential avenue for future research to further explore the relationship between economic growth, inequality, and biological well-being under different institutional contexts.

The institutions and property rights established during European colonization significantly impacted the success or failure of subsequent state-led industrialization efforts, such as those under Stalin. This stream argues that these colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic growth and development outcomes across countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1) assert that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism have a substantial impact on current income levels across countries, with relatively poor colonies experiencing industrialization and subsequent prosperity when favorable institutions were established. They emphasize that the recipients of colonial profits varied, with some cases leading to state control (Portugal), others benefiting private enterprises (Britain), and still others granting monopoly trading rights to kings (Belgium).

They find that these institutions played an essential role in the process of economic growth and industrialization, leading to the relative prosperity or stagnation observed today.

Interestingly, one downstream implication of this stream's findings is that the success or failure of state-led industrialization efforts may be influenced by the institutions established during European colonization. This suggests a potential avenue for understanding the varying outcomes of industrialization in different countries and historical contexts.

By tracing the impact of colonial institutions on economic development, this stream offers valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying state-led industrialization efforts and their outcomes.

The impact of inequality on institutional development in New World societies is a significant focus within scholarly discourse. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that greater economic disparities in these societies led to institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality over the long term. This claim is supported by Bryant's (2006, p.17) work, which highlights the dynamic nature of Eurasian societies, but also acknowledges potential issues with data reliability.

Bryant also acknowledges concerns about the data's reliability that support these claims.

The implication of this line of thought is that economic disparities can significantly shape long-term social structures and power dynamics, as seen in the cases of New World societies and late Imperial China. This insight offers valuable perspectives on the mechanisms underlying state-led industrialization efforts and their outcomes, contributing to a broader understanding of economic development under various institutional regimes.

The underrepresentation of female and child labor in historical accounts of the British Industrial Revolution is addressed by a stream of literature that sheds light on this critical aspect (Humphries 2013, p.18; de Vries 1994, p.5). Humphries (2013, p.18) argues that focusing on men and high wages alone provides an incomplete understanding of the revolution, emphasizing the importance of cheap and amenable female and child labor.

Humphries (2013, p.18) suggests that British working families were burdened with more children and experienced higher fertility rates compared to their European counterparts. As a result, British women and children were more likely to need to work for wages, contributing to the industrialization process.

This change may have been linked to the early development of wage labor and the shift towards nuclear households in the eighteenth century. The increased use of female and child labor during this period could have contributed to the persistence of traditional industries and older forms of industrial organization.

The comparative data presented by Allen et al. (2011, p.12) and Feinstein (1998, p.2) sheds light on global wage trends beyond Britain during the Industrial Revolution. According to Allen et al., real wages in leading economies in northwestern Europe, such as Britain, Germany, and the Netherlands, increased significantly from the eighteenth century to the early twentieth (Allen et al. 2011, p.12). Feinstein argues that pessimistic views on real wages and standard of living during this period in Britain are misguided (Feinstein 1998, p.2).

Allen et al. emphasize that the industrial revolution raised British wages above Dutch levels, a trend that intensified after 1870 (Allen et al. 2011, p.12). On the other hand, Feinstein notes that the controversy over the impact of early industrialization and capitalism on the standard of living of the British working class has been extensively debated since the 1830s (Feinstein 1998, p.2).

One specific mechanism these sources jointly establish is the divergence in real wage trends between leading economies in northwestern Europe and other regions, such as China. Allen et al. argue that in the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, real wages in China stagnated and were significantly lower than those in leading economies (Allen et al. 2011, p.1). This finding qualifies the upward trend in real wages observed in the leading economies, highlighting the global disparities during the Industrial Revolution.

(BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3)). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.11) focus on the comparison between unskilled workers in India and Britain, arguing that contrary to some world historians, the great divergence was already in motion before 1800.

Similarly, Broadberry et al. (2015) provide empirical evidence of India's per capita GDP decline from 1600 to 1871, a period during which Britain experienced growth.

Furthermore, Broadberry et al. This finding underscores the importance of urbanization and service sector development in driving economic growth, as observed in leading European economies during this time.

The economic growth in Western Europe after World War II was significantly influenced by a developmental deficit and disequilibrium, as argued by Temin (2002, p.8). This growth period, often referred to as the Golden Age, ended in the 1970s when this deficit was eliminated.

Temin's (2002, p.8) argument is grounded in three key factors: first, Germany started industrializing after Britain; second, Germany chose to protect its farmers from low-priced American grain in the late nineteenth century; and third, the economic growth following World War II was a response to this developmental deficit. This deficit created an imbalance that led to rapid economic expansion, particularly in agriculture.

The trend of agricultural labor share during the period under consideration supports Temin's assertions. According to Temin (2002, p.8), the agricultural labor share experienced significant growth from 1946 to 1973, indicating the disequilibrium and its subsequent resolution. This growth in the agricultural sector was not uniform across countries; however, it was a common trend among Western European economies.

One downstream implication of this developmental deficit is the structural transformation that occurred during the post-war period. As agriculture became more productive, labor began to shift towards manufacturing and service sectors, contributing to overall economic growth (Temin 2002, p.8). This transformation was particularly pronounced in countries like Germany and Italy, where agricultural employment declined significantly during this time (Bairoch, 1982).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that economic growth in Europe and Asia diverged significantly during the Industrial Revolution, requires qualification to account for diverse historical paths (Austin 2008, p.23). Austin (2008, p.23) argues that profits from Atlantic trade and slavery played a crucial role in enabling the commercial bourgeoisie of Western Europe to demand institutional changes protecting their property rights.

In support of this claim, Austin notes that Acemoglu et al. (2002c) observe that profits from Atlantic trade were associated with those from colonialism and slavery. Furthermore, Austin contends that colonial rulers had no interest in sacrificing current consumption for a gamble on longer-term industrialisation in the colonies, which might threaten metropolitan jobs (Austin 2008, p.23).

This qualification of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis has downstream implications for understanding the post-colonial pursuit of industrialisation. Although there was a significant growth of manufacturing between 1950 and Structural Adjustment, many post-colonial attempts at industrialisation failed (Austin 2008, p.23). The reasons for this failure are complex, but Austin suggests that they may be rooted in the historical legacy of colonialism and the priorities of colonial rulers.

Simson's analysis challenges the prevailing narrative that African nationalization policies of the 1960s and 1970s were mismanaged and excessively borrowing-dependent (Simson 2019, p.26).

This finding is shared with other sources, which suggest that African states were not always characterized by excessive public employment growth (Goldsmith 'Africa's overgrown state'; Rodrik 'Public employment').

It is important to recognize that the specific mechanisms and conditions underlying these budget policies varied across countries. Furthermore, the impact of these policies on economic development was influenced by factors such as the quality of governance, the presence or absence of corruption, and the ability to manage foreign aid effectively (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1).

The reasons for these differences are complex and multifaceted, involving factors such as the historical legacy of colonialism, the priorities of post-independence leaders, and the broader global economic context.

Nevertheless, the evidence suggests that post-independence budget policies in Africa were not uniformly unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. This finding complicates the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which argues that post-colonial attempts at industrialization often failed due to misguided budget policies (Austin 2008, p.23). Instead, it underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of the historical trajectory of economic development in Africa.

Hornbeck's (2010) study on barbed wire and agricultural development in the American Plains offers an intriguing parallel to the Soviet Union's industrialization under Stalin. While Stalin's policies had profound effects on industry, Hornbeck argues that the introduction of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural development in a different context.

Hornbeck contends that the widespread adoption of barbed wire reduced costs associated with protecting farmland and facilitated property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.10). This assumption, Hornbeck suggests, might be comparable to the enclosure of common lands in England during the Industrial Revolution, which increased agricultural output and labor supply (Ashton 1997).

In Hornbeck's study, barbed wire fences protected farmers' land and crops from damage by others' cattle. This improved security led to more efficient use of resources, as farmers could invest in long-term improvements without fear of loss due to unauthorized grazing (Hornbeck 2010, p.2). The resulting increase in agricultural productivity, Hornbeck posits, may have had similar ripple effects on labor supply and industrial development as the enclosure movement in England.

However, it is crucial to note that while both barbed wire fences and enclosed lands contributed to agricultural growth, the mechanisms at play were distinct. In the case of the American Plains, the introduction of a new technology enabled more efficient land management, whereas the enclosure movement involved reallocation of existing resources.

Moreover, it is important to consider that while these examples demonstrate the potential benefits of improved property rights and resource security, they do not necessarily imply that similar gains could be achieved through forced industrialization or central planning, as was the case in the Soviet Union under Stalin. Nonetheless, Hornbeck's study underscores the importance of considering diverse historical contexts when evaluating the impact of institutions on economic development.

The mid-nineteenth century divergence in real incomes was a gradual phenomenon that began as early as the 16th century (Allen 2001, p.3). This long-term trend towards income divergence is further supported by Kuznets' seminal work on economic growth and inequality (Kuznets) . Kuznets  identified a U-shaped pattern of income distribution across time, with a decline in inequality during the early stages of industrialization, followed by an increase as the process matured. The assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments is challenged by a body of scholarship led by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18). This cluster demonstrates that real wages in Africa were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, suggesting other factors played a more significant role in shaping economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) present evidence that African wage levels compared favorably with those in 19th-century London, despite lower starting points.

West African workers were attracted by the prospect of wages exceeding their opportunity costs in subsistence farming activities, indicating a more voluntary shift towards wage labor (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27).

This stream of literature implies that structural growth impediments may not have been as significant in shaping African economic development as previously thought. Instead, factors such as labor market interventions by colonial governments and the attractiveness of wages compared to subsistence farming may have played a more substantial role. This finding complicates the understanding of historical African poverty and economic development, inviting further exploration into other potential explanatory factors.

Technological advancement, institutional changes, and exploitation of redundant workers are crucial elements in the process of economic growth, particularly in less developed countries (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Kuznets's paper argues that for economic growth, a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries. The innovational requirements are conspicuous in the social and political structures that underwent 'violent changes' during highly forced industrialization under Communist auspices (Kuznets 1973, p.10).

Abramovitz (1986, p.12) supports this claim by suggesting that the catch-up process of productivity among nations is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and changes within the convergence process itself, as well as social and political concomitants of wealth. Catching up in growth before World War I was masked because farming was still significant; however, after 1913, the process left a more noticeable mark (Abramovitz 1986, p.12).

Moreover, Abramovitz argues that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied, as well as in embodied, technology. If countries at relatively low levels of industrialization contain large numbers of redundant workers in farming and petty trade, as is normally the case, there is also an opportunity for productivity growth by improving the allocation of labor (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

In this context, the Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin can be seen as a conspicuous illustration of the kind of social invention and innovation that may be involved in economic growth.

The exploitation of redundant workers is evident in the massive mobilization of labor for industrial projects, which often involved forced relocation and harsh working conditions.

It is important to note that these strategies often came at a significant social cost. The forced industrialization under Stalin was characterized by human rights abuses, famines, and widespread suffering (not cited; see  for more on the Soviet famine of 1932-1933).

The literature reveals a consensus that Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin significantly impacted economic development in the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that such institutional changes played an essential role in the process of economic growth, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies.

There is disagreement about the social costs associated with this strategy. Komlos (1998, p.16) suggests that during the early stages of modern economic growth, given the state of technology, populations experienced a downward pressure on their nutritional status due to industrialization and its concomitant increased division of labor. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) argue that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time, suggesting that while forced industrialization may have led to economic growth in the short term, it did not necessarily result in widespread suffering.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the examination of nutritional status among populations living through forced industrialization in authoritarian regimes like the USSR. Studies on societies for which reliable economic data exist would enable us to disentangle the effects of improvements in public health, medical technology, and nutritional status on the human organism (Komlos 1998, p.16). Further work could involve comparing the nutritional status of populations living under forced industrialization with those living under different institutional arrangements, such as democratic or non-Communist authoritarian systems. This thorough comparison would shed light on the extent to which forced industrialization under Stalin was responsible for widespread suffering and provide a nuanced understanding of the relationship between economic growth, institutional changes, and social wellbeing in less developed countries.
