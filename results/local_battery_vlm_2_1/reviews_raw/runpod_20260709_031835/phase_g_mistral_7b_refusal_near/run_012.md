The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development remains a subject of significant scholarly debate. At stake is the understanding of how institutional changes, technological advancements, and ideological adjustments contribute to economic growth in less developed countries, particularly those undergoing rapid industrialization under authoritarian regimes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes play a crucial role in economic growth and the surge of industrialization among formerly poor colonies. They provide support for this view by demonstrating that institutions account for a significant fraction of current income differences between these societies. Komlos (1998, p.16) argues that during the early stages of modern economic growth, populations faced a decline in their nutritional status due to industrialization and its associated processes.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) suggest that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex, with evidence from long-term national experiences indicating that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time. Kuznets (1973, p.10) also acknowledges this complexity, arguing that for economic growth, a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries.

Williamson (1996, p.24) posits that globalization and convergence in the 19th century had predictable economic impacts, leading to political alliances and policies aimed at protecting losers. Simson (2019, p.26), however, argues that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions.

The disagreement between these scholars revolves around the extent to which institutional changes, technological advancements, and ideological adjustments contribute to economic growth in less developed countries undergoing forced industrialization.

Lindert and Williamson's (1985, p.14) work, alongside Abramovitz (1986, p.12) and Kuznets (1973, p.10), collectively suggest a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between economic growth and income equality during rapid industrialization. These scholars argue that while early stages of industrialization may lead to increased income inequality, this phenomenon can coexist with economic growth over time.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) propose that the elasticity of labor supplies in traditional agrarian societies allows for accelerated rates of capital accumulation without immediately leading to rising real wages among unskilled workers. This situation can persist if demographic forces replenish the initial pool of surplus labor, as observed in contemporary Asia, Japan, and America before World War I (Lindert&Williamson_1985).

Abramovitz (1986, p.12) further elaborates on this idea, suggesting that the catch-up process of productivity among nations is influenced by both exogenous factors and changes within the convergence process itself. He argues that backwardness presents an opportunity for modernization in disembodied technology and labor allocation improvements, which can contribute to productivity growth (Abramovitz_1986).

He emphasizes that these changes are crucial components of modern growth, which often continues many older trends but at an accelerated pace (Kuznets_1973).

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.11) and their colleagues (Broadberry et al., 2015) provide compelling evidence supporting the claim that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, as they demonstrate India's economic decline relative to Britain during the early modern period.

Similarly, Broadberry et al. (2015) demonstrate that India's per capita GDP declined steadily from 1600 to 1871, falling behind Britain's growth during the same period (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3).

This decline in labor productivity and GDP growth can be attributed to several factors. One important factor is the lack of technological advancements in India compared to Europe. This trade imbalance led to a decline in India's industrial sector, as domestic industries struggled to compete with cheaper foreign goods.

Furthermore, the service sector in India also experienced stagnation due to population growth outpacing output growth (Broadberry et al., 2015, (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9)). The authors suggest that this imbalance between population and output growth can be attributed to the lack of urbanization and industrialization in India during the early modern period.

Allen et al. The implications of these findings are significant. If the gains from industrialization were not enough to improve living standards for many, then the assumption that similar processes under Stalin would lead to improved health and income may be misguided. This raises questions about the effectiveness of top-down approaches to economic development and the importance of considering broader social and health factors in such policies.

The papers of Temin (2002, p.8) offer compelling insights into the economic growth witnessed in Western Europe following World War II. Temin argues that this growth was primarily driven by a developmental deficit and disequilibrium, which persisted until the 1970s when this deficit was rectified.

Temin's central claim is supported by two key observations. Firstly, he notes that Germany, which started its industrialization after Britain, experienced a lag in economic growth (Temin 2002, p.8). Secondly, Temin points out that Germany protected its farmers against low-priced American grain in the late nineteenth century, thereby insulating itself from external competition and fostering domestic agricultural development (Temin 2002, p.16).

These protective measures and the subsequent industrial growth trajectory of Germany highlight a crucial mechanism underlying Western Europe's economic expansion during the Golden Age: the presence of developmental deficits and disequilibria. In other words, countries that had fallen behind in the early stages of industrialization were able to catch up by implementing policies aimed at addressing these deficits and promoting domestic industries.

Another downstream implication of this mechanism is that the economic growth observed during the Golden Age was not a uniform phenomenon across all regions. Countries with earlier industrialization, such as Britain, may have experienced different growth patterns or challenges due to their head start in the industrial revolution. This finding underscores the importance of understanding historical context and specific institutional arrangements when analyzing the impact of industrialization on economic development.

The downstream implication of this shift can be seen in the evolving focus of labor economics. As women's employment expanded, there was a shift towards studying labor supply and the decisions made by families and households, reflecting the changing reality of women's roles in the workforce.

Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.2) papers suggest that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was significantly influenced by the types of economic institutions that evolved and their impact on resource allocation. Their work expands upon the understanding of long-term inequality, demonstrating how institutional structures favoring elites can restrict competition and perpetuate disparities over time.

The emergence of New World societies was marked by a competition among European nations for claims and resources (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This struggle led to the adaptation or innovation of institutional forms that facilitated the exploitation of underpopulated territories and the extraction of material advantages. As Sokoloff and Engerman explain, "From Factor Endowments to Inequality... European nations competed for claims and set about extracting material and other advantages through the pursuit of transitory enterprises like expeditions as well as by the establishment of more permanent settlements" (Sokoloff & Engerman, 2000, p.4).

This competition resulted in the creation of institutions that benefited elites, thereby limiting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. For instance, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that on the eve of the Haitian Revolution in 1790, Haiti was the richest society in the world per capita (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). It was not until industrialization began in North America during the nineteenth century that a significant divergence between the United States and Canada and the rest of the hemisphere took place. The authors suggest that this gap has remained consistent since 1900 (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

They contend that institutions that favored elites restricted access to land, capital, and opportunities for the majority population, thereby perpetuating inequality over the long run. This finding underscores the importance of understanding the role of economic institutions in shaping societal disparities.
