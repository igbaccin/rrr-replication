The examination of the impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development is an essential intellectual pursuit, as this era signified a transformative juncture for numerous societies. A contentious debate among scholars persists regarding the overall outcome of these policies, with some positing that they fostered economic growth and modernization, while others assert that they led to economic decline and hardship.

One stream of scholarship refines the scope and mechanism of the topic's description. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) propose that institutional changes stemming from European colonialism account for a substantial portion of current income disparities, lending support to the notion that institutions played a crucial role in economic growth and industrialization among formerly impoverished colonies. Their work does not focus explicitly on Soviet central planning; instead, it discusses it within the broader context of institutional changes across various societies.

Similarly, Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) contend that the relationship between economic growth and equality is intricate, with evidence from long-term national experiences indicating that growth and increased income parity can coexist over time. While they do not directly address Soviet central planning, their insights contribute to a broader understanding of economic growth and inequality dynamics.

Another stream of scholarship engages the topic only indirectly by addressing a related but distinct question. Komlos (1998, p.16) asserts that the absolute income gain during the Industrial Revolution was insufficient to offset declines in health among common people, resulting in a decrease in their biological well-being compared to previous generations. Although not specifically focused on Soviet central planning, his work sheds light on the potential human costs associated with forced industrialization and economic development.

The remaining sources in the literature review align with the topic's description and offer corroborating evidence for the same pattern. Bryant (2006) argues that social trajectories must be individually tracked, implying that the revisionist position, which suggests all Eurasian societies were following a shared modernizing course, is empirically questionable and analytically incoherent. This work implies that the Soviet experience of forced industrialization under Stalin was unique and distinct from other societies' development trajectories.

Williamson (1996, p.24) posits that globalization and convergence in the 19th century had predictable economic repercussions, leading to political alliances and policies designed to shield those adversely affected. His work suggests that the shift to deglobalization and divergence, which occurred under Soviet central planning, had substantial economic implications.

The papers in this cluster provide insights into the complexities of economic growth, highlighting the role of technological advancements, institutional adjustments, and social factors. Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Abramovitz (1986, p.12), in particular, offer valuable perspectives on these matters.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that for economic growth, a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries. He offers the example of highly forced industrialization under Communist auspices as an illustration of the social invention and innovation required for such growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) builds on this by suggesting that the catch-up process of productivity among nations is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and changes within the convergence process itself, as well as social and political concomitants of wealth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.12) posits that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied, as well as in embodied, technology.

Moreover, Abramovitz (1986, p.12) suggests that if countries at relatively low levels of industrialization contain large numbers of redundant workers in farming and petty trade, as is normally the case, there is also an opportunity for productivity growth by improving the allocation of labor. This idea aligns with Kuznets's (1973, p.10) emphasis on institutional and ideological adjustments as key factors for economic growth, particularly in less developed countries.

The papers in this cluster suggest that European colonial institutions significantly influence current income levels, a finding that aligns with the broader understanding that political and social contexts can shape economic development trajectories (Bryant 2006, p.17; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that the recipients of colonial profits varied, with the Portuguese state, Belgian King Leopold, and British private enterprises obtaining concessions or monopoly trading rights in Africa (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.7).

In support of this claim, Bryant (2006, p.17) argues that the revisionist position, which suggests all Eurasian societies were progressing along a shared modernizing course, is empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. He posits that social trajectories must be tracked from advanced and prosperous regions, such as the Yangzi delta and coastal areas active in sea-borne commerce, to avoid misleading portraits based on aggregate national estimates (Bryant 2006, p.17). This focus on local trends and potentials for wider development resonates with Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.2) argument that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Furthermore, Bryant (2006, p.17) raises concerns about the reliability of data used to support claims of massive growth in late Imperial China's economy. He questions the representativeness of population dynamics in an imperial lineage compared to the vast rural population (Bryant 2006, p.22).

The role of institutions in determining economic growth is a crucial factor in understanding the success or failure of industrialization efforts, including those under Stalin (Austin 2008, p.23; North 1989, p.10). North and Allen (1989) argue that political and economic institutions play a significant role in shaping economic development, contrasting neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary determinants.

Evidence from North's paper suggests that centralized planning under Stalin involved minute regulation of the economy through guild-like structures, which imposed price ceilings on grain and controlled external trade (North 1989, p.10). This extensive regulation aimed to manage the empire's resources but often led to financial instability due to military costs outstripping revenues.

He notes that even when colonial rulers had no interest in promoting long-term industrialization in the colonies, profits from trade could still contribute to institutional changes that protected property rights (Austin 2008, p.20). This connection between trade profits and institutional development underscores the importance of considering the economic context when examining the role of institutions in shaping growth.

Furthermore, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.23). This qualification suggests that a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms linking institutions to economic development is necessary for accurate analysis.

(2011, p.12) and Peters (2004, p.30) confirm that real wages in China were significantly lower than those in leading economies in northwestern Europe, a finding consistent with the topic's assertion. Allen et al. argue that in the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, real wages in China stagnated and were significantly lower than those in leading economies in northwestern Europe (Allen et al. 2011, p.12; Allen et al. 2011, p.1). On the other hand, Peters contends that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa mask processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation (Peters 2004, p.30). This qualification suggests that the mechanisms linking property rights to economic development may vary across regions.

Allen et al.'s findings indicate that the industrial revolution raised British wages above Dutch levels, a trend that intensified after 1870 (Allen et al. 2011, p.12). In contrast, real wages stagnated in China in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and rose slowly in the late nineteenth and early twentieth, with little cumulative change for 200 years (Allen et al. 2011, p.1). This income disparity between Europe and China persisted into the early twentieth century.

Peters' work sheds light on how property rights can be manipulated to reinforce existing power structures. He discusses instances of land grabbing and disputes in Mozambique, where high-ranking government officials were complicit in taking land from smallholder farmers (Peters 2004, p.30). This example highlights the potential for property rights to exacerbate social inequality when not protected by robust institutions.

The implication is that the mechanisms linking property rights and economic development may vary across regions, with some contexts experiencing stagnation or even regression in real wages, as observed in China, while others witness the consolidation of power through manipulation of property rights, as demonstrated in Mozambique. Understanding these nuances is crucial for a comprehensive analysis of the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

The papers of Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) and Temin (2002, p.8) challenge the common assumption that economic growth and increased income equality are incompatible, suggesting instead that they can coexist over time. This finding has significant implications for development policies aimed at promoting both economic growth and social equity.

Accelerating rates of capital accumulation associated with early industrialization fail to generate rising real wages among the unskilled until the surplus labor pool is exhausted. This turning point can be delayed for some time if demographic forces are right and Malthusian population pressures or immigration continually replenish the initial pool (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14).

Temin (2002, p.8) supports this argument by examining the rapid economic growth in Western Europe during the Golden Age after World War II. He attributes this growth to a developmental deficit and disequilibrium that ended in the 1970s when this deficit was eliminated. In particular, Temin highlights Germany's industrialization, which started later than Britain's, and its protection of farmers against low-priced American grain in the late nineteenth century (Temin 2002, p.8).

This downstream implication underscores the importance of addressing developmental deficits and demographic factors to foster economic growth that is both robust and equitable. Understanding these mechanisms can inform policies aimed at promoting economic progress while minimizing social inequality.

The papers of Komlos and Feinstein provide compelling evidence that the Industrial Revolution led to declines in health among common people due to a downward pressure on their nutritional status (Komlos 1998, p.16; Feinstein 1998, p.2). Komlos (1998, p.16) argues that absolute income gains during this period were insufficient to offset these declines, resulting in a decrease in the biological well-being of ordinary individuals compared to prior generations (Komlos 1998, p.16). This downward pressure on nutritional status was intensified by the increased division of labor and market integration, which magnified its impact (Komlos 1998, p.12).

He provides estimates to demonstrate that improvements in the working class's earnings and standard of living were not as minimal as previously thought (Feinstein 1998, p.2). However, these improvements did not necessarily translate into better health outcomes, as evidenced by Komlos's findings (Komlos 1998, p.16).

This downward pressure on nutritional status during the Industrial Revolution underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between economic growth and public health. As such, it highlights the need for policies that address both developmental deficits and demographic factors to foster economic growth that is both robust and equitable, while minimizing social inequality. Understanding these mechanisms can inform policies aimed at promoting economic progress while ensuring the well-being of all citizens.

The papers in this cluster provide evidence supporting the argument that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, challenging views of some world historians who suggest it began later (Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.11); Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3)). This assertion hinges on the comparison of unskilled laborers' wages in Britain and India, with the latter found to be significantly lower (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11).

The downward pressure on Indian workers' wages underscores the early emergence of the Great Divergence. This finding is further bolstered by evidence that India's per capita GDP declined steadily from 1600 to 1871, falling behind Britain's growth during the same period (Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3)). The origins of this divergence are rooted in the early modern period, with economic leaders maintaining real wages while most European cities experienced a decline (Allen 2001, p.3).

One specific mechanism that contributes to this pattern is the impact of colonialism on India's economy. This deindustrialization further exacerbated the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia.

Moreover, the service sector in India did not grow at a pace commensurate with population growth during this period (Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9)), which also contributed to the divergence. The underdevelopment of the service sector in India relative to Europe is an important downstream implication of the colonial policies that favored British imports over Indian goods.

Humphries's (2013, p.18) study challenges the traditional understanding of the British Industrial Revolution by emphasizing the role of female and child labor. The availability of these workers, often overlooked in historical analyses, played a crucial part in facilitating the growth of new industries and sustaining traditional ones. De Vries (1994, p.5) similarly argues that the Industrial Revolution led to a shift in household economies from permeability to porosity, potentially fostering new forms of demand and reducing gender-based division of labor.

Humphries (2013, p.18) contends that the High Wage Economy (HWE) paradigm, which focuses on men and high wages, overlooks the extensive and intensive use of child workers during the Industrial Revolution. He suggests that British households were precociously nuclear, with higher fertility rates compared to their European counterparts, leading to a larger pool of potential laborers, particularly women and children (Humphries 2013, p.18) and (Humphries 2013, p.19)).

This transition may have resulted from increased commercial interaction between cities, enabling regional economic development and industrial growth (de Vries 1994, p.5). The growing urban network facilitated by this shift could potentially provide new forms of demand and further reduce the gender-based division of labor (de Vries 1994, p.8).

Simson's (2019) analysis challenges the widespread assumption that post-independence budget policies in Africa were inherently unproductive and prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Contrasting the rapid industrialization under Stalin in the Soviet Union, Simson argues that Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda's post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally unproductive or biased towards patronage networks.

Simson (2019, p.26) offers compelling evidence to support this claim. He notes a large literature that has criticized African nationalization policies of the 1960s and 1970s, mismanaged industrial policy, and excessive borrowing used to finance it (Simson 2019, p.26). Simson posits that there may be much to learn from failed industrialization efforts in the context of a global interest in state-led development, without assuming they were inherently unproductive.

Furthermore, Simson (2019, p.26) provides evidence that challenges the assumption of excessive public sector employment in Africa. He presents data showing that public sector employment as a percentage of the labor force in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda during the 1940s to 2010 was not significantly higher than in other developing countries (Simson 2019, p.13). This suggests that the common perception of Africa's budget policies being excessively focused on public employment may be overstated.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.10) study on the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains provides compelling evidence that supports the idea that significant agricultural development can be achieved through strategic infrastructure implementation, much like the impact of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. The paper argues that the introduction and near-universal adoption of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural output by reducing the costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.10).

Private enclosure of common lands in England may have contributed to the onset of the Industrial Revolution due to its impact on both agricultural output and labor supply (Ashton 1997), providing a historical parallel. Returning to Coase's setting, Hornbeck (2010, p.2) examines the impact on agricultural development of a decrease in the cost of protecting farmland: the introduction of barbed wire fencing to the American Plains in the late nineteenth century. Fences protected farmers' land and crops from damage by others' cattle, effectively increasing productivity and security.

The use of barbed wire not only improved agricultural output but also had downstream implications for labor allocation. As farmland became more secure, farmers could invest more resources into farming, reducing the need for agricultural laborers (Hornbeck 2010, p.10). This shift in labor supply is reminiscent of the labor reallocation observed during the Industrial Revolution, where increased agricultural productivity allowed workers to move towards manufacturing jobs (Komlos 1998, p.16).

The work of Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) challenges a prevailing assumption in the literature that historical African poverty was due to structural growth impediments. Instead, they argue that real wages in Africa were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time. This claim is supported by evidence showing that average annual growth rates in Accra between 1900 and 1960 were comparable to those in nineteenth-century London (Frankema & van Waijenburg 2012, p.18).

Moreover, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) suggest that the integration of labor markets in the Indian Ocean under British rule was a result of chronic labor shortages faced by colonial governments and widespread resistance among local Africans to work for wages (p.27). This finding aligns with the observed shift in labor supply during the Industrial Revolution, where increased agricultural productivity allowed workers to move towards manufacturing jobs (Komlos 1998, p.16).

It's important to acknowledge that while real wages in Africa may have been above subsistence level, they were still impacted by very low starting points. This observation underscores the ongoing challenges faced by African economies in their quest for sustained growth and development (Frankema & van Waijenburg 2012, p.18).

As Williamson suggests, globalization, being the "handmaiden" of industrialization, was instrumental in shaping economic trends during this period.

In line with this, factor-saving productivity advance accounted for almost half of the trends in the ratio of farm rents to wages (Williamson 1996, p.24). This finding underscores the potential for increased productivity in industrial sectors to drive economic change, a mechanism that was also at play during Stalin's forced industrialization.

This observation aligns with the protectionist policies implemented by the Soviet regime during its period of rapid industrialization, as a means of shielding domestic industries from foreign competition.

However, it is important to note that while globalization led to convergence in some aspects, it did not necessarily lead to uniform economic growth. As Williamson points out, whether these powerful forces influenced more generally economy-wide real wage and labor productivity convergence has yet to be determined (Williamson 1996, p.24). This observation underscores the ongoing debate about the extent to which Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin led to sustained economic growth.

The institutional changes brought about by European powers have created barriers to mobility and opportunity, perpetuating the economic disparities that existed prior to colonialism. This finding underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of the factors contributing to income levels in former European colonies, as well as the importance of addressing the legacy of colonialism in order to promote sustainable economic development in these regions.
