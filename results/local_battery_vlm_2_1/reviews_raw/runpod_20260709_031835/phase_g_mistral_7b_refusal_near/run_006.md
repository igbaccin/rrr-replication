The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development is a topic that has elicited diverse scholarly perspectives, with significant disagreements surrounding its implications for growth and development in the post-Stalin era. At stake is an understanding of the role institutional changes played in shaping the trajectory of economic progress within the former Soviet Union and its satellite states.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former colonies, providing support for the view that institutions played an essential role in economic growth and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Simultaneously, Bryant (2006, p.17) challenges the revisionist position, which suggests all Eurasian societies were progressing along a shared modernizing course, arguing that social trajectories must be tracked from their unique historical contexts (Bryant 2006, p.17). These contrasting viewpoints highlight the need to examine the specific institutional changes under Stalin's regime and their impact on economic development.

Komlos (1998, p.16) sheds light on the relationship between industrialization, nutritional status, and population growth during the early stages of modern economic growth (Komlos 1998, p.12). Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) argue that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex, suggesting that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). These findings support the notion that forced industrialization under Stalin might have had a complex impact on population dynamics and income distribution.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) emphasizes the need for advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data for economic growth, particularly for less developed countries (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Simson's (2019, p.26) work on post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda challenges the common assumption that these policies were unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending (Simson 2019, p.26). Together, these sources suggest that understanding the impact of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization requires examining not only the institutional changes implemented under Stalin but also their long-term consequences for technological advancement, population dynamics, and income distribution.

The decline in India's economic status was not limited to the industrial sector. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9) propose that service sector output growth moved more closely in line with urban population growth, suggesting a broader economic slowdown in India compared to Europe. This pattern of declining productivity and growth in India contrasts sharply with the rapid industrialization and expansion taking place in Britain during the same period.

The mechanism underlying this divergence is rooted in the differing institutional structures between Europe and Asia. In contrast, India's political and economic structure was characterized by weak property rights, inefficient administration, and a lack of incentives for innovation and investment (Broadberry et al., 2015).

The implications of this divergence were far-reaching. By the late 18th century, Britain had surpassed India as the world's leading industrial power, marking a significant shift in global economic dynamics. The conditions created by this shift would have lasting impacts on the balance of power between Europe and Asia, setting the stage for the Industrial Revolution and the subsequent rise of Western economies.

The interplay between economic growth and health outcomes during industrialization is a significant aspect of understanding broader development patterns. Komlos (1998, p.16) argues that while the Industrial Revolution led to some economic advancement, it did not significantly improve the health of common people due to factors such as poor living conditions and inadequate nutrition (Komlos 1998, p.16). This assertion finds support in Allen et al.'s work (2011), which reveals that real wages in China stagnated during the same period, being substantially lower than those in leading economies in northwestern Europe (Allen et al. 2011, p.12).

The author suggests that integration into a larger world market exacerbated these issues, as previously remote regions faced increased pressure to sell their products. Allen et al. (2011, p.12) demonstrate that real wages in China remained stagnant throughout the eighteenth and early twentieth centuries, indicating a lack of economic improvement comparable to that seen in Europe during the Industrial Revolution (Allen et al., 2011, p.1).

The downstream implication of this combined evidence is that industrialization may lead to economic growth but not necessarily to improved health outcomes for common people. This finding underscores the importance of considering both economic and social factors when examining the impact of industrialization on development patterns, as suggested by Komlos (1998, p.16). It also highlights the need for further research into the relationship between economic development, living conditions, and nutritional status during periods of forced industrialization, such as in the Soviet Union under Stalin's rule.

This emphasis on the need for more research underscores the ongoing significance of understanding the impact of institutions and economic systems on health outcomes and development patterns.

Hornbeck's (2010) study on the impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains offers compelling insights that resonate with the effects of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. Both instances significantly influenced their respective regions' economic growth by increasing productivity and security.

Hornbeck argues that the introduction and near-universal adoption of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains led to a significant boost in agricultural development due to reduced costs of land protection and enhanced property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.2). Similarly, the Soviet regime's forced industrialization under Stalin aimed at securing resources, increasing productivity, and enforcing central control over agriculture and industry (North 1989, p.10).

In Hornbeck's study, private enclosure of common lands in England may have contributed to the onset of the Industrial Revolution by increasing agricultural output and labor supplied to other sectors (Ashton 1997). This assumption appears plausible for the American Plains as well, where barbed wire fences protected farmers' land and crops from damage by others' cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.10). In a similar vein, the Soviet regime's collectivization of agriculture aimed at increasing productivity and central control over agricultural resources (North 1989, p.10).

One specific mechanism that emerges from these sources is the impact of improved property rights on economic development. The literature on productivity catch-up among nations suggests a complex process influenced by both exogenous factors and changes within the convergence process itself, as well as social and political concomitants of wealth (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Abramovitz's work highlights the early industrialization stage's impact on productivity growth, with farming still playing a significant role in many countries, masking the impress of the catch-up process (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

Simultaneously, Abramovitz underscores the opportunity for modernization through disembodied and embodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The Industrial Revolution brought about significant changes in household economies, leading to a shift from permeability to porosity and potentially fostering new forms of demand and reducing gender-based division of labor (de Vries 1994, p.5). De Vries's work, supported by Lindert and Williamson (2002), argues that this transition was marked by the emergence of a more permeable household structure, where members were no longer confined to specific roles based on gender or age.

This shift in household economies had far-reaching implications, as it allowed for greater flexibility in labor allocation. In support of this claim, Lindert and Williamson (2002) demonstrate how the Industrial Revolution led to a decrease in household self-sufficiency, making households more reliant on the market for goods and services.

Moreover, this shift in household economies also had an impact on gender roles. This is further supported by Lindert and Williamson (2002), who argue that as markets became more integrated into household economies, women's roles expanded beyond traditional domestic tasks to include wage labor.

The papers of Allen (2001, p.9) provide compelling evidence that the divergence in real incomes between European cities was largely established from as early as 1500 to 1750, laying the groundwork for the industrialization gap observed in the mid-nineteenth century. This economic disparity can be attributed to the differences in institutional structures and economic conditions that emerged during this period.

The cost function interpretation of the coefﬁcient of the price of grain, which is of the right order of magnitude according to Allen (2001, p.9), suggests that these differences were established well before the onset of industrialization. Moreover, Allen (2001, p.3) demonstrates that real wage changes were minimal in the Netherlands and Belgium but significant elsewhere on the continent, resulting in a substantial gap in real wages between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent at the beginning of the Industrial Revolution.

These economic differences set the stage for the industrialization process, as cities with higher real wages were more likely to invest in technology and infrastructure that would drive industrial growth. The cost function interpretation also implies that the price of grain played a crucial role in determining wage levels, suggesting that agricultural productivity was a key factor in shaping the economic landscape during this period.

Furthermore, Goldin (2006, p.1) highlights the role of innovation networks, which were more developed in cities with higher real wages, as a catalyst for industrialization.

This capital accumulation facilitated by slave trading contrasts with the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which emphasizes the role of European institutions in fostering economic growth.

Furthermore, Austin (2008, p.23) contends that colonial rulers often had no interest in sacrificing current consumption for long-term industrialization in the colonies, as such a move could threaten metropolitan jobs (Austin 2008, p.20). This reluctance to invest in industrialization explains, at least in part, the failure of many post-colonial pursuit of industrialization.

In qualifying this pattern, Acemoglu et al. (2002c) observe that proﬁts from colonialism and slavery were 'associated' with the profits from Atlantic trade (Austin 2008, p.23).
