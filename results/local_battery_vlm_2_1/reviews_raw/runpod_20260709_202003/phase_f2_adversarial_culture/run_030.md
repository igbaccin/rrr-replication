The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding how societies develop and sustain economic progress over extended periods. The crux of this debate revolves around whether cultural factors inherently shape the institutional frameworks that drive economic growth or if other variables such as geographic conditions, mortality rates, or initial factor endowments predominantly influence these institutions (Author Year, p.N). Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values fundamentally alter the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering long-term growth. Others posit that cultural factors are secondary to more immediate determinants like extractive institutions shaped by European settlement patterns. This disagreement highlights the need for a nuanced examination of how culture interacts with institutional frameworks to influence economic outcomes.

The debate over the role of culture in shaping institutions is exemplified by differing views on the impact of cultural beliefs and values on economic performance. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are better explained by core economic institutions such as serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. She criticizes exogenous cultural theories that suggest Catholic beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules within guilds, leading to economic growth. Putnam's theory of social capital posits that societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks are characterized by 'civic' cultures (Author Year, p.N). These cultures foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability. For instance, Putnam contends that the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times can be attributed to cultural beliefs and values associated with strong guilds and communes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Therefore, cultural factors play a significant role in shaping institutions and influencing long-term economic growth.

Cultural norms also affect the development of trust within societies. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This finding underscores how historical cultural factors can have long-lasting effects on economic institutions. The persistence of these cultural norms highlights their role in shaping contemporary economic outcomes, reinforcing the idea that culture is a critical factor in institutional development and economic growth.

Other scholars argue that geographic conditions and mortality rates significantly influence institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. contend that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, suggesting that geographic factors played a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks. This perspective challenges the primacy of cultural factors, instead emphasizing the importance of exogenous variables like mortality rates in determining institutional development.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the role of initial factor endowments. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective suggests that cultural factors are secondary to more immediate determinants like initial factor endowments in shaping institutional frameworks. The differential paths of development in Latin America highlight how variations in conditions relevant to growth such as property rights, corruption, and social capital can be traced back to differences in institutions which in turn are influenced by initial factor endowments (Author Year, p.N).

Institutional frameworks also play a crucial role in fostering economic growth through secure property rights. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because the neoclassical model holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs (North 1989, p.1; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). He contends that positive transaction costs necessitate the existence of institutions, which are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Therefore, secure property rights are essential for economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective aligns with cultural explanations of economic development that emphasize the role of values or beliefs in shaping economic outcomes.

These institutional changes reflected enhanced security in property rights, facilitating long-term capital markets. This fostered a stable market for public debt, which provided a positive externality for private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24) an effect that North and Weingast highlight as crucial for England's economic success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutions governing public choice played a necessary role in making possible both economic growth and political freedom, suggesting that cultural factors alone cannot account for the observed economic development.

The implications of these institutional changes were substantial: they enabled sustained economic growth and political stability. It is essential to recognize that the effectiveness of these institutions was contingent on broader historical contexts including the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years’ War (North 1989, p.9). This contextual dependence underscores the complexity of economic development, where institutions interact with historical events to shape long-term outcomes.

These scholars contend that the institutions which emerged from these societies were heavily influenced by the distribution of power, wealth, and resources among different groups. The primary mechanism through which this occurred was via elite capture of government policies, which restricted competition and entrenched inequality. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe as well, where institutions such as guilds and serfdom were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. Ogilvie acknowledges that these institutions are part of a larger framework and that studying them in isolation can be misleading (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Thus, the implications of distributional conflicts extend beyond specific institutions to encompass entire institutional frameworks, which in turn shape long-term economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that economic growth is driven by ideas and ideologies which foster the expansion of useful knowledge. This perspective posits that the cultural shifts in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, marked by a burgeoning rhetoric of liberty and innovation, were crucial in stimulating economic change. For example, McCloskey describes how new rhetorical frameworks in countries like Britain and France reshaped societal attitudes towards commerce and entrepreneurship. Mokyr's work echoes this emphasis on ideas, highlighting that the diffusion of useful knowledge across Europe was facilitated by broader cultural currents. In particular, Mokyr notes that tacit knowledge, skills and expertise passed through personal interactions, was a vital component in the transmission of economic know-how (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge spread not just through written texts but also through apprenticeships, conferences, and other forms of direct exchange. Mokyr acknowledges that this diffusion was uneven some regions were more receptive to new ideas due to their existing cultural predispositions (Mokyr 2005, p.56) such as tolerance for innovation or openness to new methods. Thus, while the spread of useful knowledge is a critical driver of economic growth, its impact is contingent on the prevailing cultural attitudes within different societies.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident by 1800. This divergence is attributed to differences in urban development and institutional changes. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta point out that while north-western Europe experienced significant investment in agriculture due to institutional change, this did not lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices because labor moved into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This shift in labor allocation indicates a deeper structural change that facilitated economic growth. Pamuk shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5), suggesting that this earlier divergence was influenced by historical events that reshaped labor markets and technological innovations. These changes highlight how exogenous shocks can catalyze economic transformations by altering labor dynamics and fostering innovation. This perspective on structural changes must be contextualized within the broader cultural framework of societies as these shifts are often contingent on prevailing attitudes towards innovation and adaptation to new economic realities (Author Year, p.N).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that European mortality rates in colonies significantly influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn shaped current income per capita. This dynamic is underpinned by a strong correlation between settler mortality and institutional quality, both historically and contemporaneously. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this by noting that these extractive institutions are often maintained by politically powerful groups who benefit from the status quo, even if it stifles growth. This pattern suggests that colonial legacies can lock societies into institutional frameworks that prioritize short-term gains over sustainable economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that these institutions are not static; they can evolve, but this evolution is often slow and contingent on significant political or economic shifts.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust that has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) provide evidence that this cultural legacy is not merely a historical artifact it continues to influence contemporary social dynamics. The slave trade disrupted traditional political structures, leading to long-term political instability and weakened states; this instability further eroded trust within societies (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) elaborates on this by noting that the fragmentation of states due to the slave trades has had lasting effects on economic development. This cultural legacy of mistrust can be seen as a fundamental antecedent that shapes institutions and economic outcomes in Africa today.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, as it influences both the formation and persistence of economic and political structures. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can significantly impact economic institutions, with Putnam's theory of social capital illustrating how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that enhance societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon, who demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect economic performance today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of mistrust, rooted in historical disruptions, underscores how cultural factors can shape institutions over generations. This enduring impact highlights the deep-seated influence of culture on institutional development and economic outcomes.

The evolution of secure property rights, a critical component of economic growth, is also deeply intertwined with cultural and institutional frameworks. North and Weingast show that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, which included the creation of the Bank of England, was instrumental in securing property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This development was intrinsically linked to the broader cultural and institutional context. North further emphasizes that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This reduction in transaction costs is a direct outcome of the secure property rights facilitated by robust institutional frameworks.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further evident in colonial contexts, where differences in mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. highlight that high mortality rates in colonies led to the development of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical perspective aligns with Ogilvie's argument that different frameworks of economic institutions can be interdependent and resist encroachment from external factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus multifaceted. While there is agreement on the significance of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions, the precise mechanisms through which this occurs remain a subject of debate. North and Weingast demonstrate that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), whereas Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions can persist due to historical factors such as mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of social capital and trust within societies, as highlighted by Ogilvie and Nunn and Wantchekon's work on how cultural norms influence economic performance.
