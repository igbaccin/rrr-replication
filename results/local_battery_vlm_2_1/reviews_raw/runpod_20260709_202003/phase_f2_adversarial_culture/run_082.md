The role of culture in shaping institutions and driving long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because the answer determines how societies can foster sustainable development. At one end, some researchers argue that cultural factors, such as mistrust and gender roles, significantly influence institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. On the other hand, others contend that institutional frameworks are primarily shaped by historical events and exogenous factors like European settler mortality rates. Still others propose that it is the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England which was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The live disagreement revolves around whether cultural elements directly shape institutions or if they are merely a secondary effect of broader historical forces, with some scholars arguing that culture can be an antecedent to institutional frameworks.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is evident in the impact of gender dynamics on economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight how colonial constructs codified gender roles, which had long-term consequences for production systems. This perspective is complemented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who emphasize that historical events like the slave trade have shaped cultural norms, including trust, which in turn influence economic behavior. These scholars argue that cultural factors actively shape institutional frameworks and economic outcomes (Author Year, p.N). The significance of culture is further underscored by North's observation (North 1989, p.1) that institutions matter in economic growth because they address transaction costs and secure property rights. This suggests that cultural norms can influence the effectiveness of institutions in managing these critical aspects, thereby affecting long-run growth.

The persistence of institutions over time is a crucial factor in understanding their role in economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate how differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This highlights that culture can be an antecedent to institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.25) adds that Africans' responses to their environments, including the adoption of various technologies, ultimately changed economic conditions. These findings suggest that cultural adaptations can lead to long-term changes in institutions and economic outcomes. The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by North's argument (North 1989, p.9) that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth. This implies that cultural factors influencing the security of property rights can have profound effects on economic development over time.

The evolution of public choice institutions provides additional insights into how culture shapes long-run growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Together, these scholars suggest that cultural factors influencing public choice can significantly impact institutional frameworks and long-run economic growth. This underscores how cultural adaptations can lead to institutional changes that foster economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that women's roles in economic growth are profoundly shaped by cultural norms and social dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). In pre-colonial Africa, women were integral to community reproduction and wealth transmission. For instance, among the matrilineal Akan societies, women's bloodlines defined the inheritance of office and wealth, making them indispensable to state formation and economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic highlights how cultural norms can empower women economically, even in patriarchal structures. In the U.S., Goldin (2006, p.8) identifies a quiet revolution that transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics. She notes several exogenous changes that facilitated this transformation, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts align with Akyeampong and Fofack’s observations about the impact of cultural norms on economic roles, suggesting that changes in social expectations and institutional support can significantly alter women's economic participation. Goldin also points out that these changes involved preconditions such as shifts in college majors and professional school enrollment (Goldin 2006, p.8). These factors underscore how educational opportunities and occupational changes can act as catalysts for broader cultural shifts that enhance women's economic roles. This interplay between cultural norms, social dynamics, and economic growth underscores the complex role of gender relations in long-run development.

The slave trade's legacy extends beyond immediate economic impacts, embedding cultural attitudes that persist over generations. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today as a result of historical events and has significantly impacted economic outcomes. The slave trades caused significant political instability, leading to weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a persistent barrier to economic development that manifests in contemporary attitudes toward institutions and governance. Nunn (2008, p.27) suggests that the slave trade's impact on state centralization is evident in Africa’s current underdevelopment, where historical political instability has left lasting marks on institutional quality (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn and Wantchekon also show that colonial boundaries mirror contemporary ones, implying that the effects of the slave trade are deeply embedded within national contexts. These findings underscore how historical traumas can create enduring cultural attitudes that hinder economic progress, a pattern seen in other regions where similar historical events have led to entrenched mistrust and underdevelopment.

The financial revolution in England exemplifies how institutions can create positive externalities that support broader economic development. The establishment of the Bank of England and the subsequent reduction in transaction costs illustrate a mechanism through which secure property rights can be achieved, fostering an environment conducive to economic growth. North (1989, p.1) demonstrates that even if other forces would have eventually led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without these institutions.

Colonialism brought institutional changes that fundamentally shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in these regions. High mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists; low mortality facilitated the establishment of more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences are directly linked to current income per capita, illustrating how colonial-era policies continue to affect economic outcomes. The persistence of these extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gupta (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) both share the premise that institutional variables are key to understanding development in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that the new economic history of Africa posits that current institutions in Africa are a product of historical processes extending beyond colonial rule, implying that these institutions are deeply rooted and resistant to change (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores the importance of considering precolonial histories when analyzing contemporary African economies. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further elaborates on the role of indigenous institutions, noting that contemporary African institutions are more likely to promote growth if they align with precolonial systems. This congruence enhances government legitimacy and fosters development-inducing institutions, whereas low levels of congruence can lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that the effectiveness of institutional reforms in Africa depends heavily on their compatibility with existing cultural and historical frameworks.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4; 2000, p.2) examine how different historical paths and institutional frameworks have influenced economic development. They show that the initial factor endowments of New World societies shaped the institutions that evolved, leading to persistent inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This dynamic is evident in the differentials in paths of development across Latin America, where conventional economic factors alone cannot fully explain the variation in growth. The focus on institutions highlights how the security of property rights, prevalence of corruption, financial sector structures, investment in public infrastructure and social capital, and entrepreneurial inclination were shaped by these initial conditions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). In this context, the evolution of institutions in response to historical paths and initial conditions is a critical factor in understanding long-run growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth through various mechanisms. Institutions evolve over time and are influenced by historical paths and initial conditions, which are themselves shaped by cultural factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This dynamic interplay is evident in the development of extractive versus inclusive institutions, where cultural responses to environmental challenges significantly alter institutional trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how culture can foster secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change provided a large positive externality for the development of both public and private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24), highlighting how cultural shifts can drive institutional reforms that support long-run growth.

Cultural factors also influence the gender dynamics within economic systems, which in turn affect growth. African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The matrilineal society of Asante, for instance, shows how cultural norms can integrate women into the economic fabric, ensuring community reproduction and wealth transmission (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. Culture shapes responses to environmental challenges, drives institutional reforms, and influences gender dynamics within economies. The precise remaining disagreement concerns how specific cultural norms interact with institutional frameworks to foster or hinder economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This question of the interaction between culture and institutions shows that the persistence of extractive institutions is driven by a complex interplay of historical paths and initial conditions (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), which can be settled through examining the historical evidence of how financial revolutions in England fostered secure property rights.
