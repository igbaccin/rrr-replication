The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether cultural beliefs and values are fundamental to the establishment of institutions that foster economic development over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around the extent to which culture directly influences institutional frameworks, with some arguing that culture's impact on economic growth is mediated through its effect on institutions. Others posit that culture itself is a less significant determinant compared to other factors such as geographic conditions or exogenous shocks. The interplay between cultural values and institutional development remains unresolved: are cultural beliefs the driving force behind institutions that promote long-run growth, or do they operate alongside other factors in shaping economic outcomes? This debate underscores the broader question of how societies evolve economically over time.

The stream addressing a related but distinct question engages the topic obliquely. Ogilvie's analysis focuses on distributional conflicts as an explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, arguing that these institutions were shaped by power dynamics and efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective contrasts with cultural explanations that emphasize the role of beliefs and values in shaping institutional frameworks. For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital posits that civic cultures characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This highlights a tension between explanations that emphasize the role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions and those that focus on distributional conflicts as the primary driver. Ogilvie's critique of exogenous cultural theories suggests that cultural factors may not be as influential as often assumed, while Putnam's theory emphasizes the importance of social capital in fostering economic growth. This tension reflects a broader debate over whether cultural beliefs are a fundamental cause of long-run growth or merely one factor among many.

A stream proposing rival causes that do not operate through culture offers competing explanations for institutional development and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective contrasts with cultural explanations by emphasizing geographic factors as a primary determinant of institutional development. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the complexity of historical paths and the limitations of simplistic causal explanations (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.9). The focus on mortality rates and geographic conditions suggests that culture may play a secondary role in shaping institutions compared to these exogenous factors. For example, Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows a high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the establishment of early measures of institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This indicates that geographic factors can have a direct impact on institutional development independent of cultural beliefs.

A stream identifying antecedents that flow into culture as a trigger complements the topic's claim. North emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that transaction costs and secure property rights are crucial for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with cultural explanations by highlighting the role of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England shows how institutions governing public choice were critical for securing property rights and promoting economic growth, underscoring the importance of stable institutional frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This stream suggests that cultural beliefs may operate alongside other factors in shaping institutions, being the sole determinant.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth remains an open question. While some scholars emphasize the importance of cultural beliefs in shaping institutional frameworks, others point to geographic factors or distributional conflicts as primary determinants. The interplay between these factors highlights the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of how culture interacts with other variables to shape institutions over time.

These institutions were shaped by early settlers' need to maximize their rents and promote long-run growth. For example, Acemoglu et al. show that high mortality rates in colonies led to the creation of extractive institutions, which had a lasting impact on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is evidenced by the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, indicating that institutional quality was directly influenced by colonial settlement patterns. One downstream implication of this mechanism is that the persistence of these extractive institutions can hinder long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. also note that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification highlights that while institutions are crucial, their impact on growth depends on whether they were designed to maximize rents or promote development.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in England during the seventeenth century were pivotal for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This institutional framework enabled economic growth by providing a stable market for both public and private debt. North and Weingast show how the financial revolution, marked by the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England, played a critical role in England's long-run success. This mechanism highlights how secure property rights can foster economic growth by encouraging investment and reducing uncertainty. Hornbeck supports this view by illustrating how the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development. This pattern is not universal; social norms can also play a role in protecting property rights, as seen in Ellickson's analysis of a modern California county where ranchers control cattle based on social expectations and strict legal responsibilities.

McCloskey and Mokyr both argue that ideas and ideologies were central to economic growth (McCloskey 2015; Mokyr 2005). They contend that the Great Enrichment was driven by shifts in rhetoric and the growth of useful knowledge. One specific mechanism through which this occurred is the dissemination of tacit knowledge, which Mokyr describes as skills and know-how embedded in individuals and codified in texts. This form of knowledge moved freely across borders, facilitated by personal interactions and training and formal education (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Countries like the Netherlands embraced new rhetoric and circumstances, fostering an environment conducive to economic enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.10). This dynamic underscores how cultural attitudes can either facilitate or impede the diffusion of knowledge and ideas, influencing long-term economic outcomes. These findings suggest that culture plays a critical role in shaping institutions by determining which ideas gain traction and influence societal norms, thereby impacting economic growth.

These institutions were shaped by the interests of powerful elites who used them to maintain their status and wealth through any inherent efficiency. The craft guilds, merchant guilds, serfdom, and community structures in pre-industrial Europe exemplify this dynamic (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Elites often resisted changes that could disrupt their power, even if those changes promised economic growth. The African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of development where Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This highlights how institutions can be shaped by both exogenous and endogenous factors, with distributional conflicts playing a significant role in determining which paths societies take.

The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has been argued to have had a profound and lasting impact on Africa's economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon attribute this culture of mistrust to the historical experience of the transatlantic slave trade. They note that the slave trade directly affected population levels and fostered an environment where trust was systematically undermined, which has enduring implications for social capital and economic outcomes. This perspective complements other studies that have identified historical determinants of cultural norms. Nunn's work highlights how African states' political instability can be traced back to the slave trades, resulting in weakened state structures. The legacy of this institutional weakness is evident in contemporary Africa, where corruption persists as a significant barrier to development. Recent studies underscore that a country’s history of state development is crucial for current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn also acknowledges that while the slave trade played a pivotal role, it was not the sole factor influencing Africa's underdevelopment. Other historical and contemporary factors, such as colonialism and recent events, must be considered in a comprehensive analysis of Africa's economic trajectory.

This nuanced understanding of the slave trade's impact on African economic development underscores how exogenous shocks can have profound and enduring effects on institutional frameworks, shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The role of women in economic growth and development is significant but does not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Goldin 2006, p.8; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Goldin argues that shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career roles were pivotal in transforming women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. This transformation was driven by various exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). These shifts altered female labor supply and made part-time work more accessible, breaking down barriers to the employment of married women. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production in Africa (Goldin 2006, p.8; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Women's indispensable role in community reproduction in matrilineal societies like Asante underscores the complex interplay between cultural norms and economic contributions. This suggests that while women have been crucial to economic development, their role is often shaped by broader institutional frameworks influenced by culture.

The nuanced understanding of women's roles in different contexts highlights how cultural norms can shape institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

Culture plays an instrumental role in shaping institutions that govern long-term economic growth and development. The interdependency of cultural norms and institutional frameworks is evident in how societies organize their economies and distribute resources. For instance, Ogilvie demonstrates that the structure of economic institutions can be influenced by deeply rooted cultural beliefs about efficiency and collective action (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates was influenced by European cultural attitudes towards settlement and colonization (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore how cultural norms can shape institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

The role of culture in fostering secure property rights and economic growth is further illuminated by North. He argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England played a critical role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who highlight how the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks is evident in how these societies adapted their governance structures to facilitate economic development.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is settled by the empirical evidence from specific historical contexts. The shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) is grounded in the historical evidence that shows how cultural norms shaped institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which culture influences institutions remain a subject of ongoing debate. For example, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional frameworks and their impact on long-term growth in different cultural contexts.
