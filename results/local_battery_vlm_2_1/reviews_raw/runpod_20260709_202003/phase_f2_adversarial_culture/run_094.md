The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development studies. The debate centers around whether cultural factors can shape institutional frameworks that foster sustained economic progress. Some scholars contend that culture, through its influence on beliefs and values, can drive the creation of efficient institutions that facilitate long-term growth; this perspective posits that cultural norms and practices are integral to understanding how societies develop economically over extended periods. Others argue that institutions themselves, independent of cultural influences, are the primary determinants of economic outcomes, suggesting that institutional frameworks can be shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or historical events rather than by underlying cultural values. This disagreement highlights the need for a nuanced examination of the interplay between culture and institutions in driving long-term economic growth. For instance, North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants; however, this view does not explicitly address the role of cultural factors in shaping these institutions. Understanding whether culture fundamentally shapes institutions or if institutions operate independently is crucial for developing effective policies aimed at fostering long-term economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.”

North (1989, p.1) and North & Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that the institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for securing property rights. They jointly establish that the financial revolution of this period, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution fostered a stable market for both public and private debt, providing a positive externality for economic growth. The secure environment it created allowed individuals to engage in long-term investments without fearing expropriation.

North (1989, p.1) specifies that this institutional framework emerged from the specific historical context of England's fiscal crises and political developments (North 1989, p.9). The trade-off between granting certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues illustrates how institutions shaped the economic landscape by addressing transaction costs and securing property rights. North and Weingast further underscore that this institutional development was crucial for England's long-run success, suggesting that other forces might have led to similar outcomes but at a slower pace (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt and subsequently private debt highlights the tangible benefits of these institutions. This financial revolution supported economic growth and laid the groundwork for political freedom by creating a stable environment for long-term investments.

The evolution of property rights was influenced by broader social and political changes, including shifts in public attitudes towards risk and investment. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other societal forces in driving economic growth.

Distributional conflicts and inequality are more fundamental drivers of economic institutions than culture (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional struggles can shape institutions in ways that serve the interests of powerful groups. She challenges cultural explanations for pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, by demonstrating how these institutions emerged from power dynamics between social classes. They suggest that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutional frameworks that reinforced these disparities over time. The resulting institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, influenced long-run development trajectories by perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further highlights the interconnectedness of economic institutions within broader social frameworks, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. This insight underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and the need to consider how distributional conflicts shape entire frameworks, not just individual components.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies directly influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn shaped long-run economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Higher mortality rates deterred European settlement, leading to the implementation of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonizers. This institutional framework was a product of immediate colonial interests and persisted due to the power dynamics that maintained these structures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show how this process played out in former colonies, where extractive institutions impeded economic development and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implications are clear: institutional legacies from colonial times continue to influence current income disparities, suggesting that the roots of long-run growth are deeply entwined with historical institutional changes rather than cultural factors.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight divergent historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He shows that the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous version of what they call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25), which involves Africans’ own responses to their environments. They note that in the advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages, which conferred purchasing power over tradable goods and services, were substantially lower (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This suggests that differences in institutional structures and investment patterns played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories. The dynamic nature of these processes means that cultural factors may have influenced how institutions evolved and were adopted in different regions. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how historical paths and institutional evolutions interact with cultural factors to shape long-run growth.

The new economic history of Africa underscores the significance of historical institutional variables in shaping long-run growth and poverty alleviation (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that Africa's development can be understood through gradual institutional change shaped by precolonial and even prehistoric histories, suggesting that effective contemporary institutions align with indigenous ones. Congruence between modern and traditional institutions enhances government legitimacy and fosters growth-promoting policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous notions of uniform economic stagnation. The implication is that current institutional reforms must consider these deep historical roots to be effective. The specific mechanisms through which precolonial institutions interact with modern ones remain under-explored, highlighting the need for further research on how cultural factors mediate this relationship.

Pamuk (2007, p.24) and van Zanden (Pamuk 2007, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2) explore how structural changes in Europe were initiated by the Black Death. This event led to long-term economic developments including a wage gap between northwest Europe and other regions by 1450, due to increased labour scarcity and technological innovation. The decline in interest rates and skill premiums further contributed to this divergence (van Zanden 1999, p.6). These findings show that structural changes can be triggered by exogenous shocks such as pandemics, which subsequently alter economic trajectories over centuries. The role of culture in mediating these long-term effects remains unclear. This gap leaves open questions about whether similar shocks could lead to different outcomes depending on cultural contexts, suggesting a need for further research into the role of culture in long-term economic development.

The Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge are distinct phenomena that preceded and facilitated economic growth (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). De Vries (1994, p.18) posits that the Industrious Revolution involved increased labor input and new consumption patterns. This useful knowledge was codified and embodied in tacit forms such as skills and practical experience (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The transmission of this knowledge depended on personal interactions and training, highlighting the importance of social and cultural factors in disseminating economic advances.

However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) notes that while national differences existed within Western Europe, the diffusion of useful knowledge was rapid due to international communication networks (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Cultural contexts facilitated the spread of economic knowledge despite their diversity. The implication is that similar economic shocks could lead to varying outcomes depending on how effectively different cultures integrated and utilized new information.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) together with Goldin (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8) highlight that women have played pivotal roles in economic growth and development across various historical contexts. African women were indispensable to the reproduction of communities during pre-colonial and colonial periods, contributing significantly to both social and economic systems. In matrilineal societies like Asante, women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). These contributions were not limited to specific regions or time periods; in the U.S., a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics, challenging views that portray this change as merely evolutionary. For earnings relative to comparable men, a turning point occurred around 1980 (Goldin 2006, p.8). These transformations were driven by internal shifts and exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). This indicates that while cultural norms and social structures significantly influence women's economic roles, external factors can catalyze substantial changes in these dynamics.

Cultural beliefs and values significantly shape institutional frameworks that foster economic growth. Max Weber's insights highlight how exogenous cultural theories influence micro-level economic structures, such as guilds, by motivating craftsmen with beliefs about the afterlife (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of 'social capital' further illustrates how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, leading to institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. These cultural beliefs and values, along with their institutional manifestations like strong guilds and communes, explain superior economic performance in regions with high social capital (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework underscores the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is also evident in how European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions highlights the enduring impact of historical cultural factors on economic performance. This relationship shows that institutions today are a product of past institutional frameworks shaped by cultural and environmental factors.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution, including the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in England's long-run success by providing a stable market for public debt. This development had a large positive externality for the parallel growth of a market for private debt, underscoring how institutions shaped by cultural and historical factors drive economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights. This institutional framework, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.1).

Cultural beliefs also influence gender roles and their impact on economic development. African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The sexual division of labor established during the colonial period continues to reflect the contours of the production system in many regions today. This highlights how cultural beliefs and gender roles shaped by historical factors persist and influence contemporary economic dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).
