The role of culture in dominating institutions that drive long-run growth is an intellectually contentious issue. The stakes revolve around the explanatory power of cultural factors versus other determinants such as economic efficiency and geographical conditions. One live disagreement concerns whether cultural beliefs and values fundamentally shape institutions to promote economic growth, or if these institutions are primarily the result of exogenous factors like mortality rates and colonial settlements. Another debate centers on whether cultural attributes like trust and social capital are antecedents that influence institutional frameworks over time, or if they are outcomes shaped by pre-existing institutions. The resolution of these debates has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and the potential paths for future growth.

The evolution of institutions in response to cultural beliefs is a critical dimension in explaining long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural theories can influence the structure of economic institutions, highlighting how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies characterized by high levels of trust and reciprocity foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, making possible the gains from trade and technological change (North 1989, p.1).

The interdependency between individual institutions and their broader frameworks is crucial for understanding long-term economic development. Ogilvie notes that European family systems were interconnected with labor markets and credit systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This view resonates with North's argument that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of culture extends beyond institutional frameworks to include broader social norms. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects current levels of trust and economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy highlights how historical events can shape long-term societal attitudes and behaviors, influencing contemporary economic development.

The influence of culture on institutions extends beyond social norms to broader frameworks that facilitate coordinated actions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding underscores how cultural and historical factors can shape institutional outcomes over extended periods.

The dynamic relationship between culture and economic growth is further complicated by the role of exogenous factors. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective aligns with North's view that institutions play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, as evidenced by the financial revolution in England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The interplay between culture and institutions is multifaceted. The creation of secure property rights through institutional changes, such as those in seventeenth-century England, highlights how cultural factors can influence long-term economic outcomes. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of public choice institutions was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how cultural beliefs and values shape institutional frameworks over time.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) assert that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic growth. This perspective is rooted in an understanding of how cultural shifts can influence economic outcomes. McCloskey argues that the "new rhetoric" of bourgeois values, which emphasized commerce and industry, played a crucial role in facilitating economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This shift in cultural attitudes towards enterprise and innovation created an environment conducive to economic development. Mokyr also notes that much valuable knowledge during this period was tacit, implying personal transmission and training were essential for its dissemination. This means that while ideas drive growth, their effective application requires cultural mechanisms for transmitting knowledge beyond written records.

They assert that European mortality rates during colonization determined the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonizers rather than promoting long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, rooted in colonial experiences, persists and significantly influences current economic performance, particularly income per capita. The mechanism underlying this relationship is the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic structures. This implies that while colonial institutions are crucial in determining long-run growth, the specific outcomes depend on the nature of these institutions and how they align with growth-promoting policies.

The slave trade's impact on African economic development illustrates how historical events shape cultural attitudes like mistrust, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trades had enduring effects on political instability and state fragmentation, which are closely tied to the development of extractive institutions that hinder growth. These historical legacies underscore the deep-rooted nature of mistrust in African societies, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust can be seen as a manifestation of extractive institutions that perpetuate economic underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.27). The impact of the slave trade extends beyond immediate economic losses; it has left a lasting imprint on cultural norms and political structures, creating a cycle of mistrust and underdevelopment that continues to affect African economies.

The complexity in economic catch-up processes highlights that convergence hypotheses oversimplify the multifaceted nature of technological advance and social change (Austin 2008, p.2; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This perspective underscores that countries' paths to growth are shaped by a myriad of factors beyond simple institutional frameworks. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that productivity levels reflect technological gaps and the socio-economic competence to exploit advanced methods. This nuanced view is crucial for understanding why some countries, despite having favourable institutions, struggle to achieve sustained economic growth. For instance, Abramovitz highlights that even when best-practice technology is available, social competence in exploiting it can be limited (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This insight suggests that the catch-up process involves adopting new technologies and developing social and institutional capabilities to integrate them effectively.

Austin (2008, p.25) contends that the African experience shows a dynamic interplay between environmental responses and technological adoption. This dynamic perspective aligns with Abramovitz's idea that social competence is crucial for economic growth, as it implies that the ability to adapt and integrate new technologies is not uniform across countries. The implication is that cultural factors, which influence social competence and institutional evolution, play a significant role in shaping long-term economic trajectories. This dynamic interplay means cultural factors' impact on economic growth is mediated by historical and contextual variables. This nuanced view of complexity highlights the need for a more holistic understanding of economic catch-up processes that accounts for both institutional and cultural dynamics.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions, offering an alternative to efficiency-based explanations. They show that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, guilds, and communal structures, were significantly influenced by power dynamics rather than mere efficiency considerations. For instance, Ogilvie contends that the persistence of inefficient institutions can be attributed to conflicts over the distribution of resources and power among different social groups. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutional frameworks were shaped by the unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception. Ogilvie suggests understanding these distributional dynamics is crucial for explaining why certain inefficient institutions persisted despite their apparent inefficiencies. This view must be nuanced by recognizing interdependencies between different institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights that institutions do not operate in isolation; rather, they form complex, interconnected systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For example, the European family system was intertwined with labour markets and credit systems, influencing economic outcomes over centuries. This interconnectedness implies that any analysis of institutional change must consider the broader framework within which these institutions exist.
