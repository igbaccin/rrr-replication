The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the extent to which cultural factors influence institutional frameworks that drive economic performance over extended periods. The debate pits those who emphasize cultural beliefs and values against those who focus on material or geographic determinants, while other scholars advocate for institutional evolution as a key driver. This disagreement underscores the need to assess whether culture shapes institutions directly or indirectly through broader historical processes. For instance, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth; he contends that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings, fails to account for transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). In a related vein, Nunn and Wantchekon posit that cultural norms such as mistrust can persist long after historical events like the slave trade, significantly impacting economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The first stream of literature proposes that culture exerts a direct influence on institutions by shaping collective beliefs and values. Ogilvie critiques exogenous cultural theories, noting that they often fail to account for the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique aligns with North's argument that institutions are critical to economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating efficient markets (North 1989, p.9). Both perspectives suggest that cultural factors can influence institutional development by altering collective action dynamics and efficiency norms. The precise mechanisms through which culture shapes institutions are still debated. For example, North and Weingast highlight how stable public debt markets in seventeenth-century England provided positive externalities for private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical context underscores that cultural beliefs can influence institutional frameworks by fostering economic stability and growth through secure property rights. The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show how the slave trade's legacy of mistrust persists in African societies today, impacting contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that public choice institutions were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This institutional evolution was pivotal in fostering economic growth.

Institutions play a role beyond reducing transaction costs; they also create a framework that encourages long-term investment and capital market development. The secure environment for long-term investments created by these institutions laid the groundwork for sustained economic development. Furthermore, the interplay between public choice institutions and economic growth suggests that cultural factors alone cannot account for England’s economic trajectory; institutional changes were equally if not more critical in driving growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) demonstrate that colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes in former colonies, as extractive institutions established during the colonial period persisted and had lasting effects on current income per capita. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies were a key determinant of whether Europeans could establish settlements, which in turn influenced the type of institutions implemented (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, shaped by the desire to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, created an institutional framework that hindered economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in their strong correlation with current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further emphasize this point, showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism and not geographic factors. The mechanism through which these institutions affected economic growth is clear: they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to foster long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy has profound implications for understanding the fundamental causes of long-run growth, as it highlights how historical institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic performance.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share a view that distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates this with the example of guilds, arguing that their efficiency is an outcome of power struggles among different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality and shaped institutions that favored elites. Their analysis shows how these institutions, designed to protect elite interests, persisted over time and influenced development paths (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Distributional conflicts shape institutions through both immediate gains and long-term structural advantages. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework means that changes in one area can have cascading effects on others (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness suggests that the institutions formed through distributional conflicts are robust and resistant to change, further entrenching the advantages of dominant groups. The downstream implication is that these institutional frameworks, once established, create a self-reinforcing cycle where the initial advantages of elites are perpetuated, making it difficult for economic growth to occur in ways that benefit broader segments of society.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15), along with Pamuk  (2007, p.24) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), highlight that the early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway by 1800. This divergence is attributed to factors such as the Black Death, which initiated long-term structural changes in European economies. The plague brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change among guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These shifts contributed to the rise of rural industries and ushered in an era of labour-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that these changes led to higher agricultural productivity in parts of Europe. This increased productivity did not result in low food prices or an abundance of grain because labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This pattern suggests that the institutional frameworks established during this period played a pivotal role in shaping economic development trajectories. The downstream implication is that these structural changes created a self-reinforcing cycle where initial advantages of certain regions were perpetuated, making it difficult for economic growth to occur uniformly across broader segments of society.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a key driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.56; McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.56) posits that the diffusion of this knowledge across Western nations was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. This widespread dissemination underscores the interconnectedness of intellectual movements within Europe, suggesting that cultural exchanges played a crucial role in spreading ideas. McCloskey (2015, p.10) complements this view with the observation that the "Great Enrichment" was a result of new rhetoric creating new circumstances which created new rhetoric. The cultural milieu in countries like the Netherlands and Britain fostered an environment conducive to innovation and economic growth, where ideas and ideologies drove change along with institutional structures.

Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and implicit forms. This tacit knowledge required personal transmission and training, highlighting a condition where cultural practices and social interactions were essential for knowledge dissemination. McCloskey's (2015, p.10) observation that "the privileged young engineers-in-training" at institutions like the École Polytechnique in France still march around in uniforms under a banner inscribed with a motto that would strike students at other institutions as antique and unbusinesslike implies cultural practices often lag behind economic changes, suggesting that cultural inertia can slow the pace of economic growth.

The persistence of mistrust in Africa today shows how the legacy of the slave trade can affect contemporary economic outcomes by reducing trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) points out how the African story illustrates an endogenous process where Africans' responses to their environments, using available technologies, ultimately altered their paths. This dynamic process highlights the importance of historical context in understanding economic development and suggests that cultural changes, such as the development of mistrust due to the slave trade, can have long-lasting effects on institutions and economic growth. This shared finding underscores the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary institutions and economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) further supports this by noting that economists see patterns in institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe. The implication is that cultural practices and social interactions are essential for knowledge dissemination and economic growth. Austin notes the difficulty of testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively, suggesting that more research is needed to fully understand how historical events shape contemporary institutions and their impact on economic outcomes.

Culture plays a critical role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Institutions are not merely structures; they embody cultural practices and norms that facilitate or hinder economic development. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which underpinned economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework further underscores the importance of culture. Ogilvie highlights how the European family system and community-based welfare systems were interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency shows that cultural practices and social interactions are essential for knowledge dissemination and economic growth. Austin further supports this by noting that economists see patterns in institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of culture in dominating institutions is also evident in how historical events shape contemporary institutions and their impact on economic outcomes. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement lies in the detailed analysis of institutional frameworks and their cultural underpinnings. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework shows that cultural practices and social interactions are essential for knowledge dissemination and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North 1989, p.9).
