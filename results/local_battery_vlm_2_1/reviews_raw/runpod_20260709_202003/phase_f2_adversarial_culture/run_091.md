The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue within economic historiography and development studies. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural variables in shaping institutional frameworks that drive economic performance over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering or hindering growth; or if instead, other factors such as mortality rates, settler institutions, or the slave trade are more critical determinants. This debate has significant implications for understanding historical trajectories of development and for informing contemporary policy aimed at fostering long-run growth. The central tension lies between those who argue that cultural factors are intrinsic to institutional evolution and those who posit rival causal mechanisms.

This disagreement is evident in the work of Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. who offer competing explanations for the role of institutions in economic development. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are key drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective challenges the notion that cultural beliefs directly influence institutional structures and efficiency. Instead, it suggests that the interplay between different institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding economic growth. Acemoglu et al. propose a different mechanism: European mortality rates significantly shaped colonial institutions, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This view emphasizes exogenous factors like disease environments over cultural influences.

The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks is a recurring theme that bridges these competing views. Ogilvie highlights how various institutions, such as the European family system, community-based welfare systems, and contractual and property rights frameworks, are interconnected and collectively influence economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, particularly through their role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights (North 1989, p.9; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin further elaborates on the dynamic nature of institutional evolution, emphasizing how different historical periods and paths shape long-run economic performance. This shared finding underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when examining the causes of growth, including cultural beliefs and exogenous shocks.

North and Weingast's work provides a nuanced view of how institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This perspective complements Austin's dynamic approach by highlighting the specific historical contexts in which institutional changes occurred. Nunn and Wantchekon add another layer to this discussion by showing how the slave trade influenced cultural norms of trust within Africa, which persist today and affect economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). Their findings suggest that cultural factors can have long-lasting effects on institutions and growth, even if these effects are mediated through historical events like the slave trade. This interplay between culture, historical events, and institutional frameworks underscores the complexity of understanding long-run growth.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). Their work highlights how the evolution of these institutions led to significant declines in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This stability created a positive externality that supported broader economic growth. The interplay between these institutional frameworks and historical events underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run growth.

Colonial institutions significantly affected current income per capita, challenging the idea that culture is the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies influenced both settlement patterns and institutional development. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the strong correlation between settler mortality, early colonial institutions, and contemporary institutional frameworks. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic performance. For instance, political power dynamics within colonies often prevented institutional changes that could have benefited broader economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The legacy of colonial institutions thus continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, underscoring the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a profound adverse effect on economic development, suggesting that historical events like slavery can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn argues that slavery had an adverse effect on economic development by causing long-term political instability and weakened states. This historical legacy of political instability continues to influence contemporary economic performance, suggesting that Africa's current underdevelopment is partly rooted in its precolonial history. However, Nunn (2008, p.27) also notes that the slave trades may have contributed to modern corruption, highlighting another mechanism through which historical events shape present-day economic outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.1). This implies that the enduring effects of slavery are multifaceted, encompassing both political instability and institutional decay.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic growth in Northwestern Europe during 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey asserts that the shift from traditional institutions to new ones was driven by a change in rhetoric, which then influenced circumstances. Mokyr builds on this idea by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge was a critical factor in driving economic growth. He argues that much of this knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and practices. The diffusion of this knowledge within Europe facilitated economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.14).

McCloskey's argument is bolstered by Mokyr’s emphasis on the importance of useful knowledge. Mokyr highlights that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was crucial for economic growth, as it allowed for the spread of new ideas and technologies across borders. This diffusion included tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr notes that this process varied depending on the cultural and institutional contexts within each nation (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between ideas and economic growth, suggesting that while ideas are crucial, their impact is mediated by local conditions.

Austin's re-evaluation of historical economic theories (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) highlights that the dynamics of institutional evolution and distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe played a significant role in shaping long-run growth. This cluster emphasizes the importance of understanding how these factors interacted with cultural contexts, offering insights into why certain institutions were adopted while others were not. Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced approach to institutional history (Austin 2008, p.25). He argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that African economic development was partly endogenous, shaped by Africans' own responses to their environments. She contends that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Ogilvie's analysis highlights how cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. This insight is crucial for understanding how culture shapes institutional frameworks over time, as it underscores the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Broadberry and Gupta's work on early modern economic divergence highlights that significant differences between Europe and Asia were evident well before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This divergence is attributed to institutional changes in agriculture, which led to varying levels of labor productivity across regions. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the high investment in southern Indian agriculture was driven by significant investments made during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These investments, however, did not lead to an abundance of grain or low food prices because labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This shift is crucial as it illustrates how institutional changes in one sector can catalyze broader economic transformations.

Pamuk (2007, p.24) suggests that the Black Death played a pivotal role in initiating long-term structural changes, which led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This divergence was deeply rooted in the institutional responses to demographic shocks. The scarcity of labor following the Black Death drove technological innovations that were labor-saving, further altering the economic landscape (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These changes highlight how exogenous shocks can trigger institutional adaptations that have lasting effects on economic growth, underscoring the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to external pressures. This interplay between demographic shifts and institutional change is a key factor in understanding the early modern great divergence.

The role of culture in long-run growth is indirectly illuminated by examining technological and institutional changes in agriculture, which have significantly shaped agricultural practices and economic behavior (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; de Vries 1994, p.18). For instance, the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This innovation facilitated more efficient land use and increased productivity, demonstrating how technological changes can drive economic growth. The effectiveness of these technological advancements is contingent upon the broader institutional framework. In some cases, social norms played a crucial role in complementing legal responsibilities, as seen in modern California where ranchers controlled cattle through shared expectations (de Vries 1994, p.5). This interplay between technology and social norms highlights how cultural factors can influence the adoption and impact of new technologies, even if they are not the primary drivers. The Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, further illustrates this dynamic.

Culture plays an important role in shaping institutions that underpin long-run growth by influencing collective action and institutional frameworks. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can motivate economic behavior at the micro-level. She cites Richardson's contention that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, whereas the Protestant Reformation led to their decline. This shows how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence both the structure of economic institutions and overall economic efficiency by altering collective action dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Nunn and Wantchekon build on this idea by demonstrating that historical events, such as the slave trade in Africa, have created enduring cultural traits like mistrust, which affect economic outcomes today. Their work shows how trust is a key determinant of economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The role of culture extends beyond individual behaviors to shape broader institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights that European family systems were interdependent with labor markets and credit markets, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, North and Weingast emphasize the importance of institutions in facilitating secure property rights and economic growth through their analysis of seventeenth-century England's financial revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

This interplay between culture and institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persist today. Their work underscores how these institutions significantly affect current income per capita by influencing economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis adds another layer to this discussion by highlighting that different historical periods and paths must be considered when examining institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2).

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is shown clearly by these studies. Both Ogilvie and Nunn and Wantchekon agree that cultural beliefs and norms significantly influence economic behavior and outcomes, establishing the underlying point of agreement (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement lies in whether specific historical events or broader institutional frameworks are more critical in shaping long-run growth. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that mortality rates, a proxy for environmental factors, played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions established in colonies and their impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This evidence suggests that historical events like the slave trade, which shaped cultural norms such as mistrust, are more critical than broader institutional frameworks. Comparative analysis of trust levels between regions with different histories of the slave trade can settle this disagreement by providing empirical support for the enduring effects of historical events on economic outcomes.
