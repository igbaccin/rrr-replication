Culture's role in shaping institutions that drive long-term economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding how deeply rooted societal beliefs and practices influence the development of economic systems over centuries. One camp posits that cultural factors are pivotal in establishing institutions that either foster or hinder growth, while another argues that these institutions are shaped by more immediate material conditions like mortality rates or factor endowments. A third perspective identifies antecedent triggers such as trust which flows into the causal variable of culture and influences institutional formation. This review explores how culture interacts with other factors to shape institutions and economic outcomes.

The impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions is evident in historical contexts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Cultural theories have been applied at the micro-level to explain phenomena like guilds' efficient rules and their decline with Protestant Reformation. Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how cultural values such as trust and reciprocity influence institutional frameworks that promote growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North’s assertion about institutions' role in economic growth, particularly when transaction costs are positive, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and financial innovations like the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.1).

North and Weingast extend this by arguing that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, highlighting how institutions provided necessary conditions for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and stable public debt, which in turn facilitated private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

Their work highlights how the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. The development of a stable market for public debt provided a substantial positive externality for private debt markets, demonstrating how institutions can create conditions conducive to economic growth. This perspective contrasts with views that emphasize cultural factors as primary drivers of economic development. The institutional framework in seventeenth-century England provided conditions for economic growth and ensured these gains were sustained over time, highlighting enduring impact of well-designed institutions on economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are the key driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. This perspective posits that the primary function of these institutions is to allocate resources among different groups, rather than merely enhancing efficiency or being shaped by cultural factors. In pre-industrial Europe, serfdom, communal structures, and guilds were designed to manage conflicts over resource distribution between elites and commoners. This dynamic is evident in the differential paths of development observed across Latin America, where institutional variations significantly influenced growth trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie notes that these institutions are interdependent within a broader framework. The European family system, for instance, was intricately linked with labor markets, credit systems, and collective welfare provisions, all of which contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness suggests that while distributional conflicts shape institutional structures, the efficiency and sustainability of these institutions depend on their integration within a cohesive framework.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change, especially during the early modern period. McCloskey contends that economic growth was influenced by a shift in rhetoric that valued commerce and innovation over traditional aristocratic values. This ideological transformation allowed for an environment where new ideas could flourish, leading to significant economic advancements. Mokyr echoes this perspective by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. He notes that the diffusion of knowledge across Europe was facilitated by various means such as periodicals, translations, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This widespread dissemination of knowledge created a fertile ground for innovation and economic development. The interplay between ideas and their practical application is crucial here: tacit knowledge, which includes skills and implicit understanding, played a significant role in the economic growth of Europe during this period (Mokyr 2005, p.14; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This tacit knowledge, often transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships, complemented codified knowledge to drive technological advancements. However, Mokyr points out that the diffusion of useful knowledge was not uniform across all regions; cultural attitudes towards commerce and innovation varied significantly. This suggests that while ideas and ideologies were instrumental in driving economic change, their impact was mediated by regional cultural contexts.

The Industrious Revolution, which preceded the Industrial Revolution, played a pivotal role in shaping economic growth trajectories by increasing labor input and altering consumption patterns (de Vries 1994, p.18; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This period saw significant changes in societal attitudes towards work and productivity, with households releasing more labor reserves into the market. Abramovitz underscores that these social changes were active drivers of economic growth, influencing both productivity levels and the adoption of new technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.12; de Vries 1994, p.18). De Vries highlights how Europe's urban network facilitated regional economic development, creating a framework where industrial growth could flourish.

The implications of these findings are profound for understanding long-run growth. The Industrious Revolution demonstrates that economic growth is driven by underlying social changes and cultural shifts towards increased labor input and productivity. This period's societal transformations, including the release of labor reserves into the market, indicate a deep-seated cultural shift that facilitated economic development.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of economic growth and income disparities among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the mortality rates faced by Europeans in their colonies influenced the type of institutions established, which in turn shaped long-term economic performance. This mechanism is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who demonstrate that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists were not conducive to long-run growth; the equilibrium institutions in these colonies prioritized short-term gains over sustainable economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutional legacies helps explain current income disparities, as former colonies with extractive institutions lag behind those that developed more inclusive ones. This suggests that while initial colonial institutions played a crucial role, subsequent changes and adaptations have also shaped long-term economic trajectories.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that the slave trade was instrumental in shaping African culture towards mistrust, which has enduring economic consequences. The mechanism behind this cultural transformation involves the political instability and fragmentation caused by the slave trades, which weakened state structures and hindered economic development. This historical disintegration is linked to current economic performance, as recent empirical research indicates that a country's history of state development is crucial for its contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn further elaborates on this point by suggesting that slavery can be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1), highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on present-day economic conditions. This underscores how the cultural legacy of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, interacts with institutional frameworks to shape long-term growth trajectories in Africa.

The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-term economic growth is profoundly influenced by historical legacies and societal norms. Cultural beliefs can alter the structure of collective action games, influencing institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, the Protestant Reformation's impact on guilds demonstrates how cultural shifts can reshape economic institutions. Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights that trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, fostering growth and accountability in societies with high social capital (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that European colonies' mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which persistently affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic conditions is further underscored by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth. He argues that transaction costs are a crucial factor, and secure property rights are essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who emphasize the role of public choice institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The legacy of mistrust stemming from the slave trade exemplifies how cultural factors can interact with institutional frameworks to shape long-term growth trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy of mistrust interacts with contemporary institutions, influencing economic performance and growth trajectories. The historical development of institutions governing public choice in England illustrates how secure property rights can foster long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England. This event demonstrates how institutions can play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, providing a large positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The impact of cultural beliefs on institutional frameworks, as demonstrated by Putnam's theory of social capital, further underscores the importance of trust, norms, and networks in facilitating coordinated actions that foster growth and accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
