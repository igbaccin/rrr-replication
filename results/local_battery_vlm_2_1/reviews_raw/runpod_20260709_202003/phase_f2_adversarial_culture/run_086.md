The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is our understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. A key disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional change that fosters economic growth or if other factors such as geography, technology, or exogenous shocks predominate (Author Year p.N). Some scholars contend that culture shapes institutions by influencing societal norms, values, and behaviors, thereby creating frameworks conducive to economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Others argue that cultural explanations are insufficient on their own and must be supplemented with other factors such as mortality rates or colonial legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17), while some highlight the importance of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25).

The debate is nuanced by the recognition that institutions themselves are complex and interdependent systems, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. This complexity means that cultural factors may interact with other variables in ways that are not immediately apparent. For instance, the role of culture in shaping institutional frameworks that support long-term economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9). The creation and evolution of institutions reflect a dynamic interplay between cultural beliefs and material conditions, suggesting that culture is an integral part of the institutional landscape. This perspective underscores the need for a more holistic approach to understanding long-run economic growth, one that acknowledges the multifaceted nature of cultural influence on institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that historical events like the slave trade in Africa have deeply influenced long-term cultural attitudes, particularly fostering a culture of mistrust. This legacy is an active force shaping contemporary social dynamics. The transatlantic slave trade disrupted African societies by forcibly removing large populations and creating conditions that nurtured mistrust among communities. The disruption extended to gender roles, with colonial constructs codifying gender relations in ways that had enduring consequences for economic production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). These historical traumas left deep imprints on societal perceptions of trustworthiness and cooperation, altering social structures and norms. This historical legacy has significantly impacted the development of contemporary institutions and cultural frameworks by shaping long-term attitudes towards trust.

Ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. North (1989, p.1) and McCloskey (2015, p.10) assert that the rise of new financial instruments and institutions, such as the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth.

She notes that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's decline in these beliefs led to a reduction in guild influence (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic illustrates how ideologies can alter institutional structures and efficiency by changing collective action games. The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks further underscores this point; for example, Laslett speculates that the European family system’s integration with various markets contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

McCloskey (2015, p.10) emphasizes the cultural struggle in France as a key driver of economic change. She argues that the 'Franco-French War' reflects deep-seated ideological conflicts that shaped economic policies and outcomes (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This perspective aligns with North's assertion about the importance of ideologies in economic development, showing how cultural struggles can either hinder or facilitate growth depending on the prevailing beliefs. The implication is that understanding the role of culture and ideology in shaping institutions is crucial for comprehending long-term economic trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their argument is anchored in the evolution of English institutions during the seventeenth century, which created a stable market for both public and private debt. This financial revolution was pivotal to England's long-term success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Acemoglu et al. extend this perspective by demonstrating that differences in European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, highlighting how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic development depending on their design and the interests they serve (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implication is that understanding these institutional dynamics is crucial for comprehending why some colonies flourished while others stagnated, a pattern that persists to this day.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) share a critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, highlighting that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This simplification obscures the dynamic processes through which institutions evolve in response to environmental changes (Austin 2008, p.25). The nuanced view posits that Africans’ own responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, ultimately altered economic trajectories (Bryant 2006, p.35). Bryant (2006, p.35) argues that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. This critique underscores the importance of considering cultural norms as part of the broader institutional framework that shapes economic development paths. The implication is that a comprehensive understanding of long-run growth requires acknowledging the multifaceted interplay between institutions, technologies, and cultural responses to environmental challenges.

Van Zanden and Broadberry and Gupta show that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This early divergence can be attributed to institutional changes in the developing parts of north-western Europe, which led to high levels of investment in agriculture and consequently increased agricultural labour productivity. This higher productivity did not result in an abundance of grain or low food prices because labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services (van Zanden 1999, p.6). The Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that contributed to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This period saw interest rates decline, skill premiums follow suit, and an era of labor-saving technological innovations emerge (Pamuk 2007, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.6). Pamuk's analysis shows that increased mobility and institutional change in guilds were crucial factors. These structural changes and the associated technological advancements played a pivotal role in shaping economic trajectories across Europe.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) share a posture that examines regional economic and social changes without directly addressing how culture influences long-run growth (Goldin 2006, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Goldin's work highlights the role of exogenous changes in transforming women’s employment, education, and family dynamics. These shifts were driven by increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century, which created new job opportunities. Additionally, advancements in household production technology and institutional changes that facilitated part-time work and removed barriers to married women's employment were pivotal (Goldin 2006, p.8). This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and their ability to foster development-inducing institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that these regional changes are tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions.

Stern (2004, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.24); (Hornbeck 2010, p.6) argue that the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth has been overshadowed by an emphasis on environmental and technological factors. Cultural norms, as Stern argues, influence how societies approach economic development and environmental management. The Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis suggests that economic growth initially increases pollution but eventually reduces it as countries develop. Van Zanden's analysis, which focuses on human capital formation and the skill premium, shows how labor productivity in agriculture is linked to population growth and urban-rural wage gaps. This dynamic underscores the importance of agricultural development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.23). Hornbeck (2010, p.6) qualifies this perspective by demonstrating that social norms can significantly influence property rights and externalities management in agricultural settings. This qualification suggests that while technological factors are crucial, cultural norms also play a pivotal role in determining the effectiveness of institutions and their impact on economic growth. The interaction between environmental changes, technological advancements, and cultural norms thus shapes long-run economic development in complex ways.

The growth of useful knowledge is pivotal in driving economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.56) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) both emphasize, highlighting the importance of technological advancement and social competence. This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.56)(Abramovitz 1986, p.21) underscores that the diffusion of tacit knowledge, skills and implicit understanding, is crucial for economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.14) elaborates that much of this useful knowledge was not codified but rather transmitted through personal interactions and training, which emphasizes the significance of social networks and institutional frameworks in facilitating the spread of knowledge. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) qualifies this by noting that social competence for exploiting advanced technologies can be limited, especially in latecomer economies. This limitation suggests that while technological advancement is necessary, it is not sufficient on its own to drive economic growth; social and institutional factors play a critical role in enabling societies to fully capitalize on new knowledge.

Nunn (2008, p.1) provides a historical context for this dynamic by linking Africa's slave trades to long-term political instability and weakened state institutions. This historical perspective underscores the enduring impact of institutional development on economic performance, illustrating how past disruptions can hinder a society's ability to leverage new knowledge effectively.

De Vries (1994, p.18; 1994, p.5) argues that the Industrious Revolution, marked by heightened labor input and evolving consumption patterns, preceded and catalyzed the Industrial Revolution. This phenomenon was driven by cultural shifts towards valuing industriousness, which fostered a more dynamic workforce and paved the way for subsequent industrial growth. These changes were deeply ingrained in societal values and norms, which enabled societies to harness their labor force more effectively (de Vries 1994, p.5). The release of household labor into the market increased economic activity and productivity (de Vries 1994, p.18), as households began to outsource domestic tasks to market-based production. This integration further connected households with broader economic systems.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth by influencing societal values and norms, which in turn affect how labor is harnessed and integrated into broader economic systems. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, showcasing how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of institutions in economic growth, highlighting that gains from trade are key to the wealth of nations and that specialization and division of labor have significantly improved productivity (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The integration of households into market-based production systems increased economic activity and productivity, as households began to outsource domestic tasks. This dynamic is evident in North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further underscored by Ogilvie's discussion on Putnam’s theory of 'social capital', which refers to features of social organization such as trust, norms, and networks that can improve the efficiency of society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective shows how cultural beliefs and values encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a dynamic, partly endogenous approach to understanding economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how gender roles during pre-colonial and colonial periods significantly influenced economic growth and development in Africa. This underscores the importance of cultural norms and social structures in shaping economic outcomes, as women remained indispensable to community reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The shared finding is that culture influences institutions by shaping societal values and norms, which in turn affect how labor is harnessed and integrated into broader economic systems. The remaining disagreement centers on whether these cultural shifts are endogenous or exogenous to institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin’s emphasis on a dynamic approach contrasts with Ogilvie's focus on exogenously driven cultural theories. The historical evidence of colonial constructs and codification of gender roles in Africa provides specific empirical support for the endogenous view, showing how these constructs shaped long-term economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Austin 2008, p.25).
