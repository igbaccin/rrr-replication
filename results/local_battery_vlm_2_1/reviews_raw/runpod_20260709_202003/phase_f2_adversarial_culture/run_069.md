The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake intellectually is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions over the long term. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional frameworks are primarily shaped by exogenous cultural factors or if they emerge from endogenous processes driven by economic incentives. One position argues that cultural beliefs directly influence economic outcomes through their impact on institutions. For instance, "civic" cultures characterized by trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks are posited to encourage institutions that promote economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Some scholars argue that cultural explanations often overlook the complex interplay between different institutions within a broader framework and fail to account for the dynamic nature of institutional change. This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how culture interacts with other factors in shaping economic outcomes.

Institutional frameworks are not static entities but are interdependent with various markets and social systems. For example, the European family system was linked to labor and credit markets, as well as collective provision systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that cultural influences on institutions must be considered within a broader context of economic and social interactions. The dynamic nature of institutional change further complicates the role of culture, as institutions evolve in response to changing economic conditions and technological advancements.

The relationship between culture and long-run growth is further complicated by the role of institutions in mediating the effects of cultural values on economic outcomes. For example, the English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, led to a significant decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change fostered economic growth by creating a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of private debt markets. Institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The role of culture in shaping these institutional changes remains contentious.

This influence is particularly evident in how cultural norms around gender have shaped the roles of women and their participation in economic activities, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Goldin 2006, p.8). In pre-colonial West Africa, Akan societies relied on women for community reproduction due to their matrilineal social structure, where wealth and office transmission were tied to female bloodlines (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Women's indispensable role in these societies underscores how gender dynamics can drive economic growth. This transformation was driven by shifts in demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology. Goldin notes that the turning point for earnings relative to men occurred later around 1980. This shift highlights how cultural changes around gender roles can influence economic growth through increased labor supply and altered social norms (Goldin 2006, p.8). The role of women in economic growth underscores how cultural norms and gender dynamics significantly shape long-run development, with both historical and contemporary evidence illustrating the profound impact of these factors on economic outcomes.

This perspective is echoed by McCloskey's argument that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Mokyr 2005, p.56; McCloskey 2015, p.10). In this view, the "bourgeois virtues" such as honesty, hard work, and open trade fostered an environment conducive to innovation and growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) expands on this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge was a critical component in driving economic development during this period (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The diffusion of ideas across Europe facilitated technological advancements and economic progress as new knowledge became widely available through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. This sharing of ideas included tacit knowledge transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr's emphasis on the role of tacit knowledge highlights that the dissemination of ideas depended on interpersonal interactions, which further facilitated economic growth. McCloskey notes that this process encountered resistance. The cultural struggle in France, for instance, illustrates how entrenched ideologies can hinder economic progress (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Ideological shifts were pivotal in overcoming these barriers and enabling the Great Enrichment.

One such legacy is the culture of mistrust that emerged from the slave trade in Africa and continues to impact trust levels today, as noted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that this mistrust is a direct consequence of the historical trauma inflicted by the transatlantic slave trade, which has left lasting psychological scars on African societies. This distrust manifests in various forms, including reduced social capital and weaker institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These cultural shifts have profound implications for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates that the political instability caused by the slave trades led to weakened and fragmented states, which in turn hindered economic growth. This long-term political instability is a critical factor in understanding Africa's current underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.1). The legacy of slavery extends beyond immediate economic impacts; it also includes institutional development. Nunn and Wantchekon acknowledge that while the slave trade is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory. Other historical and contemporary factors also play crucial roles in shaping the continent's development path.

Acemoglu et al. and other researchers argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the fundamental cause of long-run economic development, often overshadowing cultural factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were shaped by European colonists who designed them to maximize their rents rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The impact of these extractive institutions persists, significantly affecting current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. exploit differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance, finding a strong correlation between settler mortality and contemporary institutional quality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This relationship underscores how colonial-era institutions have enduring effects on economic outcomes, even after accounting for geographic factors. However, the extent of this impact varies across regions, with some colonies experiencing more pronounced effects than others due to differences in initial conditions and subsequent policy changes.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights in seventeenth-century England. They identify the creation of the Bank of England as a pivotal moment that dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. This financial revolution was a political event driven by the need to address fiscal crises and secure property rights over time. In this context, institutions like the Bank of England played a critical role in establishing a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the development of private credit markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The evolution of these institutions provided a large positive externality, fostering economic growth by ensuring that individuals could confidently invest in long-term ventures. This institutional framework was essential for England's economic success and political freedom, contrasting with views that attribute economic development primarily to cultural factors.

Pamuk (2007, p.24) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) share the view that economic divergence in Europe was well underway by 1800. They argue that during this period, northwest Europe began to experience significant growth while other regions stagnated. This divergence was influenced by institutional changes that fostered investment in agriculture and industry, leading to increased agricultural labour productivity (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These changes facilitated a shift of labour into industrial and service sectors, which did not occur uniformly across Europe. Van Zanden (1999, p.6) notes the complexity of economic development and living standards during this period; it was not straightforward or continuous. Pamuk (2007, p.5) adds a layer to this narrative by showing how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that further exacerbated these regional differences. The pandemic led to labour shortages and increased wages, which in turn drove technological innovations and institutional adaptations. These changes were not uniform across Europe; regions with pre-existing strong institutions were better positioned to capitalize on these shifts (Pamuk 2007, p.24).

The literature on African economic performance and institutional reforms highlights the significance of institutional variables in explaining development outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Hopkins (2009, p.8) posits that these institutions are deeply rooted in precolonial history. This perspective is echoed by Broadberry and Gardner, who show how local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions have shaped economic growth trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions plays a pivotal role in this dynamic. Englebert's commentary, as cited by Hopkins (2009, p.8), argues that congruent institutions enhance government legitimacy and foster development-inducing policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The effectiveness of these institutions can be undermined by patrimonial politics, which lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. This interplay between institutional congruence and political stability is crucial for understanding long-run growth in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.8) suggests that historical investigation into precolonial periods is essential for comprehending the gradual evolution of African institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The nuanced relationship between culture and institutions in Africa remains a fertile area for further research, particularly in understanding how cultural practices can either reinforce or undermine institutional effectiveness.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Bryant (2006, p.35) argue that historical factor endowments lead to different institutional structures and inequality levels in New World societies. The impact of these factors on development is mediated through the institutions they shaped, which in turn influenced economic growth paths. The initial inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to the evolution of institutions that favored elites and restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutional arrangements persisted over time, affecting long-run growth trajectories. Bryant (2006, p.4) highlights that this persistence is deeply rooted in the cultural norms and social relations inherited from prior developments; these factors are crucial for understanding how institutions evolve over time. The differentials in development paths are thus not only a result of economic factors but also of the institutional frameworks shaped by historical inequalities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Technological and agricultural developments played crucial roles in facilitating long-run growth through mechanisms like property rights protection and increased labor input (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; de Vries 1994, p.18). Hornbeck (2010, p.6) illustrates this with the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which enabled farmers to better protect their property rights. This was achieved by reducing externalities from livestock damage through the enforcement of social norms among ranchers (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This mechanism allowed for more efficient use of agricultural land and resources, thereby enhancing productivity. The Industrious Revolution also contributed to long-run growth by increasing labor input and fostering new consumption patterns. De Vries (1994, p.18) argues that this revolution was a distinct phenomenon preceding the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.18). One downstream implication of this increased labor input is the release of household labor reserves into the market, which further stimulated economic activity. de Vries (1994, p.18) notes that this process also led to the dissolution of traditional household functions and structures, thereby altering societal norms and consumption patterns (de Vries 1994, p.5). These developments highlight how technological and agricultural advancements can drive long-run growth by reshaping institutional frameworks and social behaviors.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Austin 2008, p.2) highlight the complexities of institutional evolution in historical causation and economic institutions. Distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions by influencing their adoption and persistence over time. For instance, the presence of serfdom, craft guilds, merchant guilds, and community structures in pre-industrial Europe can be attributed to these conflicts rather than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) posits that African economies experienced a dynamic interplay between environmental factors and technological responses, illustrating the sophisticated geography approach which emphasizes endogenous factors influencing economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay is crucial in understanding how distributional conflicts can shape institutional frameworks. This underscores how distributional conflicts can influence not just individual institutions but entire institutional frameworks that drive long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2) cautions against oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of these dynamics (Austin 2008, p.25).

The literature on technological advance and social change in economic growth does not explicitly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; van Zanden 2009, p.24). Abramovitz's (1986, p.21) work highlights the complexity of the catch-up process, where both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change shape economic growth. This complexity is underpinned by institutional changes that can limit growth in advanced economies (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). The role of technology in this process is multifaceted, as Abramovitz notes that productivity levels were initially erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.12), suggesting that social competence for exploiting advanced methods was still limited. This limitation implies that the adoption of new technologies depends on the societal ability to integrate them effectively.

This finding underscores that the impact of technological advances on growth is mediated through social structures and institutions. Van Zanden illustrates this by showing how unskilled labourers' wages in urban sectors were linked to agricultural productivity, which was influenced by population growth (van Zanden 2009, p.23). This connection suggests that the economic benefits of technological advances are distributed unevenly across different segments of society, with implications for overall growth dynamics. The interplay between technological progress and social structures thus shapes long-run economic performance in ways that go beyond simple convergence hypotheses.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that underpin long-run economic growth by influencing the development of social structures and norms that facilitate or hinder economic performance. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can significantly impact economic institutions and efficiency by altering the dynamics of collective action (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.1). The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding how societies achieve sustained economic development.

The historical evidence shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as those in seventeenth-century England, was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that the financial revolution in England played a critical role in this process by providing a stable market for public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework supported economic growth and facilitated political freedom, illustrating how cultural norms and institutional development are interlinked. The case of seventeenth-century England highlights that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Acemoglu et al. show that the determinants of European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This historical evidence underscores how cultural and institutional factors interact to shape long-run economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, showing that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Austin 2008, p.25; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The historical evidence of Akan state formation in West Africa illustrates how cultural beliefs and social structures influenced economic development. This demonstrates the importance of considering the dynamic interplay between culture, institutions, and economic performance.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe and colonial Africa shows that cultural beliefs and social norms significantly influence the development of economic institutions. This interplay between culture, institutions, and economic performance highlights the need to consider the dynamic nature of these factors in understanding long-run growth. The historical comparison of European and African experiences provides a clear measurement type for settling this disagreement: the relative stability and security of property rights over time.
