The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue that has sparked intense debate among scholars. At stake is whether cultural factors can shape economic trajectories by influencing institutional structures. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which culture determines the establishment and persistence of institutions that either foster or hinder economic development. One position argues that cultural norms and beliefs are pivotal in shaping institutions, leading to long-term economic outcomes. Another perspective suggests that institutions themselves are shaped more directly by exogenous factors such as geography or colonial policies, with culture playing a secondary role. A third view posits that the relationship between culture and institutions is dynamic and bidirectional, with each influencing the other over time. The interplay of these perspectives underscores the complexity of understanding how cultural factors can dominate institutional frameworks to drive long-run growth. The debate hinges on whether cultural norms are endogenous or exogenous to institutional development.

One stream develops the claim that culture operates through institutions to influence economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) argue that secure property rights, facilitated by institutions governing public choice, were crucial for England's long-run success. Austin (2008, p.25) echoes this view, emphasizing the importance of institutions in delivering secure private property rights as key to economic growth. Institutions thus provide a mechanism through which cultural norms can exert influence on economic development. This perspective indicates that cultural factors are intrinsically linked to institutional frameworks, suggesting they are more than just exogenous determinants.

A related stream offers a more specific instance of this claim, focusing on the role of gender in shaping institutions and economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight how gender-based colonial constructs significantly impacted long-term gender production dynamics. This dynamic highlights that cultural norms around gender roles evolve within institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.25) also acknowledges the importance of endogenous responses to environments, suggesting a more dynamic relationship between culture and institutions. These contributions indicate that cultural factors can shape economic trajectories through their influence on institutions, particularly in contexts where colonial legacies have entrenched certain norms.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further elucidated by examining the impact of historical events such as the slave trade. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, affecting economic outcomes through its influence on social capital. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth by shaping the environment within which economic activities occur (North 1989, p.1). The enduring effects of historical events on cultural norms and subsequent institutional frameworks illustrate how culture can dominate institutions over long periods.

The relationship between culture and institutions is not unidirectional; rather, it is a complex interplay where each influences the other. North's assertion that "institutions matter" (North 1989, p.1) underscores the idea that cultural factors are endogenous to institutional development. The interplay between these factors highlights how cultural norms can dominate institutional frameworks, driving long-run growth through their influence on economic activities and social capital.

Understanding this dynamic relationship is crucial for comprehending how cultural norms can influence long-term economic outcomes through their impact on institutions. The interplay between culture and institutions highlights the need for a nuanced approach to studying economic growth, one that recognizes the complex and bidirectional nature of these relationships.

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This development underscores that economic growth was contingent on institutional changes that secured property rights and reduced transaction costs, rather than being driven primarily by cultural factors. This nuanced view highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run economic outcomes through secure property rights.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) assert that colonial institutions significantly influenced current income per capita and thereby the long-run growth trajectory of former colonies. The mechanism linking these factors is extractive institutions established during colonial periods, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than fostering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions persisted over time, shaping economic outcomes by inhibiting private property rights and discouraging investment in productivity-enhancing activities. This dynamic suggests that the legacy of colonial rule is a critical factor in understanding contemporary economic disparities among former colonies. These qualifications highlight that while colonial institutions play a significant role in shaping long-run growth, their effects are not deterministic and can be mediated by additional variables. The complexity of this interaction underscores the need for further investigation into how these institutions interact with other economic and social factors to determine long-term growth trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that societies in the New World with pronounced inequality were marked by institutions that favored elites through policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). These policies often took the form of land distribution and labor regulations, which entrenched wealth disparities and limited economic mobility. Nunn (2008, p.27) corroborates this mechanism with evidence from Africa, noting that slave trades, by causing political instability, led to weak state structures that were unable to enforce property rights or invest in public infrastructure (Nunn 2008, p.1). This suggests that inequality persists due to institutional frameworks perpetuating elite advantages. Thus, while institutions shaped by inequality may initially seem responsive to cultural norms, they are fundamentally driven by structural factors that maintain power imbalances over time.

McCloskey and Mokyr both argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey notes that cultural shifts drove economic growth, particularly highlighting how new rhetoric fostered new circumstances. This dynamic is exemplified by the "Franco-French War, " a protracted cultural struggle that influenced economic development in France and beyond. Mokyr explains that useful knowledge was central to this process; he argues that while explicit codified knowledge was important, tacit knowledge, skills and know-how passed through personal transmission, was crucial for technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The interplay of ideas and ideologies with the growth of useful knowledge created an environment conducive to economic enrichment. Mokyr's emphasis on the diffusion of knowledge within Europe further underscores this point; the movement of tacit knowledge facilitated by personal interactions and training was essential for economic development. This suggests that cultural factors, such as the dissemination of ideas and the sharing of tacit knowledge, played a pivotal role in driving economic growth during this period.

One specific mechanism through which this complexity manifests is the interaction between environmental factors and endogenous responses. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that Africans' own responses to their environments, utilizing various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay suggests that cultural adaptations to geographical conditions play a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) point out that high levels of investment in agriculture did not necessarily lead to abundant grain and low food prices in developing parts of north-western Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This observation underscores that institutional changes do not uniformly result in economic development across different regions, even when similar conditions are present. The implications of this finding are significant for understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth. It suggests that cultural norms and responses to environmental challenges can either facilitate or hinder the adoption of institutions conducive to economic growth, depending on the specific historical and geographical context.

This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.25). They show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This historical context underscores how cultural responses to environmental challenges can either facilitate or hinder institutional evolution over time.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is also evident in the long-run effects of historical events such as the slave trade. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy has profound implications for institutional development and economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement lies in whether current institutions are primarily shaped by historical environmental conditions or by enduring cultural norms (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from the persistent effects of colonial gender constructs on contemporary production systems. This shows that while environmental conditions play a role, it is the enduring influence of cultural norms that ultimately shapes institutional evolution and long-run economic growth.
