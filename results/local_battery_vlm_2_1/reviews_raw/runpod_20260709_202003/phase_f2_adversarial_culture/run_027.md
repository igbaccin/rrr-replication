The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars, one that has profound implications for understanding economic development and stagnation. Institutions are widely recognized as critical drivers of economic performance, but disagreements persist over whether cultural factors shape these institutions or if other forces are at play. Some argue that culture can significantly influence the evolution of institutions, while others posit that geographical or historical factors hold more sway. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy and development strategies. North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) point out how institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17) argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These differing views underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of how culture interacts with institutional frameworks over time.

The role of culture is often considered through its effects on social norms and trust. Some scholars argue that cultural attitudes toward risk-taking, cooperation, and innovation are deeply rooted in historical experiences and can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) posit that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day. This cultural legacy actively shapes contemporary economic behavior and institutional structures (Author, Year, p.N). The enduring impact of historical events on current cultural norms highlights how deeply embedded these factors are in societal frameworks.

The interaction between institutions and cultural factors is complex and multifaceted. Institutions, as defined by North (1989, p.1; 1989, p.9), encompass the rules of the game that structure social interactions, including formal rules such as laws and informal constraints like norms and conventions. These institutional frameworks are not static; they evolve in response to cultural shifts and historical events. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.25) notes that most economists see behavior of older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe. The dynamic nature of institutions means that cultural factors can both influence and be influenced by institutional changes over time.

The relationship between culture and institutions is further complicated by the role of gender dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4; 2014, p.6) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite marginalization in historical narratives. This perspective challenges traditional views that overlook the contributions of women to economic development, highlighting how gender roles and cultural norms can shape institutional frameworks.

Cultural factors also play a role in shaping economic behavior through their influence on trust and social capital. Trust is a critical component of economic interactions, facilitating cooperation and reducing transaction costs. The historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior are important for understanding contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) show that the levels of education and democracy in eighteenth century Europe are important determinants of interpersonal trust today, underscoring how historical events can have lasting effects on cultural attitudes toward trust and cooperation.

The interplay between culture and institutions is a critical factor in long-run economic growth. Institutions provide the framework within which economic activities occur, while cultural factors shape the norms and behaviors that influence these activities. Understanding this dynamic relationship is essential for developing effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development.

The role of women and gender dynamics in economic growth highlights how cultural norms and gender relations significantly influence long-run economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, women were indispensable to the reproduction of communities, contributing significantly to economic growth. This dynamic is not unique to Africa; Goldin (2006, p.8) describes a similar transformation in the U.S., where shifts in social norms and educational opportunities altered female labor supply. These colonial constructs may have been as sticky as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. Furthermore they may explain counterfactual evidence from economic dependency models (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This highlights how historical gender dynamics can have enduring effects on economic structures, influencing long-run growth by shaping the division of labor and social norms. These findings underscore that understanding the role of women in economic development requires examining both historical and contemporary cultural factors, which together shape the institutional frameworks that drive growth.

This is particularly evident in the development of a stable market for public debt, which provided significant externalities for private debt markets. Jeffrey Williamson's work further corroborates this thesis by showing that the financial revolution accelerated England's success, suggesting that while other factors might have contributed to growth under different political regimes, they would not have done so as effectively or swiftly. The creation of institutions supporting secure property rights thus emerges as a critical mechanism through which long-run economic development is achieved. This perspective underscores how institutional frameworks can shape the broader trajectory of economic growth by creating conditions that foster both public and private investment.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persist today. These institutions significantly affect current income per capita by shaping the economic environment in ways that either foster or hinder growth. This mechanism underscores how colonial policies, driven by European interests, laid the groundwork for long-term economic trajectories in former colonies. However, this institutional impact is not uniform across all contexts; Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and economic outcomes, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by the specific historical and political contexts in which they operate. The legacy of these institutions continues to shape economic development in many regions today.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and inequality in economic growth are discussed without explicit reference to cultural factors (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This thesis is built on the idea that poor countries were once rich, but their economic fortunes reversed due to institutional changes and colonial policies (Austin 2008, p.25; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This dynamic highlights how unequal institutional frameworks can be sustained over time through elite control. Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25), where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed economic outcomes. This perspective underscores how local adaptations and agency can modify broader institutional frameworks, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, they are not deterministic in shaping economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa focuses on institutional reforms as a means to eliminate poverty, but does not directly address the role of culture in long-run growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory, thus calling for historical investigation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, tied closely to foreign trade and export markets. This suggests that local adaptations and agency can modify broader institutional frameworks and cultural factors play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18). However, while this historical perspective underscores the importance of congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions, it also highlights the challenges posed by patrimonial politics, which can lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. These dynamics illustrate how cultural factors can both support and hinder institutional effectiveness in promoting long-run growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is seen as a rival explanation to broader cultural factors influencing growth. The slave trades caused long-term political instability and weakened states across the continent, resulting in fragmented governance structures that hindered economic development. Nunn argues that slavery is one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1), a finding that complements the broader understanding of how historical events shape contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of mistrust and weakened state structures are critical in explaining Africa's current underdevelopment, as they directly impact societal cohesion and institutional effectiveness.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident through historical and contemporary analyses that highlight the persistence of institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. show how European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they shape the transaction costs and property rights that underpin long-term economic development (North 1989, p.1). The historical evidence from Acemoglu et al. demonstrates that differences in settler mortality led to varying institutional outcomes, which in turn shaped economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North's work complements this by emphasizing the critical role of secure property rights and low transaction costs in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.1).

The persistence of cultural legacies, such as mistrust, further underscores the institutional impact on long-run growth. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly influences contemporary economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin complements this view by highlighting how African responses to their environments, shaped by various technologies and historical contexts, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). The shared finding is that cultural legacies, whether rooted in mistrust or environmental adaptations, have enduring effects on institutional effectiveness and economic outcomes. This legacy of mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon show, accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin's analysis further illustrates how Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the specific historical contexts of England and Africa. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional development provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, highlighting the interconnectedness of public and private institutions in driving economic growth. Austin's work on Africa shows that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Austin 2008, p.25). The role of women in economic development underscores how cultural and institutional factors intersect to shape long-run growth.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which cultural legacies, such as mistrust, directly impact contemporary economic outcomes. While Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), Austin highlights how Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of trust levels in societies affected by the slave trade versus those that were not. This comparison, already used in Nunn and Wantchekon's work, shows that mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
