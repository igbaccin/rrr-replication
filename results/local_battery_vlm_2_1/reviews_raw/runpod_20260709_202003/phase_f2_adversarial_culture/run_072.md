The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography and development studies. At stake intellectually is whether cultural beliefs and values can shape institutional frameworks that facilitate economic growth over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous cultural forces or endogenous economic dynamics. Some scholars contend that culture, through its influence on social norms and collective action, underpins the creation of efficient institutions that promote long-term economic development. Others argue that the efficiency-driven formation of institutions is more critical in determining growth trajectories, with culture playing a secondary role at best.

One prominent position posits that cultural beliefs can significantly influence the structure of economic institutions. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape institutional frameworks and thus affect economic efficiency. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that cultural influences on one institution can have cascading effects across the entire system.

These arguments underscore the importance of considering cultural factors in understanding institutional frameworks and long-run growth. The underlying theme is that cultural beliefs and values can influence the creation and maintenance of institutions that facilitate or hinder economic growth. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to exogenous forces, emphasizing instead the endogenous role of culture in shaping institutional environments. The significance of cultural beliefs is further underscored by Austin (2008, p.25), who notes how Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed over time with available technologies.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The evolution of these institutions was instrumental in establishing a framework where transaction costs declined dramatically. This decline stemmed from the creation of financial instruments, such as those introduced during the English financial revolution, which significantly reduced the cost of transacting. North and Weingast note that the Bank of England's role in managing public debt provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework enhanced the security of property rights over time, creating a stable environment conducive to long-term capital investment and economic growth. The implications of these institutions extend beyond immediate financial gains; they established a foundation for sustained economic development by ensuring that property rights were secure enough to incentivize long-term investments.

The relationship between institutional evolution and economic outcomes is complex. North emphasizes that the neoclassical model's assumptions of zero transaction costs are unrealistic and that institutions matter significantly in an environment with positive transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). This perspective underscores how secure property rights, facilitated by robust institutional frameworks, are essential for fostering economic growth. These institutions emerged through a process influenced by historical contingencies such as the fiscal crises during the Hundred Years War. These crises led to the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, highlighting how political and economic factors intertwined to shape institutional development (North 1989, p.9). The institutions that emerged from these historical contexts played a crucial role in making possible both economic growth and political freedom.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) assert that Europe's early modern economic divergence from Asia is evident in wages and prices. This indicates that purchasing power over tradable goods was greater in Europe than in Asia. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) supports this view by noting that economic development in Europe, as reflected in silver wages, varied across regions and was influenced by urban growth and industrial shifts (van Zanden 1999, p.6). The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 involved geographical factors, along with institutional and economic developments. This finding underscores how regional differences in economic institutions and development patterns contributed to the great divergence, with implications for understanding long-run growth dynamics.

However, this view does not directly address cultural influences on long-run growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) acknowledges that economic growth did not necessarily lead to improvements in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This observation suggests that while economic development was occurring, its benefits were unevenly distributed and did not always translate into widespread prosperity. The implications of this finding are significant for understanding the role of culture in shaping long-run growth trajectories.

The role of women in economic growth and development is significant, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8). Both sources underscore that women's contributions to economic activities have been crucial but often overlooked. The gendered division of labor established during colonial periods in Africa has had lasting impacts on economic production, with pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs continuing to shape contemporary economic dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Similarly, the transformation of women’s employment and education in the U.S. has been driven by a series of exogenous changes that altered both demand and supply factors in the labor market (Goldin 2006, p.8). These shifts were preconditions for broader social changes, such as increased college enrollment and occupational mobility among women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The implications of these findings are profound: they suggest that cultural norms and institutional structures play a pivotal role in determining economic outcomes. However, Goldin notes that the quiet revolution in women's employment and education was not merely evolutionary but involved significant shifts in social expectations and household production technologies (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation underscores how broader societal changes can facilitate economic growth by enhancing women’s participation in productive activities. This dynamic interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks highlights the importance of considering gender dynamics when examining long-run growth trajectories, as it reveals that economic development is not solely driven by market forces but also by deeply embedded social structures.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust that persists and significantly impacts trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural legacy continues to influence contemporary social dynamics. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade has deeply embedded itself in societal norms, affecting interpersonal relationships and collective behaviors. Nunn (2008, p.27) posits that this mistrust has been a significant barrier to economic development by fostering political instability and weak state institutions. This long-term political instability, which is consistent with historical accounts of the slave trades, has resulted in fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). Furthermore, this institutional weakness can be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1), highlighting how cultural norms shaped by historical events continue to influence current economic and political landscapes. The persistence of this mistrust suggests that cultural norms, once established, can have enduring effects that transcend generations and shape long-run growth trajectories.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight how these conflicts provide an alternative explanation to cultural determinants of long-run growth by influencing the development of core economic institutions. They both argue that distributional conflicts create institutions that advantage elites, thus maintaining inequality over time. Pre-industrial Europe exemplifies this dynamic, where serfdom, community structures, and guilds were shaped by struggles between different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, in the New World, societies with greater initial inequality developed institutions that protected elite interests, restricting competition and hindering economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of these unequal institutions over time underscores how distributional conflicts can have lasting effects on economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) also notes that different institutional frameworks are interconnected, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts permeates entire systems. This interdependency means that addressing inequality requires a holistic approach to institutional reform.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the establishment of extractive institutions, which maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth, played a critical role in this reversal; these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who designed them to secure their interests (Austin 2008, p.25; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanism through which colonialism influenced institutional development is complex. High mortality rates among Europeans in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This perspective underscores how initial conditions set by colonial powers can have lasting effects on economic outcomes, even if these conditions are not directly related to cultural factors. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this view by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This suggests that while institutional changes introduced by colonialism are crucial, they do not operate in isolation from other factors such as endogenous responses to environmental conditions. The dynamic interplay between exogenous shocks and endogenous adaptations highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development.

The European family system, according to Laslett, was interdependent with labour markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions; this whole interlinked institutional system contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

This shows how cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant impact on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).
