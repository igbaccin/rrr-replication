The role of culture in dominating institutions is central to understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions, defined as rules structuring interactions, shape economic outcomes by influencing productivity and resource allocation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). At stake intellectually is whether cultural factors can fundamentally alter institutional frameworks, thereby driving or hindering economic development over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around two main positions: one asserting that culture directly influences institutional efficiency and economic growth, while the other posits that institutions evolve independently of cultural factors, driven primarily by economic and political forces. This debate is crucial because it determines whether cultural interventions can be effective tools for economic policy or if they are merely superficial adjustments to deeper structural issues.

Ogilvie and North and Weingast both highlight the importance of institutional frameworks in economic growth, but they differ on the role of culture within these frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. For instance, she notes that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to a decline in guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that cultural shifts can alter institutional frameworks and thereby affect economic outcomes. Their work suggests that institutional changes are driven by political and economic forces rather than cultural beliefs.

The debate over the role of culture in shaping institutions extends to the specific mechanisms through which this influence operates. Austin and Acemoglu et al. offer complementary insights into how exogenous factors, including cultural elements, can shape institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors, such as beliefs about health and mortality, can indirectly influence institutional frameworks by shaping the economic and political environments. Austin's work complements this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This implies that cultural factors may have played a role in shaping institutional evolution over time, but their influence is complex and multifaceted. Austin suggests that African responses to environmental challenges, influenced by available technologies, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the interdependencies within institutional frameworks. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both recognize the importance of studying entire institutional frameworks rather than individual institutions in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie notes that the European family system was interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This suggests that cultural factors may influence multiple aspects of an institutional framework simultaneously, leading to complex and interconnected effects on economic growth.

The role of trust as a cultural factor in shaping institutions is highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective suggests that historical cultural factors can have long-lasting effects on institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. North also emphasizes the importance of trust in economic growth, noting that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This suggests that cultural factors, such as trust, can influence institutional frameworks by shaping economic interactions and reducing transaction costs.

Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing effective policies aimed at promoting economic growth through institutional reform.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) demonstrate that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal to securing property rights and fostering economic growth. This nuanced understanding underscores how secure property rights, underpinned by robust institutional frameworks, can drive long-term economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Broadberry and Gupta (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) argue that economic development in pre-industrial Europe was shaped by complex interactions between institutional frameworks. Institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were embedded within a broader system of distributional conflicts and urbanization patterns, which influenced economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that these institutions were efficient and the result of distributional conflicts. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) suggests that economic development and changes in living standards have a complex relationship; periods of growth do not always improve living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This implies that while institutional frameworks were crucial for economic development, their impact on long-term growth and living standards varied significantly.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) examine how economic institutions and inequality have shaped development paths in different regions. They argue that the impact of economic institutions on growth is shaped by historical contexts, particularly those involving inequality. This mechanism is evident when considering the role of institutions in determining access to resources and opportunities. In societies with high levels of inequality, institutional frameworks often favor elites, limiting competition and perpetuating disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For example, in New World societies, initial factor endowments led to extreme wealth inequalities from the outset, which influenced the development of institutions that reinforced these disparities over time.

This dynamic is evident in how economic institutions evolve to reflect power structures. Austin (2008, p.25) suggests a more nuanced view of historical trajectories, rejecting simplistic geographical determinants of economic development. He highlights how endogenous responses to environmental conditions shaped institutional paths, reflecting the interests of dominant groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who emphasize that differing factor endowments in New World colonies led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, affecting institutional development and long-term economic outcomes.

The relationship between institutions and growth is complex. This implies that while economic institutions play a crucial role in shaping development paths, their impact on long-term growth is mediated by historical contexts and power dynamics within societies.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) contend that the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were ideas and ideologies. These scholars challenge interest-only theories by highlighting the role of cultural shifts in fostering economic growth. McCloskey's narrative emphasizes the ideological underpinnings, suggesting that new rhetoric created new circumstances, which then reinforced this rhetoric (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This dynamic is evident in the "Franco-French War," where cultural struggles shaped economic outcomes. Mokyr complements this perspective by identifying useful knowledge as a crucial driver of economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe facilitated economic development, underscoring the importance of shared intellectual movements (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr also notes the limitations of codified knowledge, emphasizing the role of tacit knowledge and personal transmission in spreading useful skills (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This highlights a nuanced mechanism: while ideas and ideologies drive economic change, the practical application of this knowledge requires both codified and tacit forms. This interplay between ideological shifts and the dissemination of knowledge suggests that cultural factors are integral to long-run growth, shaping how institutions and interests operate within societies.

This interplay between ideology and knowledge has profound implications for understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that fundamentally drove long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These changes were shaped by European mortality rates in colonies, which determined the type of institutions established and their persistence over time. High mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This mechanism is evident in the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutional quality, which in turn affects economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al.'s work underscores that these institutional legacies persist, influencing contemporary income levels. The extractive nature of these institutions was a deliberate design to secure political power and maximize rents for the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

This perspective is crucial as it emphasizes that the extractive nature of these institutions was an equilibrium outcome, shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over long-term growth. The implication here is that the institutional framework established during colonial rule has had lasting effects on economic development, with former colonies still grappling with the legacy of these extractive institutions. This suggests that while cultural factors may influence how knowledge is disseminated and applied, the institutional framework set by colonial powers plays a dominant role in shaping long-term growth trajectories.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that Africa's slave trade left an enduring legacy of mistrust within societies. The trade undermined the development of strong, centralized states by fostering political instability, which in turn limited the ability to build effective institutions (Nunn 2008, p.1). This institutional weakness persists and contributes to contemporary economic underdevelopment. The slave trade depleted human capital and eroded social cohesion.
