The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is an intellectually contentious question because it engages with the very nature of economic development and its determinants. At stake are rival explanations for why some societies prosper while others stagnate. One position emphasises the primacy of cultural beliefs and values, arguing that these shape economic institutions in ways that either facilitate or hinder growth. Another position focuses on the institutional framework itself, viewing it as the primary driver of long-run economic outcomes. The tension between these views is rooted in their differing emphases on endogenous versus exogenous factors in economic development.

The disagreement centres around whether cultural beliefs are an antecedent to the formation of institutions that drive growth or if institutions themselves are the key factor. This debate extends to how different frameworks can influence institutional development and the extent to which cultural values, such as trust and reciprocity, impact economic outcomes. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with some scholars arguing that cultural beliefs can directly influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency. Others contend that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as secure property rights, is crucial for fostering long-run growth. This tension highlights the need to understand how cultural values interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic performance over time. The debate also involves considering whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors like mortality rates or endogenous ones like cultural norms and beliefs. Understanding this dynamic can provide insights into why certain societies have achieved sustained economic growth while others have not. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The English financial revolution exemplifies this dynamic. North (1989, p.1) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional change was pivotal in establishing a long-term capital market, which is fundamental for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast elaborate that this financial revolution provided a significant positive externality by facilitating the development of private debt markets alongside public debt markets. However, North (1989, p.1) acknowledges that other forces might have led to similar outcomes under different circumstances; the role of institutions was critical in accelerating and solidifying these changes.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). She and Austin both argue that the role of cultural beliefs is often overstated. Ogilvie also notes that these cultural explanations may not fully account for the broader institutional frameworks and their interdependencies. She argues that institutions like serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency or culture.

Austin supports this view by critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). Austin argues that African responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, ultimately changed these environments. This dynamic process suggests a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution than cultural explanations alone can provide.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and useful knowledge were the primary drivers of economic growth in Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey illustrates this by critiquing interest-only theories, asserting that institutional change is not merely a matter of calculation based on elite interests. This perspective is supported by Mokyr's emphasis on the diffusion of useful knowledge across borders, which facilitated economic growth. Mokyr highlights that much of the valuable knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills rather than codified in written form (Mokyr 2005, p.14), suggesting that personal transmission and training were crucial for its dissemination.

McCloskey further contends that the cultural struggle in Europe, exemplified by France's "Franco-French War," influenced economic growth trajectories. This dynamic interplay between rhetoric and circumstances highlights how ideologies shaped economic outcomes. McCloskey illustrates this with the example of Spain, where conservative reasons throttled economic growth despite examples set by other European countries (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr’s observation that useful knowledge was widely diffused within Europe supports McCloskey's argument by showing that cultural and institutional barriers did not impede the spread of knowledge. This diffusion of ideas and knowledge across borders underscores how ideologies and useful knowledge collectively drove economic growth in Northwestern Europe during this period.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality shaped economic development by advantaging elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The institutional environment was profoundly influenced by initial factor endowments, which varied significantly across different colonies. For instance, regions with abundant land and natural resources relative to labor saw the emergence of highly unequal societies from the outset. The persistence of these institutional differences over time highlights how initial conditions can have long-lasting effects on economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia further underscores this point. They argue that the great divergence was already underway before 1800, with prosperous parts of Asia resembling stagnating regions in Europe rather than the developing northwestern parts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This suggests that institutional differences rooted in historical contexts played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic development, independent of cultural factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of economic outcomes in former colonies. They contend that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions, which persist and significantly affect current income per capita. This perspective emphasizes that the persistence of extractive institutions can be attributed to powerful groups who design them to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show how these institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust is not only a legacy of historical exploitation but also a significant barrier to economic development. The mechanism through which this occurs is multifaceted: mistrust hinders social cohesion and collective action, both crucial for sustainable growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrates that the slave trades led to long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political fragmentation further exacerbates mistrust by undermining institutional effectiveness and public service delivery. The downstream implication is that the legacy of slavery continues to shape Africa's economic trajectory through its impact on social capital and state capacity. Nunn also notes that this history does not exclusively determine current underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.1). Other factors, such as colonial rule and recent economic policies, must be considered alongside the enduring effects of slavery to fully understand Africa's economic challenges today.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital illustrates how cultural norms such as trust and reciprocity can enhance economic performance by fostering institutions that promote growth and accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. The development of secure property rights, facilitated by institutional changes like the creation of the Bank of England, exemplifies how cultural shifts can underpin long-term economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further emphasize the importance of institutions governing public choice in fostering economic growth. The financial revolution in seventeenth-century England highlights how institutional changes can create positive externalities that support both public and private debt markets, thereby promoting economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The role of culture in dominating institutions has complexity. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks over time. Acemoglu et al.'s work on European mortality rates and colonial institutions further illustrates this complexity, showing that differences in mortality led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting how historical cultural factors can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. This evidence complements Nunn's findings on the legacy of slavery in Africa, where mistrust shaped by the slave trade continues to influence contemporary social capital and state capacity (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The underlying point of agreement is that culture shapes institutions fundamentally, which then affect long-run growth. In particular, North asserts that "institutions matter" (North 1989, p.9). Yet the precise mechanisms by which culture influences these institutions remain a subject of debate. Austin highlights how the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25), while Acemoglu et al.'s work on European mortality rates and colonial institutions shows that differences in mortality led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement involves examining how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks over time. This can be done by comparing the long-term effects of different colonial policies on economic development across regions with varying mortality rates and levels of social capital, as measured by trust and reciprocity norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
