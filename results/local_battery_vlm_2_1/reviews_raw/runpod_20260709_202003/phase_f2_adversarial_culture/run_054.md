Culture shapes institutions that govern economic growth over the long run. This question is central to understanding the persistence of inequality and prosperity across societies. The disagreement focuses on whether culture directly influences institutions to fundamentally determine economic outcomes or if other factors such as geography, mortality rates, or institutional legacies play a more dominant role. One position emphasizes that cultural norms and values embedded within societies drive the formation of institutions that either promote or hinder growth. Another position suggests that exogenous factors like disease environments and colonial policies are the primary determinants of institutional development and economic performance.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that mortality rates faced by European settlers significantly influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which have persisted to affect current income per capita. This perspective highlights how initial conditions shaped by disease environments can lead to enduring institutional frameworks. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.30)  demonstrate how the evolution of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. These studies underscore that exogenous factors and historical events are critical in shaping institutions that govern long-run economic performance. Culture also plays a role in influencing institutional outcomes.

The role of trust within societies is another key aspect of cultural influence on institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show how the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and affects economic development. This finding complements North's argument that secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Both perspectives highlight how cultural norms can either support or undermine institutional frameworks that drive economic performance.

The relationship between culture and institutions is further complicated by the dynamic nature of historical processes. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths, oversimplifies causation. This perspective suggests that cultural responses to environmental conditions over time are crucial in shaping institutional outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These studies highlight how historical paths and cultural responses to different environments can lead to varied institutional outcomes.

One theme connecting these perspectives is that culture fundamentally influences institutional development, which in turn governs long-run economic growth. The interplay between exogenous factors like disease environments and endogenous cultural responses shapes the institutions that drive economic performance over time.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional evolution was a catalyst for long-term economic growth and not merely a response to existing conditions. This institutional change provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets, further stimulating economic activity. This institutional framework ensured secure property rights and fostered a climate of economic stability and growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The implications are profound: without these institutional changes, England's economic trajectory would have been slower and less decisive. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in how they create a stable environment for financial transactions and secure property rights.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective is supported by Mokyr, who posits that the growth of useful knowledge underpins long-run economic growth. The cultural struggle for new ideas and ideologies fostered an environment where innovation thrived, despite resistance from entrenched interests. McCloskey highlights how countries like France and Spain struggled with conservative forces that throttled economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that the diffusion of knowledge was facilitated through various means such as periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56).

The dissemination of ideas and ideologies played a crucial role in fostering an environment conducive to economic growth. This is evident in how tacit knowledge, skills and techniques passed down through personal training, moved across Europe, contributing significantly to the Industrial Revolution (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr explains that access to knowledge depended on written records as well as personal transmission and training, underscoring the importance of cultural exchange in disseminating useful knowledge. This mechanism highlights how ideas and ideologies drove economic growth by creating a climate where innovation and technological advancement were valued and promoted. The implication is clear: without the widespread adoption and diffusion of new ideas and ideologies, the economic trajectory of Northwestern Europe would have been markedly different.

Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established, extractive rather than inclusive, and these institutions persisted over time, shaping current economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. exploit differences in settler mortality as exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance, finding a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This institutional framework is crucial because it suggests that the design of colonial institutions was aimed at extracting rents for European colonists and not maximizing long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implication here is that the persistence of extractive institutions, shaped by initial settler experiences, has had a lasting impact on economic development in former colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s focus on mortality rates as a determinant of institutional quality overlooks other potential cultural and historical factors that might also influence long-run growth trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) shared finding that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous version of what they call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed economic development trajectories. This idea contrasts with the simple geography thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) rejects this static view and emphasizes that institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others. The implications of this dynamic view are significant because they highlight the importance of considering multiple historical periods and pathways when examining economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) demonstrate that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced by 1800. The mechanism behind this divergence is rooted in institutional changes that drove agricultural investment and productivity, particularly in northwestern Europe. This regional disparity highlights how early modern developments set the stage for later growth trajectories. van Zanden (1999, p.6) underscores these institutional shifts by noting that silver wages reflected broader economic trends across Europe, with England and London emerging as pivotal centers of development. The implication is that institutional frameworks influenced regional economic performance long before industrialization. The role of culture in shaping these institutions remains an open question.

The slave trade's impact on African development underscores how historical events shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, while Nunn (2008, p.27) shows how this mistrust contributed to political instability and weakened state structures. This mistrust has tangible economic consequences, as it erodes social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. The legacy of the slave trade thus manifests in contemporary African economies through reduced trust and weakened institutions, which hinder economic development.

The historical mistrust engendered by the slave trade persists in shaping African development trajectories and illustrates how cultural attitudes shaped by past events continue to influence present-day governance and economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of this culture of mistrust is a direct result of the enduring impact of historical traumas on contemporary economic outcomes. This dynamic highlights how institutions, shaped by historical events, play a crucial role in determining long-run growth.
