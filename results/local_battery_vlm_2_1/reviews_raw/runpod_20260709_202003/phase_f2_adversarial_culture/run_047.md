The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins sustained economic prosperity over centuries. Some scholars contend that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering growth. Others argue that geographic factors, such as mortality rates, are more decisive in shaping institutions that either promote or hinder economic development. Additionally, some researchers propose that the slave trade has left a lasting imprint on African culture, creating a legacy of mistrust that impedes economic progress. This disagreement highlights the complex interplay between cultural norms, institutional frameworks, and long-term economic outcomes.

The debate over whether cultural values directly influence institutions is central to understanding economic growth. Ogilvie argues that efficiency explanations are insufficient for understanding pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions. She contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. This perspective challenges the idea that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today. They suggest that this cultural legacy accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, implying a direct link between historical trauma and contemporary economic outcomes. This stream underscores the enduring impact of cultural norms on institutions and economic performance. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions are pivotal in driving long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). North (1989, p.1) establishes this by showing that the English financial revolution of the 17th century, which included the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically lowered transaction costs. This development reflects increased security in property rights over time, a critical factor for both capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a significant positive externality for the development of private debt markets. The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt quickly extended to private operations, highlighting how institutional changes can have widespread economic impacts (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). However, while North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions are necessary for growth, he also acknowledges that other forces could ultimately lead to success, albeit more slowly and less decisively. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, they may not be the sole determinant of long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both engage in the debate on economic divergence by focusing on institutions and geographical factors as key drivers of development. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights how different historical periods and paths complicate simple causal narratives, emphasizing that the role of institutions is more complex than merely securing private property rights (Austin 2008, p.2).

Austin also emphasizes the importance of period-specific relationships between institutions and growth, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic development can vary over time (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.15) finding that institutional changes in Europe led to investment in agriculture, which in turn drove high agricultural labor productivity. This productivity did not necessarily translate into abundant grain supplies or low food prices due to labor shifts into other sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). In the context of long-run growth, these findings suggest that while institutions are crucial for economic development, their effects are contingent on various factors including technological advancements and endogenous responses to environmental conditions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) examine how economic institutions and distributional conflicts shape pre-industrial societies. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective challenges exogenous cultural theories, which posit that cultural beliefs directly influence economic institutions and efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this framework by showing that New World societies with greater inequality had government policies that advantaged elites and restricted competition. The institutional frameworks of these societies, characterized by high levels of inequality in wealth and political power, persisted over time and affected the course of development. These findings suggest that institutions designed to manage distributional conflicts can have long-lasting effects on economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. She notes that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, entire institutional frameworks must be examined. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) work on Latin American development, which highlights the importance of understanding how differences in institutions arise and persist over time. Instead, these studies focus on how institutional frameworks, shaped by distributional conflicts, influence economic development. The downstream implication is that understanding the interplay between institutions and distributional conflicts can provide insights into long-term economic performance. This perspective does not address the role of cultural factors in shaping these institutions or their effects on growth.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth and income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. argue that mortality rates faced by Europeans led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), which significantly affected current income per capita. The institutional framework established during colonial times, shaped by European settlements and early measures of institutions, persists today and influences economic performance. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutions can be extractive even if they do not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions are often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than fostering economic development. For instance, European colonists might have established institutions that prioritized their immediate gains over the long-term economic benefits of the colonies. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism can be both enduring and detrimental to growth if it is not aligned with developmental goals.

The extractive nature of these institutions often results from the interests of powerful groups who benefit from maintaining the status quo. This mechanism highlights how colonial legacies can shape current economic trajectories by influencing the development of institutions that either promote or hinder growth.

The persistence of these institutions underscores their role in sustaining long-run economic outcomes, even if they were not originally designed for such purposes.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic change in Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective is built on the mechanism where cultural changes lead to a shift in rhetoric, which in turn shapes new circumstances. McCloskey (2015, p.10) illustrates this through the "Franco-French War," where the cultural struggle influenced economic growth trajectories by altering the rhetoric and thereby the circumstances (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This rhetorical shift is about a deeper ideological transformation that permeates societal values and institutional changes driven by elite interests. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further elaborates on this by highlighting how useful knowledge, often tacit and embodied in skills, drives economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of this knowledge across Europe facilitated the Great Enrichment, suggesting that the spread of ideas and ideologies was crucial for economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies this by noting that while national differences existed, the commonalities in the dissemination of useful knowledge were more significant (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This implies that the role of culture in dominating institutions is less about national idiosyncrasies and more about the broader intellectual movements that transcended borders.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade was a catalyst for the development of a culture of mistrust in Africa. This cultural shift is rooted in the historical context of widespread political instability, which resulted from long-term effects of slave exports on state centralization and fragmentation. Nunn (2008, p.27; 2008, p.1) further elaborates that this institutional weakness has persisted over time, contributing to contemporary corruption levels as a legacy of Africa's precolonial history. The mistrust engendered by these historical events continues to influence current social norms, which in turn affect economic outcomes.

The effects of the slave trade on African culture are multifaceted. While the mistrust engendered by the slave trade is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of Africa's economic development. The broader intellectual movements that facilitated the Great Enrichment in Europe also played a role in shaping African economic outcomes. For instance, the diffusion of useful knowledge and ideologies across Europe highlights how cultural factors can transcend national boundaries. This suggests that while mistrust is a significant cultural factor, it is part of a larger web of influences on long-run growth.

The legacy of the slave trade's impact on trust levels and economic development underscores the need for further investigation into the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. The persistence of mistrust as a historical legacy points to the enduring effects of cultural factors on contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks further underscores the role of culture in shaping economic development. Ogilvie highlights that individual institutions are not isolated but rather part of an interconnected system, with examples from pre-industrial Europe illustrating how cultural beliefs and values can influence institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and reduced transaction costs as critical factors (North 1989, p.9; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical development of financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, exemplifies how institutional changes driven by cultural shifts can foster economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further evident in the evolution of public choice institutions. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England was crucial for securing property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical evidence shows how cultural factors, through institutional changes, can drive long-run economic development.
