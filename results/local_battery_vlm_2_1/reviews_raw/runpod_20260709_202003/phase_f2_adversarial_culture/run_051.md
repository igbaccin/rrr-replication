The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth remains a contentious issue among scholars; it is central to understanding how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are fundamental drivers of institutional change and economic growth or merely peripheral influences. Some argue that cultural beliefs and values directly shape the institutions that govern economic activity, thereby influencing growth trajectories. Others contend that institutions are primarily shaped by other factors such as geographic conditions or political dynamics, with culture playing a secondary role at best. Additionally, some scholars posit that cultural factors may interact with other antecedent variables to trigger institutional changes that ultimately affect long-run growth. The central question is whether cultural values and beliefs are essential in the formation of institutions that sustain economic development over time, or if they are overshadowed by more immediate determinants such as geography or political structures. This review explores these positions through distinct streams of scholarship, each offering unique perspectives on the relationship between culture, institutions, and long-run growth.

One stream of research highlights how cultural beliefs can significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie points out that exogenous cultural theories often explain institutional frameworks by focusing on micro-level interactions. For instance, Richardson's analysis suggests that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules efficiently; this dynamic shifted with the Protestant Reformation, leading to a decline in guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of social capital further underscores how cultural beliefs and values, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These perspectives highlight that cultural factors can fundamentally shape the institutional landscape. This stream complements Acemoglu et al.'s work on how European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interplay between these cultural and geographic determinants underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run growth.

Another stream focuses on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's broader argument about how institutions matter in economic growth; he posits that secure property rights and low transaction costs are crucial for long-term economic development. North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that these institutional frameworks are about political delivery of secure private property rights. This stream of research suggests that cultural factors, while important, must be considered within a broader framework of distributional conflicts and political dynamics.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illuminated by examining how institutions governing public choice evolved. North and Weingast highlight the critical role of the financial revolution in England's long-run economic success, arguing that secure property rights were essential for fostering growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective aligns with Austin’s critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This stream underscores how cultural factors interact with other antecedent variables to shape institutions that drive long-run growth.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the role of African women in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's analysis of how cultural beliefs can shape institutional frameworks; for instance, the matrilineal society in Asante highlights how women’s roles were indispensable to community reproduction. Akyeampong and Fofack note that colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This stream illustrates how cultural beliefs about gender roles can influence economic institutions, thereby affecting long-run growth.

The slave trade's legacy in Africa includes a cultural shift towards mistrust that persists today and affects trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) identify this mistrust as a significant barrier to economic development. This dynamic is further supported by the work of Nunn (2008, p.27), who argues that African slave trades have had adverse effects on economic development, including the weakening of state institutions and increased political instability. The negative impact of slavery on state centralization (Nunn 2008, p.27) implies a broader institutional erosion; this process has been shown to correlate with lower levels of trust in contemporary societies. This mechanism extends beyond direct economic impacts, as weakened states struggle to implement effective policies and maintain lawful order. However, the extent to which these factors directly influence long-run growth remains complex, as Nunn (2008, p.27) suggests that precolonial origins might not solely account for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). The interaction between cultural mistrust and institutional weakness highlights a multifaceted view of economic development in Africa.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts within societies fundamentally shape economic institutions in ways that can persist over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These authors propose the structure of economic institutions is deeply influenced by power dynamics between different social groups. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial inequalities in wealth and power led to institutional frameworks that reinforced these disparities. In particular, their work highlights how the extreme inequality in factor endowments, such as land relative to labor, influenced the development of institutions that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, including those regulating private interactions and property rights, were shaped by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie acknowledges the complexity of institutional frameworks, noting different institutions are often interdependent within a broader system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency means that understanding how distributional conflicts shape economic institutions requires considering the entire institutional framework rather than isolating individual components. The enduring impact of these conflicts on long-run growth is about persistent effects of institutional structures favoring certain groups over others.

Economic divergence in Europe is rooted in structural changes that unfolded over centuries and were shaped by multiple factors such as urban development and wage gaps, which contributed to the economic growth of specific regions (Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia was driven by differences in economic institutions and productivity. The authors observe that while grain wages were comparable across advanced parts of India, China, and north-western Europe, silver wages were substantially lower in Asia (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This wage disparity reflected broader structural changes that favored certain regions over others: labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services in developing parts of north-western Europe, leading to higher agricultural productivity without a corresponding abundance of grain or low food prices. This highlights how the economic divergence in Europe was influenced by both institutional changes and demographic shifts. Pamuk's analysis also underscores that structural changes were significant, but they did not fully account for the long-term impacts on economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Hopkins 2009, p.8) jointly argue that African economic performance is deeply rooted in historical institutional patterns, which have shaped contemporary development trajectories. These authors highlight that the gradual evolution of institutions over centuries has been a crucial factor in Africa's economic growth, with precolonial history playing a significant role in shaping current institutional frameworks. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the long-term impacts of institutional changes on economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) builds on this by arguing that effective modern institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions, enhancing government legitimacy and fostering development.

The congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions is critical for sustainable economic growth in Africa. When there is a high degree of congruence, it underpins the legitimacy of governments and enhances their capacity to implement institutions that drive development. Low levels of congruence can lead to patrimonial politics, regionalism, centrism, and corruption, which hinder economic progress. This dynamic illustrates how historical institutional patterns continue to influence contemporary economic performance in Africa. The interplay between modern and indigenous institutions highlights the need for policies that respect and integrate local institutional frameworks to achieve long-term growth.

The Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge are distinct phenomena that preceded and facilitated economic growth but do not directly address culture as the fundamental cause (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). De Vries (1994, p.18) emphasizes that the Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, laid a crucial foundation for later industrialization. The Industrious Revolution transformed household economies by integrating more family members into market activities, thereby increasing overall productivity. This shift involved significant changes in societal behaviors and attitudes towards work.

The dissemination of practical knowledge across Europe facilitated technological innovations that were essential for industrial development. Knowledge, whether codified or tacit, played a pivotal role in enhancing productivity and fostering economic growth. This growth was a broader European phenomenon, underscoring the interconnectedness of useful knowledge diffusion.

Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that accessibility of useful knowledge depended on both written records and personal transmission, suggesting cultural factors influenced its spread. The interplay between cultural norms and the dissemination of practical knowledge highlights how societal attitudes towards work and innovation can significantly impact economic outcomes. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with economic development. Cultural norms and values significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. reveal that differences in European mortality rates led to distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the enduring impact of cultural factors on institutional development.

The interdependency between cultural norms and economic institutions is further highlighted by the role of social capital. Ogilvie discusses Putnam's theory of 'social capital, ' where trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

Cultural factors also play a significant role in shaping gender dynamics within economic institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production, demonstrating the enduring impact of cultural beliefs on economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need to consider dynamic, partly endogenous versions of geography in understanding economic development (Austin 2008, p.25).

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is a shared finding among scholars. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1). Austin discusses how the 'new institutionalist' approach emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.25). The enduring impact of cultural factors on institutional development is evident in the interplay between economic institutions and societal attitudes towards work, innovation, and gender roles. This dynamic underscores the importance of considering both economic and cultural factors when examining the fundamental causes of long-run growth.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The remaining disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs or institutional frameworks are more determinative. Akyeampong and Fofack show that colonial constructs significantly impacted gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4), while North and Weingast reveal how institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The specific historical evidence of the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, settles this disagreement. This period shows that institutions are shaped by cultural beliefs but also exert their own independent influence on economic growth through secure property rights.
