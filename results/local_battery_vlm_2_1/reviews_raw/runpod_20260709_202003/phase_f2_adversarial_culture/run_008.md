The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can illuminate why some societies thrive economically while others stagnate. At the heart of the debate lies a disagreement over whether cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic institutions, fostering growth, or if other factors, such as institutional frameworks and extractive practices, are more decisive. Some scholars argue that cultural norms shape economic institutions by encouraging trust, reciprocity, and coordinated actions, which in turn facilitate economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others posit that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as secure property rights, is crucial for economic development and that culture plays a secondary role (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This disagreement underscores the need to examine how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive long-run growth.

North (1989, p.1) elaborates on this by showing that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, thus enhancing security in long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). The development of a stable market for public debt provided positive externalities for private debt markets. The institutions underpinning this growth were critical for economic expansion.

Institutions such as the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs. This institutional framework enabled England to achieve economic success more rapidly and decisively than it might have without these developments. This nuance highlights the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and other factors in economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.56) and de Vries (1994, p.18) share a posture that useful knowledge is pivotal to economic growth (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr (2005, p.56) asserts that the growth of useful knowledge, which encompasses both codified and tacit forms, drives economic development. This useful knowledge includes technological advancements and intellectual innovations that foster long-term economic progress. De Vries (1994, p.18) illustrates this through the Industrious Revolution, where increased labor input and new consumption patterns preceded industrialization (de Vries 1994, p.5). The growth of useful knowledge facilitates a shift in labor allocation, moving from traditional household tasks to market-oriented activities. This transformation is crucial because it releases labor reserves, enabling economic productivity gains.

De Vries (1994, p.18) notes that this process involved the redistribution of consumption goods and services, as households increasingly relied on market- and government-supplied resources (de Vries 1994, p.18). Mokyr's assertion about useful knowledge implies that economic growth depends on both institutional frameworks and the dissemination and application of new ideas (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective underscores the role of technological change in shaping economic development, highlighting how advancements in useful knowledge can drive productivity gains.

This process presents challenges. Mokyr (2005, p.56) points out that much of the useful knowledge during this period was tacit, meaning it was embedded in skills and practices rather than written down (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge required personal transmission and training, which could limit its diffusion. The accessibility of codified versus tacit knowledge underscores a critical condition for economic growth: the effective transfer of useful knowledge relies on both formal education systems and informal learning networks.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) argues that ideas and ideologies were primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) shares this finding by noting that institutional changes are a critical factor in economic growth dynamics.

McCloskey's argument hinges on the role of cultural shifts, such as the "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable" rhetoric that drove innovation and growth. This rhetoric fostered an environment where new ideas could flourish, leading to significant economic advancements (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Abramovitz complements this by highlighting how social competence for exploiting advanced technologies varied over time and across regions (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). The interplay between cultural ideologies and technological adoption underscores a key mechanism: the effective dissemination of ideas relies on both cultural receptivity and institutional frameworks that facilitate their application.

The process is not linear. This limitation suggests that while ideas and ideologies can drive change, the rate and extent of economic growth also depend on the societal capacity to absorb and implement these ideas. The complexity of this process highlights a crucial condition for long-run growth: the alignment of cultural ideologies with institutional frameworks that support technological advancement.

The role of women in economic growth underscores the significance of gender dynamics and cultural norms in shaping long-term economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) demonstrate that African women's contributions to economic growth were crucial during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the role of matrilineal societies in sustaining communities. Women's bloodlines defined wealth transmission, illustrating how gender roles shaped economic structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Goldin (2006, p.8) extends this perspective by showing that shifts in women’s employment and education in the U.S. were driven by changes in social norms and expectations regarding future work. These shifts included increased demand for female office workers and institutional changes that made part-time work available, altering labor supply dynamics (Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin describes a quiet revolution that altered women’s family and career trajectories as well as societal views on gender roles. This transformation had profound implications for economic growth, as it expanded the workforce and changed occupational structures. Therefore, while changes in women's roles can drive economic growth, the enduring influence of cultural beliefs may hinder progress unless actively addressed.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental cause of long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persist today and significantly affect current income per capita. These findings reveal an important mechanism by which colonial institutions shaped long-run economic performance: differences in settler mortality rates led to varying institutional frameworks, which in turn impacted economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Acemoglu et al. support this perspective; they argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes (Acemoglu et al.). Nevertheless, Acemoglu et al. caution that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development; instead, they might be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, such as European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overgeneralizes by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). This critique underscores that the thesis oversimplifies causation in economic development. For instance, African societies' responses to their environments involved dynamic adaptations and not static environmental constraints. These adaptations included leveraging available technologies to alter economic trajectories significantly over time. The African story suggests a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with local conditions. Bryant posits that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent, noting that cultural norms and social structures play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes (Bryant 2006, p.4). This perspective aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution. By focusing on short-term processes, revisionists risk missing how long-term cultural and institutional legacies influence economic development. The interplay between culture and institutions involves deeply embedded historical factors. For example, legal-juridical developments that extended rights and protections to persons and property were pivotal in Europe's transition to modernity (Bryant 2006, p.4). These developments highlight how cultural norms and institutional frameworks can create sustained economic growth over centuries. Austin’s critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.25) is further reinforced by Bryant's assertion that effective causality involves a complex interplay of technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms (Bryant 2006, p.35). This interaction underscores how the long-term impacts of institutional changes are contingent on broader historical contexts. The revisionist view, which often dismisses the role of culture in economic development, fails to account for these deeper historical factors. These insights suggest that a more comprehensive understanding of long-run growth requires acknowledging the multifaceted ways culture dominates institutions over time.

Ogilvie's and Sokoloff and Engerman's research on distributional conflicts and New World societies demonstrate how institutions are molded by the interests of dominant groups. These scholars argue that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions than efficiency does (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). One specific mechanism through which these distributional conflicts operate is via the control over political and economic policies that elites exert to maintain their dominance. This control can be seen in how European guilds were tools for maintaining elite power as well as efficient structures (Ogilvie). Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman observe that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality over time. This dynamic suggests that cultural norms reinforcing elite dominance can perpetuate institutional structures that hinder economic growth, as seen in the persistence of unequal institutions in Latin America despite changes in other factors.

Ogilvie also acknowledges that institutions are interdependent within a wider framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Cultural norms and distributional conflicts operate as part of a complex web of interactions. For instance, the European family system was interlinked with various markets and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. This interconnectedness highlights how cultural norms can influence multiple aspects of institutional development, reinforcing the idea that culture plays a fundamental role in shaping institutions over the long run.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) highlight that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. This is evident in the economic performance and urban development trends of the time. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) provides a nuanced perspective on this, noting that there existed "a number of studies of the long run performance of the British economy between 1068 and 1800" (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The divergence highlights how economic development in different regions can lead to varying standards of living. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) point out that "in the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change is usually seen as bringing about investment in agriculture, leading in turn to high agricultural labour productivity" (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This economic development did not necessarily translate into abundant grain and low food prices. Instead, it facilitated a shift where labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services. Thus, the economic performance before 1800 shows that even in regions with similar wage levels, significant differences in economic development could occur due to varied institutional changes and investment patterns. This indicates that cultural norms influencing these institutions played a critical role in shaping long-run growth trajectories.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing and the Black Death initiated structural changes that significantly influenced long-term economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; Pamuk 2007, p.24). The former technology enhanced agricultural productivity on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce livestock damage, while the latter event sparked institutional change in guilds and fostered rural industries. These technological and structural changes in agriculture illustrate how specific innovations can drive economic growth over the long run. The role of culture in shaping these institutions remains underexplored within this cluster. While Hornbeck (2010, p.6) highlights the importance of social norms in encouraging ranchers to control cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.6), Pamuk (2007, p.24) shows that labour-saving technological innovations were a direct consequence of scarcity and high costs of labour (Pamuk 2007, p.5). The interaction between technology, institutions, and cultural norms is critical for understanding the fundamental causes of long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade was instrumental in shaping cultural attitudes such as mistrust. The African slave trades had a profound impact on economic development by causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This historical legacy of state weakness is an important determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization can be linked to the historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability. This instability, in turn, resulted in weakened and fragmented states, which has had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development.

The cultural attitudes shaped by the slave trade have persisted over time, influencing social capital and interpersonal trust. This mistrust is a critical factor in understanding the long-term economic and social outcomes in Africa, as it directly affects the development of social capital and interpersonal relationships. In essence, the historical impact of the slave trade has created enduring cultural norms that continue to shape contemporary African societies.

The new economic history of Africa underscores that institutional variables are pivotal in shaping development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that African institutions must be understood as the product of a long historical process. These institutional variables are deeply influenced by cultural norms and historical legacies. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further elaborates that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth when congruent with indigenous ones. This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and their capacity to foster development-oriented institutions. Low levels of congruence can lead to conditions favoring patrimonial politics, regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. This suggests that cultural factors, rooted in historical and precolonial contexts, play a critical role in shaping the efficacy of institutional reforms. These findings underscore the importance of considering cultural dimensions when implementing institutional changes to achieve long-term economic growth in Africa.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth by influencing the design and effectiveness of economic frameworks. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates how cultural beliefs about trust, reciprocity, and social networks can foster "civic" cultures that encourage institutional manifestations favoring growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing the role of secure property rights enabled by effective political models (North 1989, p.9). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9) underscores how cultural norms can either align with or resist institutional frameworks, affecting their ability to promote long-term economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further support this by showing that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were critical for fostering secure property rights essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical evidence demonstrates the interdependence of culture and institutions in driving economic outcomes.

Institutional frameworks are not isolated entities; they are interconnected with broader cultural contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that individual institutions within a wider framework are interdependent, influencing each other's effectiveness (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency is also evident in Acemoglu et al.'s work, which shows that favourable contracting and property rights institutions are partially interdependent, affecting economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) shares this view by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, emphasizing the need to consider different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.25) reinforces the idea that cultural contexts shape institutional frameworks in complex ways. The evolution of institutions is a dynamic interaction between cultural norms and economic structures.

Cultural factors rooted in historical and precolonial contexts are critical for understanding the efficacy of institutional reforms. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the role of gender dynamics within cultural frameworks (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth by emphasizing secure property rights as a critical dimension for long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). The shared finding (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; North 1989, p.9) illustrates how cultural norms, including gender roles and social structures, influence institutional effectiveness. This historical evidence shows that the interplay between culture and institutions is crucial for fostering economic growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in both pre-industrial Europe and colonial Africa. The underlying point of agreement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9) is that cultural norms shape institutional frameworks by influencing the design and effectiveness of economic structures. A remaining disagreement (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Austin 2008, p.25) lies in whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis accurately captures the complexity of historical causation or oversimplifies it. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic interaction between cultural norms and institutional frameworks over different periods, as shown by Austin (2008, p.25). This empirical comparison demonstrates how various technologies, when available, ultimately changed economic outcomes in Africa.
