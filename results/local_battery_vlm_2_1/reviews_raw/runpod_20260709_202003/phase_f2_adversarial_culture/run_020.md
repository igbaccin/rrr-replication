The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue within economic historiography and development studies. At stake is whether cultural factors can be considered enduring determinants of institutional quality that shape societal outcomes over centuries. The live disagreement revolves around two main positions. One position holds that culture, broadly defined to include norms, beliefs, and values, plays a critical role in shaping institutions that foster or hinder economic growth. This perspective often draws on historical evidence to illustrate how cultural factors have influenced institutional development and, consequently, long-term economic performance. The opposing view contends that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography, technology, and political dynamics, with culture playing at best a secondary role. This debate is significant because it informs policies aimed at fostering economic growth in developing countries, where cultural factors may be deeply ingrained and resistant to change. Understanding the extent to which culture influences institutional development can help design more effective strategies for promoting sustainable economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), highlighting how the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights. They demonstrate that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of long-term capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework was pivotal for England's economic success and political freedom, illustrating how institutions can drive growth through secure property rights. Furthermore, North contends that neoclassical theories overlook the importance of institutions in economic growth, emphasizing that gains from trade are key to national wealth (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The creation of financial markets and instruments, which reduced transaction costs, reflects increased security in property rights. This underscores how institutions can create a stable environment for long-term capital markets and economic growth.

The evolution of these institutions in the seventeenth century fostered secure property rights by mitigating transaction costs. This institutional framework was pivotal for economic growth because it ensured that gains from trade could be realized through secure property rights. North and Weingast acknowledge that while other forces might have ultimately led England to success, the institutional changes accelerated this process decisively. This highlights how institutions can create a stable environment for long-term capital markets and economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Austin 2008, p.2) make the case that distributional conflicts were instrumental in forming the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The institutional landscape was influenced by efficiency concerns as well as power dynamics between groups seeking to protect their interests. For instance, serfdom and guild systems were maintained not solely for economic efficiency but also as a means for ruling elites to control labor and resources. These institutions often emerged from struggles over wealth distribution rather than from a straightforward pursuit of growth. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores that the historical paths of institutional evolution are complex and multifaceted, involving dynamic responses to environmental changes (Austin 2008, p.25). This complexity is further highlighted by Ogilvie's observation that institutions within a broader framework are interdependent (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Understanding this interdependency is crucial because it means that studying one institution in isolation can lead to incomplete or misleading conclusions. The implications of these findings indicate that institutional frameworks fostering long-term economic growth were outcomes of deeply entrenched distributional conflicts and power struggles, in addition to efficiency-driven mechanisms.

Extending this analysis to New World societies, Sokoloff and Engerman, alongside Broadberry and Gupta, focus on how institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Sokoloff and Engerman's work reveals that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in these societies was virtually present from their outset. These differences in institutions were driven by the different environments in which European colonies were established, leading to distinct degrees of inequality across societies. Broadberry and Gupta's findings suggest that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting the role of institutional differences in economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Sokoloff and Engerman observe that while conventional economic factors are considered, explanations for contrasting records in growth often focus on institutions. This suggests that the relationship between inequality and economic development is complex and multifaceted, involving various factors beyond just institutional differences (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and ideologies are pivotal in driving economic growth, challenging perspectives that focus solely on institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.10)(Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey highlights how the cultural struggle between traditionalist and liberal ideas shaped Europe's economic trajectory. Mokyr emphasizes that useful knowledge, often transmitted tacitly through personal interactions and training, was crucial for economic development. This implies that the diffusion of ideas and knowledge within a society can significantly impact its economic performance, even if such knowledge is not always codified in written form (Mokyr 2005, p.14). However, this perspective on the role of ideas and ideologies does not negate the importance of institutions; rather, it suggests that the effectiveness of institutions may depend on the broader cultural context in which they operate. This interplay between ideas, institutions, and culture underscores the complex nature of economic development.

Building on this interplay, Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective posits that the types of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-run growth trajectories, independent of cultural factors. The mechanism behind this effect is rooted in the differential mortality rates experienced by European colonizers, which shaped the institutional frameworks they implemented. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists rather than fostering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These extractive institutions persisted over time, locking former colonies into paths of underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the institutional legacy of colonialism is pivotal in understanding current income disparities among former colonies, noting that equilibrium institutions often prioritize rent extraction over sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that even when institutions are designed to maximize short-term benefits for colonizers, they can have lasting negative impacts on economic performance. The persistence of these extractive institutions underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutional changes continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes in former colonies.

Nunn (2008, p.27) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that Africa's slave trades have shaped the continent’s cultural landscape in enduring ways. The transatlantic slave trade created deep-seated mistrust within African societies, a legacy that persists today and significantly influences contemporary trust levels. This mistrust is a living force that affects economic development by undermining social capital and cooperation (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.1) suggests that the slave trade also contributed to institutional underdevelopment, particularly in state centralization and political stability, which are crucial for long-term economic growth. This institutional weakness, characterized by fragmented states and political instability, has been linked to Africa's current underdevelopment. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.15) further elaborate that this mistrust has had a pervasive effect on contemporary trust levels within Africa. This cultural shift is significant because it directly impacts the ability of African societies to build the social capital necessary for sustainable economic growth, as well as the institutions required to support such development. The slave trade's legacy, therefore, extends beyond immediate historical events and continues to shape Africa’s current economic landscape through both cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal for long-run economic growth. Institutions are central to economic performance because they define the structure within which individuals interact and make decisions (North 1989, p.1). The development of secure property rights, facilitated by stable institutions, has been a critical factor in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). For example, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England, such as the Bank of England, played a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

This institutional framework evolves over time, influenced by cultural factors. Ogilvie highlights that cultural beliefs can significantly impact economic institutions and efficiency, as seen in the decline of guilds with the Protestant Reformation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital underscores how cultural values like trust and reciprocity can foster institutions that support economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These findings align with Acemoglu et al.'s work, which shows that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The legacy of historical events, such as the slave trade, continues to shape contemporary cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and impacts the ability to build social capital necessary for sustainable economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This cultural shift is significant because it directly affects the institutions required to support development. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework underscores the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions, as Ogilvie notes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture plays a fundamental role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). There remains disagreement on the specific mechanisms through which cultural beliefs influence institutional development. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural factors alone can explain institutional differences or if other exogenous variables, such as geographic conditions, must be considered. This debate is settled by North's emphasis on the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence of the English financial revolution shows that stable institutions can overcome geographic obstacles and foster long-term economic development.
