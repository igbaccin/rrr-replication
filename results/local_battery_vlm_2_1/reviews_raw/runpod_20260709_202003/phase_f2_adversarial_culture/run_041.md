The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The fundamental question at stake is whether cultural factors can be considered as the primary cause of institutional development and economic performance or if other mechanisms such as geographic determinants or political structures should be prioritized. Some researchers argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the formation of institutions that facilitate economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while others emphasize the importance of exogenous factors such as mortality rates and settler populations in determining institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The debate hinges on whether culture operates through institutions to drive long-term growth, or if it is merely one of several interdependent factors within a broader framework. This disagreement underscores the need to understand how cultural elements interact with other determinants to shape institutions and economic trajectories over time; institutions that foster trust and reciprocity, for example, may arise from specific cultural contexts but also depend on historical events and geographical conditions.

The relationship between culture and institutional frameworks is complex, as evidenced by studies that highlight the interdependency of various institutions within a broader system. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial European economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts, efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights, which are essential for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of financial instruments and the creation of institutions like the Bank of England exemplify how secure property rights can foster economic growth by lowering transaction costs and enhancing market stability. This interdependency suggests that cultural beliefs and values may influence institutional frameworks, but these frameworks are also shaped by historical events and geographical conditions.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. In their view, financial innovations such as the creation of the Bank of England were pivotal. This institution provided a stable market for public debt and significantly lowered transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution enabled England to manage its fiscal crises effectively, thereby securing property rights over time and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.1) underscores that the creation of new financial instruments was a critical driver of long-term economic development. This perspective challenges the notion that cultural factors alone can explain economic growth, highlighting instead the transformative role of institutional change in shaping economic outcomes. However, it is important to note that while North and Weingast emphasize the importance of institutions, they also acknowledge that other forces could have influenced England's success. The interplay between secure property rights and financial stability underscores the broader significance of institutional frameworks in driving long-term economic development, independent of cultural influences.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that colonial institutions are fundamental in shaping long-term economic growth rather than cultural factors. Their argument hinges on the idea that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional framework is crucial because it establishes a direct link between historical colonial policies and contemporary economic outcomes. Austin's analysis further supports this view by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into one narrative, which does not account for the dynamic nature of institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.25). However, Acemoglu et al. acknowledge that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that while colonial institutions are pivotal in shaping economic development, their impact is mediated by the specific design and implementation of these institutions, which can vary significantly across different colonies. The complexity of institutional change underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical policies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes through enduring institutional frameworks.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by distributional conflicts, efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Distributional conflicts among elites and non-elites drove the formation of economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, which served to consolidate political and economic power for elites. These institutions reflected the balance of power between competing groups. The persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutional frameworks that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie highlights how different economic institutions are interdependent within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may not fully capture its impact. This interconnectedness of institutions implies that the broader institutional context must be considered when examining long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The focus on distributional conflicts underscores the importance of power dynamics and political structures in shaping economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) share a perspective that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This intellectual shift is exemplified by the cultural struggle in France, where the "Franco-French War" was a significant force shaping economic outcomes. McCloskey (2015, p.10) highlights that this period saw a rhetorical change that fostered new circumstances, ultimately leading to the Great Enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates on this by asserting that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. The diffusion of knowledge across Western nations underscored the importance of intellectual movements in driving economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective highlights how ideas and ideologies were pivotal in shaping long-run growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further notes that much of this useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and personal transmission, which underscores the role of cultural factors in disseminating economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This emphasis on ideas and ideologies suggests that cultural shifts were fundamental in driving long-run growth, as they facilitated the spread of knowledge and new circumstances.

Historical events such as the slave trade shaped cultural attitudes in Africa, particularly mistrust, which persists and affects long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) both identify how the impact of the slave trade on African development highlights the enduring effects of historical events. This is seen in the way that mistrust, a cultural attitude, has been shown to reduce trust levels and thus negatively affect economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) shows how this mistrust, which was fostered by the slave trade, can be linked to long-term political instability. This instability led to weakened states, which in turn hindered economic development. This suggests that the cultural attitudes and institutional weaknesses stemming from the slave trade have had lasting effects on Africa's economic trajectory, shaping the cultural landscape in ways that continue to influence long-term growth.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that Europe's economic divergence from Asia was already evident before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This early divergence is supported by van Zanden who shows that while there were periods of growth in European GDP per capita between the 16th and 18th centuries, these did not necessarily translate into significant improvements in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The economic development in parts of Europe was marked by institutional changes that led to investment in agriculture, which in turn increased agricultural productivity. This higher productivity did not lead to abundant grain supplies or lower food prices because labor shifted from agriculture into industrial and service sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15).

The divergence between Europe and Asia is further highlighted by the differing trajectories of wages and economic development. In advanced regions of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in northwestern Europe, but silver wages, indicative of purchasing power over tradable goods and services, were significantly lower (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This disparity suggests that while basic subsistence levels might have been similar across these regions, the economic structures and purchasing power differed markedly. The role of urban development and real wages in shaping this divergence is pivotal: cities like London became hubs of economic activity, reflecting broader European economic trends (van Zanden 1999, p.6).

The role of culture in shaping institutions that foster long-run growth is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. Ogilvie's work demonstrates how cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions, with Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivating medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural framework shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can alter the structure of collective action games, which in turn affects the efficiency of the economy. Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how societies characterized by high levels of trust and norms of reciprocity tend to have institutions that favor economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This underscores the importance of cultural values in shaping institutional frameworks that promote long-run growth.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie points out that the European family system, labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems were interconnected and contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence suggests that studying individual institutions in isolation may not fully capture their role in fostering long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. support this by showing how favourable contracting institutions are partially interdependent with property rights institutions, both of which are critical for economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These findings indicate that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting long-run growth is enhanced when they operate within a cohesive and supportive framework.

The evolution of secure property rights and financial institutions has been pivotal in fostering economic growth. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflects increased security of property rights over time, highlighting the importance of institutional stability. North and Weingast further emphasize that the institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

Cultural factors also play a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects contemporary economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical trauma has had lasting effects on social capital and trust, which are essential for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of these cultural norms underscores the deep-rooted influence of historical events on institutional development and long-run growth.

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is clear. Institutions matter because they create the conditions for economic growth, with secure property rights being a critical factor (North 1989, p.1). The extent to which cultural beliefs directly influence these institutions remains debated. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The historical evidence from European settlements and colonial institutions settles this disagreement by demonstrating how exogenous factors like mortality rates can shape institutional frameworks that either promote or hinder long-run growth.
