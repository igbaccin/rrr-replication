The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the social sciences. At stake is our understanding of how societies evolve and why some achieve sustained prosperity while others lag behind. Two prominent positions exist on this topic: one emphasizes cultural factors as foundational to institutional development, asserting that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions; the other views culture as a secondary factor, overshadowed by more immediate determinants such as mortality rates or exogenous shocks. Some scholars argue that cultural norms can motivate specific economic behaviors and shape institutions accordingly (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife are posited to have influenced medieval craftsmen's adherence to guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation is suggested to have led to a decline in these guilds due to changes in cultural beliefs. Other scholars contend that European mortality rates significantly impacted institutional development, highlighting how differences in settler mortality influenced current income per capita through the establishment of extractive institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The disagreement centers on whether culture is a primary driver or a secondary influence on institutional frameworks and economic growth.

The degree to which institutions are influenced by cultural norms remains debated. North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework, which emerged from political changes, significantly reduced transaction costs. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments exemplifies how these institutions facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) show that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's sustained success, suggesting that even if other factors might have eventually led to similar outcomes, they would have done so more slowly. This institutional framework provided a large positive externality for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The implications are clear: secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, facilitated by robust institutions, are essential for economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both argue that the structure of economic institutions is shaped by the distribution of power among different social groups. For instance, serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were outcomes of struggles between elites and commoners over resource allocation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that culture dominates institutions by highlighting how institutional arrangements can be manipulated by powerful groups to maintain their advantage. The implications for long-run growth are significant: if institutions are primarily shaped by distributional conflicts, then cultural explanations may overlook the critical role of power dynamics in economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illustrate this point with evidence from New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality and shaped institutional frameworks that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, contributing to divergent paths of economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this analysis by emphasizing the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that studying individual institutions in isolation can be misleading (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence indicates that distributional conflicts shape specific institutions and influence the overall institutional environment, which in turn affects economic outcomes. Instead, it suggests that culture and distributional conflicts are intertwined, with power dynamics often determining which cultural norms are institutionalized.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These authors show that the type of institutions introduced by European colonizers was heavily influenced by initial conditions, particularly mortality rates experienced by early settlers. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed for short-term exploitation. This pattern is evident in colonies where Europeans faced significant health risks, leading to institutional frameworks that prioritized resource extraction over sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound implications for economic performance, with many former colonies still grappling with the legacy of extractive governance. Acemoglu et al.'s findings also show variation among colonial experiences; where European settlement was more successful, institutions tended to be inclusive and growth-oriented. This variation underscores the complex interplay between initial conditions, institutional design, and long-term economic trajectories. The presence of extractive institutions in many former colonies today is a direct consequence of their historical institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) share that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic change (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey posits that the ideological shifts in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were crucial for economic growth. This perspective shows how intellectual changes drove economic transformation. Mokyr emphasizes the role of useful knowledge as a key driver of economic growth; he argues that the dissemination of knowledge within Western nations was pivotal. The interrelation between ideas and knowledge is evident in McCloskey's observation that new rhetoric led to new circumstances, which further stimulated economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, requiring personal transmission and training; this implicit form of knowledge moved through networks within Europe (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The spread of ideas and knowledge depended on social and cultural factors facilitating their dissemination. This underscores how culture played a significant role in shaping economic growth by enabling the transmission of ideas and useful knowledge, which in turn fostered innovation and development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) examine historical institutional paths and economic divergences, focusing on how different regions evolved economically over time. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) similarly argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, challenging views that place the divergence much later. This emphasis on endogenous factors implies that institutional changes within regions played crucial roles in shaping long-term economic trajectories. Austin notes that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25), indicating that institutional impact on growth can vary over time. This variability underscores the complexity of institutional paths and their influence on economic development.

The slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists today and affects trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade resulted in long-term political instability. This is supported by Nunn's findings, which indicate that the slave trades caused weakened and fragmented states due to the historical accounts of long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust has a direct impact on economic performance as recent research shows a positive correlation between state antiquity and real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27), suggesting that the fragmentation of states due to slave trades contributed to reduced economic development. This mistrust and political instability have created conditions that significantly impact trust levels today; an effect that persists beyond the immediate period of slavery.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. Ogilvie describes how pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, serfdom, community, and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of cultural beliefs and values in determining institutional frameworks. For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights that societies with high trust levels and norms of reciprocity tend to foster institutions conducive to economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Meanwhile, North emphasizes that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This aligns with the idea that secure property rights, facilitated by robust institutions, underpin long-term economic development. The English financial revolution, for example, significantly lowered transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The legacy of colonial institutions also sheds light on how culture influences contemporary economic performance. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persist today and affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that historical institutional frameworks, shaped by cultural and environmental factors, have lasting impacts on economic development. Moreover, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its interaction with cultural factors over time.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further illustrated by the impact of the slave trade on African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy has significant implications for contemporary economic development, as trust is a crucial component of social capital. North and Weingast highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The creation of the Bank of England exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to positive externalities, such as the development of a stable market for private debt. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating long-term economic growth.

Ultimately, culture shapes institutions that are fundamental to long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise mechanism by which this occurs remains contested: Ogilvie shows how distributional conflicts shaped pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Austin highlights the dynamic, partly endogenous nature of African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available (Austin 2008, p.25). The historical evidence from North and Weingast on seventeenth-century England shows that institutional changes leading to secure property rights were critical for fostering economic growth.
