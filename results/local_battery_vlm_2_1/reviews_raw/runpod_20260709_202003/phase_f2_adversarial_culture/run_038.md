The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the explanation for persistent economic disparities across regions and over time. One position asserts that cultural beliefs directly shape economic institutions, influencing efficiency and growth trajectories. This view posits that deeply ingrained cultural values can either facilitate or hinder institutional frameworks conducive to economic development. Another position contends that cultural factors are less direct determinants of long-run growth; instead, they suggest that the causal chain is mediated through other variables, such as extractive institutions or geopolitical dynamics. The debate hinges on whether culture acts as a primary driver or a secondary influence on institutional frameworks and their economic outcomes. A third position posits that cultural norms are themselves shaped by historical events, such as the slave trade, which can have enduring effects on trust and cooperation within societies. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending how institutions evolve and impact long-run growth. The interplay between culture and institutions remains a pivotal area of inquiry, with implications for both historical economic analyses and contemporary development strategies. This interplay extends beyond mere institutional frameworks to encompass the broader social and political contexts in which these institutions operate. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. In this context, the financial revolution played a pivotal role, as it dramatically lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of long-term property rights. North (1989, p.1) explains that this process involved the creation of new financial instruments and institutions such as the Bank of England, which facilitated secure transactions and fostered economic expansion (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of these institutions ensured that the gains from trade were maximized, leading to increased productivity and technological advancements. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further illustrate how the growth of a stable market for public debt provided positive externalities for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework facilitated economic growth and laid the groundwork for political freedom, demonstrating that institutions can drive both economic and political progress.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are central to the formation of economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. This perspective is grounded in the idea that these institutions evolved as a result of power struggles, being efficient responses to economic demands. For instance, Ogilvie shows how core institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by the conflicting interests of different social groups. These distributional conflicts influenced the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman elaborate on this by demonstrating that in societies with greater inequality, institutions often favoured elites through various government policies, restricting competition and thus perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can reinforce existing power structures, leading to persistent disparities in wealth and political influence. Ogilvie also acknowledges the interdependence of different institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that changes in one institution can ripple through the entire system, affecting overall economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The implication is that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the complexity of institutional frameworks means that understanding long-run growth requires a holistic view of how various institutions interact.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies drove economic change in Europe from 1600 to 1848; the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of this process. McCloskey's critique of interest-only theories highlights how accumulated information combined with ideological shifts led to institutional changes that fostered economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10). This perspective is echoed by Mokyr, who emphasizes that the diffusion of useful knowledge across Europe was crucial for economic development. The spread of knowledge through personal communications and tacit forms, such as skills and practical know-how, facilitated the transfer of ideas across borders (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). This knowledge-sharing process relied heavily on interpersonal training and apprenticeships, not just written texts.

The ideological shifts McCloskey discusses were not merely intellectual exercises; they had tangible economic implications. The "bourgeois" ideologies that emerged in countries like the Netherlands and Britain fostered a cultural environment conducive to innovation and entrepreneurship (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr's focus on the tacit nature of much useful knowledge underscores how this ideological environment encouraged the spread of practical skills, which were essential for economic growth. This qualification suggests that while ideology played a crucial role, the effectiveness of these ideas depended on the local context and institutional frameworks.

The interplay between ideas, institutions, and economic growth illustrates that cultural factors are integral to understanding long-run economic performance. The success of ideologies in driving economic change was contingent on their ability to align with existing cultural norms and institutional settings. This dynamic highlights how culture can both facilitate and hinder the adoption of new ideas, shaping the trajectory of economic development over the long term.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established there. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists, not fostering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, once in place, persisted over time and had a lasting impact on economic performance. The correlation between settler mortality and current institutions underscores the enduring effects of colonial policies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize that these extractive institutions were designed to secure rents for the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, shaped by historical mortality rates and the interests of European settlers, continues to influence contemporary economic disparities among former colonies. The persistence of these institutions highlights how initial conditions set during colonial rule can have profound and lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory. The legacy of colonial institutions remains a critical factor in understanding current income differences between countries, illustrating the deep-rooted impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-term economic outcomes.

The reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis fails to account for the dynamic, endogenous responses of African societies to their environments. Instead, it presents a more nuanced view where Africans' own actions and technologies shaped their economic development. This perspective highlights the importance of local agency and adaptation in economic growth, challenging the notion that environmental obstacles are static and unchanging (Austin 2008, p.25). Bryant (2006, p.35) builds on this by emphasizing the interconnectedness of historical developments. He asserts that every transition from past to present is governed by a causal connectedness, where prior developments provide the resources and opportunities for subsequent changes. This implies that economic growth is deeply rooted in accumulated technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms, not merely driven by immediate processes (Bryant 2006, p.4). Bryant (2006, p.35) also notes that this interconnectedness can lead to explanatory distortions if shorter-term processes are prioritized over longer-term developments. This qualification suggests that while historical legacies are crucial, they must be understood in conjunction with contemporary dynamics. The interplay between long-term institutional frameworks and immediate economic conditions thus shapes the trajectory of growth.

Abramovitz highlights that productivity levels are erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology; moreover, social competence for exploiting advanced methods varies across countries and time periods (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This variability implies that economic growth is driven by technological progress and the ability of societies to adapt and utilize these advancements. Goldin's analysis of women’s employment and education underscores how shifts in social norms and institutional changes can alter labor supply and occupational dynamics, further illustrating the complex interplay between technological progress and social adaptations (Goldin 2006, p.8). Exogenous changes such as increased demand for office workers and advancements in household production technology significantly altered female labor supply, demonstrating that broader social and technological shifts drive economic transformations (Goldin 2006, p.3). The interaction between these factors suggests that long-run growth is deeply rooted in the accumulated effects of both technological progress and social competence.

The slave trade in Africa has left enduring cultural legacies that have shaped long-term economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrate that the transatlantic slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. This mistrust is a persistent social norm that continues to influence contemporary economic behavior, not merely a historical artifact. The impact of this mistrust extends beyond social interactions, affecting broader institutional frameworks and economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.27) further illustrates how the slave trade weakened state centralization and political stability, creating fragmented states that struggled to foster economic growth. This long-term political instability has had profound implications for Africa's development trajectory, as recent empirical research indicates a strong correlation between historical state development and current economic performance. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is deeply embedded in the institutional fabric of African societies, perpetuating cycles of underdevelopment. Nunn (2008, p.1) argues that slavery is one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, highlighting how historical events can shape contemporary economic outcomes. Therefore, understanding the enduring effects of the slave trade on cultural attitudes and institutions is crucial for comprehending Africa's development challenges.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and institutional frameworks that have shaped societies over time. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial for understanding economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights how distributional conflicts are key to explaining core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, being efficient responses to economic demands. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they address the transaction costs inherent in neoclassical models (North 1989, p.1). The creation of secure property rights and the development of financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, reflect increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate that the financial revolution played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This aligns with Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, emphasizing instead a dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the course of their economic development, highlighting the role of cultural and institutional adaptations in shaping growth (Austin 2008, p.25).

The legacy of the slave trade further complicates the role of culture in dominating institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This cultural impact is intertwined with the institutional fabric of African societies, perpetuating cycles of underdevelopment. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization suggests that historical events can shape contemporary economic outcomes by influencing cultural attitudes and institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains a subject of debate. A shared finding is that institutions matter for economic development, but there are disagreements over whether this is primarily due to exogenous cultural factors or endogenous institutional adaptations (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical evidence shows that secure property rights and financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, were critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) finding that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the role of secure property rights and financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, in fostering economic growth.
