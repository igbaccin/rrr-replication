The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods and whether cultural factors are pivotal drivers or mere epiphenomena. The debate revolves around whether cultural beliefs and values significantly shape economic institutions, thereby influencing growth trajectories. One camp posits that culture directly influences institutional frameworks, which in turn drive long-term economic performance. Another perspective suggests that the impact of culture on institutions is indirect at best, arguing that other factors such as geography or political structures play more decisive roles. This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced examination of how cultural elements interact with institutional development and economic growth over time. The question is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of why some societies prosper while others stagnate.

A key stream of scholarship focuses on the direct influence of culture on economic institutions, highlighting how cultural beliefs and values shape institutional frameworks that foster or hinder long-term economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can significantly alter collective action games within an economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies characterized by high levels of trust and norms of reciprocity (stemming from 'civic' cultures) are more likely to develop institutions conducive to economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Nunn and Wantchekon complement this view by demonstrating how historical events like the slave trade can instill enduring cultural traits, such as mistrust, which continue to affect institutional development and economic outcomes in contemporary Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Acemoglu et al. propose a different causal pathway for long-run growth, focusing on the role of extractive institutions established during colonial periods. They argue that European mortality rates influenced the types of institutions implemented in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persist today and significantly impact current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors are less direct determinants than geographical and epidemiological conditions which shaped institutional frameworks over time. Austin's work further complicates this narrative by questioning the oversimplification of causation in the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that different historical periods and paths must be considered to accurately understand long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the importance of temporal dynamics in institutional evolution and their impact on economic outcomes.

While debates continue about whether culture directly influences institutions or if other factors play a more significant role, there is a shared consensus that institutions matter for economic growth. North emphasizes that institutions are critical because they reduce transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast build on this by showing how the evolution of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Together, these scholars underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating long-term economic development. The interplay between culture and institutions remains a complex issue, with ongoing research needed to fully understand how cultural beliefs and values interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes over time.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust that has persisted and impacted trust levels significantly (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate how historical events like the transatlantic slave trade have shaped contemporary African societies, including their cultural norms. They argue that this mistrust is a deeply ingrained cultural trait that continues to influence social interactions and economic outcomes. This instability has hindered the development of strong, centralized institutions capable of enforcing property rights and contracts, which are crucial for fostering economic growth. Nunn extends this argument by showing that the legacy of slavery includes pervasive corruption in addition to mistrust, further complicating institutional development in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.1). The implications of these findings underscore how historical events can have enduring effects on contemporary societies, shaping their cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks in ways that hinder long-run economic growth.

Ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey's framework highlights that the interplay between cultural shifts and ideological changes fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. This perspective is supported by Mokyr, who emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge was a critical factor in economic development. For instance, Mokyr describes how tacit knowledge, skills and know-how embedded in individuals and communities, played a significant role in facilitating economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of ideas and ideologies across Europe during this period enabled countries to adopt new economic practices more readily. The translation of these ideas into tangible economic gains was not uniform. McCloskey illustrates how France's cultural conservatism and Spain’s historical resistance to change impeded their economic growth despite exposure to innovative ideas (McCloskey 2015, p.9). While Mokyr highlights that useful knowledge could be diffused across borders through various means such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences, the absorption of this knowledge was contingent on local conditions. This underscores that while ideas and ideologies were crucial drivers of economic change, their impact varied significantly depending on the cultural and institutional context in which they were introduced.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers have enduring economic impacts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high settler mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. This is because the type of institutions established was not driven by geographic factors, but rather by the ability or inability of Europeans to settle and establish long-term colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, Acemoglu et al. found a strong correlation between settler mortality rates and current institutional quality in former European colonies. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had profound effects on economic development, with former colonies characterized by poor institutional quality lagging behind in economic growth. The legacy of colonial institutions thus continues to shape economic outcomes in many parts of the world today.

The literature on complexity in economic growth processes highlights that the multifaceted nature of technological advance and social change significantly influences long-run economic development (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that the catch-up process is influenced by both exogenous factors and endogenous dynamics, such as institutional changes. This perspective underscores how the interaction between technology, institutions, and social competence shapes economic growth trajectories. These dynamics are complex and not reducible to simple convergence hypotheses; instead, they involve a nuanced interplay of factors that can either facilitate or hinder economic development. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) elaborates on this point by noting that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, highlighting the importance of social competence in exploiting advanced methods (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). He argues against static geographical determinants, instead proposing a more endogenous approach that considers Africans' responses to their environments as pivotal factors in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). The implication here is that cultural and social dynamics play a crucial role in determining how institutions evolve and impact growth. However, Austin (2008, p.2) also notes the challenge of quantitatively testing institutional theories and explaining why some countries adopt growth-enhancing institutions while others do not (Austin 2008, p.2), pointing to the need for further research into the specific mechanisms by which culture influences institutional outcomes. Cultural factors can dominate institutions in ways that are not fully captured by simple geographical or institutional frameworks, suggesting a deeper layer of complexity in understanding long-run economic growth.

Institutions such as the Bank of England, which played a critical role in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), provided a large and positive externality for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution and falling transaction costs reflect increased security of property rights over time, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

North (1989, p.1) also observes that gains from trade are the key to the wealth of nations. Specialization and division of labor have made possible the improved productivity that arises from technological change, better resource allocation, and specialized production, which are underlying features of modern economies (North 1989, p.1). The financial revolution in England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth by increasing the security of property rights over time. This development was crucial for the evolution of institutions governing public choice, which in turn supported secure property rights and long-term economic growth.

This implies that institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

Distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Serfdom and craft guilds did not emerge from efficiency considerations but rather from struggles over power and resources among different social groups. These inequalities led to policies that restricted competition and maintained elite dominance, thereby perpetuating inequality.

The mechanisms through which distributional conflicts shape institutions can be seen in the case of craft guilds. Ogilvie points out that these guilds were instruments used by craftsmen to secure control over their trades (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This control was often achieved at the expense of other social groups, including merchants and consumers. The persistence of such institutions can be traced back to the power dynamics within societies, where dominant groups used their influence to maintain advantageous institutional frameworks. A downstream implication of this dynamic is that changes in distributional conflicts can lead to significant shifts in economic institutions. For instance, the decline of craft guilds in England was linked to broader social and political changes that altered the balance of power among different groups.

Ogilvie notes that interdependencies between institutions further complicate this picture (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The European family system, for example, was interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems. These interdependent frameworks contributed to early industrialization and capitalism by creating a supportive environment for economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman caution that the persistence of inequality can hinder institutional change (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This highlights the importance of understanding how distributional conflicts shape institutions and influence their evolution over time.

The implications are clear: distributional conflicts fundamentally alter the trajectory of institutional development.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie highlights how distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs influenced the structure of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by Nunn and Wantchekon's finding that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects contemporary trust levels, thereby impacting long-run growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The interdependency of institutions within broader frameworks further underscores the importance of cultural influences. Ogilvie notes that the European family system was interconnected with various markets and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in institutional development is also evident in the evolution of property rights and financial institutions. North argues that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and foster a stable market for both public and private debt (North 1989, p.9). This view is supported by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the development of institutions governing public choice was crucial for establishing secure property rights and promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The impact of culture on institutional frameworks is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al.'s study, which shows that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25).

The underlying point of agreement is that culture fundamentally shapes institutions. North shows that secure property rights, fostered by cultural and institutional developments, are critical for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The precise remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which culture influences institutional frameworks. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that historical events, such as the slave trade, have lasting cultural impacts on trust and growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Settling this disagreement requires examining specific historical evidence or empirical comparisons, such as those used by Acemoglu et al. to link mortality rates with institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), which show that exogenous cultural factors can significantly alter the trajectory of institutional development over time.
