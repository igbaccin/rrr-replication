The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is whether cultural factors can be seen as the driving force behind institutional structures that foster economic growth over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which cultural norms and historical precedents shape institutions, versus whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or technology.

One stream of scholarship argues that institutions are endogenous to culture. Acemoglu et al. assert that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The relationship between settler mortality and current institutions is strong: higher mortality correlated with fewer European settlements, which in turn led to the development of less inclusive political and economic institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This dynamic highlights how cultural factors, such as attitudes towards risk and settlement, can shape institutional outcomes. North and Weingast complement this view by arguing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, characterized by the creation of the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Another stream proposes that institutions are largely determined by external forces independent of cultural influences. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1). This perspective suggests that institutional frameworks may be influenced by historical events and demographic factors as well as cultural determinants.

The role of women in economic development is another antecedent that flows into the topic's causal variable as a trigger. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The gender-based colonial constructs had significant long-term consequences for gender production, reflecting the sexual division of labor established during the colonial period (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This perspective underscores how cultural norms around gender roles can influence economic outcomes by shaping institutional frameworks that either empower or marginalize certain groups.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic development, shaping contemporary economic outcomes through their enduring influence on institutional frameworks. This perspective is grounded in the idea that European mortality rates during colonization drove the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism here is that European settlers introduced institutions tailored to maximize rents for colonists, promoting long-term growth. This institutional framework, established during colonial rule, has had a lasting impact on economic development in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions does not imply an inevitable path to underdevelopment; Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also note that institutional changes can lead to significant income differences among formerly poor colonies. The implication is that while colonial institutions initially hindered growth, subsequent institutional changes can reverse this trend and drive economic development. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is dynamic, with potential for both stagnation and growth depending on later institutional adaptations.

This institutional framework facilitated economic development by reducing transaction costs, which in turn enhanced productivity through specialization and division of labor. This financial revolution was pivotal for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North & Weingast's analysis highlights that these institutions provided a large positive externality for the development of both public and private debt markets, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic activities (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These institutional changes were instrumental in driving economic growth; cultural factors also played a significant role.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift was a persistent legacy that continues to shape societal dynamics. This mistrust, in turn, has had profound implications for economic development. For instance, it has hindered the establishment of effective institutions by fostering an environment where cooperation and collective action are difficult to achieve. Nunn's research underscores that this legacy of mistrust is intertwined with broader issues of state centralization and political stability (Nunn 2008, p.27). The historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states, are consistent with empirical findings showing that a country’s history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade has affected interpersonal relationships as well as institutional frameworks, creating a vicious cycle where weak institutions further exacerbate mistrust and hinder growth.

The new economic history of Africa highlights that historical institutional variables play a crucial role in shaping current development outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective emphasizes that institutional change is gradual and requires an examination of the long-term historical context, including precolonial and even prehistoric periods. The effectiveness of contemporary institutions in promoting growth hinges on their congruence with indigenous institutions. When there is a high degree of congruence, governments gain legitimacy and are better equipped to foster development-inducing institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This alignment enhances the ability of institutions to promote economic growth by reducing regionalism, centelism, and corruption. Where institutional reforms fail to align with historical contexts, they may inadvertently perpetuate patrimonial politics, which can undermine long-term growth prospects.

The role of women in economic development intersects with cultural and institutional factors, as both Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Goldin 2006, p.8) show that women's contributions to economies have been significant. In pre-colonial Africa, women were indispensable for community reproduction; their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth in matrilineal societies such as Asante (Akyeampong&Fofack_2014). This role extended into economic spheres. Goldin shows that women's participation in the U.S. economy was transformed by exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts altered female labor supply and made part-time work readily available, thereby dispelling barriers to the employment of married women. This variability suggests that cultural norms and institutions play a crucial role in determining how women contribute to economic development, which may have long-term implications for growth patterns.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods, while Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) contend that the great divergence was already underway before 1800. Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth by influencing how societies respond to economic challenges. Institutions are critical for long-term economic growth as they provide the framework within which economic activities occur (North 1989, p.1). The significance of culture lies in its ability to shape institutional trajectories, thereby determining economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that settler mortality rates influenced the types of institutions established in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This highlights how cultural factors, such as attitudes towards risk and collective action, can shape institutional development.

Austin argues that cultural norms may have influenced decisions about adopting institutions favourable for economic growth (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective aligns with North's view that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights, both of which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how cultural factors can foster secure property rights and economic growth. For example, the financial revolution in England led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further underscored by Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women's contributions to economic growth. They show that pre-colonial gender roles and cultural beliefs significantly influenced production systems, which continue to reflect the sexual division of labor established during colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This indicates that cultural norms can persist over time, shaping institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.
