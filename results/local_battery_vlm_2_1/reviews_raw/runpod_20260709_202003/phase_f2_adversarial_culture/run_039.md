The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth and reducing global inequality. One position argues that cultural beliefs directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby impacting economic performance. Another position contends that institutional frameworks evolve independently of cultural factors, driven primarily by exogenous shocks or political dynamics. A third perspective posits that culture shapes institutions indirectly through its impact on social norms and trust, which in turn affect economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether culture is a fundamental cause of long-run growth or merely an ancillary factor within a complex web of institutional and environmental determinants.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) underscore the critical role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, particularly through the evolution of public choice mechanisms in seventeenth-century England. They argue that the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced security for long-term capital markets. This institutional framework was crucial for England's long-run economic success. Similarly, North (1989, p.1; 1989, p.9) emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that focus on population and savings as principal determinants. He highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional evolution was pivotal for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She critiques exogenous cultural theories, such as those proposed by Max Weber and Putnam, which suggest that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. Ogilvie contends that these cultural approaches often overlook the interdependencies between individual institutions within a wider framework. Instead, she suggests that a holistic view of institutional frameworks is necessary to understand their impact on economic growth.

The relationship between culture and institutions extends beyond direct causal links. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) explore how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical component of social capital and institutional effectiveness. The enduring impact of historical events on contemporary cultural norms highlights the complex interplay between culture and institutions in shaping long-run growth.

A rival view to the role of culture in dominating institutions comes from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17), who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. They suggest that these institutions, which persisted over time, significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective emphasizes the role of exogenous factors, such as mortality rates and settler populations, in shaping institutional frameworks rather than cultural beliefs.

Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.25) shares a rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Instead, he proposes a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al call the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This view suggests that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the trajectory of African economies.

The interplay between culture and institutions in driving long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. While cultural beliefs can directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, they also shape social norms and trust, which in turn affect economic outcomes. Institutions themselves evolve through a combination of exogenous shocks, political dynamics, and cultural influences, creating a dynamic interplay that drives long-run growth.

The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly lowered transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The development of a stable market for public debt provided a positive externality for private debt markets.

The financial revolution's framework was more than an immediate response; it laid growth foundations. This underscores the idea that institutional changes are pivotal in shaping long-term economic trajectories. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, for example, played a significant role in shaping England's financial institutions and property rights system. This highlights how external shocks can catalyze institutional change, which in turn drives long-run growth.

These institutions' evolution was part of a broader process including political negotiations and social changes. The trade-off between certain rights and revenues to Parliament during the Hundred Years' War illustrates how political dynamics influenced economic outcomes (North 1989, p.9). This interplay between politics, institutions, and long-term growth indicates that institutional frameworks are shaped by complex interactions rather than being solely a product of cultural norms or individual decisions.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts shape economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective focuses on how the struggle for power between different societal groups influences the development of key economic structures. In this view, the institutions that emerge are those that best serve the interests of the dominant groups. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies studied by Sokoloff and Engerman, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutional frameworks that perpetuated this inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, designed to protect elite interests, restricted competition and stifled economic growth. The interdependence of these institutions within a broader framework further complicates the picture: Ogilvie notes that individual institutions do not operate in isolation but are part of an interconnected system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency means that changes in one institution can have ripple effects throughout the entire framework, influencing long-term economic outcomes. The downstream implication is that distributional conflicts not only shape initial institutional structures but also their evolution over time, which has significant implications for growth trajectories across societies.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey's work shows that intellectual shifts can alter the course of history even when institutions remain constant; Mokyr (2005, p.56) builds on this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge, not just institutional frameworks, is crucial for sustained economic development. The diffusion of ideas and knowledge across societies, as well as within them, fosters an environment conducive to innovation and economic growth.

The mechanism through which ideas drive economic change involves both explicit and tacit knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr (2005, p.56) highlights that while codified knowledge is important, it is the tacit knowledge, skills, know-how, and implicit understanding, that often underpins significant technological advancements. However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies this by noting that the diffusion of knowledge across nations within the Western world was more significant than national differences in institutions and culture (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The implication is that while local cultural struggles can shape economic trajectories, the broader dissemination of ideas and knowledge is essential for long-term growth.

The complexity of economic growth and technological advance is influenced by various exogenous factors and social dynamics, but the role of culture as a dominant factor in long-run growth is not explicitly addressed in this cluster (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This complexity is further highlighted by Austin (2008, p.2), who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, suggesting that social competence for exploiting advanced methods was still limited, particularly in earlier years and among more recent latecomers (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This implies that knowledge diffusion and technological advancement depend on institutional frameworks and broader societal factors including culture. Austin (2008, p.2) shares this view, arguing that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). Abramovitz's focus on the role of exogenous factors and social dynamics in technological advance underscores the importance of understanding how cultural shifts can influence long-term growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has significantly affected current income per capita. This relationship is evidenced by the strong correlation between settler mortality and both early and contemporary institutional measures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that these early institutions, shaped by colonial powers to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth, persist over time. This persistence highlights how extractive institutions can lock in economic underperformance for extended periods. The design of equilibrium institutions often prioritizes the interests of politically powerful groups which may not align with long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This means that while colonial institutions can be a significant driver of growth or stagnation, their effects are mediated by the political dynamics within the colonies themselves. The legacy of extractive institutions thus underscores the complex interplay between historical institutional changes and contemporary economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)(Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is a direct consequence of historical trauma inflicted by the transatlantic slave trade. The mechanism through which this mistrust manifests is complex: it erodes social capital, which is crucial for economic development. Social capital, encompassing networks and shared norms, facilitates cooperation and reduces transaction costs (Nunn 2008, p.27). The legacy of the slave trade has fragmented these networks creating a society where individuals are less likely to trust one another hindering collective action and economic growth. This fragmentation is exacerbated by the political instability that resulted from the slave trades, leading to weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). Recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization underscores how the slave trade disrupted social trust and undermined institutional strength further impeding long-term economic development. This institutional weakness is a critical factor in understanding Africa's contemporary economic challenges, as it creates an environment where corruption thrives and effective governance is hindered (Nunn 2008, p.27).

Culture plays an intricate role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. The historical impact of cultural norms on institutional development is evident in the evolution of European economic institutions. Ogilvie highlights how distributional conflicts, rooted in cultural beliefs and values, shaped key economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, in turn, influenced economic efficiency and growth trajectories across different regions in Europe. The interdependency between these institutions within a broader framework underscores their collective impact on long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate how differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that cultural factors can influence institutional frameworks over extended periods.

The role of culture in economic growth is also evident in the development of financial institutions. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which was crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution secured property rights fostering a stable market for public debt providing a positive externality for private debt markets. North and Weingast emphasize that the institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The evolution of these institutions highlights how cultural and institutional factors intertwine to drive long-term economic development.

The impact of culture on trust and social capital is another critical aspect. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This cultural legacy has significant implications for contemporary economic challenges in Africa, where corruption thrives due to weak institutions. The historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as those studied by Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales, show that social capital is deeply rooted in historical events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Ogilvie's analysis of European guilds further supports this, highlighting how non-economic cultural beliefs influenced the structure of economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The underlying agreement across these sources shows that culture fundamentally shapes institutions that drive long-term growth. North's emphasis on the interdependency between institutions and transaction costs aligns with Ogilvie's findings on the influence of distributional conflicts and cultural norms on institutional frameworks (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement centers on whether historical events or contemporary factors have a more significant impact on current economic performance. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.25). This view is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of European mortality rates and their impact on current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement involves comparing the long-term effects of extractive versus inclusive institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. in their study of European colonies with high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison highlights how cultural and institutional factors interact over time to shape economic growth trajectories, demonstrating the enduring influence of culture on dominating institutions.
