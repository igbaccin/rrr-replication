The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and historical scholarship. At stake is whether cultural factors can be harnessed to drive institutional development, thereby fostering sustained economic progress. One position argues that cultural beliefs shape economic institutions by influencing collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that specific cultural values can lead to the creation of institutions that promote efficiency and growth. A rival view posits that extractive institutions, shaped by European mortality rates in colonies, significantly affect current income per capita through a pathway that does not involve culture (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional change or if other exogenous variables play a more significant role. A complementary approach suggests that African women played an important role in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, indicating that cultural beliefs about gender roles may have influenced economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This stream of thought underscores the need to consider the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth.

Institutions are highlighted as a fundamental determinant of economic growth (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Institutions shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide empirical support for this perspective by showing a strong relationship between settler mortality rates and the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persist over time and have significant effects on current income per capita, underscoring their role as a fundamental determinant of long-run growth. This correlation suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial institutions have lasting effects on economic performance. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this perspective by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their impact depends on the specific context and power dynamics at play. Institutions shape long-term economic trajectories by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for productive activities.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency or cultural factors, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective highlights how power dynamics and elite interests shaped institutions. For instance, the persistence of serfdom and guilds can be attributed to their role in maintaining the political and economic dominance of ruling elites; these groups resisted institutional changes that might have undermined their control (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic underscores how distributional conflicts led to the formation of institutions designed to secure elite interests rather than promote efficiency or growth. This suggests that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were largely shaped by conflicts over resource allocation rather than by cultural beliefs or efficiency considerations.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that historical events like the slave trade can shape cultural norms in lasting ways (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today. Nunn and Wantchekon link this mistrust to the enduring effects of historical events on contemporary social dynamics. The legacy of these historical patterns is evident in the continued relevance of colonial constructs, which shape both gender relations and broader socio-economic structures. This interplay between cultural norms and economic outcomes underscores the deep-seated nature of cultural influences on long-run growth.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views by arguing that these perspectives oversimplify causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). He contends that this simplification is evident in the compression of different historical periods and paths. Austin's critique underscores a broader issue: the 'reversal of fortune' thesis fails to account for the dynamic, partly endogenous processes that shape economic development. This criticism is particularly relevant to Africa, where local responses to environmental challenges have historically driven technological adoption and institutional change (Austin 2008, p.25). Bryant extends this critique by arguing that revisionist positions dismissing Eurocentric views are both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. He emphasizes the interconnectedness of cultural norms, social relations, and institutions in driving historical transitions (Bryant 2006, p.35). This perspective highlights how the cumulative effects of past developments, including technological advancements, legal reforms, and demographic shifts, are essential for understanding long-run growth. The implication is that Eurocentric views are not merely a matter of geographical determinism but rather a complex interplay of endogenous factors and historical legacies. Bryant also acknowledges the challenges in overturning established understandings of European modernity (Bryant 2006, p.4). This recognition suggests that while cultural norms and institutions play a crucial role, their influence is often intertwined with broader socio-economic dynamics.

North and Weingast show that secure property rights were instrumental to England's economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), while Hornbeck illustrates how specific technological innovations like barbed wire fencing enabled farmers to protect their property rights on the American Plains (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). These studies highlight that institutional changes and technological innovations, by strengthening property rights, can significantly enhance economic development. The financial revolution in England exemplifies this dynamic: it fostered a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This interconnectedness of institutions and technology underscores the importance of secure property rights in driving long-run growth. However, Hornbeck's analysis of social norms in California reveals that legal frameworks are not always the primary determinant of property protection; sometimes social norms play a more critical role (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This suggests that while institutional changes and technological innovations are crucial, their effectiveness can be conditioned by broader cultural and social factors.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Pamuk (2007, p.24; 2007, p.5) demonstrate that structural changes in the economy and women's roles are integral to understanding long-run growth. These transformations have shifted economic paradigms by altering labor dynamics and technological innovations. Goldin shows how the increased demand for female office workers and educational expansion altered female labor supply, fostering a quiet revolution in employment patterns (Goldin 2006, p.8). This shift in labor dynamics was not merely evolutionary; it was driven by exogenous changes such as advancements in household production technology and institutional reforms that made part-time work more accessible to married women (Goldin 2006, p.3) The Black Death, as Pamuk (2007, p.24) argues, similarly initiated long-term structural changes in Europe. By increasing labor scarcity and cost, it drove technological innovations and institutional changes within guilds, thereby altering the economic landscape by 1450. These structural shifts underscore how broader cultural and social factors can influence economic growth beyond institutional frameworks alone. However, Acemoglu et al. note that these structural changes are not always uniformly positive; they can also create new forms of inequality (Goldin 2006, p.3).

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.1). Austin (2008, p.2) echoes this interdependence, noting that African responses to their environments ultimately changed the economic landscape through technological adaptations and institutional frameworks. This highlights how cultural factors can drive institutional changes that foster growth, even in the face of environmental challenges (Austin 2008, p.25).

Cultural values also shape social capital, which in turn influences institutional development. Putnam's theory of 'social capital' posits that trust, norms, and networks improve societal efficiency by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural approach explains the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) similarly argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, showing how institutional frameworks can be shaped by broader cultural values (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The role of culture in dominating institutions is further underscored by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4), who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of gender roles and social norms in shaping economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in how cultural beliefs and values shape both social capital and institutional frameworks. North (1989, p.1) shows that institutions matter in economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, while Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9). The precise mechanisms through which culture influences institutional development remain a subject of debate. While Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in cross-country comparisons, which suggest that favourable 'contracting' institutions (those regulating interactions between private citizens) may be partially interdependent with favourable 'property rights' institutions (those constraining the behaviour of political and economic elites). This shows how cultural factors can shape institutional development in ways that are not always efficient but nonetheless drive long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).
