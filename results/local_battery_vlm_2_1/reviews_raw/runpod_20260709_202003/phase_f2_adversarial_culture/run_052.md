The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high for understanding economic development, as the position one takes on this question shapes their view of both history and policy. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting efficiency and growth. Others posit that institutions themselves shape cultural norms over time. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for how societies can foster economic development. The disagreement hinges on whether culture drives institutional change or if institutions mold cultural attitudes. For example, some scholars assert that the decline of guilds in medieval Europe was due to changes in religious beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) while others contend that the financial revolution in England led to secure property rights and economic growth through institutional changes (North 1989, p.9). The interplay between culture and institutions is complex.

However, North and Weingast, along with North (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1), argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional change fostered a stable market for both public and private debt, providing a large positive externality that drove economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

This political model underscores how institutional changes can create long-term capital markets and stimulate economic growth. However, North and Weingast acknowledge that while institutions played a necessary role in England's success, other forces might have eventually led to similar outcomes, albeit more slowly and less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, suggesting that while institutional changes were crucial, they were not the sole drivers of economic growth.

Building on this idea, McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey suggests that the "new rhetoric" of bourgeois values fostered an environment conducive to economic innovation; this cultural shift was not merely a response to institutional changes but a driving force in its own right. Mokyr elaborates on this idea, highlighting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He emphasizes that much of this knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills rather than codified in written texts (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge facilitated innovation and economic development by enabling personal transmission and training across borders. Mokyr qualifies this mechanism: while useful knowledge diffused widely within the Western world, cultural differences between nations still played a significant role in shaping how this knowledge was applied and integrated into local economies. This nuanced view suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial drivers of economic growth, their impact was moderated by cultural contexts, which influenced how knowledge was interpreted and utilized.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. establish this relationship through the mechanism of extractive institutions, which were established in colonies with high European mortality rates and persisted over time. The persistence of these institutions is attributed to their design by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize rents rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic highlights that European settlers' ability to establish enduring institutions was contingent on the geographical and epidemiological conditions they encountered. Colonialism's influence on economic outcomes is a result of institutional legacies shaped by initial settlement patterns. The implications for long-run growth are significant: where extractive institutions took root, economic development was stunted, and these effects remain evident in contemporary income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

Consequently, distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key factor in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations. These institutions reflected power struggles among different social groups. This dynamic is evident in how the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of these institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic growth, as they created barriers to entry and restricted innovation. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; the entire institutional framework must be considered (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need to understand how different components of an economic system interact.

The slave trade is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects long-run growth (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that the historical legacy of slavery has had enduring effects on social capital, particularly trust. This mistrust is a deeply ingrained response to centuries of exploitation and institutional decay. The slave trade disrupted African societies, leading to political instability and weakened state structures (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the long-term effects include reduced economic development and increased corruption. This is because the historical trauma of slavery has left a lasting imprint on societal norms, making it difficult for modern institutions to function effectively. The persistence of mistrust in Africa means that efforts to foster growth must address both current institutional frameworks and the deep-seated cultural legacies of past injustices.

In light of this, Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) examine the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia. They highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions to understand these phenomena. Austin (2008, p.2) further notes that African responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, shaped economic outcomes in dynamic ways (Austin 2008, p.25). This suggests that endogenous factors played a significant role in shaping institutional changes and economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.1) highlight the importance of investment in agriculture and subsequent institutional change in driving high agricultural labour productivity in Europe. They argue that this productivity did not lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices because labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This dynamic interplay between endogenous factors and institutional changes underscores the complexity of economic development paths, illustrating how cultural responses can dominate institutions over time.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident through various historical and empirical lenses. Ogilvie's work highlights that cultural beliefs can significantly influence economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that cultures characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks foster institutions conducive to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates has had a lasting impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows how cultural responses to environmental factors can shape institutional frameworks over time.

The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding economic development paths. Ogilvie emphasizes that the European family system, community-based welfare systems, and labour markets were interconnected and contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Moreover, North and Weingast illustrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Cultural responses to environmental factors play a significant role in shaping institutional frameworks. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy highlights how historical events can shape long-run growth trajectories through their impact on institutions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the dynamic interplay between cultural responses, institutional changes, and economic development.

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains a subject of debate. There is agreement that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.9), but there is disagreement over whether cultural factors or exogenous shocks drive institutional change. Nunn and Wantchekon show that historical events like the slave trade can shape cultural norms, which in turn influence institutional frameworks (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how different historical periods and paths interact to shape economic development. This debate is settled by empirical evidence from Nunn and Wantchekon that shows how cultural norms of trust, shaped by historical events like the slave trade, persist today and affect economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).
