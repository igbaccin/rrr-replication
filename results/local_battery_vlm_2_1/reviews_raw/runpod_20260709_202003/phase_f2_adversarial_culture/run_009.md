The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is the extent to which cultural factors shape economic outcomes over extended periods. One position holds that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby driving long-term growth. Proponents of this view argue that specific cultural traits, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, foster institutions that promote economic development. Another perspective posits that institutions themselves are the primary drivers of growth, with cultural factors playing a secondary or even negligible role. This debate is crucial because it informs our understanding of how societies evolve economically and politically over time. If culture indeed dominates institutions in shaping long-run growth, then policies aimed at fostering economic development must consider cultural contexts. The opposing view suggests that institutional frameworks are more malleable and can be engineered to promote growth regardless of cultural backgrounds. This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how culture interacts with institutions over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This cultural legacy significantly impacts contemporary trust levels, which are crucial for economic development. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment where individuals were less likely to collaborate and trust one another, thereby impeding collective efforts toward growth. Akyeampong and Fofack elaborate on how gender roles in Africa during the colonial period have had long-lasting effects on production systems (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This suggests that the mistrust engendered by the slave trade may be compounded by persistent gender-based divisions of labor, further complicating economic development. These divisions are rooted in historical practices and continue to influence contemporary socio-economic structures. The deep-seated nature of these cultural norms and beliefs underscores the challenges Africa faces in overcoming the legacy of the slave trade.

The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting how institutional frameworks shaped by colonial practices have long-lasting economic impacts (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48). The impact of these institutions on economic development is profound. Acemoglu et al. show that high settler mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persist today and significantly affect income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This relationship between colonial practices and current economic outcomes underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks. The persistence of these institutions is further explained by their design to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This extractive nature of institutions, designed to benefit a select few, has limited economic development in many former colonies. These findings highlight the critical role that institutional frameworks play in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The new economic history of Africa emphasizes that institutional variables are more significant than other factors in explaining development (Bryant 2006, p.35; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. A causal connectedness governs every transition from past to present, and even the most revolutionary transformations are emergent "re-making" or "going beyond" on the basis of resources and opportunities that have been accumulating through prior developments (Bryant 2006, p.35). This emphasis on institutional change being gradual calls for historical investigation because current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory. One of the most interesting contributions to this perspective, which so far few economists have considered, is by Pierre Englebert who argues that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective in promoting growth if they are congruent with indigenous institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). High degrees of congruence underline the legitimacy of governments and enhance their ability to foster institutions that induce development. This suggests that understanding the historical context of institutional development is crucial for implementing effective reforms, as current institutions are deeply rooted in precolonial history.

Austin (2008, p.2); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) both challenge the oversimplification of causation in economic divergence narratives by highlighting distinct historical paths and institutional evolutions. Austin rejects the 'simple geography' thesis, emphasizing that Africans' responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, shaped their economic trajectories dynamically. This perspective underscores how endogenous factors influenced African development. Broadberry and Gupta extend this idea by showing that European industrialization was already diverging from Asia before 1800, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of institutional change and economic growth. The implication is that regional investment in agriculture and subsequent technological advancements are pivotal to long-term economic success.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that agricultural productivity gains did not necessarily translate into higher wages or lower food prices due to labor shifts into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This pattern suggests that institutional frameworks facilitating such transitions are crucial for sustained growth. While Broadberry and Gupta focus on Europe, Austin's work on Africa highlights how different responses to environmental challenges can lead to varied economic outcomes. Austin shows that Africans' use of technologies available at the time influenced their economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This underscores the importance of endogenous factors in shaping institutional frameworks conducive to growth. The implication is that understanding these dynamic, region-specific processes is essential for implementing effective reforms.

North and Weingast argue that institutional frameworks governing public choice were fundamental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of institutions such as the Bank of England played a pivotal role in this process, significantly reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional evolution actively shaped economic conditions by creating a stable market for public debt that had positive externalities for private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The institutions governing public choice thus facilitated long-term capital markets and overall economic growth, highlighting how endogenous institutional changes can drive economic development. These institutions were intricately linked with broader political and social dynamics. North (1989, p.1) points out that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War were instrumental in catalyzing this institutional change, underscoring the complex interplay between economic and political factors (North 1989, p.9). This suggests that while institutions are crucial for long-run growth, their effectiveness is contingent on broader socio-political conditions. The implication is that understanding these dynamics is essential for implementing effective reforms aimed at fostering sustained economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both argue that distributional conflicts significantly shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe highlights how serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were the outcomes of power struggles among different social groups (Ogilvie 2011). These institutions evolved in ways that reflected the interests of dominant elites, ensuring their control over resources and labor. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this perspective by showing how New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, such as policies restricting competition and perpetuating wealth disparities. This pattern of institutional development has lasting implications for economic growth. For instance, the persistence of inequality in these societies can be traced back to early colonial institutions that advantaged elites, which in turn influenced long-term economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These findings suggest that understanding how distributional conflicts shape institutions is crucial for grasping the fundamental causes of long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also acknowledges the interdependency of institutions within broader frameworks, noting that studying one institution in isolation may overlook critical interactions and feedback loops (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and their multifaceted impact on economic development.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. This dynamic is evident in both micro-level and macro-level analyses of economic development. In pre-industrial Europe, cultural beliefs significantly influenced the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can shape collective action games within economic institutions. Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights that societies with high levels of trust and reciprocity foster institutions conducive to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). In northern Italy, civic cultures encouraged strong guilds and communes, which in turn explained superior economic performance compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks further underscores the role of culture. For example, the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective welfare systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying one institution in isolation may overlook critical interactions and feedback loops. Cross-country comparisons also reveal that favorable contracting institutions are partially dependent on property rights institutions, emphasizing the need to examine entire institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Cultural factors extend beyond Europe, influencing economic trajectories globally. In Africa, for instance, pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs significantly impacted gender production systems. These norms, often codified during colonial periods, continue to reflect the sexual division of labor established then (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This historical continuity highlights how enduring cultural practices can shape long-term economic outcomes.

The role of culture is also evident in the evolution of institutions governing public choice. In seventeenth-century England, the financial revolution and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was crucial for England's long-run success, demonstrating how cultural and institutional factors can interact to drive economic development (North 1989, p.1).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by the impact of European mortality rates on colonial institutions. High mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence shows how cultural and institutional factors can shape economic trajectories over centuries.
