The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with significant implications for understanding economic development trajectories across different regions and periods. At stake are competing explanations for why some societies prosper while others stagnate. A key disagreement centers around whether cultural factors are intrinsic to institutional evolution or merely epiphenomenal to other causal drivers such as geography or political structures. Some researchers posit that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, influencing growth trajectories by fostering or hindering coordination and cooperation. Others argue that the impact of culture is overstated, with geopolitical factors and institutional frameworks playing more decisive roles in determining long-run economic performance. The debate hinges on whether culture operates through specific mechanisms to influence institutional outcomes or if it is merely a secondary factor subsumed under broader structural constraints. This review synthesizes key streams of scholarship to elucidate the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.9; McCloskey 2015, p.10). This perspective aligns with the idea that cultural shifts, along with institutional changes, were vital for economic growth. Ideas about liberty and dignity underpinned new values that encouraged innovation and entrepreneurship, leading to what McCloskey terms "the Great Enrichment." These ideas reshaped societal norms and expectations, creating an environment where economic activity could flourish.

The cultural struggle within France exemplifies how ideologies can shape economic outcomes. The French elite's adherence to traditional values stifled economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This pattern is evident in Spain as well, where conservative ideologies hindered economic progress despite the examples set by other European nations such as the Netherlands and Britain. These cases illustrate that cultural beliefs and ideals play a significant role in determining whether a society embraces or resists economic change.

The relationship between ideas and economic growth is bidirectional. The evolution of economic institutions can also influence the development of new ideas. For instance, the establishment of bourgeois values in countries like the Netherlands and Britain facilitated the adoption of innovative economic practices (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This interplay between culture and institutions highlights the complex dynamics at work in long-run growth.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War played a pivotal role in this transformation, as the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues laid the groundwork for future stability (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast show that these institutions provided a large positive externality for the development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England's operations began shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt, further solidifying this financial infrastructure. These institutional changes fostered secure property rights, which had implications beyond economic growth; they were also necessary for political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism's legacy of extractive institutions is the key driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They show that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persist today. These institutional differences are strongly correlated with current income per capita and economic performance. Acemoglu et al. exploit variations in settler mortality as exogenous factors influencing colonial institutions, demonstrating a robust relationship between these early institutions and contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional path dependency suggests that the extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, has had enduring effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. observe that while institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, this does not necessarily mean they encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes in former colonies.

Austin (2008, p.2); (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) challenge simplistic views of causation in economic development by re-evaluating historical economic institutions and paths. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights that compressing different historical periods can oversimplify causal processes, which may have led to a failure to fully account for the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic is also present in Ogilvie’s analysis, where she argues that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe more than efficiency considerations. This perspective suggests that institutional frameworks are interdependent systems where changes in one area can ripple through the entire structure.

Ogilvie’s examination of various European economic institutions underscores this interconnectedness. She posits that the European family system, for instance, was intricately linked with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision mechanisms, all contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin's critique aligns with this view by emphasizing that the adoption of growth-promoting institutions varies across countries due to period-specific relationships between institutions and economic outcomes. This variation suggests that the dynamic interplay between institutions and cultural factors is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth, as the same institutional framework may yield different results in different contexts over time. The complexity of this interaction underscores the need for a more nuanced approach to studying economic development, one that considers not just the presence of certain institutions but also how they evolve within their specific historical and cultural contexts.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15; 2006, p.1) argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Africa was already pronounced by 1800. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further support this claim by showing that African economies in the early twentieth century had levels of per capita income above subsistence, contrary to previous characterizations. The divergence can be traced back to differences in institutional changes and investment patterns, with Europe experiencing significant agricultural productivity gains due to institutional reforms, while Africa faced constraints on economic development tied to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This pattern suggests that the historical paths of these regions were shaped by distinct institutional frameworks and market dynamics. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors might have influenced these institutional differences over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon, together with Nunn's work on African slave trades (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27), argue that the transatlantic slave trade engendered a pervasive culture of mistrust in Africa. This cultural shift occurred because slavery disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment where cooperation was risky and trust was scarce. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has had enduring effects on economic development by shaping institutional frameworks and social dynamics.

The absence of strong central governance limited Africa’s ability to develop robust institutions capable of fostering economic growth. This institutional weakness is a critical factor in understanding Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn also notes that while the slave trade was devastating, it is not the sole determinant of Africa's current underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.1). Other historical factors, such as colonial rule and more recent events, must be considered alongside the legacy of slavery to fully understand Africa’s economic challenges.

The impact of mistrust on social capital further compounds these institutional weaknesses. Trust is a vital component of social capital, which in turn supports economic growth by facilitating cooperation and reducing transaction costs (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring effects of the slave trade have thus left African societies with lower levels of trust and social capital, hindering their ability to develop strong institutions that support long-run economic growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The cultural beliefs and values embedded within societies influence the structure of economic institutions by altering the dynamics of collective action games. For instance, Ogilvie argues that Catholic beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, while Protestant Reformation led to their decline in response to changing cultural norms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interplay between culture and institutions is evident in Putnam's theory of social capital, where trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that support economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The enduring effects of historical events, such as the slave trade, have also significantly impacted African societies' cultural fabric. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This legacy of mistrust further compounds institutional weaknesses by eroding social capital, a critical component in facilitating cooperation and reducing transaction costs. The lower levels of trust and social capital in African societies hinder their ability to develop strong institutions that support long-run economic growth, as highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's argument that institutions matter because they define the incentives and constraints within which individuals operate. North contends that secure property rights are essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita. The impact of these historical institutional frameworks on long-run growth is evident in the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The legacy of slavery and the associated mistrust have left African societies with lower levels of social capital, which in turn hinders their ability to develop strong institutions that support long-run economic growth. The precise historical evidence that can settle this disagreement comes from measuring the impact of institutional frameworks on current income per capita (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
