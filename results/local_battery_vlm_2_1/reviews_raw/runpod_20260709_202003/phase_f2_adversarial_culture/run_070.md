The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and historical scholarship. At stake is whether cultural factors shape institutions that drive economic development over centuries. The live disagreement involves two main positions. One camp argues that institutions are shaped by political forces, such as colonial policies and local power structures, which determine their extractive or inclusive nature (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Austin 2008, p.25). Another position posits that cultural norms, influenced by historical events like the slave trade, create social attitudes that affect institutional outcomes and economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This debate is crucial for understanding whether long-term economic development can be attributed to enduring cultural factors or if it is primarily driven by institutional changes. The resolution of this question has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of historical trajectories. Institutions evolve over time, shaped by political dynamics and societal norms among other factors.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies played a pivotal role in economic change from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). The cultural struggle for new ideas was an ongoing battle for betterment. This cultural shift was not isolated in France; it also extended to Spain, where conservative ideologies initially throttled growth despite the Dutch and British examples. The new rhetoric actively shaped existing circumstances, creating a feedback loop that drove economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr's work underscores the importance of useful knowledge in driving economic growth; this knowledge was often tacit and embodied in skills rather than codified in books. Access to such knowledge required personal transmission and training, facilitating its spread across Europe through conferences, periodicals, and translations (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). The dissemination of tacit knowledge is essential for economic growth; this includes both the explicit transfer of skills and the implicit learning that occurs through social interactions. This highlights the importance of cultural factors in shaping economic outcomes by facilitating the spread of knowledge and ideas.

This interplay between ideas and knowledge dissemination underscores how cultural factors can drive long-term growth.

Building on this framework, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1) emphasize the pivotal role of institutional evolution in fostering secure property rights. They identify the creation of the Bank of England as a pivotal moment, noting that this financial revolution dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change fostered a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of private debt markets. The institutions established during this period provided significant positive externalities, facilitating long-term capital markets and driving economic growth(North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North argues that secure property rights are essential for economic growth, as they incentivise investment and innovation by ensuring that individuals can reap the benefits of their efforts over time (North 1989, p.1). The institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England thus played a necessary role in making possible both long-term economic growth and political freedom.

This perspective contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions are more critical than cultural factors alone in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Their research identifies mortality rates as a key determinant of the type of institutions established by European colonizers. This institutional framework is crucial because it establishes a direct link between colonial policies and long-term economic performance, suggesting that the persistence of extractive institutions can significantly impair economic growth. The relationship between settler mortality and current institutions is strong: European settlements in areas with lower mortality rates were more likely to develop inclusive institutions, which in turn correlated with higher income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism underscores how colonial policies shaped by mortality risks set the stage for enduring institutional structures that continue to influence economic outcomes today.

This divergence is further illuminated by studies showing varying levels of per capita income and urban development between Europe and Africa (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) share the finding that these regions experienced significant differences in economic growth. This divergence is evident in wage levels: grain wages were comparable across advanced parts of India and China to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages, indicative of purchasing power over tradable goods and services, were markedly lower in Asia. This challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of regional growth patterns. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further note that institutional change brought about investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). However, this higher productivity did not translate into abundant grain supplies or low food prices because labor shifted out of agriculture into industry and services. This dynamic underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development, where cultural and institutional factors interplay with geographic endowments and market forces. The implication is that understanding long-run growth requires examining how these diverse elements interact over time to shape economic outcomes.

The complexity of catch-up processes in economic growth involves both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change, highlighting that simple convergence hypotheses are insufficient to explain long-run growth patterns (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Austin (2008, p.2) share this view. The catch-up process in economic growth is intricate because it depends on the ability of countries to exploit technological advances, which in turn relies on their social competence. This competence involves a country's capacity to understand, adapt, and implement new technologies effectively (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Social competence evolves over time as countries gain experience and develop institutional frameworks that support technological adoption (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic nature of social competence means that even if two countries start with similar productivity levels, their growth trajectories can diverge significantly due to differences in how they adapt to new technologies. For instance, a country with strong institutions may be better equipped to absorb and implement advanced technologies, leading to sustained economic growth. A country lacking such institutional support might struggle to keep pace despite initial similarities in productivity levels. This underscores the importance of considering both exogenous factors and endogenous dynamics when examining long-run growth patterns. The interplay between technological advance and social change highlights that simple convergence hypotheses do not fully capture the complexity of catch-up processes; it is essential to account for the evolving nature of social competence and its role in shaping economic outcomes over time.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. It is clear that historical events, such as the slave trade, have profoundly influenced contemporary social and economic conditions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The mistrust engendered by these historical experiences persists today, undermining collective action and governance effectiveness (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15; Austin 2008, p.25).

The institutions shaped by culture are critical for fostering long-term growth. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which in turn facilitates economic growth (North 1989, p.1). For example, the financial revolution in England led to a significant decline in transaction costs and enhanced property rights security, both of which are essential for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also crucial. North and Weingast highlight that the institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large positive externality for private debt markets in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This development underscores how institutional changes can have far-reaching economic impacts, fostering both political freedom and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The role of culture extends beyond historical events to include the enduring effects of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth, illustrating how cultural and institutional legacies can shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The debate on the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth centers around the persistence of historical legacies. North shows that institutions are central to economic performance because they reduce transaction costs and enhance property rights security (North 1989, p.1). Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions designed by European colonists have had lasting negative effects on income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement lies in the correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies and current institutional outcomes. This correlation highlights how cultural factors, such as mistrust from the slave trade, and institutional legacies from colonial rule have shaped contemporary economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).
