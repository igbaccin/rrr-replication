The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a pivotal question for understanding economic development. At stake is whether cultural factors can be isolated from other variables to explain persistent differences in institutional quality and economic performance across regions. A live disagreement exists between scholars who view culture as a primary driver of institutional change and those who see it as a secondary factor, with other antecedents such as geography, technology, or political structures playing more significant roles. The debate hinges on whether cultural norms can be endogenous to institutions or exogenous, shaping them independently.

Some scholars argue that the impact of culture on economic growth is mediated through its influence on institutional development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. highlight how mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies influenced the type of institutions established and their persistence over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors, such as settler mortality, can shape long-term economic outcomes by determining the nature of institutions. Similarly, Austin acknowledges the dynamic interplay between culture and institutions, emphasizing how African responses to environmental challenges shaped institutional evolution in ways that were partly endogenous (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This dynamic view aligns with North's argument that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The relationship between culture and institutions operates in multiple directions. Some scholars point out that cultural norms can be both a cause and an effect of institutional change. For example, Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate how pre-colonial and colonial gender roles influenced economic development in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This perspective suggests that cultural factors, such as gender norms, can shape institutions and economic outcomes over long periods. Additionally, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects current economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with cultural factors sometimes acting as antecedents to institutional change. Goldin highlights how shifts in social norms concerning women's roles influenced their employment and education patterns in the U.S., thereby affecting long-term economic growth (Goldin 2006, p.8). This perspective suggests that changes in cultural norms can precede and drive institutional changes, ultimately shaping economic outcomes.

Institutional development is a complex process influenced by multiple factors. The role of culture in this process is significant but not deterministic. Cultural norms can shape institutions and economic outcomes over long periods, but they are also shaped by these very same institutions and outcomes. This dynamic interplay underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with other variables to drive institutional change and economic growth.

The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly impacted long-run economic growth by shaping institutional development and perpetuating inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persistently affected current income per capita. This finding is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The specific mechanism through which mortality rates influenced institutional development involved the difficulty Europeans faced in settling colonies with high disease environments. High mortality rates hindered Europeans from establishing settlements, prompting extractive institutions that benefited a small elite group while limiting broad-based economic growth. This pattern is evident in the contrasting records of growth observed in societies with different institutional frameworks. North and Weingast illustrate how secure property rights, fostered by stable institutions, are crucial for long-run economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutional development in shaping economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis adds nuance by demonstrating that property rights' security alone is insufficient; the nature of these rights and their distribution within society are crucial. The implication is that while secure property rights are necessary for growth, the type of institutions governing these rights significantly influences economic outcomes. The extractive institutions established in high-mortality colonies not only restricted competition but also shaped long-term economic trajectories by perpetuating inequality and limiting broad-based development.

The historical trauma from the slave trade has deeply ingrained a lack of trust within African societies. This mistrust became ingrained within social structures, affecting long-term economic and institutional development. Akyeampong and Fofack show how colonial constructs and gender roles further reinforced these cultural beliefs (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The trans-Saharan and trans-Atlantic trading networks intensified state formation among the Akan groups, and women were indispensable to community reproduction. This dynamic highlights that while mistrust may have been a direct result of the slave trade, it was perpetuated through social norms and institutional frameworks that evolved over time. Moreover, the persistence of these cultural beliefs has significant implications for contemporary economic development. The legacy of mistrust created by the slave trade continues to shape African institutions today, affecting everything from interpersonal relationships to broader economic policies.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.27) share a finding that institutional variables are crucial in understanding Africa's development trajectory (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.27). Both authors argue that the legacy of historical events such as slavery and colonial rule has profoundly shaped Africa’s current institutions, which in turn influence economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.27) also proposes that the legacy of slavery may have contributed to modern corruption, suggesting that historical events' institutional damage extends beyond political instability to encompass broader societal issues (Nunn 2008, p.1). This underscores the complex interplay between historical events, institutional development, and long-term economic growth in Africa.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Goldin 2006, p.8) both highlight that significant shifts in employment dynamics are driven by exogenous changes. In the U.S., Goldin's analysis shows that these shifts began with increased demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, which made "nice" jobs available (Goldin 2006, p.3). This trend was reinforced by the growth of educational institutions and technological advancements in household production. These factors combined to alter female labor supply and dispense with barriers to employment for married women. Similarly, Broadberry and Gardner observe that African economies experienced growth tied closely to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These economic dynamics illustrate how exogenous factors can drive significant changes in employment patterns and economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner also observe that the field of African economic history has undergone a 'renaissance' based on newly digitized sources and innovative techniques (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This suggests that while exogenous factors are crucial, the availability of data and analytical methods can profoundly influence our understanding of economic dynamics.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey highlights how the rhetorical shift towards bourgeois values was instrumental in fostering an environment conducive to growth. This cultural transformation, which emphasized practical innovation and entrepreneurial spirit over traditional aristocratic norms, laid the groundwork for sustained economic development. Mokyr complements this perspective by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. He notes that the diffusion of knowledge across Europe facilitated the spread of new technologies and practices, which were crucial for industrial advancement. However, Mokyr also points out that much of the valuable knowledge during this period was tacit, embedded in skills and personal training rather than written texts (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This underscores the importance of social networks and apprenticeships in transmitting practical know-how, a factor that could have significant implications for understanding how cultural practices influence economic outcomes. Thus, the interplay between ideologies promoting innovation and the dissemination of useful knowledge created a dynamic environment where economic growth could flourish.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) (Austin 2008, p.2) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of economic development. They propose that colonialism's institutional legacies are key to understanding why some former colonies have thrived economically while others have lagged behind. This perspective highlights the role of institutions in shaping long-term growth trajectories. Austin (2008, p.25) observes that African environments have evolved dynamically through technological responses, demonstrating that endogenous factors can drive economic development and suggesting that the impact of colonialism is contingent on local adaptations.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that institutions are shaped by powerful groups who may prioritize short-term rents over long-run growth, suggesting a mechanism through which colonial institutions could have stunted development in some regions. This dynamic interplay between endogenous responses and exogenous institutional changes underscores the complexity of economic development trajectories across former colonies. This highlights how cultural and institutional factors interact in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Cultural responses to environmental challenges have been pivotal in shaping economic development trajectories across Africa. Austin and Nunn and Wantchekon show that local adaptations can mitigate the impact of exogenous institutional changes over time. Specifically, Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the trajectory of African economies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15; Austin 2008, p.25). Moreover, women played a significant role in these adaptive processes. Akyeampong and Fofack illustrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting their indispensable role in community reproduction and wealth transmission (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).

The interaction between culture and institutions is further illuminated by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that secure property rights are crucial for long-term economic growth. This dynamic is evident in England's financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident across these studies. This dynamic interplay between endogenous responses and exogenous institutional changes underscores the complexity of economic development trajectories across former colonies. The historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on trust within Africa, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon, settles this disagreement by highlighting the persistent cultural effects of historical events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
