The role of culture in shaping institutions and driving long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether cultural factors are fundamental causes that directly influence institutional frameworks and thereby economic performance over the long term, or if they merely serve as secondary influences that reinforce pre-existing institutional arrangements. One side argues that cultural beliefs and values shape the structure of institutions by influencing collective action games and shaping societal norms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective posits that culture can be a direct driver of economic efficiency through its impact on institutional design. The opposing view contends that the influence of culture is more indirect, with broader factors such as mortality rates and geopolitical conditions determining institutions, which in turn shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural elements interact with institutional frameworks to foster or hinder long-run growth. The resolution of this disagreement could fundamentally alter our grasp of historical economic development and inform policy strategies aimed at fostering sustainable growth in diverse cultural contexts.

North and Weingast argue that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The role of secure property rights is paramount in this framework. North (1989, p.1) traces the significance of institutions to the English financial revolution, which dramatically reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was crucial for fostering economic growth and political freedom (North 1989, p.9). The Bank of England's establishment marked a pivotal shift, providing a stable market for public debt that also facilitated private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North and Weingast highlight that the financial revolution was a necessary condition for England's long-run success (Author Year p.N). This perspective underscores that institutions governing public choice were essential in creating an environment conducive to economic growth.

The role of secure property rights is influenced by the broader context of political and fiscal crises. The trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the Hundred Years' War highlights how fiscal pressures can drive institutional change (North 1989, p.9). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness is contingent on the historical and geopolitical contexts that shape them. Institutions are fundamental drivers of long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. and Nunn and Wantchekon share that colonial institutions fundamentally determine long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In this view, the institutional framework imposed by European colonizers significantly influenced development trajectories in colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutions were shaped to maximize rents for European colonists, not promoting long-term economic growth in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is crucial because it reveals how extractive colonial institutions can persist and hinder economic development over extended periods. As a result, the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to affect economic performance today.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) find that the slave trade caused a cultural shift towards greater mistrust among Africans. This mistrust has deep historical roots that continue to affect economic outcomes today (Nunn 2008, p.1; Nunn 2008, p.27). The mechanism underlying this effect can be traced back to the political instability and weakened state structures caused by the slave trades. Nunn and Wantchekon observe that the persistence of this mistrust is not solely due to historical political instability (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). They argue that cultural factors such as mistrust play a significant role in shaping long-term development trajectories, independent of broader institutional frameworks.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This intellectual shift was a catalyst for economic growth, responding to institutional frameworks. McCloskey (2015, p.10) highlights how cultural and ideological changes, such as the rise of bourgeois values, fostered an environment conducive to innovation and economic development in countries like the Netherlands and Britain (McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.56) complements this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge was central to economic progress. He notes that much valuable knowledge during this period was tacit, requiring personal transmission and training for dissemination (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge played a pivotal role in driving economic growth by facilitating the transfer of skills and expertise across regions. Mokyr also points out that the diffusion of this knowledge depended on social networks and institutional contexts that varied across Europe (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). This variability suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, their impact was moderated by local conditions and the effectiveness of knowledge transmission mechanisms. The significance of cultural factors in shaping economic outcomes is evident in how different regions responded to the same intellectual movements, highlighting the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth.

Distributional conflicts and institutional arrangements play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) jointly argue that the persistence of inequality is deeply rooted in the institutions that evolved from different factor endowments. This perspective underscores how initial conditions, such as the abundance of land relative to labor, influenced the development of institutions that either fostered or hindered economic growth. The mechanisms by which distributional conflicts shaped these institutions are complex and varied; they include policies that restricted competition and advantaged elites, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework can complicate this picture (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, their impact on economic growth is mediated by the overall institutional environment.

Ogilvie's analysis highlights how different frameworks, such as community-based welfare systems and labor markets, can interact to either promote or hinder economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional arrangements suggests that while culture may influence economic outcomes through its impact on institutions, it is not the sole determinant. The broader institutional framework within which these conflicts play out is equally important.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They suggest that economic growth results from complex interactions between institutions, investment, and policy experiences over time. This dynamic view challenges static explanations of economic development, such as the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to growth (Austin 2008, p.25). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This pattern can be seen in other regions too: for example, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) describe how high levels of investment in southern Indian agriculture led to increased productivity during the 17th and 18th centuries (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This investment did not necessarily lead to abundant grain and low food prices because labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This pattern suggests that economic growth is determined by how societies respond to and utilize their resources over time, in addition to institutional frameworks. The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth remains complex, as it interacts with various historical paths and periods.

Culture significantly influences institutions as fundamental causes of long-run growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions by motivating behavior and altering collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. present evidence that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This suggests that cultural factors, such as beliefs about the afterlife or social norms, can interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic outcomes. Culture's role in shaping institutions is further supported by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of public choice institutions fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This indicates that cultural norms and values can influence the development of institutions that support long-run growth.

The interplay between culture and institutions is complex. Ogilvie highlights how different institutional frameworks, such as family systems and welfare systems, were interdependent with cultural factors like trust and reciprocity (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Austin 2008, p.25). Austin argues that African responses to their environments, influenced by available technologies and cultural practices, played a crucial role in shaping economic development paths. This dynamic interaction between culture and institutions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how these factors contribute to long-run growth.

The historical evidence shows that culture plays a pivotal role in dominating institutions as fundamental causes of long-run growth. The shared finding is that cultural beliefs, norms, and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The precise mechanisms by which culture interacts with institutions remain debated. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25), while Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of trust levels across different cultural contexts. This approach shows how cultural norms of behavior can be traced back to historical determinants like colonial rule and slavery, providing a clearer understanding of their enduring effects on economic institutions and long-run growth.
