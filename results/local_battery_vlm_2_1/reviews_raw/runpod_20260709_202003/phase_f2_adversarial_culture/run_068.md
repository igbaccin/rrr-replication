The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue at the heart of economic development scholarship. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors versus other variables in shaping economic trajectories. The debate centers around whether institutions are shaped by cultural beliefs and values that, in turn, drive economic growth, or if other factors such as geographic conditions, extractive institutions, or gender dynamics play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency. Others contend that distributional conflicts and institutional frameworks provide better explanations for economic outcomes. Additionally, the impact of European mortality rates on colonial institutions and the contributions of African women to economic growth highlight the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and long-run development. Understanding this relationship is crucial for grasping how societies evolve economically over time.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the role of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions and their efficiency. Cultural theories posit that non-economic beliefs can influence institutional structures. For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's decline in these beliefs led to a decrease in guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam’s theory of 'social capital,' which suggests that cultural values such as trust and reciprocity facilitate coordinated actions and encourage institutions favoring economic growth. This view is supported by studies showing that societies characterized by high social capital, due to their 'altruistic' or 'civic' cultures, have better economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework makes this narrative complex. For example, European family systems and community-based welfare systems were interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and insurance provision, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. Cultural influences may be part of a larger institutional ecosystem and act in conjunction with other factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Another stream of scholarship argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency. Ogilvie contends that the European family system, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical factors on economic development. The strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions underscores the importance of these historical legacies (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This view is further supported by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous approach to understanding economic development. The African story highlights how Africans' responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Austin 2008, p.25).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also explored through the lens of transaction costs and property rights. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast's argument that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, indicating that institutions were necessary for making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). This view is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack's argument that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant impacts on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This suggests that cultural beliefs and values, along with institutional frameworks, play a complex role in shaping economic development.

In sum, culture plays a multifaceted role in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth. Cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, while distributional conflicts and institutional frameworks provide additional explanatory power. The interplay between cultural factors, institutional ecosystems, and historical legacies underscores the complexity of economic development, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of these relationships.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that secure property rights are essential for economic growth. They demonstrate this through the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England. The development of financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework fostered a stable market for both public and private debt, which in turn facilitated long-term capital investment and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The English financial revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can create positive externalities that support broader economic development. Institutions also shape the distribution of power and resources within society; their role is not limited to reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) highlights that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises reflects a strategic institutional adaptation. This suggests that while secure property rights are crucial, the path to achieving them is contingent on the specific historical and political contexts which shape institutional evolution.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic development. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers in the colonies determined their settlement patterns and, consequently, the type of institutions established. Colonies with high mortality rates were less likely to see permanent European settlements, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term resource extraction rather than fostering long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions persist over time, exerting a significant influence on current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The mechanism by which colonial institutions affect economic development is rooted in their extractive nature. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritize maximizing rents over promoting long-term growth. This institutional design has lasting effects on economic outcomes, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies.

The impact of colonial institutions varies across different contexts. The persistence of these institutions and their effect on economic development can vary depending on local conditions and subsequent historical events.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. They show that these conflicts can shape institutional frameworks to favor elites at the expense of broader economic growth. Ogilvie illustrates this by examining how European guilds and communities were structured to serve the interests of their members, often at the cost of efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is further illuminated by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality. These inequalities persisted because institutions evolved to protect elite interests, restricting competition and maintaining power imbalances (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Ogilvie elaborates that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework can reinforce these distributional outcomes. She notes that labor markets, credit markets, and welfare systems are interconnected, and changes in one area can have cascading effects on others (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence means that institutional change is embedded within a complex web of social and economic relations.

Ogilvie acknowledges the challenge of studying institutions in isolation. The complexity of these interdependencies suggests that policy interventions must consider the entire institutional framework to be effective. She underscores that understanding how different institutions interact can provide insights into why certain policies succeed or fail in promoting economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa which persists today. This culture of mistrust persists as a force shaping contemporary social and economic interactions; it is not just an artifact of history. The legacy of mistrust manifests in various ways, including reduced cooperation and weakened institutions, both of which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates that the slave trades led to long-term political instability, resulting in fragmented states. This fragmentation has hindered the development of strong, centralized institutions capable of fostering economic growth. The historical context of mistrust and political instability creates a challenging environment for institutional reforms aimed at promoting development. Nunn (2008, p.27) suggests that this history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance, highlighting the deep-seated nature of these issues. Therefore, addressing the cultural legacy of the slave trade is essential for understanding Africa's long-run growth and for designing effective policies to promote development.

The 'reversal of fortune' literature emphasizes historical and institutional paths to economic divergence without directly addressing cultural determinants (Austin 2008, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.24). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the African experience illustrates a more dynamic version of geographical determinism. Africans' responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately altered their developmental trajectories. This perspective is consistent with a broader view that historical and institutional factors played pivotal roles in shaping economic paths. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) contend that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. They argue that advanced parts of India and China during this period exhibited characteristics similar to stagnating regions in Europe, and not the developing northwestern parts.

This early divergence underscores the importance of historical context in shaping economic outcomes. Pamuk (2007, p.24) suggests that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes contributing to a wage gap between northwest Europe and other regions by 1450. The impact of the Black Death included declining interest rates, increased mobility, institutional change within guilds, and technological innovations driven by labor scarcity. These factors collectively highlight how historical events can catalyze enduring economic shifts. This dynamic interplay between historical events and endogenous responses underscores the complexity of economic development paths. The 'reversal of fortune' literature emphasizes that understanding these historical and institutional factors is crucial for grasping long-term economic divergence.

The role of women in economic development intersects significantly with cultural and institutional factors, shaping long-run growth trajectories (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods were substantial. This perspective counters views that marginalize their role by highlighting how gender-based colonial constructs influenced the dynamics of gender relations with enduring consequences for production systems. Goldin observes a similar dynamic in the U.S., where shifts in women's employment and education were driven by changes in social norms and expectations regarding future work. These transformations began in the late 1960s and 1970s, influenced by factors such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.8). Goldin also notes that exogenous changes, including advancements in household production technology and institutional shifts allowing part-time work, altered female labor supply. These changes dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women, illustrating how cultural and institutional factors can facilitate economic participation for women over time (Goldin 2006, p.3). The role of women in economic development is thus deeply intertwined with broader cultural and institutional contexts, which shape their contributions to long-run growth.

Bryant (2006, p.35) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) together examine how different societies have progressed over time by exploring the interplay of culture, institutions, and economic development. They share a focus on how historical inheritances shape contemporary outcomes (Bryant 2006, p.35; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Bryant (2006, p.35) argues that transitions to industrial modes of production are enabled by technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms accumulated through prior developments. This perspective underscores the importance of long-term socio-cultural factors in shaping economic trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.8) extends this view by highlighting how institutional variables, influenced by historical contexts including precolonial Africa, significantly impact development outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The implication is that effective institutions for growth are more likely to emerge when they align with indigenous cultural norms and historical legacies. Bryant cautions against oversimplifying the causal mechanisms at play. He notes that focusing solely on immediate processes can lead to explanatory distortions (Bryant 2006, p.4), suggesting a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with institutions over time is necessary for a comprehensive view of economic development. This perspective highlights the complex role of culture in shaping institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the need for historical context in analyzing contemporary development outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Institutions shape economic outcomes by facilitating coordinated actions and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast demonstrate that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, highlighting how institutional changes in seventeenth-century England fostered a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical example underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic development. Similarly, Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs and values influence economic institutions by altering collective action games, thereby affecting efficiency and growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The interplay between culture and institutions is evident in how social capital, trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This dynamic is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Austin builds on this by emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed technological availability and economic outcomes over time (Austin 2008, p.25).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further illustrated by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This perspective challenges marginalizing views that downplay their role, showing how gender-based constructs and codification of roles had long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth is evident in the historical evidence from these studies. Historical legacies and cultural norms continue to shape institutional frameworks that influence contemporary development outcomes. While there is agreement on the fundamental role of institutions in long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), disagreement persists over the specific mechanisms by which cultural factors interact with institutions. Austin shows how different responses to environmental challenges can lead to varied economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of historical contexts and institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.25). The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs directly influence economic institutions or if they do so indirectly through altering collective action games. This debate is settled by the empirical comparison of settler mortality rates and their impact on current institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al., demonstrating that historical institutional frameworks have a lasting effect on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).
