The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal to understanding long-run economic growth. Culture, encompassing shared beliefs and values, can influence institutional frameworks that either foster or hinder economic development. This question is contentious because it intersects with the debate over whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or merely a reflection of deeper cultural forces. Some scholars argue that cultural norms directly shape institutions, while others contend that institutions evolve independently of culture, driven by factors such as geopolitical circumstances and economic necessities. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship could provide insights into why some societies thrive economically while others stagnate. The disagreement centers on whether the causal arrow runs from culture to institutions or whether other factors, such as geographic conditions and historical events, play a more decisive role.

The debate over the influence of culture on institutional frameworks is deeply rooted in the analysis of pre-industrial European economic systems. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs, particularly those related to afterlife expectations and social norms, significantly influenced the structure of guilds and other economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of "social capital, " which posits that societies characterized by high levels of trust and reciprocity are more likely to develop institutions that support economic growth. This view is complemented by North's assertion that institutions matter because they govern the costs of transactions, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast further emphasize the role of secure property rights, facilitated by institutional changes such as the English financial revolution, in fostering long-term economic development. The English financial revolution played a crucial role in England's success, demonstrating how institutions can provide necessary conditions for sustained growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The influence of culture on institutional frameworks is also evident in the historical context of colonial societies. Acemoglu et al. highlight that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors, such as beliefs about health and survival, can indirectly shape institutional frameworks through their impact on early colonial policies. Nunn and Wantchekon extend this analysis by arguing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy of mistrust is linked to reduced social capital, which in turn hinders institutional development and economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by examining the impact of inequality on institutional frameworks. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective suggests that cultural norms around social hierarchy and power dynamics can influence institutional development in ways that perpetuate economic disparities. This interplay between culture, inequality, and institutional frameworks underscores the complex nature of economic development.

Institutional frameworks are not static; they evolve in response to cultural norms and historical events. Ogilvie highlights the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective is supported by North's assertion that institutions govern transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, as analyzed by North and Weingast, further illustrates how institutional changes can foster secure property rights and economic growth. These findings underscore the dynamic nature of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were pivotal drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey illustrates this through the "Franco-French War, " where cultural struggles and new rhetoric shaped economic circumstances, leading to growth. Mokyr's analysis of useful knowledge complements McCloskey's perspective by showing how tacit knowledge, transmitted through personal interactions and training, facilitated economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr notes that the diffusion of this knowledge spread widely within the Western world, unconstrained by national borders. This implies that cultural shifts, as exemplified by changes in rhetoric and ideologies, can create an environment conducive to the accumulation and dissemination of useful knowledge, thereby fostering long-run economic growth.

However, Mokyr's emphasis on the tacit nature of much useful knowledge suggests a qualification: while ideas and ideologies are crucial, their impact is mediated through practical skills and embodied knowledge. This implies that cultural factors alone may not be sufficient to drive economic growth without the concomitant development of tangible, applicable knowledge. Therefore, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth must be understood within a broader framework that includes the practical application of ideas and ideologies through useful knowledge.

The literature on complexity in economic growth processes and social change highlights the multifaceted nature of growth dynamics, influenced by technological advances and social changes (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Goldin 2006, p.8). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) posits that catch-up processes involve complex interactions between exogenous factors and endogenous dynamics. This complexity is exemplified by shifts in productivity levels, which are erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology; social competence for exploiting advanced methods was initially limited but improved over time (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Goldin (2006, p.8) shows that changes in women's employment and education patterns were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and institutional changes that made part-time work more accessible. These shifts involved preconditions like occupational change and alterations in household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.8). The social norms concerning women’s family and career began to change in the late 1960s and 1970s, indicating a broader societal transformation that influenced economic growth. This dynamic interplay between technological advances and social changes underscores the intricate nature of long-run growth processes. However, this complexity does not explicitly link these factors to cultural drivers as the primary cause of long-run growth, suggesting that while culture may influence these dynamics, it is one among many contributing factors.

The institutions leading to this stability were crucial for fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further argue that these institutional developments played a necessary role, suggesting that without them, England's success would have been slower and less decisive. The extent to which these institutions were influenced by cultural factors remains an open question.

These authors propose that the core economic institutions, serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by power struggles between different social groups rather than by cultural factors or efficiency considerations. Ogilvie highlights how these institutions emerged from the complex dynamics of class conflicts and political negotiations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions evolved to maintain elite control over resources and labor, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

The mechanism through which distributional conflicts shape economic institutions involves the strategic actions of elites to secure their positions. Ogilvie shows how guilds and communities were structured to protect the interests of those in power, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument by illustrating that in societies with extreme inequality, institutions were designed to restrict competition and maintain elite dominance over resources such as land and labor.

The interdependence of institutions within a broader framework complicates this picture. Ogilvie notes that individual institutions are part of an interconnected system where changes in one area can have ripple effects across others, making it challenging to isolate the impact of any single institution. For instance, the European family system was linked with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they operate within a complex web of institutional interactions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions are the fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by European mortality rates upon arrival and influenced long-term economic performance through their impact on property rights, political stability, and economic policies. For instance, high settler mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonists rather than fostering long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism is supported by evidence showing a strong correlation between settler mortality rates and current institutional quality in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions over time has significant implications for economic development, as they continue to influence income per capita and overall economic performance today.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth varies based on the specific institutional changes introduced by colonizers. For example, colonies with high settler mortality rates often developed extractive institutions that hindered economic growth, while those with lower mortality rates established more inclusive institutions that promoted development. This pattern highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

The persistence of these institutions is actively maintained by politically powerful groups who benefit from the existing institutional arrangements (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding the political economy of institutional change in former colonies. The implications are significant for policy and development efforts, as they suggest that addressing the legacy of colonial institutions requires not only economic reforms but also political changes that redistribute power away from entrenched interests.

Nunn and Wantchekon, together with Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)(Nunn 2008, p.27), demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust. This phenomenon is rooted in the long-term political instability caused by the slave trades, which resulted in weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.1; Nunn 2008, p.27). The legacy of this instability persists through contemporary corruption, a direct manifestation of the historical trauma inflicted by slavery and its destabilizing effects on African societies (Nunn 2008, p.27)(Nunn 2008, p.1). This dynamic is further compounded by the fact that the slave trade also led to a reduction in trust levels within African societies. This legacy of mistrust has tangible economic consequences, affecting everything from investment in public goods to the effectiveness of governance structures.

Culture plays a fundamental role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth by influencing social norms and collective action. The mistrust engendered by historical events such as the slave trade persists today and undermines social capital necessary for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This legacy of mistrust has tangible economic consequences, affecting everything from investment in public goods to the effectiveness of governance structures. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This interconnectedness highlights how cultural beliefs and values influence economic outcomes by shaping institutions that either favor or hinder growth.

The impact of culture on institutions can be seen through the lens of historical events and their long-term effects on social norms. The slave trade, for instance, has left a lasting imprint on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that continues to affect contemporary social and economic dynamics (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This mistrust undermines the social capital needed for collective action and cooperation, which are crucial for economic development. The persistence of this cultural legacy shows how historical events can shape institutions over the long term, influencing economic outcomes through their impact on social norms and collective behavior.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illustrated by the evolution of financial institutions during England's financial revolution. This period saw the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The secure property rights that emerged from these institutional changes were critical for both economic growth and political freedom (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4), highlighting how cultural factors can influence the creation of institutions that support long-run growth. This shared finding demonstrates the importance of credible commitment mechanisms in fostering economic development.

The precise relationship between culture, institutions, and long-term growth remains debated. One point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The disagreement lies in whether cultural factors or exogenous shocks primarily drive institutional change. Some scholars argue that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). However, others contend that historical events such as the slave trade have left a lasting imprint on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that continues to affect contemporary social and economic dynamics. The precise impact of these cultural factors can be measured through comparative analysis of trust levels across regions with different historical experiences (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).
