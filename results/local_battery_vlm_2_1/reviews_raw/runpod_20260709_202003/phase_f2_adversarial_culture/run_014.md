The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within economic historiography and institutional economics. At stake is the fundamental question of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One position holds that cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic outcomes by shaping institutions that either facilitate or hinder growth. This perspective highlights the endogenous nature of institutions, asserting they are products of societal norms and values as well as constraints (Author Year p.N). A rival view posits that geographic factors, such as mortality rates and disease environments, play a more significant role in determining institutional frameworks. These frameworks, in turn, shape long-term economic performance. Another perspective highlights the importance of secure property rights and financial institutions in fostering growth, suggesting that cultural factors are secondary to these structural elements. The debate thus centers on whether culture is a primary driver or a mere facilitator of institutional change, with implications for understanding both historical trajectories and contemporary development strategies. One stream of scholarship focuses on how cultural beliefs influence economic institutions directly, shaping outcomes through collective action games. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution in seventeenth-century England illustrates this dynamic; the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development provided a large positive externality for the parallel growth of private debt markets, fostering economic growth. The political model that led to this revolution was contingent on specific historical circumstances: namely, the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast (1989, p.24) emphasize that these institutions provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported private operations. The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt was pivotal to this process; its formation shortly after the financial revolution highlights how institutional changes can have cascading effects on economic development.

Building on this, Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) emphasize that the distribution of power within societies is a key determinant in how economic institutions evolve. They argue these conflicts drove the formation of key institutions such as serfdom and guilds, along with cultural factors. Ogilvie shows that the rules governing these institutions reflected power dynamics between different social groups in addition to being efficient (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). In particular, the persistence of inequality in New World societies, as Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate, was reinforced by institutional frameworks that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This suggests that the institutional landscape in these societies was shaped by distributional conflicts, where elite interests were protected through various government policies that restricted competition. Ogilvie's framework underscores how the interdependency of institutions within a broader system can influence economic outcomes. She argues that studying individual institutions in isolation is insufficient; instead, one must consider entire institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective challenges the idea that culture alone drives long-run growth, as it shows how power dynamics and distributional conflicts play crucial roles. These insights suggest that even when cultural factors are present, their impact on economic development may be mediated by underlying power structures and institutional arrangements that favor certain groups over others.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) extend this by showing that the colonial legacy of institutions continues to influence long-term economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita. This argument is supported by the finding that a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions exists (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further substantiate this claim, demonstrating that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played an important role in economic growth and industrialization among former colonies. This implies that the legacy of colonial institutions can be seen as a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes; therefore, it is not just the cultural factors but also the extractive nature of these institutions that have significantly shaped current economic disparities. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth, suggesting that the persistence of these institutions is not merely a result of cultural factors but also a consequence of power dynamics and institutional design favoring certain groups over others.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15), among other scholars (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), illustrate that understanding economic divergence requires examining the historical context of institutional changes. They argue that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods, paths, and institutional evolution into a single narrative. This complex interplay involves various factors such as investment patterns, policy experiences, and technological innovations (Austin 2008, p.25). For instance, Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis emphasizes that Africans' responses to their environments, influenced by available technologies, ultimately changed the economic trajectory over time. This dynamic approach suggests that environmental obstacles evolve in response to endogenous factors such as institutional changes and technological advancements (Pamuk 2007, p.5). Pamuk (2007, p.24) further illustrates this point by showing how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450, challenging views that attribute this divergence solely to later developments.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) further demonstrate that institutional reforms can be pivotal in addressing contemporary economic challenges. This cluster of literature posits that historical institutional change is gradual and extends beyond the colonial period into precolonial history, a pattern that requires deep contextual understanding to foster effective modern institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous ones (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) support this by showing that export production in African economies was closely tied to foreign trade, influenced by a range of factors including political institutions. This underscores the importance of congruence between contemporary and historical institutions for economic development. The effectiveness of these reforms depends on local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This suggests that while institutional reforms are crucial, their success is also contingent on broader historical and economic conditions, which may limit their direct applicability to long-run growth through culture (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that women's roles in economic development are pivotal but often overlooked. They highlight how colonial constructs and gender norms profoundly shaped production systems and gender relations in Africa, with long-term consequences for economic growth. The sexual division of labor established during the colonial period remains influential today. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) show that in matrilineal societies like Asante, women were essential to community reproduction; their bloodlines defined the transmission of wealth and office. This dynamic underscores how cultural beliefs and social norms can perpetuate economic structures, even as broader institutional changes occur. This suggests that while cultural and institutional factors are interconnected, they do not operate in isolation; broader historical and economic conditions also play a crucial role in shaping long-run growth through culture.

Nunn and Wantchekon's work on the slave trade highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade is shown to have caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today. This mistrust has had significant implications for economic development, with Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrating that the slave trades led to long-term political instability and weakened states. This instability is crucial because it undermines state centralization, which recent empirical research shows as an important determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.1). The weakening of African states due to political instability has had profound effects on economic development, including corruption. This suggests that while mistrust has hindered African development by eroding social capital, targeted interventions could potentially rebuild trust and foster greater economic cooperation.

Culture plays an intricate role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth by influencing economic outcomes and institutional development. Putnam's theory of social capital shows how cultural beliefs and values can foster institutions that promote economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, the civic cultures which emphasize trust, reciprocity, and horizontal networks have historically encouraged institutions that favor both economic growth and political accountability, as evidenced in Northern Italy compared to Southern Italy. Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, indicating that cultural factors alone do not determine institutional trajectories.

The role of culture in institutional development is further illuminated by examining the historical evolution of institutions governing public choice. North and Weingast argue that the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These secure property rights were critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. Austin shares this perspective, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

The interplay between culture and institutions is also evident in the contributions of African women to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack show that women remained indispensable to community reproduction, highlighting their role in transmitting wealth and office within matrilineal societies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). These roles underscore how cultural beliefs and gender dynamics can significantly influence institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement about the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is found in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. This has been recognized by studies of European economic institutions, such as those examining the English family system and its link to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The evidence shows that cultural factors are integral components of complex institutional frameworks that drive long-run growth.
