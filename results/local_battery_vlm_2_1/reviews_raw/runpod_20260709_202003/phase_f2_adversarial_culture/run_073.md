The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature, pitting those who see cultural factors as central against those who view them as peripheral. At stake is how to understand and promote sustained economic growth, with implications for policy and historical interpretation. On one side are scholars who argue that cultural beliefs influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby affecting long-run growth. These scholars often emphasize the role of shared values such as trust, reciprocity, and civic engagement in fostering institutional environments conducive to economic development. On the other hand, there are those who propose rival causes for long-term growth, focusing on factors like extractive institutions or endogenous responses to environmental conditions.

The cultural approach to understanding long-run growth often highlights how specific cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions. For example, Ogilvie's work shows that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to a decline in these guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high levels of trust and civic engagement tend to have institutions that foster economic growth. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis indicates that cultural factors alone do not explain long-run growth; historical paths are complex and multifaceted (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate underscores the importance of understanding how cultural beliefs interact with institutional frameworks.

The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is recognized in studies of European economic institutions, which show how systems like the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England were linked to various markets and family structures. This interconnectedness suggests that cultural factors may have played a role in fostering economic growth by influencing institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The role of culture in these developments remains under-explored. While North and Weingast's analysis underscores the importance of institutions, it leaves open questions about how cultural factors might have influenced or been influenced by these institutional shifts, particularly regarding the long-run growth of economies.

Pamuk argues that long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This divergence was driven by a series of interconnected economic shifts. These developments were further reinforced by increased mobility and flexibility within guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Broadberry and Gupta emphasize that these structural transformations led to a distinct economic trajectory in northwestern Europe. The region experienced higher agricultural labor productivity, which did not necessarily translate into lower food prices due to labor migration towards industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15).

The wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450 highlights how demographic shocks can catalyze structural changes that drive economic divergence. These shifts had long-lasting implications for economic development, demonstrating how historical events like the Black Death could reshape economic landscapes over centuries.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions, with inequality playing a crucial role in this process. Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe highlights how the core economic institutions, such as serfdom, community structures, and guilds, were shaped by struggles over wealth and power rather than efficiency or cultural factors. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman’s analysis of New World societies, where initial differences in factor endowments led to extreme inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These inequalities were perpetuated through institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and limiting opportunities for broader economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman point out that these institutional frameworks were deeply influenced by the political and social dynamics that emerged from unequal distributions of wealth and power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is further illustrated by Ogilvie's examination of how different institutions within a broader framework are interdependent, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. For instance, the European family system was intertwined with labor markets, credit systems, and collective provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness underscores how institutional frameworks that emerged from distributional conflicts could have long-lasting effects on economic development.

Ogilvie acknowledges the importance of these interdependencies; she also notes the complexity of studying institutions in isolation. The difficulty lies in understanding how these institutions interact within a wider framework and resist changes imposed by external factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This challenge is compounded by the fact that distributional conflicts can create entrenched interests that may resist institutional reforms aimed at promoting growth.

Acemoglu et al.  argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective highlights the role of extractive institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates among European settlers, which persistently affected income per capita and shaped current economic performance. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), a pattern that is consistent across former colonies and has significant implications for understanding the reversal in relative incomes between these regions and their colonial powers. This mechanism underscores how initial conditions such as mortality rates can have enduring effects on economic outcomes through the pathway of institutional design.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Hopkins 2009, p.8) focus on how historical economic patterns and institutional reforms have influenced African economies. This cluster identifies that the impact of colonial institutions is contingent upon broader socio-political factors and the congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions. These factors play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes, as evidenced by Broadberry and Gardner's findings that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates on this theme, suggesting that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth if they align with indigenous institutions. High degrees of congruence enhance government legitimacy and foster development-inducing institutions, while low levels of congruence create conditions that favor patrimonial politics, regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. This highlights the importance of institutional congruence in achieving sustainable economic growth.

The role of women in economic development is an important aspect that intersects with cultural and institutional factors but does not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) underscore that African women played a pivotal role in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. These dynamics are also evident in African societies, where women remained indispensable to community reproduction, as seen in matrilineal Akan states (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The impact of these cultural shifts on economic development is profound, as they altered social norms and expectations regarding women's roles in society, fostering changes that supported growth. However, Goldin (2006, p.8) notes that exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions, also played a significant role in altering female labor supply (Goldin 2006, p.3). This qualification suggests that while cultural factors are important, they interact with broader economic and institutional changes to shape long-run growth.

The slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that has endured and significantly influenced economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods, suggesting that the impact of institutions on long-term growth is period-specific. This culture of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it affects social capital and interpersonal relationships necessary for cooperation and collective action. This cultural legacy of mistrust can hinder economic growth by reducing the effectiveness of institutions that rely on social cooperation and trust, such as markets and legal systems. Austin notes that Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed over time, suggesting that while the culture of mistrust is a significant barrier, it is not an insurmountable one. The dynamic nature of cultural evolution means that policies aimed at fostering trust and social capital could potentially mitigate some of the long-term economic impacts of the slave trade.

Cultural beliefs and values significantly influence economic outcomes by shaping institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). For example, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' highlights how trust, norms, and networks can enhance societal efficiency through coordinated actions that foster institutions conducive to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is evidenced in the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times. These findings underscore the enduring influence of cultural legacies on institutional frameworks, which in turn drive economic growth.

This historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that cultural legacies can be overcome through institutional innovation, as seen in the evolution of English institutions during the financial revolution (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
