The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars studying economic development. At stake intellectually is the extent to which cultural factors shape institutional frameworks and how these frameworks influence sustained economic prosperity. The central disagreement revolves around whether cultural elements such as trust and social norms directly impact economic outcomes or if they operate through institutions, indirectly affecting long-term growth. Some scholars posit that culture itself is a critical determinant of institutional quality, emphasizing the enduring effects of historical experiences on contemporary economic performance. Others argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, with culture playing a secondary role in shaping these institutions. This debate underscores the complexity of understanding how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to influence long-term economic development.

Culture's impact on institutions is evident through its influence on trust and social norms. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that historical events like the slave trade have left lasting imprints on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy directly affects institutional quality by undermining cooperative behavior and reducing the effectiveness of governance structures. North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England shows how cultural shifts towards greater trust in public institutions facilitated the development of secure property rights, which are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These findings indicate that culture shapes institutions by influencing the social norms and attitudes that underpin institutional frameworks.

Institutions govern economic growth through their role in securing property rights. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, thereby enabling economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The enduring impact of these institutions highlights how cultural factors can influence long-term economic outcomes through their effects on institutional quality. North and Weingast further illustrate this point by demonstrating that the financial revolution in England played a critical role in fostering secure property rights, which were essential for sustained economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This suggests that culture shapes institutions through ongoing processes reinforcing institutional frameworks over time (Author Year p.N). The interplay between cultural factors and institutional quality is thus central to understanding long-run economic growth.

The relationship between culture, institutions, and economic development is further complicated by the dynamic nature of these interactions. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks over time. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that New World societies with greater inequality had institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic interaction between culture and institutions suggests that historical experiences shape contemporary economic outcomes through their effects on institutional quality. The enduring impact of these interactions highlights the importance of understanding how cultural factors influence long-term economic development.

They demonstrate how the evolution of these institutions facilitated long-term capital markets, which are critical for economic development. North's argument hinges on the role of transaction costs; he explains that when transaction costs are positive, institutions matter because they lower these costs and thus enable more efficient economic exchanges (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). In this context, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn increased the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework was pivotal for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9) . This mechanism highlights the importance of institutional changes in facilitating economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic growth. The mechanism of this influence involves new ideas fostering a shift in rhetoric which creates new circumstances conducive to economic growth. For example, McCloskey discusses how the "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries" such as the Netherlands developed a new rhetoric that facilitated economic enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr also points out the limitations of codified knowledge, emphasizing the importance of tacit knowledge and personal transmission in the spread of useful skills (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This qualification underscores the complexity of how ideas translate into economic growth. The interplay between new ideas and the practical dissemination of knowledge thus shapes long-run economic growth in ways that go beyond institutional frameworks.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the institutional legacies of colonialism can overshadow these intellectual contributions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This mechanism is rooted in the idea that high mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonizers and not long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. 2001 show that these early institutional structures had a direct impact on current income per capita, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions persists over centuries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these extractive institutions were designed to secure rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the enduring effects of institutional frameworks established during colonial periods on contemporary economic outcomes. This relationship is qualified by the recognition that equilibrium institutions may be shaped by politically powerful groups leading to extractive practices that do not encourage development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth thus becomes less straightforward when considering the lasting impacts of colonial institutional legacies.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia began well before 1800, a perspective shared by Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This view challenges the notion that cultural factors were the primary drivers of long-term economic growth. Instead, these historians emphasise the role of historical events such as the Black Death and urbanization patterns in shaping early modern economic divergence.

This perspective is supported by Pamuk, who argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. Pamuk demonstrates how the Black Death brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change, contributing to the rise of rural industries (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This historical event also ushered in an era of labour-saving technological innovations. These findings suggest that economic divergence was driven by structural changes and not cultural factors.

This perspective on early modern economic divergence does not fully account for the role of institutions. This omission leaves open the question of whether cultural influences might have played a more significant role than historical events alone in shaping long-term economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) illustrate how the slave trade's impact on cultural attitudes of mistrust can have lasting effects on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today. They complement this claim with evidence from Guiso et al. (2007b), who empirically linked differences in social capital within Italy to the independence of cities during the eleventh to fourteenth centuries (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical context is important because it shows that mistrust can have long-lasting effects on economic development, as it has done in Africa. This mistrust can hinder investment and cooperation, which are vital for growth. Nunn further elaborates on the adverse effect of slave trades on African political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization is consistent with historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This pattern suggests that cultural attitudes of mistrust can have a direct impact on institutional development, as weak governance structures limit economic growth. Bockstette et al. (2002) and Chanda and Putterman (2005) find that "state antiquity," measured using an index of the depth of experience with state-level institutions, is positively correlated with real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27). This implies that a country's history of state development can significantly influence its current economic performance. The long-term impact of the slave trade on African economic development underscores how cultural attitudes shaped by historical events can persist and affect economic outcomes over centuries.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) show how different historical paths have led to varying levels of inequality. These different paths are often influenced by the specific institutions that evolved in response to environmental factors and initial conditions, which in turn shaped economic development trajectories. For instance, the factor endowments of New World societies led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. He argues that economic historians need to consider the dynamic, partly endogenous responses of societies to their environments over time. These responses can significantly alter the institutional landscape, affecting long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between historical paths, institutional evolution, and economic outcomes underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms by which different societies have navigated their unique challenges over time.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal for understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions, as outlined by North (1989, p.1), are critical for economic development because they define the framework within which individuals interact and make decisions. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al., who highlight that historical European settlement patterns influenced current institutional structures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutions established during colonial periods, shaped by initial mortality rates among settlers, have enduring effects on contemporary economic performance. These historical paths determined the nature of institutions and their persistence over time influencing long-term growth trajectories.

The dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is further evident in how different societies navigated unique challenges. Sokoloff and Engerman illustrate that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional landscape, shaped by historical paths and cultural responses to environmental conditions, restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. Austin supports this view, noting the importance of understanding specific mechanisms by which societies adapted over time (Austin 2008, p.25). The African story suggests a more dynamic version of institutional evolution, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed long-term economic outcomes.

Culture also plays a significant role in shaping societal norms and trust, which are crucial for economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, persisting to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, illustrating how historical events can shape contemporary social norms and economic behavior. North and Weingast further emphasize that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The evolution of these institutions provided a large and positive externality for the development of both public and private debt markets, highlighting the interconnectedness of cultural norms, institutional frameworks, and economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The remaining disagreement centers on whether historical paths or contemporary factors are more influential in shaping these institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that the determinants of European settlement patterns, such as mortality rates, have a significant effect on current institutions and economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the importance of historical paths in shaping institutional frameworks. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies and contemporary institutional structures. This correlation shows that historical settlement patterns have a lasting impact on current institutions, underscoring the enduring influence of culture on long-run economic growth.
