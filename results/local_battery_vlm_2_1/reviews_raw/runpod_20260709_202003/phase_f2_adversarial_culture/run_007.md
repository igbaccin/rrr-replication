The role of culture in shaping institutions and thereby influencing long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has sparked debate among scholars. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural factors can be considered as primary drivers of institutional change, which then foster economic development over extended periods. The disagreement centers around whether cultural beliefs directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, or if other factors such as geographic conditions, political dynamics, or technological advancements play a more significant role in shaping these institutions. Some scholars argue that cultural values and norms can create an environment conducive to economic growth by fostering trust, reciprocity, and cooperative behavior. Others contend that the impact of culture on long-run growth is mediated through its influence on institutional frameworks, which in turn determine economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) both acknowledge the importance of historical determinants in shaping current economic outcomes, although their focus areas differ. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, highlighting how cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon, on the other hand, focus on the impact of the slave trade in Africa, arguing that it caused a culture of mistrust that persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. They highlight how historical events can shape cultural norms, which then influence economic behavior and outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) both discuss the significance of institutions in facilitating economic growth. Ogilvie highlights how the development of financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This financial revolution was critical for England's long-run success, demonstrating how institutions can foster economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. North also emphasizes the role of institutions in facilitating growth, noting that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments played a crucial role in England's economic development.

The relationship between culture and institutions is complex and multifaceted, as evidenced by the interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.15) both highlight how cultural factors can influence institutional development over time. For instance, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective suggests that cultural beliefs and values can shape economic institutions and outcomes in ways that are not immediately apparent. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This finding underscores the enduring impact of historical events on cultural norms, which in turn influence institutional development and economic outcomes.

Culture influences long-run growth by fostering trust and reciprocity within societies, which are essential for the development of efficient economic institutions. Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) focus on settler mortality rates in European colonies provides evidence that historical events can shape institutional frameworks, which then determine long-term economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011, p.4) study complements this by showing how the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and impacts current economic behavior. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) is that historical events shape both cultural norms and institutional frameworks, which together determine long-term economic performance.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies, along with the growth of useful knowledge, were crucial drivers of economic change. McCloskey highlights how cultural shifts, particularly changes in rhetoric and ideology, facilitated economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective is evident in his account of France, where he notes that even in the modern era, institutions like the École Polytechnique are steeped in antiquated ideologies that hinder economic progress (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr complements this by emphasizing that the diffusion of useful knowledge was a critical factor in economic growth. He posits that the spread of knowledge within Europe, facilitated through periodicals, translations, and international conferences, was more significant than national differences in institutions or culture (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This shared emphasis on ideas and ideologies suggests that cultural shifts, rather than institutional frameworks alone, were pivotal in fostering economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p. 14) further elaborates on the role of tacit knowledge, noting that much useful knowledge was embodied in skills and practices transmitted through personal interactions. This highlights a nuanced mechanism through which ideas and ideologies could influence economic outcomes: by shaping the cultural context in which knowledge is shared and applied. However, Mokyr's emphasis on tacit knowledge also qualifies this mechanism, suggesting that the impact of ideas and ideologies might be more subtle and context-dependent than McCloskey implies.

The interplay between ideas, ideologies, and useful knowledge underscores a complex relationship where cultural norms not only shape institutional frameworks but also directly influence economic behavior through the dissemination of knowledge. This perspective offers a compelling alternative to explanations that focus solely on institutions or culture as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.

The slave trade in Africa has led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural legacy is not merely an artifact of history but actively shapes contemporary economic behavior. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that this mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, directly influences current social capital and interpersonal trust within African societies. The mechanism through which this occurs involves the erosion of institutional frameworks that could otherwise foster economic development. This mistrust has led to weakened state centralization (Nunn 2008, p.27), a condition that Nunn posits as a critical factor in Africa's underdevelopment. Trust is essential for effective governance and economic growth, as it facilitates cooperation and reduces transaction costs. The historical instability caused by the slave trade has left a lasting imprint on African institutions (Nunn 2008, p.27). This institutional weakness, in turn, hinders economic development by impeding the establishment of credible commitments and stable property rights that are necessary for long-term investment and growth. While this cultural legacy of mistrust is a significant factor in Africa's economic trajectory, it is not the sole determinant. Other historical factors, such as colonial rule and more recent events, also play crucial roles in shaping Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.1).

North and Weingast's work on seventeenth-century England highlights that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets by reducing transaction costs. North describes how the creation of the Bank of England, along with new financial instruments, dramatically lowered these costs. North and Weingast argue that this institutional framework was fundamental to England's long-term success. They show how the financial revolution played a crucial role, suggesting that even if other forces would have led to success under different rulers, they would have done so less decisively (North 1989, p.9). The institutions supporting public debt markets also provided positive externalities for private debt markets. North and Weingast describe how shortly after its formation, the Bank of England began intermediating both public and private debts. This institutional evolution illustrates a mechanism by which secure property rights can be established through financial innovations. However, while these developments were instrumental in fostering economic growth, they also had significant implications for political stability. The institutions that supported long-term capital markets required credible commitments from the government to maintain investor confidence. North and Weingast show how the Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt was crucial for this stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This underscores the interconnected nature of economic growth and institutional stability, where secure property rights and reliable financial institutions are mutually reinforcing factors.

Broadberry and Gupta show that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Van Zanden supports this perspective by noting that economic development in Europe did not uniformly translate into improvements in living standards during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The mechanism driving this early divergence can be traced to institutional changes, such as those in agriculture and labour markets. In northwestern Europe, these changes led to higher agricultural productivity, which in turn stimulated industrial and service sectors without necessarily lowering food prices due to labour migration out of agriculture (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). However, van Zanden qualifies this by highlighting that the development of silver wages reflected broader economic trends across Europe, indicating that while certain regions experienced growth, others stagnated or even declined. This suggests that the relationship between economic development and living standards was complex and varied regionally within Europe itself (van Zanden 1999, p.6).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a pivotal role in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. established that mortality rates faced by Europeans significantly influenced the type of institutions implemented in their colonies, which in turn shaped long-run economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic was driven by the political and economic interests of European colonizers, who designed institutions to maximize their own rents rather than promote long-term growth for the local populations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin qualifies this mechanism by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex historical processes at play. He argues that Africans' responses to their environments and the technologies available to them also played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous version of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective underscores the importance of considering local agency and environmental factors alongside colonial institutions when examining long-run growth patterns.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight distributional conflicts as a key factor shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality. They argue that the persistence of certain institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies can be attributed to the ways in which these frameworks served the interests of elites, even when they were not the most efficient or growth-promoting. Ogilvie illustrates this through her analysis of serfdom, communities, and guilds in pre-industrial Europe, demonstrating that these institutions often emerged from power struggles rather than efficiency considerations. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing how initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites and restricted competition (Ogilvie 2007; (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)). For example, the unequal distribution of resources in Latin America created a political environment where elites could manipulate policies to maintain their dominance. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality in these societies over centuries, as institutional frameworks were designed to protect elite interests rather than promote widespread economic growth. This perspective underscores that institutions are not merely reflections of cultural norms but are actively shaped by power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Thus, understanding long-run growth requires examining how these conflicts influence institutional development and perpetuate inequality.

This analysis does not negate the role of culture in shaping institutions; rather it suggests that the interplay between culture and distributional conflicts is more complex than often acknowledged. The persistence of certain cultural norms can be reinforced by institutional frameworks that serve elite interests, creating a feedback loop where cultural beliefs and institutional structures mutually support each other. Ogilvie acknowledges that this framework may not fully explain all aspects of economic development. For instance, the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. This nuanced view highlights the need for a comprehensive approach that considers both cultural factors and distributional conflicts when examining long-run growth patterns.
