The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars who study economic development. The stakes are high intellectually because they relate to the broader question of why some societies thrive while others stagnate. Some argue that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, thereby influencing growth trajectories. Others posit that extractive institutions, shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates, are the primary determinants of long-run economic performance. Still others emphasize the evolution of institutions governing public choice as crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The live disagreement revolves around whether culture is a direct driver of institutional frameworks or if it operates through other mechanisms, or else if it is not even a necessary component at all.

The role of culture in shaping economic institutions has been extensively debated. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency by changing the equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that civic cultures characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). However, Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic and partly endogenous version of geographic approaches to understanding economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). This critique implies that cultural factors may not be as straightforwardly causal as some theories suggest. Despite these differences, the interplay between culture and institutions remains a critical area of study, highlighting the complex nature of long-run growth dynamics.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that the role of women in economic growth is pivotal to understanding long-run development. This perspective is particularly evident in pre-colonial Africa, where women's contributions were integral to community reproduction and wealth transmission. For instance, in matrilineal Akan societies, women's bloodlines defined the inheritance of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This dynamic highlights how cultural norms and gender roles can shape economic structures and long-run growth. Similarly, Goldin's analysis of U.S. labor market changes shows that shifts in social norms and educational opportunities significantly altered women's employment patterns and earnings relative to men (Goldin 2006, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions, which dispensed with barriers to married women's employment. However, while these transformations had profound impacts on economic development, Goldin notes that they also led to significant social adjustments in family dynamics and life satisfaction expectations (Goldin 2006, p.8). The interconnectedness of cultural norms, gender roles, and economic structures underscores the complex interplay between culture and institutions in driving long-run growth.

This historical context is further illuminated by the slave trade's impact on African development, which demonstrates how cultural attitudes like mistrust can affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today. This mistrust is a significant factor in reducing interpersonal trust, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The mechanism by which this historical trauma manifests as enduring mistrust involves the long-term political instability and weakened state structures that emerged from the slave trades. This connection is consistent with recent empirical research showing that a country's history of state development, marked by deep experience with state-level institutions, positively correlates with current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). The downstream implication of this historical mistrust is evident in contemporary corruption levels, where slavery can be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. Nunn further notes that Africa's history explains part of its current underdevelopment, highlighting the enduring effects of the slave trade on economic and social outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.1).

Moving to a different historical context, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1)(North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs, thus enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional development reflected a broader political model where certain rights were traded for revenues during fiscal crises, ultimately fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutions that supported the growth of public debt also provided significant externalities to private debt markets, further bolstering financial stability and economic expansion (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The implications of these institutional changes are profound. By securing property rights and reducing transaction costs, England's financial revolution created an environment conducive to sustained economic growth. This institutional framework not only facilitated the development of a stable market for public debt but also enhanced private credit markets, laying the groundwork for modern economic systems. The evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England underscores the critical role that secure property rights play in long-run economic success, highlighting how institutional changes can drive economic development over centuries.

This focus on inequality is shared by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) who, along with Ogilvie (2007, p.12), highlight how institutions shape development paths and the distribution of wealth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This focus is evident in their examination of how institutional frameworks in New World societies, shaped by extreme inequality, advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, highlighting that institutions can perpetuate economic disparities over time. For instance, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the differentials in paths of development are central to understanding the contrasting records in growth across societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). In this view, institutions like secure property rights and low corruption play a crucial role in fostering economic growth, but these institutions themselves are shaped by underlying social dynamics. The institutional frameworks that emerge from societal conflicts and power dynamics are deeply influenced by the underlying social structures. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, as noted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), further complicates this picture (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). If such interdependencies are widespread, then we cannot study one institution in isolation; instead, we must look at entire institutional frameworks to understand their impact on economic growth. This underscores the importance of considering the broader social and cultural contexts in which institutions operate, as these contexts can significantly influence the effectiveness and sustainability of institutional frameworks over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that these institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal to long-run growth. They find these institutions have direct effects on economic performance. The mortality rates faced by European settlers and the resulting extractive nature of early colonial settlements significantly influenced current income per capita, which indicates a strong link between initial colonial institutions and modern economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This relationship is further supported by the high correlation observed between settler mortality and both historical and contemporary institutional measures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these extractive institutions over time underscores their enduring impact on economic development, providing a framework for understanding how early institutional frameworks can shape long-term growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) acknowledge that equilibrium institutions benefit powerful groups, such as European colonists. This nuance highlights the complex interplay between colonial legacies and economic development, suggesting that the institutional framework established during colonial rule can have lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory. The role of culture in shaping these institutional frameworks remains an important area of inquiry, as it may influence how institutions are implemented and sustained over time.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This perspective emphasizes Africans' own responses to their environments, utilizing various technologies when available. These responses ultimately changed 22the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). Bryant elaborates that the enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms, all mediated inheritances from the past, are essential for phased conversion to industrial modes of production or imperial forms of hegemony. This causal connectedness governs every transition from past to present, highlighting the importance of long-term historical processes in shaping economic development (Bryant 2006, p.4). Yet Bryant notes that any attempt to overturn our reigning comprehension of the key determinants in Europe's trajectory would appear to be an audacious, unpromising venture.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie highlights how cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by North's emphasis on the importance of secure property rights, which are underpinned by cultural norms and institutional frameworks that facilitate economic transactions and growth (North 1989, p.9). The interdependency between culture and institutions is further illustrated by the role of social capital in fostering economic development. Putnam’s theory of social capital underscores how trust, norms, and networks, all culturally derived elements, facilitate coordinated actions that enhance societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural underpinning of institutions is crucial for understanding the long-term historical processes that shape economic development.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England exemplifies how cultural factors influence economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, was a critical factor in securing property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional development was a cultural phenomenon as well as an economic one, reflecting the broader societal values and norms that supported secure property rights. The financial revolution provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, highlighting how cultural and institutional changes can have far-reaching economic impacts (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

Cultural factors are not limited to European contexts; they also play a significant role in African economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This contribution was deeply rooted in cultural norms and social structures that valued women's roles in community reproduction and wealth transmission (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is evident in how African societies adapted to changing environments and technologies, ultimately shaping their economic trajectories.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that culture fundamentally shapes the institutions that drive long-run growth. This is shown by North's work on secure property rights (North 1989, p.9) and Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.25). The remaining disagreement lies in how to measure the relative importance of cultural versus institutional factors. Acemoglu et al.'s study of European mortality rates and their impact on current income per capita provides a quantitative approach to understanding this relationship (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17), while Austin's emphasis on dynamic, partly endogenous processes highlights the need for more nuanced historical analyses. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of institutional frameworks across different regions and time periods, as shown by Ogilvie’s examination of pre-industrial European institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).
