The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development scholarship. At stake is understanding whether cultural factors can shape institutional frameworks to foster sustained economic progress. The debate centers around two main positions: one that emphasizes culture's direct influence on institutions and another that sees institutions as primarily shaped by exogenous factors, with culture playing an indirect or negligible role. One view of the relationship between culture and institutions ascribes a direct causal role to cultural values and beliefs in shaping institutional frameworks. For example, some scholars argue that cultural beliefs about reciprocity and trust can foster institutions that promote economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is often linked with theories of social capital, which suggest that societies with high levels of trust and norms of reciprocity are more likely to develop efficient institutions that support long-run growth. On the other hand, some scholars propose that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates or geographic conditions, which in turn influence economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective challenges the direct causal role of culture, arguing instead that institutional frameworks are determined by factors outside of cultural beliefs and values. The live disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors can directly shape institutions to promote long-run growth or if institutions are primarily influenced by exogenous determinants with culture playing a secondary role.

The debate over the influence of culture on institutions is further complicated by the recognition that institutions operate within complex frameworks, where individual components are interdependent (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying one institution in isolation may not fully capture its impact on economic growth. For example, North and Weingast highlight how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The financial revolution in England, characterized by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This development reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework highlights the importance of considering how cultural factors might influence multiple aspects of institutional development, focusing on isolated components.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutions introduced by colonialism significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies. This influence is mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions in regions where European settlers faced high mortality rates, which in turn shaped long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) explain that these institutions were not designed to maximize long-term growth but rather to extract resources for the benefit of European colonists. This dynamic highlights a specific mechanism by which colonial institutions, initially shaped by high settler mortality rates, led to the persistence of extractive economic frameworks in former colonies. The resulting institutional legacies are a critical determinant of current income disparities among former European colonies, illustrating how exogenous factors during colonization can have enduring effects on economic performance.

McCloskey and Mokyr share that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic growth from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective is grounded in the idea that the dissemination of useful knowledge was a primary driver and not merely a byproduct of institutional changes. Mokyr's work underscores that the growth of useful knowledge, being readily diffused across Europe through various channels such as periodicals and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56), was not confined within national boundaries. The importance of tacit knowledge, which is often embodied in skills and personal transmission, further highlights how ideas spread beyond codified forms. This diffusion facilitated the Great Enrichment, a period marked by significant economic growth, particularly in countries like the Netherlands, Britain, and even France. McCloskey elaborates on this process, noting that the interplay between new rhetoric and changing circumstances was crucial. The cultural shifts, exemplified by the "Franco-French War, " illustrate how ideologies influenced economic outcomes (McCloskey 2015, p.9). However, Mokyr's emphasis on tacit knowledge introduces a nuanced perspective: while ideas were indeed central, their transmission often relied on personal interactions and training, suggesting that the diffusion of useful knowledge was not solely an intellectual exercise but also a social one. This underscores how cultural dynamics, though influential, are just part of a broader framework where both explicit and tacit knowledge play critical roles in economic development. The interplay between ideas and their practical application through tacit knowledge remains a central theme for understanding long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that the fundamental causes of long-run growth are rooted in distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These scholars point to the role that economic institutions play in shaping these conflicts, with Ogilvie emphasizing how pre-industrial Europe's core institutions, serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by power dynamics between different social groups, not just efficiency concerns. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who show that New World societies' institutional frameworks were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). One specific mechanism by which these distributional conflicts influence growth involves the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework. Ogilvie highlights this interdependency, noting how different institutions, such as labor markets, credit markets, and welfare systems, are interconnected and collectively contribute to economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness implies that changes in one institution can have cascading effects on others, shaping the overall institutional framework and its impact on growth. This perspective is qualified by the recognition that cultural beliefs also play a role in shaping institutions. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional frameworks, and cultural dynamics in determining long-run growth. The downstream implication of this perspective is that understanding the causes of long-run growth requires a nuanced analysis of how these factors interact within specific historical contexts.

Austin highlights that economists often view the behavior of older industrial economies through the lens of institutional evolution and policy experience stretching back to Europe's original industrialization (Austin 2008, p.2). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, a critique shared by Broadberry and Gupta who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). They assert that parts of Asia resembled stagnating regions of Europe more than the developing northwestern parts. This points to distinct trajectories rooted in endogenous factors rather than exogenous environmental obstacles, suggesting that African responses to their environments shaped economic development through various technologies (Austin 2008, p.25). The dynamic nature of these processes implies that cultural factors influencing institutions could have played a significant role in determining long-run growth paths.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They assert that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was pivotal in driving long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Moreover, North and Weingast's work highlights how securing property rights through institutions like the Bank of England provided a positive externality for both public and private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The implication is that even if other factors might have eventually led to success in seventeenth-century England, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without these institutional developments. This underscores the fundamental role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories.

The role of cultural factors in influencing these institutional developments cannot be overlooked. The specific mechanisms by which culture shapes institutions are complex and multifaceted. This interplay between culture and institutions suggests that cultural factors may have played a significant role in determining the long-run growth paths of different regions. Understanding how cultural influences interact with institutional frameworks is crucial for a comprehensive analysis of economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon show how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is a mechanism through which historical events like the slave trade shaped long-term economic outcomes. This mistrust, in turn, influences modern economic development by undermining social capital and cooperation. Nunn (2008, p.27) extends the analysis of this mechanism to political instability. He shows that slave exports led to weakened state centralization, a condition that persists today (Nunn 2008, p.27). The implication is that historical traumas like the slave trade can have long-lasting effects on institutional development by creating lasting cultural attitudes that undermine economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that slavery was one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident through various historical and institutional lenses. Institutions matter because they shape economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). The English financial revolution, for instance, illustrates how institutional changes can lead to a decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This perspective is further supported by the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, which were crucial for securing property rights and promoting economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The impact of culture on institutional frameworks is profound. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of these cultural attitudes is evident in the long-lasting effects of historical traumas like the slave trade, which created lasting cultural attitudes that undermine economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He contends that economists see modern economic behaviour in the context of patterns going back to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding with North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (Austin 2008, p.25) shows that institutions are a necessary component for economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions shape long-run growth by influencing transaction costs, securing property rights, and fostering economic development. Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation represents the remaining disagreement (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This dispute is settled by the specific historical evidence of the English financial revolution, which shows how institutional changes can lead to a decline in transaction costs and foster economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.9).
