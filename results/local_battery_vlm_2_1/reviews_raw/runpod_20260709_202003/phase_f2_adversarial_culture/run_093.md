The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is whether cultural beliefs and values significantly shape economic trajectories through their influence on institutional frameworks. The live disagreement centers around two primary positions: those who argue that culture directly influences economic growth by fostering efficient institutions, and those who contend that other factors, such as geographic or demographic conditions, are more determinative. Some scholars emphasize the importance of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions, suggesting that these beliefs can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on their alignment with institutional efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Some scholars concentrate on external factors such as mortality rates and colonial settlement patterns; these factors are argued to have a more direct impact on institutional development and economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The debate extends to the specific mechanisms through which culture operates. This perspective is complemented by studies that examine the interdependencies between various institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that cultural factors influence not just individual institutions but entire systems that support economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Culture's role in shaping institutions extends beyond economic efficiency to include distributional conflicts and political accountability.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illuminated by historical analyses. For instance, the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was crucial for fostering economic growth and political freedom, highlighting how cultural and institutional factors can work synergistically to drive long-term economic development. The role of culture in this process is evident in how institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for securing property rights and promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical context underscores the importance of understanding how cultural norms and values interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic trajectories.

Moreover, the impact of cultural factors on economic growth can be observed through the lens of gender dynamics. For example, in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, women played a significant role in economic development, despite being marginalized by colonial constructs (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This highlights how cultural beliefs about gender roles can influence economic institutions and outcomes, further emphasizing the multifaceted nature of culture's impact on long-run growth. The dynamic interplay between cultural norms, institutional frameworks, and economic performance is a complex phenomenon that requires nuanced analysis to fully understand its implications for long-term economic development.

The relationship between culture and institutions in driving economic growth is not straightforward. For example, the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests that simplistic causal explanations may overlook the dynamic and endogenous nature of cultural and institutional interactions (Austin 2008, p.25).

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the institutions governing public choice played a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. By establishing a stable market for public debt, these institutions generated positive externalities that facilitated the growth of private debt markets. This institutional framework supported economic development and underpinned political freedom by providing a secure environment for long-term investments and economic activities (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The extent to which these institutions can be credited with economic growth is qualified by considerations of other exogenous factors. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, suggesting that while secure property rights are essential for growth, they operate within a wider set of conditions that include cultural and social dynamics.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were shaped by the mortality rates faced by early European settlers. High mortality led to extractive institutions that maximized rents for colonists; this did not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, as shown by the strong correlation between settler mortality and contemporary economic performance (Austin 2008, p.25).

He posits a dynamic, partly endogenous version of geography where Africans' responses to their environments changed over time. This perspective challenges the notion that environmental obstacles are unchanging (Austin 2008, p.25). Acemoglu et al (2001) support this view by demonstrating that institutions designed to maximize colonial rents did not encourage economic development, highlighting the enduring impact of these institutional legacies on current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The dynamic interplay between colonial institutions and local responses underscores the complex nature of long-run growth, suggesting that cultural adaptations played a role in shaping economic outcomes.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These scholars propose that institutional frameworks evolved to serve elite interests, shaping policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality. The European family system exemplifies this dynamic: interdependent with labour markets for youths and women and credit markets to amass savings, it contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) analysis of New World societies, which highlights how initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This institutional framework underscores how distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes, as these institutions were designed to maximize rents for those in power rather than promote broad-based growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate this through their study on the historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior. Their findings highlight that the slave trade had lasting effects on social capital within African societies, affecting interpersonal trust to a significant extent. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates on this by showing that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states. These historical events have shaped current economic performance in Africa, with state centralization being a critical determinant of real per capita GDP. The implications of these findings are profound, as they suggest that the legacy of the slave trade continues to influence contemporary African economies through its impact on institutions and social norms. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that slavery is one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, which further compounds the challenges faced by African societies in achieving sustained economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) (Pamuk 2007, p.24), together with van Zanden (1999, p.2), argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This divergence can be traced through institutional changes, wage gaps, and urban development. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the prosperous parts of Asia during this period resembled stagnating regions in Europe, not its developing northwest. The key factor driving this divergence was institutional: high levels of investment in agriculture led to increased productivity in southern India; north-western Europe saw similar gains from institutional changes that facilitated agricultural investment (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Van Zanden (1999, p.2) supports the importance of these structural changes by highlighting how silver wages reflected broader economic development across Europe. The role of institutions is evident in the differing outcomes between regions; higher agricultural labour productivity did not universally translate into abundant grain and low food prices due to varying levels of institutional support (van Zanden 1999, p.6). Pamuk (2007, p.24) further qualifies this by showing that the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450. These changes included declining interest rates, increased mobility, and labour-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.5), all of which contributed to long-term economic development. Institutional frameworks and structural changes were pivotal in shaping the early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia.

Institutional reforms and historical economic patterns have significantly influenced African economies, shaping their long-run growth trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This is evidence that, while structural changes like declining interest rates and increased mobility were pivotal in shaping economic development elsewhere, as seen in Pamuk's (2007, p.24) account of Europe, these factors did not universally apply to Africa at the same time. The influence of local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions on export production varied both between and within countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These variations suggest that the effectiveness of institutional reforms in promoting growth is contingent upon their congruence with indigenous institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores this point by arguing that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective if they align with precolonial ones, enhancing government legitimacy and fostering development-inducing institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that the success of institutional reforms in Africa depends on their ability to integrate with existing cultural frameworks.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) (Goldin 2006, p.8), alongside Goldin (2006, p.3), show that women's economic contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods were pivotal in shaping African societies. The gendered division of labor, where women played crucial roles in reproduction and community sustainability, was instrumental to the economic development of these communities. In matrilineal Akan society, for instance, women's bloodlines transmitted office and wealth, underscoring their indispensable role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The quiet revolution in U.S. employment and education further illustrates how transformations in women’s roles can drive economic growth. This transformation was a result of exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions. These shifts highlight that when women's roles are redefined, it catalyzes broader social and economic changes (Goldin 2006, p.8). Goldin also notes that these transformations were contingent on various factors including household production technology; institutional changes facilitated part-time work and removed barriers to married women’s employment (Goldin 2006, p.3). The implication is clear: the integration of women into economic activities, when supported by enabling institutions, can significantly enhance long-run growth. This dynamic suggests that cultural norms and institutional frameworks play a critical role in determining the extent to which women can contribute to economic development, highlighting a mechanism through which culture influences long-run growth.

Culture's role in shaping institutions is evident through its influence on economic practices and social norms. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can significantly alter the structure of economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to their decline, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence institutional efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The integration of women into economic activities, supported by enabling institutions, can significantly enhance long-run growth, highlighting how cultural norms and institutional frameworks determine the extent to which different groups contribute to economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For example, African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, demonstrating how cultural norms and institutions can shape economic outcomes over time.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by the role of social capital in economic development. Ogilvie highlights that societies characterized by high social capital, such as those with 'altruistic' or 'civic' cultures, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Determinants of whether Europeans could settle in the colonies have an important effect on institutions today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, showing how institutions provided a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These findings underscore the importance of cultural norms in shaping institutional frameworks that support long-term economic development.

The underlying point of agreement is that culture plays a fundamental role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. The empirical comparison of European colonies with high versus low mortality rates shows that the institutions established due to differing mortality rates directly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), settling this debate.
