The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography and institutional theory. At stake is whether cultural values shape institutions that drive economic development over centuries, or if other factors such as geography, technology, or political structures play more significant roles. One position contends that cultural beliefs about trust, reciprocity, and social networks directly influence the efficiency of collective action and thus foster growth-enhancing institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective posits a direct causal link between cultural norms and economic outcomes through institutional channels. Other scholars argue that geography and mortality rates determine institutional quality by shaping colonial settlement patterns and subsequent economic development paths (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These scholars maintain that the impact of culture is secondary to these exogenous factors in determining long-run growth. A third position suggests that institutions matter for growth, but their adoption depends on complex historical processes that include cultural influences among other factors  (North 1989, p.1).

The role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions has been explored through various lenses, with some scholars arguing that these beliefs can directly influence the structure and efficiency of institutions. This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can alter the collective action game, thereby influencing economic outcomes. North argues that the neoclassical model's assumption of zero transaction costs overlooks the importance of institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). He contends that the creation of secure property rights and financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth. This perspective underscores the necessity of institutional frameworks for sustained economic development.

The interplay between institutions and cultural beliefs has been further elucidated by scholars who emphasize the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie highlights that the European family system was interconnected with labor, credit, and insurance markets, contributing to early industrialization. Laslett's analysis supports this view by noting how the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was linked with various markets and family structures, potentially enhancing economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These studies suggest that institutions are part of a complex web where cultural beliefs and norms influence their formation and efficiency (Author Year p.N). Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective acknowledges the dynamic nature of institutional evolution, where cultural factors interact with other variables such as technology and policy to shape long-term economic growth. Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis aligns with this view, emphasizing that Africans' responses to their environments, influenced by available technologies and cultural beliefs, ultimately changed economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic approach suggests that culture operates within a broader framework where multiple factors interact to determine institutional quality and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth and industrialization. They posit that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates; these institutions persist and significantly affect current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) analysis supports the view that institutions played a pivotal role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among former colonies. In this framework, institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups to maximize their rents; this means equilibrium institutions may be extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions reflect the interests of those in power at the time (Author Year p.N). Austin shares Acemoglu et al.'s rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, emphasizing that African responses to their environments ultimately changed economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This institutional framework enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The financial revolution in England exemplifies how institutional changes can foster economic growth by providing a stable market for public debt, which in turn supports private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast argue that the institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England.

Ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Mokyr 2005, p.56; McCloskey 2015, p.10). McCloskey argues that these ideas fostered a new rhetoric that, in turn, created new circumstances conducive to growth. This process was about institutional changes driven by elite interests, as well as a cultural shift that permeated society and enabled the Great Enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.10; de Vries 1994, p.18). Mokyr's analysis of the role of useful knowledge further illuminates this dynamic; it shows that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was not confined to national boundaries. This transnational flow of ideas underscores how cultural shifts drove economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). The Industrious Revolution in early modern Europe exemplifies this process, as it involved a profound cultural change in labor input and consumption patterns that preceded industrialization. This shift was a deeply rooted cultural transformation that redefined societal norms around work and consumption (de Vries 1994, p.18; de Vries 1994, p.5). This perspective challenges the notion that economic growth is primarily driven by institutions or interests, suggesting instead that culture plays a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both highlight that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These authors argue that the persistence of inequality is central to understanding institutional development. The specific factor endowments of New World societies, such as abundant land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This inequality was reinforced through institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and maintaining power imbalances (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie extends this perspective by examining how distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. She argues that serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were efficient responses to economic conditions, influenced by struggles over resource distribution. This framework challenges the notion that culture alone drives long-run growth, suggesting instead that institutional outcomes are profoundly shaped by underlying conflicts over wealth and power (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie also notes that these institutions operated within interdependent frameworks, where changes in one area could ripple through the entire system (Author Year p.N).

Goldin (2006, p.8); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) both identify the role of women as pivotal in economic development. They establish that women's contributions are often overlooked but have significant long-term impacts on societal growth and development. This is evident through the changes in expectations regarding future work, social norms concerning women’s family and career dynamics, and factors accounting for women’s life satisfaction (Goldin 2006, p.8). These shifts were not merely evolutionary; instead, they were driven by various exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. For example, in pre-colonial Africa, women played indispensable roles in community reproduction, with their bloodlines defining the transmission of both office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The dynamic interplay between gender relations and economic systems highlights how cultural norms and institutional frameworks can shape long-term economic outcomes. These contributions are often overshadowed by broader narratives that focus on other factors (Author Year p.N). The endurance of these colonial gender dynamics suggests that pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs may also persist, influencing economic dependency models today. This persistence underscores the importance of considering women's roles in understanding long-run growth, as their contributions are deeply embedded within cultural and institutional frameworks.

Pamuk (2007, p.24) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway by 1800, driven by institutional changes in agriculture. Broadberry and Gupta point out that the development of high agricultural labor productivity in northwestern Europe led to investment in industry and services, while agricultural labor moved away from grain production. This shift did not result in lower food prices due to labor mobility into other sectors (Pamuk 2007, p.24). In this context, Pamuk's analysis highlights how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change within guilds (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.6), which contributed to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. These developments underscore the importance of historical events in shaping economic trajectories, as the Black Death's impact on labor markets and technological innovations further catalyzed the divergence already underway.

The shared finding (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.6; Pamuk 2007, p.5) that institutional changes and historical events significantly influenced economic development suggests that cultural factors may have played a role in these processes. Van Zanden notes that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe from 1500 to 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This observation implies that while institutional changes and historical events were pivotal, cultural factors might have influenced how these changes manifested across different regions.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) share with Hopkins (2009, p.8) that institutional variables are crucial in explaining African economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). They argue that institutions shape the economic performance of African economies. Broadberry and Gardner's historical analysis shows that many Anglophone African countries had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, contradicting previous views about their economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). Broadberry and Gardner also note that export production varied significantly across countries due to factors like local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation highlights the importance of institutional congruence with indigenous systems in promoting growth. Hopkins elaborates on this by citing Englebert's work, which suggests that contemporary African institutions are more effective when they align with precolonial ones (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This alignment enhances government legitimacy and fosters development-inducing institutions. This congruence varies across Africa, leading to varied economic outcomes (Author Year p.N). A downstream implication of this institutional incongruence is the rise of patrimonial politics in some regions, which can lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Bryant and others suggest that Eurocentric views on modernization are empirically grounded and analytically coherent (Bryant 2006, p.35; Bryant 2006, p.4). The revisionist position, which dismisses these views, is problematic because it underestimates the cumulative nature of historical developments. Bryant argues that transitions to modernity are governed by a causal connectedness that relies on accumulated resources and opportunities from prior developments, including technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms (Bryant 2006, p.35). This perspective is supported by the work of other scholars who emphasize the multifaceted contributions of European culture and society to its modernization process. For instance, Braudel, McNeill, Barrington Moore, Eric Wolf, Gellner, Worsley, Wallerstein, Anderson, Jones, Finer, Hall, and Mann are noted for their comprehensive analyses of European modernity (Bryant 2006, p.4). These scholars highlight various facets such as civic urbanization, legal developments, productive innovations, military reforms, bureaucratic polities, scientific advancements, print culture, class relations, nationalism, demographic transitions, and secularizing dynamics. The interplay of these factors underscores the complexity and depth of European modernization, making any attempt to dismiss Eurocentric views both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. The revisionist dismissal of Eurocentrism risks overlooking the interconnectedness of historical processes that have shaped contemporary societies.

The slave trade's impact on African development underscores how historical events shape cultural attitudes such as mistrust, which then influence long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the enduring legacy of the slave trade has fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust is a persistent factor that continues to affect contemporary economic performance by reducing trust levels within societies (Author Year p.N). The mechanism through which this occurs involves the erosion of social capital, which is crucial for economic development. Nunn extends this argument by highlighting how the slave trades led to political instability and weakened state structures in Africa. This long-term political instability has been a significant barrier to economic growth, as it undermines the institutional frameworks necessary for sustainable development. By examining the historical context of Africa's underdevelopment, Nunn (2008, p.27) provides evidence that slavery may have been one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. This perspective underscores how enduring cultural attitudes and institutional weaknesses, both rooted in historical events like the slave trade, continue to influence contemporary economic development.

Culture has a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Institutions, as defined by North (1989, p.1), are the rules of the game in a society, and they matter because they structure incentives to guide human interaction. This is evident in how cultural beliefs and values influence the structure and equilibrium of collective action games, ultimately affecting economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights that societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks tend to have institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework further underscores the role of culture. Laslett's analysis of the European family system and Solar's examination of pre-industrial England's community-based welfare system illustrate how interconnected institutional frameworks can drive economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that favourable contracting institutions are partially interdependent with property rights institutions, emphasizing the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Cultural attitudes and historical events also play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic development. The legacy of slavery and colonialism has left enduring marks on contemporary economic performance by influencing cultural attitudes and institutional weaknesses (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). For example, the trans-Atlantic slave trade and colonial rule created gender-based constructs that had significant impacts on production systems and gender roles, with long-term consequences for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).

Shared finding:  the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is evident in how the Bank of England's financial revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The creation of new financial instruments and institutions played a critical role in England’s long-run success, highlighting the necessary role of institutions in economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns how to measure the impact of institutions on current income per capita. Acemoglu et al.'s empirical analysis shows that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This is evident in how African women's contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods are often marginalized (Austin 2008, p.25; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the comparative analysis of mortality rates faced by soldiers, bishops, and sailors in the colonies versus European settlements. This comparison shows a high correlation between these factors and current institutions, providing empirical support for the impact of institutions on economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).
