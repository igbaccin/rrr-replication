The role of culture in shaping institutions is a contentious issue in understanding long-run economic growth. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are primary drivers that influence institutional frameworks directly, or if they operate through other mechanisms such as economic efficiency and distributional conflicts. One position argues that cultural beliefs and values significantly impact the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting growth. This perspective is often supported by studies emphasizing the importance of social capital and civic cultures in fostering economic development. The opposing view contends that institutional frameworks are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates or financial innovations, which then influence long-run growth independently of cultural influences.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further complicated by the interplay between cultural beliefs and the efficiency of economic systems. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency, highlighting how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence institutional structures and thereby affect economic outcomes. Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital underscores how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These findings indicate that cultural factors play a pivotal role in shaping institutions by influencing the collective action games that underpin economic systems.

The relationship between culture and institutions is complex. Ogilvie also notes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may be misleading (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This view aligns with Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings suggest that while cultural factors may influence institutional frameworks, they are not the sole determinants. Instead, a complex interplay between cultural beliefs, economic efficiency, and exogenous factors shapes institutions over time.

North's work emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that with positive transaction costs, institutions matter (North 1989, p.1). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who highlight how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These studies underscore the role of financial innovations and secure property rights in driving long-run economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings suggest that while cultural factors may influence institutional frameworks, they operate within a broader context shaped by economic efficiency and financial innovations.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). McCloskey shows that the Great Enrichment was driven by a new rhetoric, which made new circumstances, which then again made new rhetoric. This rhetorical shift is not merely a matter of calculation based on accumulated information and interest (McCloskey 2015, p.9); it involves a deeper cultural transformation. Ogilvie further illustrates this point with her analysis of pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) observes that distributional conflicts explain the persistence of serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds better than efficiency does. This suggests that cultural factors, such as ideologies and beliefs, played a significant role in shaping these institutions. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework further supports this idea. For instance, the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness highlights how cultural beliefs and values can influence not just single institutions but entire frameworks, thereby affecting economic growth over the long run.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is further substantiated by Ogilvie's examination of the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England. This system was interdependent with various markets and family systems, potentially fostering economic growth before industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The implications of these findings suggest that cultural factors can shape institutional frameworks in ways that are not solely driven by efficiency or financial innovations.

This institutional evolution provided a large positive externality for both public and private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24), thereby laying the groundwork for sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.1) further elaborates that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as primary determinants. This perspective underscores how secure property rights, enabled by robust institutions, can drive long-term economic growth. It is important to note that while these institutions were crucial, cultural factors also shaped the institutional landscape. For instance, the interdependence of community-based welfare systems with markets and family structures in pre-industrial England suggests that cultural elements influenced economic growth even before industrialization [Ogilvie 2007]. This interplay between culture and institutions highlights that while institutions are fundamental, they operate within a broader cultural context that can either facilitate or hinder their effectiveness.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) show that extractive institutions established in colonies have lasting economic impacts. The specific mechanism was how mortality rates shaped European settlement patterns, which in turn determined the type of institutions implemented. High mortality rates deterred European settlement, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonial powers rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern highlights that institutional frameworks established during colonial periods have profound and enduring effects on economic development. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between initial conditions, institutional design, and economic performance in colonial contexts.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) highlight that the growth of useful knowledge drives economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This knowledge encompasses both codified information and tacit skills, which together form the basis for technological advancements. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that productivity levels can be erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, suggesting that social competence for exploiting advanced methods is a critical factor in economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of tacit knowledge, which is often transmitted through personal interactions and training rather than written records. This form of knowledge can be particularly influential in shaping long-term economic performance, as it embodies skills and expertise that are not easily codified (Mokyr 2005, p.14). However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) also points out that the diffusion of useful knowledge within Europe was facilitated by international conferences, translations, and personal communications, indicating that cultural factors played a significant role in the spread of technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This highlights how culture can be integral to the dissemination of knowledge and therefore to economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) assert that African women have been crucial to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This perspective is shared by Goldin's analysis of the U.S., which reveals a significant transformation in women’s employment, education, and family dynamics (Goldin 2006, p.8). One key mechanism through which these contributions were facilitated was the role of women in reproductive labor and community building. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack describe how women's roles in matrilineal societies ensured the continuation of communities and wealth transmission (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This underscores the importance of cultural norms and social structures that recognize and integrate women’s contributions into economic systems. Goldin further elaborates on this by highlighting how changes in household production technology and institutional barriers influenced female labor supply, thereby altering economic dynamics in the U.S. (Goldin 2006, p.3). These transformations involved significant shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's roles. The downstream implication of these cultural recognitions is that economic growth can be sustained by valuing diverse contributions to production systems. Cultural factors thus play a critical role in shaping institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, highlighting the interconnectedness between social norms and long-run growth.

This historical lens is further illuminated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) demonstrate how historical events can shape cultural attitudes that affect long-term economic outcomes by examining the impact of the slave trade on African economic development. The slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and has significantly influenced economic performance. This political fragmentation can inhibit economic growth by undermining state capacity and creating an environment where corruption thrives. Nunn's analysis shows how the negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization highlights this dynamic, suggesting a direct link between historical mistrust and contemporary underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.27). The downstream implication of this cultural legacy is that trust-building initiatives could be crucial for economic development in regions affected by such historical traumas. However, Nunn also notes how the persistence of corruption in these areas complicates efforts to rebuild trust and foster economic growth, thus qualifying the potential impact of such initiatives (Nunn 2008, p.27). The challenge remains to identify effective strategies that can overcome the entrenched cultural attitudes stemming from historical events like the slave trade.

This is evident in Putnam’s theory of social capital, which highlights how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie discusses how the European family system was interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North and Weingast also emphasize that secure property rights are essential for fostering economic growth, as evidenced by the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England which led to a stable market for public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This highlights how institutions governing public choice can create positive externalities for private markets.

Cultural factors, particularly those stemming from historical traumas like the slave trade, have long-lasting effects on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, illustrating how historical cultural legacies can hinder contemporary economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate gains from trade and improve productivity through specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.1). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persist today and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

While there is broad agreement on the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutions, disagreement persists over the specific mechanisms through which these influences operate. North and Weingast highlight that secure property rights are essential for fostering economic growth, as evidenced by the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). In contrast, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, showing how cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

To resolve this disagreement, empirical comparisons between regions with different historical traumas but similar contemporary conditions would be useful. In particular, the case of Africa shows that the culture of mistrust stemming from the slave trade persists today and significantly affects trust levels in the region (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This specific historical evidence settles the disagreement by demonstrating how cultural factors can shape institutional frameworks over time, highlighting the importance of understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions for long-run economic growth.
