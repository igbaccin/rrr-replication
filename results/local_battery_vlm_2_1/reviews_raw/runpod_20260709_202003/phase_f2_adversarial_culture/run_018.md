The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins sustained economic prosperity: are cultural beliefs and values the primary drivers that shape institutions, or do other forces play a more significant role? One position argues that cultural factors significantly influence institutional frameworks through mechanisms like social capital and collective action norms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another view posits that external factors, such as mortality rates in colonial settings, determine the type of institutions established, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are primary drivers or secondary influences in the institutional frameworks that support long-run growth. The interplay between these views underscores the complexity of institutional development and its relationship with cultural and environmental determinants. This raises a critical question: how do cultural beliefs interact with other factors to shape institutions that drive long-run growth?

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutional framework of public debt markets, which emerged during this period, played a pivotal role. This development was a necessary condition for economic growth (Author Year, p.N). North and Weingast emphasize that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This reduction in costs enhanced the security of property rights over time, which was essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The institutions that supported public debt markets provided a substantial positive externality to the development of private debt markets as well (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North argues that this institutional framework was crucial because it allowed for specialization and division of labor, key features of modern economies that drive productivity gains through technological change and better resource allocation (North 1989, p.1). This perspective underscores how secure property rights, enabled by robust institutions, can foster economic growth. The positive externality from public debt markets to private debt markets further highlights the systemic impact of institutional development on economic outcomes. The institutional framework encompassed political and legal structures that supported financial instruments (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast's analysis shows how institutions shaped by political dynamics can create conditions for long-term growth, even if other factors might have eventually led to similar outcomes, albeit more slowly.

The institutional framework introduced by colonial powers is a pivotal determinant of long-run economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced current economic performance. The mortality rates faced by European settlers were a key factor in determining whether extractive or inclusive institutions would be implemented. The specific mechanism here involves the political dynamics of colonial rule, where European settlers implemented institutions designed to maximize their own rents. This extractive approach was reinforced by the reluctance of politically powerful groups to initiate institutional change that would not directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. note that these equilibrium institutions could vary widely in their impact on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Despite this variability, the overarching implication is clear: colonial institutions have left a lasting imprint on the economic trajectories of former colonies, shaping their growth patterns in profound ways.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie demonstrates that these institutions, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were not primarily driven by efficiency but rather by power struggles among different social groups who sought to maximize their own interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through policies that restricted competition. These policies were shaped by the specific factor endowments of each colony, leading to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The implications of Ogilvie’s framework are significant: institutions that emerge from distributional conflicts can be highly resistant to change because they are embedded within a broader institutional framework. This interdependency means that altering one institution often requires changing the entire system. For instance, Laslett's study on pre-industrial Europe highlights how the family system was interconnected with labor markets and credit markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Thus, institutions shaped by distributional conflicts can persist over long periods, influencing economic outcomes in profound ways. This dynamic underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on long-run growth.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, along with Broadberry and Gupta's account of economic divergence between Europe and Asia (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), both highlight how distinct historical paths shape institutional evolutions. The dynamic responses of societies to their environments play a crucial role in shaping these paths. Austin's analysis suggests that the African experience demonstrates a complex interplay between geography and endogenous factors; this interaction actively shapes economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gupta’s assertion that the great divergence was already underway before 1800, driven by investment patterns and institutional changes in agriculture. It is important to note that while these factors are significant, they do not fully account for the cultural underpinnings of institutions. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta show that higher agricultural productivity did not necessarily lead to abundant grain supplies and low food prices (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuance underscores how economic outcomes are influenced by a complex interplay between institutional frameworks and broader socio-cultural contexts. In this way, the role of culture in shaping institutions remains a critical factor in understanding long-run growth dynamics.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) demonstrate that women have historically played crucial roles in economic growth and development. This is evident both in pre-colonial Africa, where women were vital to community reproduction and wealth transmission, and in the U.S. where changes in educational institutions and household production technologies altered female labor supply. The role of women in these contexts illustrates how gender dynamics can significantly influence long-run growth through shifts in labor markets and social norms. For instance, as Goldin notes (Goldin 2006, p.3), the increased demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century created new job opportunities that transformed women's employment patterns. This transformation was not merely evolutionary; it was driven by exogenous changes such as institutional reforms and technological advancements. These shifts did not occur uniformly across societies. This underscores the importance of considering how cultural factors can both enable and constrain women's contributions to long-run growth. The persistence of these gender-based norms suggests that while institutional changes can drive economic progress, they must be understood within broader socio-cultural contexts.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show how the transatlantic slave trade instilled a pervasive culture of mistrust in African societies. This cultural shift persists today as a significant barrier to economic development. The absence of trust within society hampers cooperation and collective action, which are essential for fostering economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) highlights that the slave trades contributed to long-term political instability, resulting in weakened states that struggled to enforce property rights and provide public goods effectively. This political fragmentation further exacerbated the adverse effects of mistrust on economic development. While Nunn (2008, p.1) notes that slavery is one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, it is important to consider how this historical legacy intersects with broader cultural factors. The enduring impact of the slave trade underscores the deep-rooted nature of cultural influences on long-run growth, suggesting that addressing these issues requires a multifaceted approach that considers both historical and contemporary socio-cultural contexts.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident across various historical and contemporary contexts. Cultural beliefs and values significantly influence economic institutions and their efficiency. Ogilvie shows that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. highlight that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This interplay between culture and institutions underscores the enduring impact of historical legacies on economic development.

The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding long-run growth. Ogilvie argues that the European family system was interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for secure property rights and a robust capital market (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success by providing a stable market for public debt, which had positive externalities for private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Austin shows that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of gender dynamics in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The role of culture is evident in how institutions governing property rights and public choice evolved differently across regions.
