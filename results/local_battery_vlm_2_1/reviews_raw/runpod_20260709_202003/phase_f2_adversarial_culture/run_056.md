The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins sustained economic development over centuries. Some argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence institutional frameworks and their efficiency, thereby impacting economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others contend that extractive institutions, shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates in colonies, play a more significant role in determining long-term income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Additionally, the dynamic interplay between environmental responses and technological adaptations suggests a more complex relationship between geography and economic development. This debate underscores the need to understand how cultural norms and institutional frameworks interact to foster or hinder growth.

The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks highlights the complexity of understanding long-run economic growth. Ogilvie maintains that pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were formed by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is complemented by North's assertion that institutions matter significantly in economic growth because they address transaction costs, which are ignored in neoclassical models (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further elucidate this point by demonstrating how the evolution of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England provided secure property rights, fostering both economic growth and political freedom. This historical context underscores that cultural norms and institutional frameworks are intricately linked, with each influencing the other over time. The interdependency of institutions suggests that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, entire institutional frameworks must be examined to understand their impact on long-run economic performance.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were critical in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize how the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. The institutions that emerged during this period provided significant positive externalities, facilitating the development of both public and private debt markets. North and Weingast highlight how these institutions were instrumental in England's long-run success, suggesting that even if other factors might have eventually led to similar outcomes, they would not have done so as effectively or decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The evolution of these institutions underscores the crucial role of secure property rights in economic development.

This perspective is consistent with McCloskey and Mokyr's view that economic growth in Europe from 1600 to 1848 was primarily driven by ideas and ideologies (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey emphasizes the role of a new rhetoric that emerged in countries like the Netherlands, which fostered a culture conducive to economic growth. This rhetorical shift facilitated the development of new circumstances, creating a feedback loop where ideas shaped economic outcomes and vice versa. Mokyr complements this perspective by highlighting how useful knowledge was generated through tacit learning processes, which were crucial for technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of this knowledge across borders, facilitated by personal communications and international conferences, underscores the importance of a shared intellectual movement that transcended national boundaries. Mokyr also notes that while ideas are critical, the codification of knowledge into explicit forms was essential for its widespread dissemination (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This suggests that cultural factors, such as the willingness to share and adopt new ideas, played a significant role in driving long-term economic growth. McCloskey's work implies that culture is a driver of institutional change through the mechanism of ideas (McCloskey 2015, p.9).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrate that European mortality rates were pivotal in determining the type of institutions established in colonies; high mortality led to extractive institutions, which in turn shaped current income per capita. European settlers faced with high mortality rates imposed extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the simplicity of this narrative, noting that African responses to their environments, and their use of available technologies, played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. This suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, they did not operate in isolation from local adaptations and technological advancements. Therefore, the impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth must be understood within the broader context of endogenous responses and technological adoption by indigenous populations.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These institutions were deeply intertwined with broader societal structures. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights how serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds emerged from power struggles (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's view that institutional frameworks in New World societies were influenced by initial factor endowments and the distribution of wealth and power. The resulting distributional conflicts are a fundamental driver of institutional change. This suggests that understanding long-run growth requires examining how cultural and social factors influence institutional frameworks and economic outcomes from the outset.

This early divergence challenges the notion that industrialization alone explains the gap between Europe and Asia (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of economic development in the early modern period reveals a nuanced picture where Asian regions, despite comparable grain wages, lagged behind north-western Europe due to lower silver wages. This discrepancy is indicative of deeper structural differences that were already shaping economic outcomes long before industrialization (van Zanden 1999, p.6). The divergence in silver wages suggests that cultural and institutional factors played a significant role in determining economic trajectories. These early disparities highlight the importance of understanding how cultural influences shaped institutional frameworks, which in turn affected economic development.

The implications of this early divergence are profound as it underscores the long-term impact of initial conditions on economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.6). Understanding why silver wages were lower in Asia compared to Europe requires delving into the institutional and cultural contexts that governed economic activities. This perspective aligns with the broader argument that institutions, shaped by cultural factors, are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth. Thus, examining the early modern period provides crucial insights into how cultural influences can dominate institutions and set the stage for enduring economic disparities.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) identify how historical events like the slave trade have shaped cultural attitudes such as mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The transatlantic slave trade created a legacy of reduced social cohesion that persists to this day, with contemporary levels of trust significantly lower in regions more heavily affected by slavery. Nunn (2008, p.27) elaborates on the institutional impact of the slave trades, showing it has led to long-term political instability and weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.1). The mechanism is that the slave trade disrupted state formation processes, leaving many African societies with fragmented governance structures. This fragmentation inhibits effective economic management and policy implementation. Nunn (2008, p.27) further argues that slavery contributed to modern corruption, a finding supported by recent empirical studies linking colonial experience to current economic development (Nunn 2008, p.1). The historical mistrust fostered by the slave trade thus becomes an antecedent for cultural attitudes, which in turn influence institutional effectiveness and long-term economic outcomes. This dynamic suggests that addressing contemporary African underdevelopment requires recognizing and mitigating the enduring effects of historical events on cultural norms and institutions.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal for understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions are the rules of the game that structure interactions, and cultural norms influence their formation and effectiveness (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical mistrust fostered by the slave trade exemplifies how cultural attitudes can persist and shape institutional outcomes, as seen in contemporary African underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This dynamic is not limited to Africa; it extends globally. For instance, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' highlights that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, tend to have institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural influence on institutions is evident in the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times. The historical experience of slavery and its enduring effects on cultural attitudes illustrate how deeply rooted these influences can be, shaping not just individual behaviors but also institutional frameworks over centuries.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework further underscores the importance of culture in long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie argues that the European family system was interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. Similarly, Acemoglu and Johnson suggest that favourable 'contracting' institutions are partially interdependent with favourable 'property rights' institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; the entire institutional framework must be considered. The historical mistrust fostered by the slave trade thus becomes an antecedent for cultural attitudes, which in turn influence institutional effectiveness and long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This dynamic suggests that addressing contemporary underdevelopment requires recognizing and mitigating the enduring effects of historical events on cultural norms and institutions.

The critical role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth is well-documented. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security was crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. Similarly, North and Weingast highlight how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, showing how institutions were necessary for making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These findings align with the broader argument that cultural norms influence institutional effectiveness, which in turn drives long-term economic outcomes.

The consensus is clear: institutions matter fundamentally. Cultural attitudes shape institutions, which in turn drive economic growth. The disagreement lies in how to measure and compare these effects across different historical contexts (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This is settled by the empirical evidence of African underdevelopment following the slave trade. The historical mistrust fostered by this experience persists today, shaping cultural norms and institutional effectiveness.
