The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains a contentious issue in economic and historical scholarship. At stake is whether cultural beliefs and values directly influence institutional frameworks that foster economic development over centuries. One position holds that cultural norms shape collective action problems and thus determine which institutions get adopted, while another argues that distributional conflicts dictate which institutions persist. This debate underscores the complex interplay between culture and institutions in shaping long-term growth trajectories.

The relationship between cultural beliefs and economic institutions has been a topic of extensive study. Ogilvie notes that exogenous cultural theories can influence the structure of economic institutions by altering the equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules efficiently. The Protestant Reformation's disappearance of these beliefs led to a decline in guilds. This dynamic illustrates how cultural shifts can reshape institutional frameworks and economic outcomes over time.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on long-term growth is period-specific, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how cultural beliefs interact with institutional frameworks over time. Austin's argument complements Ogilvie's findings by emphasizing the dynamic nature of cultural influences on economic institutions. This interaction between culture and institutions evolves in response to historical changes and developments.

The role of institutions in fostering long-term growth has been a central theme in North’s work, who argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). Institutions are critical for economic growth as they provide the framework within which individuals make choices. This perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and stable markets for public debt, both of which were crucial for England's long-run success. North and Weingast further elaborate on this by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

North and Weingast’s analysis highlights the significance of stable financial institutions in promoting economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a large positive externality for the parallel development of private debt markets. These findings complement Austin’s argument by demonstrating how stable institutions can facilitate long-term economic growth through reduced transaction costs and enhanced market stability.

In another stream, Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This perspective suggests that geographic factors play a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks and long-term growth trajectories. These findings complement North’s work, which emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and stable financial institutions. Both streams highlight how cultural beliefs and geographic factors interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes over time.

The relationship between culture and trust is another important aspect of this debate. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective suggests that historical events can have lasting effects on cultural norms and institutional frameworks. The enduring impact of the slave trade on African societies underscores how cultural beliefs shape collective action problems and determine which institutions get adopted.

The interplay between culture and institutions in shaping long-term growth trajectories is complex, with both cultural norms and geographic factors playing crucial roles. This dynamic relationship highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how these factors interact to influence economic development over time.

Their argument centers on how institutional changes during this period created a stable market for public debt, which in turn fostered private debt markets and overall economic development. This reduction was critical to securing property rights over time, a dimension essential for long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth. The institutions developed during this period provided a positive externality that supported both public and private debt markets, highlighting the importance of secure property rights in economic development. This underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, suggesting that secure property rights are a critical yet not singular determinant of long-term growth.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Mokyr 2005, p.56), challenging institutional theories. This perspective underscores the importance of cultural shifts in fostering economic growth; McCloskey (2015, p.10) highlights that the "Great Enrichment" was driven by a new rhetoric that created new circumstances, which in turn generated further rhetorical changes (McCloskey 2015, p.10). The dynamic interplay between ideas and material conditions is evident in Mokyr’s assertion that useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, played a crucial role in economic growth. This knowledge was diffused through personal transmission and training as well as written texts, highlighting the embedded nature of useful knowledge within society (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The circulation of this knowledge across Europe facilitated the spread of ideas and technologies that drove economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies the role of tacit knowledge by noting that its diffusion was influenced by the technology and costs of codification. This underscores the complex interplay between cultural factors, such as ideologies and useful knowledge, and material conditions in driving long-run growth. Nevertheless, the fundamental driver remained the diffusion of useful knowledge, which was facilitated by the cultural and intellectual movements of the time.

Ideas and ideologies thus emerge as a powerful force in shaping long-run growth, operating through the dissemination of useful knowledge and the creation of new circumstances that foster economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates had a lasting impact on current income per capita, as these institutions persisted over time and influenced subsequent economic outcomes. This correlation exists between early settler mortality and the types of institutions established: high mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional framework set by colonial powers thus shaped the economic trajectory of these regions, with extractive institutions hindering development. However, the persistence of these institutions is not solely due to their initial design but also to the political dynamics that maintain them. This suggests that while colonial institutions initially hindered growth, their long-term effects are mitigated by subsequent institutional changes, which can either reinforce or alter these dynamics.

Distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key factor in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates that the persistence of pre-industrial Europe’s core economic institutions, such as serfdom, guilds, and merchant organizations, can be better explained by distributional conflicts than by efficiency. Elite interests shaped these institutions to maintain power and wealth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) similarly show that the colonial New World's extreme inequality led to institutional frameworks favoring elites. This dynamic is evident in policies restricting competition and access to resources, which further entrenched inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The mechanisms through which distributional conflicts shape institutions are multifaceted. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) points out that the interdependency of economic institutions within a broader framework means that changes in one area can ripple through the entire system, reinforcing inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness suggests that addressing inequality requires comprehensive institutional reform. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that these institutions may resist change due to their interdependency, highlighting the challenge of implementing effective reforms. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further emphasize that the persistence of inequality is not solely due to initial factor endowments but also to the institutional frameworks that evolved to maintain elite advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This indicates that long-run growth is fundamentally influenced by the ability of institutions to adapt or resist change in response to distributional conflicts.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate this relationship by showing that the legacy of the slave trade includes deep-seated distrust, which is still measurable today. This mistrust has a profound impact on economic development through its influence on social capital. It is important to note that while colonial boundaries are nearly identical to current country boundaries, so the estimates already control for any effects of colonial rule that vary at the national level (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The slave trade undermined state centralization and created long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27), which in turn affects current economic performance by limiting the effectiveness of governance structures. This institutional weakness is a critical factor in understanding Africa's long-run growth trajectories, as it creates an environment where credible commitments are difficult to enforce.

The complexity of the catch-up process in economic growth highlights the multifaceted nature of factors influencing productivity levels and technological advancement (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective emphasizes that simple convergence hypotheses are insufficient to explain long-run growth dynamics. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) underscores that the catch-up process is influenced by both exogenous factors and the intricate dynamics of technological advance and social change, suggesting that institutional changes can limit economic growth over time (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This interaction between environment and technology is crucial as it shows how endogenous factors can shape economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). Austin (2008, p.2) notes that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic approach underscores the importance of considering both exogenous and endogenous factors in understanding long-run growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident through the enduring influence of cultural beliefs on economic outcomes and institutional frameworks. Ogilvie shows that cultural theories can explain the structure of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Cultural values, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Acemoglu et al. further demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates has significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The historical evidence from Africa, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories, supports the dynamic nature of cultural influence.
