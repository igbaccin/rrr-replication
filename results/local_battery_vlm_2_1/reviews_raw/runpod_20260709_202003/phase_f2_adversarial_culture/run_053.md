The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The debate centers around whether cultural factors are fundamental causes or mere epiphenomena of institutional development. One camp argues that culture shapes the institutions that govern economic activity, while another posits that institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors such as geography and technology. A third perspective sees culture as an antecedent to institutional development, influencing how societies adapt to their environments. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship could reshape how we approach economic policy and development strategies. If culture fundamentally shapes institutions, then policies aimed at fostering growth must consider cultural contexts. If institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors, cultural interventions may be less effective. The key question is whether cultural norms and values directly influence the institutional frameworks that underpin long-term economic performance or if they are secondary to other determinants. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This cultural influence is particularly evident in how ideas and ideologies have shaped institutional frameworks, as illustrated by McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey (2015, p.10) explains this process through the lens of rhetoric, asserting that new ideas about liberty and dignity altered incentives for innovation and entrepreneurship. Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates on this mechanism, suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge is a mainspring of economic growth. The spread of such knowledge was facilitated by personal networks and tacit knowledge transmission. This diffusion was hindered by commonalities in institutions and culture among Western countries where the Enlightenment took root (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr (2005, p.56) further clarifies that much of the useful knowledge was embodied in skills and other forms of tacit knowledge, which moved about through personal transmission and training. The spread of ideas and ideologies was a social phenomenon requiring interpersonal networks for effective dissemination (Mokyr 2005, p.14). However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) acknowledges that the codification of useful knowledge is itself a function of technology and costs, suggesting that while tacit knowledge remains essential, its conversion into codified forms can enhance diffusion. The role of culture in driving long-run growth evolves with technological advancements that facilitate the spread of ideas.

The historical context of seventeenth-century England provides a compelling example of how institutions shaped by culture can drive economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically declined transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth. The role of institutions in securing property rights is further supported by the parallel development of markets for public and private debt, which provided significant positive externalities (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Efficiency gains from these institutional changes required credible commitments from political actors to uphold property rights.

This historical legacy is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who demonstrate the lasting impact of colonial institutions on current economic conditions. This stream demonstrates how the institutional frameworks shaped by colonial practices have long-lasting economic impacts, highlighting a key mechanism: high mortality rates among European settlers led to the implementation of extractive institutions. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further elaborate that these institutional frameworks played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among former colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences. The persistence of extractive institutions is due to their initial design and because they can be maintained by politically powerful groups who benefit from them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores that the stability of these institutions relies on the continued political power of those who benefit from them, even if it means foregoing long-run economic growth.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa exemplifies how historical events can shape cultural attitudes that persistently influence long-term economic outcomes, as noted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon contend that the slave trade engendered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts current trust levels. This cultural shift is part of a broader pattern where historical events leave enduring marks on societal norms. The process begins with the slave trade disrupting social cohesion, leading to a pervasive atmosphere of distrust that hinders collective action and economic cooperation. Nunn complements this view by highlighting how the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states. This political fragmentation further exacerbates mistrust as different groups vie for power and resources, creating an environment where cooperation is difficult. The implications of these historical events are profound: the erosion of trust and political stability hinders economic development by making it challenging to establish and maintain institutions that support growth. However, Nunn also notes that this mistrust can be a source of modern corruption, suggesting that while the slave trade's legacy is detrimental, it also shapes contemporary governance challenges in complex ways. This interplay between historical events, cultural attitudes, and economic outcomes underscores the enduring impact of the slave trade on African development.

This historical divergence in Europe was significantly influenced by events like the Black Death and urbanization patterns, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gupta (Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These scholars show that the economic development of northwestern Europe diverged from other regions due to institutional changes, increased labour productivity, and technological innovations. Pamuk (2007, p.5) explains this pattern as a result of the Black Death which led to greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change in guilds, contributing to the rise of rural industries and labour-saving technological innovations. Broadberry and Gupta note that in developing parts of northwestern Europe, higher agricultural productivity did not lead to low food prices because labor moved into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This shift was crucial for economic growth as it facilitated investment in agriculture and other sectors. This perspective on early modern economic divergence emphasizes historical events and institutional changes as drivers of long-run growth.

This complex interplay between historical trajectories and economic outcomes is further explored by Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They highlight how different paths of development have shaped economic performance over time, with Austin emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Sokoloff and Engerman build on this notion by showing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic highlights how initial conditions and institutional responses to them can set a country on a particular developmental path. Austin further elaborates that economies today are influenced by patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience dating back to original industrialisation (Austin 2008, p.25). This does not preclude the possibility of endogenous change within these trajectories. This interaction between historical paths and institutional evolution suggests that cultural factors, though not explicitly mentioned, may have played a role in shaping these institutional responses.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Both Acemoglu et al. and Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how initial conditions shape institutional trajectories, with cultural factors playing an implicit but significant role (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that mortality rates in colonies influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted over centuries and impacted economic performance today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that different environments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, affecting development through institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These findings suggest that cultural responses to environmental conditions may have shaped institutional evolution. Austin extends this historical continuity by showing how patterns of institutional evolution go back at least to original industrialisation, suggesting a long-term influence on growth trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25).

North and Weingast emphasise the importance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, illustrating this with England's financial revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This supports North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, countering neoclassical theories that focus on population and savings (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). Nunn and Wantchekon provide a cultural lens by showing how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact highlights how historical events can shape long-term economic outcomes through institutions.

The precise mechanisms by which culture influences institutional trajectories remain debated. Shared findings on the persistence of institutions over time show that initial conditions matter for long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The key disagreement lies in the extent to which cultural factors directly influence these institutions. North and Weingast demonstrate how specific historical events like England's financial revolution fostered secure property rights, which were crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This points to a more direct institutional path to growth compared with the indirect cultural influences highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon. Historical evidence on the financial revolution in England provides a clear example of how institutions can drive long-run growth through secure property rights, settling this debate.
