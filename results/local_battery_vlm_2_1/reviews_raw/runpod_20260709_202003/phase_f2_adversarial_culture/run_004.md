The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is understanding whether cultural beliefs and values can shape institutional frameworks to promote sustained economic performance. One side of this debate posits that culture directly influences economic outcomes by shaping institutions, while another side argues that geographical factors or extractive institutions are the primary drivers of long-term growth, with culture playing a secondary role. A third stream identifies gender dynamics as an antecedent to cultural norms and their influence on institutional frameworks, highlighting how pre-colonial social norms shaped by women's roles can persist over time.

The relationship between culture and institutions is complex, often mediated through various economic factors. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts drove the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that cultural beliefs directly influence institutional frameworks by highlighting how distributional struggles can shape economic outcomes independently of cultural values. Meanwhile, North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24), suggesting that institutions fostering secure property rights are crucial for long-term economic development. This emphasis on institutional credibility aligns with the idea that culture can shape institutions by promoting norms of trust and reciprocity, which in turn facilitate coordinated actions beneficial for economic growth. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks is further complicated by the interdependence of individual institutions within a wider framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency highlights the importance of studying entire institutional systems when examining the role of culture in long-run growth.

The impact of culture on institutions extends beyond economic outcomes to include political accountability. Putnam's theory of social capital, which emphasizes trust, norms, and networks, illustrates how cultural beliefs can influence both economic efficiency and political governance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that cultures characterized by high levels of social capital are more likely to develop institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability. The role of culture in shaping institutions is complex. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how cultural beliefs interact with other factors, such as geography and technology, to shape institutional frameworks over time. The dynamic nature of this interaction indicates that culture's influence on institutions evolves in response to changing economic and social conditions.

Culture's role in shaping institutions is further complicated by the antecedent role of gender dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This perspective underscores how pre-colonial social norms, shaped by women's roles, can persist over time and influence institutional frameworks. The enduring impact of these gender-based constructs on production systems highlights the importance of considering cultural beliefs in their historical context when examining the role of culture in long-run growth. Moreover, the persistence of these constructs suggests that cultural beliefs can have long-term effects on institutions, even as economic and social conditions change.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is also influenced by geographical factors and extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This perspective suggests that geography can shape institutional frameworks independently of cultural beliefs, highlighting the complex interplay between various factors in determining long-run growth. The correlation between mortality rates and institutional quality underscores the importance of considering multiple determinants when examining the role of culture in shaping institutions.

The impact of culture on institutions is further complicated by the role of financial innovations in promoting economic growth. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This perspective highlights how financial innovations, such as the development of new financial instruments, can reduce transaction costs and promote long-term capital markets. The role of these innovations in fostering economic growth underscores the importance of considering institutional frameworks when examining the impact of culture on long-run growth.

Their argument hinges on the development of robust financial institutions. The English financial revolution, marked by innovations such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated economic growth by providing a stable market for both public and private debt, which in turn encouraged investment and economic activity (North and Weingast; [North 1989]). The evolution of these institutions also fostered political freedom. North and Weingast's work underscores the importance of secure property rights as a fundamental precondition for long-term growth. While they emphasize the role of financial innovations in reducing transaction costs, the broader implications of cultural factors remain under-explored.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the persistence of institutions introduced by colonialism is the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mechanism driving this process involves differences in European mortality rates, which influenced the types of institutions established during colonization: high mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonists rather than fostering long-term growth. These institutions, once in place, persisted and significantly shaped current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that there is a strong correlation between settler mortality and the establishment of European settlements, which in turn influenced early and contemporary institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The implications of this dynamic are profound: extractive institutions designed for short-term gains can have long-lasting effects on economic development. This implies that while colonial institutions played a crucial role, their impact was not uniform across different contexts and time periods. The dynamic nature of institutional responses to environmental factors further complicates this picture, suggesting that Africans' own technological adaptations also influenced economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) (Goldin 2006, p.8) build on this by examining how women's roles in economic development are influenced by the interplay of cultural and institutional factors. They highlight that gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles significantly impacted long-term economic dynamics, reflecting a sexual division of labor established during the colonial period. This dynamic is further illustrated by Goldin's observation that shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes (Goldin 2006, p.8). For instance, increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century made "nice" jobs available, and the growth of educational institutions greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. Changes in household production technology also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts underscore how cultural norms and institutional frameworks can both enable and constrain women's economic participation. This persistence highlights a broader implication: while institutional changes can facilitate women's economic roles, deeply ingrained cultural norms may resist transformation.

Distributional conflicts and inequality are examined as key factors shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies, highlighting how these conflicts can lead to persistent inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial for understanding economic growth. This perspective underscores that institutions such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, which aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman’s (2000, p.4) argument that the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutional frameworks favoring elites. These frameworks restricted competition and maintained elite advantages through various government policies, illustrating how pre-existing inequalities were reinforced by institutional structures. This dynamic suggests that institutions designed to manage distributional conflicts may inadvertently perpetuate economic disparities over time. The implication is that persistent inequality in New World societies can be traced back to the early establishment of unequal institutional frameworks that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Pamuk's research (Pamuk 2007, p.24) and others (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24) underscore the significance of historical events like the Black Death in molding long-term economic development. These scholars argue that the Black Death initiated structural changes that significantly influenced Europe's economic trajectory by 1450, with effects that were not limited to immediate mortality impacts but also included shifts in wages and technological innovation. Pamuk (2007, p.24) notes that the Black Death led to a decline in interest rates, an increase in labour mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes within guilds, as well as the rise of rural industries driven by scarcity and high labour costs. These dynamics fostered labour-saving technological innovations, which contributed to economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) extend this perspective by noting that investment in agriculture led to higher agricultural productivity in Europe, though it did not necessarily result in abundant grain or low food prices due to labour migration into industry and services. The implication is that while historical events like the Black Death were pivotal, they do not fully account for long-run economic growth without considering cultural factors that influenced institutional responses to these events.

The slave trade's impact on African development highlights how historical events shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon identify a causal pathway where the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust levels, which is a key component in economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn complements this finding by showing that the adverse effects of the slave trades include long-term political instability. This instability resulted in weakened and fragmented states across Africa, which further impeded economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27). These findings underscore a broader pattern: historical events such as the slave trade can profoundly influence cultural norms, including trust and political stability, thereby shaping long-run economic outcomes. This relationship between historical events and cultural attitudes is particularly significant for understanding Africa's underdevelopment. Nunn (2008, p.27) notes that the effects of the slave trades are not isolated; they interact with other factors such as colonialism to create a complex web of influences on African development (Nunn 2008, p.1).

Historical events profoundly shape cultural norms, which in turn influence long-run economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The relationship between historical legacies and cultural attitudes is particularly salient for understanding Africa's underdevelopment. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both show that institutions can be shaped by historical events and these institutions then reinforce the effects of historical legacies on long-run growth. For example, the slave trade not only affected cultural attitudes toward trust and political stability but also interacted with other factors such as colonialism to create a complex web of influences on African development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of these historical events underscores their enduring impact on economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in mediating the effects of culture on long-run growth is critical. North (1989, p.1) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that secure property rights are essential for fostering economic growth, but they also acknowledge that cultural beliefs can influence how these institutions function (North 1989, p.1; Austin 2008, p.25). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England provides a clear example: the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This highlights how cultural attitudes can interact with institutional frameworks to shape long-run economic outcomes. Furthermore, North (1989, p.1) demonstrates that institutions matter in economic growth by showing how transaction costs are influenced by the security of property rights, which in turn affects economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The interaction between culture and institutions is not unilateral; rather, it is a dynamic process where cultural beliefs can both influence and be influenced by institutional frameworks. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) illustrate this dynamic through their analysis of the financial revolution in England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting how cultural norms around gender roles can shape economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are crucial for fostering long-run economic growth by shaping the security of property rights. This finding is shared across Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30). The remaining disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs can influence the structure and equilibrium of collective action games, thereby affecting institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the analysis of the financial revolution in England. This event shows how institutions governing public choice evolved to foster secure property rights and economic growth, demonstrating the dynamic interaction between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks.
