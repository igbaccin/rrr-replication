The question of what role culture plays in shaping institutions as fundamental drivers of long-term growth has sparked significant debate among scholars. The stakes are high because the answer to this question informs our understanding of how societies develop and why certain regions flourish while others lag behind. One live disagreement centers around whether cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, directly influence economic outcomes through institutional channels or if other factors, such as geography and extractive institutions, predominate. Some scholars argue that the impact of culture on economic growth is mediated through its effect on institutions, which in turn shape economic performance. Others posit that the role of culture has been overstated, and that more direct causal mechanisms, such as extractive institutions or exogenous shocks like disease environments, are more influential. The resolution of this debate hinges on understanding how cultural beliefs translate into institutional frameworks that support or hinder long-term growth.

Culture is widely seen as a critical factor in shaping economic institutions, with scholars emphasizing the role of social norms and trust in fostering growth. Trust, for instance, facilitates coordinated action and reduces transaction costs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Cultural beliefs about reciprocity and horizontal networks can lead to the development of strong guilds and communes, which are associated with superior economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). In this view, culture actively shapes institutional frameworks as an active force (Author Year p.N). Institutions, in turn, create the conditions for economic growth by providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The relationship between institutions and growth is further complicated by their interdependencies within wider frameworks. For example, the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was linked to various markets and the nuclear family system, potentially furthering economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence suggests that institutions must be studied as part of a broader framework rather than in isolation. The evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights and financial innovations fostered long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings underscore the importance of understanding how cultural factors influence institutional frameworks, which in turn shape economic outcomes.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is also evident in the historical development of European colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates had lasting effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This suggests that cultural and institutional factors can interact to produce long-term economic outcomes, even if the initial conditions were shaped by exogenous shocks like disease environments.

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights were crucial for economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). This claim is substantiated by the evolution of institutions governing public choice, which facilitated financial innovations such as the creation of the Bank of England. The development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights over time. The role of institutions extends beyond mere financial mechanisms; they also provided a large positive externality for private debt markets by fostering a stable market for public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The implications are clear: without these institutional developments, England's long-term success would have been much slower and less decisive.

The role of culture in shaping economic institutions may not always be direct or decisive. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, and New World societies than exogenous cultural theories. Distributional conflicts can shape economic institutions by creating incentives for elites to establish rules that maintain their power and wealth, even if these rules are inefficient. In pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts between landlords and peasants led to the creation of serfdom, where the power was concentrated in the hands of a few elites, who would then use this power to create institutions that maintained their dominant position. The persistence of these unequal institutions can hinder economic growth by limiting opportunities for innovation and investment. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.27) also notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial to understanding institutional evolution and its impact on economic performance.

Colonial institutions are a significant driver of long-run economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize colonial rents (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework was a direct result of colonial policies and power dynamics. The persistence of extractive institutions can be attributed to the reluctance or inability of politically powerful groups to initiate institutional change, as they may not directly benefit from the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), a pattern that aligns with the broader theme of elite interests shaping institutions. This suggests that the legacy of colonial rule is deeply embedded in the institutional structures of former colonies.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10)(Mokyr 2005, p.56) jointly argue that ideas are the primary drivers of economic change. This perspective is exemplified by the role of intellectual shifts in shaping economic outcomes; McCloskey posits that cultural changes, particularly those stemming from new rhetorical frameworks, were pivotal in driving economic growth in Northwestern Europe between 1600 and 1848. Mokyr (2005, p.56) supports this view by emphasizing the significance of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic development; however, he qualifies it with a nuanced understanding that access to such knowledge was not merely a matter of written records but also relied heavily on personal transmission and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This highlights the importance of tacit knowledge in economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.10) describes how new ideas transformed societal rhetoric, which then shaped new circumstances conducive to economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). The cultural struggle in France, for instance, illustrates how deeply embedded ideologies can both hinder and propel economic development. This interplay between ideas and institutions underscores the broader theme of how cultural shifts can fundamentally alter the trajectory of long-run growth by influencing the adoption and effectiveness of institutional frameworks.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) jointly argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal pathways in economic development. They point out that different historical periods and paths are compressed into a single narrative, which obscures the nuanced interplay between institutions, culture, and economic growth. This limitation is critical because it highlights how cultural factors can influence which institutions are adopted and how effectively they function. Austin (2008, p.25) further elaborates on this by rejecting the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Instead, he proposes a more dynamic view where Africans' responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. This dynamic approach underscores how cultural adaptations can drive institutional changes and, consequently, economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) add depth to this discussion by challenging the notion that the great divergence between Europe and Asia began only after 1800. They argue that this divergence was already evident before 1800, with prosperous parts of Asia resembling stagnating regions in Europe. This historical context is crucial because it shows how cultural and institutional differences can lead to divergent economic paths over extended periods. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.1) further emphasize that in advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages, indicating purchasing power over tradable goods and services, were substantially lower. This wage disparity suggests underlying cultural and institutional factors that influenced economic development differently across regions. The implication is that cultural norms and values can shape the effectiveness of institutions, thereby influencing long-run growth trajectories. These findings collectively underscore how culture plays a pivotal role in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth by shaping the adoption and efficacy of institutional frameworks.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is seen as a rival explanation to broader cultural factors influencing growth. The mechanism involves the historical legacy of political instability and state fragmentation caused by the slave trades, which weakened institutional development. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states, which has been empirically linked to current economic underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.1). The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization underscores how this institutional weakness persists over time, influencing modern corruption levels. This institutional decay is a result of historical factors that have shaped Africa's development trajectory.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply intertwined with historical institutional frameworks and their impact on economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that cultural beliefs can significantly influence economic institutions and efficiency by altering the structure and equilibrium of collective action games, such as guilds and communes. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). These frameworks show how cultural values, embedded within institutional structures, can foster or hinder economic development over time.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This historical context supports Austin's assertion that understanding long-term economic growth requires examining patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over centuries (Austin 2008, p.2). The shared finding is that cultural beliefs and values, when integrated into institutions, can create frameworks that either support or impede economic growth.

The persistent effects of historical events on current economic conditions are evident in the work of Nunn and Wantchekon. They argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is the empirical link between trust and economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade's cultural impact on mistrust has enduring effects on African economies today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical evidence, combined with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that early institutions shaped by mortality rates have long-lasting impacts on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), shows that culture plays a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks that drive economic growth.
