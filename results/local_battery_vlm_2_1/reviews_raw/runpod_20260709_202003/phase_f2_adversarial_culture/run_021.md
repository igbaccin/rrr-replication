The role of culture in shaping long-run growth through dominating institutions is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic progress over centuries. One position argues that cultural factors are crucial determinants of institutional quality, which in turn shapes long-term economic performance. These scholars contend that certain cultural traits can foster or hinder the development of efficient institutions. Their argument highlights how deeply ingrained beliefs and values influence societal norms, ultimately affecting economic outcomes. One perspective holds that institutions are the primary drivers of growth, with culture playing a secondary role at best (Author Year p.N).

The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are antecedent or epiphenomenal in shaping institutional frameworks. This question matters because it affects how policymakers and scholars understand and address economic underdevelopment. If culture is indeed fundamental, then policies aimed at fostering growth must consider deeply rooted social norms and beliefs. If institutions drive growth, development efforts should focus on reforming governance structures and legal frameworks.

The role of cultural factors in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is evident in the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary economic performance. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European mortality rates significantly influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth due to their role in reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The historical context of institutional development is crucial; for instance, the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations reduced transaction costs and fostered economic growth by securing property rights (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The cultural underpinnings of institutions are further illustrated by the long-term consequences of colonial constructs on gender relations. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting how gender roles shaped production systems (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This cultural dimension intersects with institutional frameworks, as the sexual division of labor established during colonial times continues to influence contemporary economic dynamics. The persistence of these gender-based constructs underscores how deeply ingrained cultural norms can shape long-term economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon's work on Africa’s slave trade underscores that this historical trauma fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust has had enduring effects on economic development by shaping institutional frameworks and social dynamics. The slave trade decimated populations and disrupted social cohesion, leading to long-term political instability and weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn argues that the negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization is consistent with historical accounts of how the slave trades caused long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). This instability resulted in fragmented states, which in turn hindered economic performance. The legacy of these weakened institutions persists today, influencing contemporary economic outcomes. Colonial boundaries, nearly identical to current country boundaries, already control for any effects of colonial rule that vary at the national level (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical context is crucial for understanding how cultural norms of mistrust, born out of the slave trade, continue to affect African economies. The deep-rooted mistrust can impede economic development by reducing social capital and hindering collective action, which are essential for sustained growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated the emergence of a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the growth of private debt markets. This institutional evolution was intertwined with broader political and social changes. This historical context underscores the importance of secure property rights as a foundation for economic development, highlighting how institutions can shape growth trajectories by influencing investment and capital markets.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) share an emphasis on ideas as key drivers of economic growth, highlighting the pivotal role of intellectual shifts in transforming Northwestern Europe from 1600-1848. McCloskey explicitly challenges interest-only theories by highlighting how cultural struggles and new rhetoric drove economic changes (McCloskey 2015, p.9; McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr elaborates on this mechanism, noting that useful knowledge was central to modern growth and that the diffusion of this knowledge was facilitated through various channels such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This diffusion enabled a common intellectual movement to take root across different nations. In other words, ideas spread through social networks, shaping economic outcomes by influencing collective beliefs and values.

Yet Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that the codification of knowledge was not just about written texts but also involved tacit knowledge, skills and know-how passed down through personal transmission and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This implies that the diffusion of ideas required social interactions and institutional frameworks that facilitated learning and skill transfer. Consequently, while ideas were crucial, their impact on economic growth was mediated by the social structures that enabled their dissemination and application.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 was already underway according to Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). In the advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe. Silver wages conferred lower purchasing power over tradable goods and services. The divergence is evident in the economic development patterns of these regions; prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 resemble stagnating southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe more than developing north-western parts. This pattern suggests that while agricultural productivity was high in certain Asian regions, it did not translate into broader economic growth as seen in Europe. The divergence could be attributed to differences in institutional frameworks and investment patterns between these regions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10; 2022, p.1) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This indicates that regional variations in economic development can be attributed to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The varying timing and scale of growth in export production across different regions further highlights the complex interplay between these factors and economic outcomes. This does not directly address cultural influences as a fundamental cause of long-run growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that institutions and gender play pivotal roles in shaping economic development, but these factors do not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. The historical context of colonialism is particularly illuminating for understanding these dynamics. The persistence of these colonial legacies suggests that cultural factors can deeply influence institutional development, even in the face of significant historical changes.

The specific mechanisms by which colonial gender roles influenced production systems are particularly noteworthy. In matrilineal societies like Asante, women were crucial to community reproduction and wealth transmission (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic highlights how gendered social structures can be integral to economic development, as women's roles in these societies directly impacted the transmission of office and wealth. The enduring influence of these colonial constructs on contemporary economic systems underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping long-run growth trajectories.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita. This institutional framework was shaped by the need to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that these colonial institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among former colonies, accounting for current income differences. The persistence of extractive institutions highlights how initial institutional designs can have enduring effects on economic development.

These institutions influence social structures and cultural practices in addition to economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). For instance, extractive institutions can perpetuate inequality by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few, thereby limiting opportunities for broader societal advancement. This dynamic underscores how institutional legacies from colonialism continue to shape contemporary economic trajectories, often independently of cultural factors.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex, yet shared findings from multiple sources highlight its significance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Institutions matter because they shape economic outcomes by establishing rules and structures that govern behavior. These rules can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on their nature. For instance, the development of secure property rights in England during the seventeenth century played a crucial role in fostering long-term economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical context underscores how institutions that ensure the security of property rights contribute to sustained economic prosperity.

The persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates illustrates how initial institutional designs can have lasting effects on economic trajectories. These institutions, characterized by their concentration of power and resources, limit opportunities for broader societal advancement and perpetuate inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The legacy of colonialism continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, often independently of cultural factors. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding historical contexts in examining current disparities.

The interplay between culture and institutions is evident in the development of a culture of mistrust within Africa due to the slave trade. This culture persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The long-term consequences of historical events, such as the transatlantic slave trade, illustrate how cultural norms can be shaped by institutional legacies.

The fundamental cause of long-run growth is rooted in the interplay between institutions and culture. Institutions provide the framework within which economic activities occur, while cultural norms influence how individuals interact with these institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). For instance, the development of a stable market for public debt in England provided a positive externality for the parallel development of a private debt market (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical example shows that institutional frameworks can foster economic growth by facilitating the accumulation of capital and promoting investment.

The precise impact of culture on institutions remains debated. While some argue that cultural factors significantly influence institutional outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), others contend that initial institutional designs play a more determinative role in shaping long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The historical evidence from colonialism shows how extractive institutions can perpetuate inequality by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few, thereby limiting opportunities for broader societal advancement. This dynamic underscores the importance of examining the historical context of institutional formation in understanding current disparities.

The measurement of settler mortality rates provides a robust method for assessing the impact of initial institutional designs on long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Settler mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies with high mortality are highly correlated with European settlements, early measures of institutions, and institutions today. This correlation highlights how historical factors can have enduring effects on contemporary economic trajectories. The use of settler mortality as a proxy for institutional quality shows that extractive institutions can significantly affect current income per capita.
