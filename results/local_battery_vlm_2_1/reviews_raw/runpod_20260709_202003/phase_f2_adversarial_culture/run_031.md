The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among economic historians and social scientists. At stake is how to reconcile the profound influence of cultural norms on institutional development with the equally compelling arguments that emphasize the primacy of exogenous factors such as geography, technology, or political structures. The debate hinges on whether cultural beliefs fundamentally shape institutions that drive long-term growth or if these beliefs are merely epiphenomena of deeper structural forces. This disagreement is reflected in two primary positions: one emphasizes the endogenous role of culture in shaping institutions and economic outcomes, while the other posits that exogenous factors, such as geography or colonial history, determine institutional frameworks and cultural norms. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the complexity of understanding long-run growth dynamics through a lens focused on the causal role of culture.

The first stream addresses how culture influences the creation and persistence of institutions, which in turn affect economic performance. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts drove the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the belief that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, suggesting instead that these beliefs influenced the structure and equilibrium of collective action. Similarly, North and Weingast emphasize how secure property rights, fostered by institutional developments in seventeenth-century England, were crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This argument complements Ogilvie's view by illustrating how cultural norms can shape institutions that facilitate long-term economic prosperity. The interplay between these views underscores the importance of understanding how culture influences institutional frameworks.

The next stream identifies an antecedent that flows into the topic's causal variable as a trigger. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the dynamics of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience are more complex than what is often presented. Austin's critique aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, highlighting the need to consider multiple factors beyond just cultural norms (North 1989, p.1). This interplay underscores how different historical periods and paths can influence the development of institutions, which in turn affect long-run growth dynamics.

Finally, a stream addresses how culture influences institutional frameworks through the lens of trust. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural norm persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, illustrating how historical events can shape cultural beliefs that influence institutional development. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective complements Nunn and Wantchekon's argument by highlighting how exogenous factors such as mortality rates can shape cultural norms and institutional frameworks. The interplay between these views underscores the complex relationship between culture, institutions, and long-run growth dynamics.

By examining the interplay between cultural beliefs, institutional development, and exogenous factors such as geography or colonial history, scholars can gain deeper insights into how these elements influence long-run growth dynamics.

The institutions established during this period were critical for fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of long-term investments, thus enabling a dynamic capital market to flourish. North (1989, p.9) elaborates that the creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights over time. This institutional framework was instrumental in creating a stable environment for both public and private debt markets, thereby facilitating long-term economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) recognize that these institutions provided large positive externalities; however, the development of a market for private debt was contingent on the parallel growth of a stable market for public debt. This interdependency highlights how institutional frameworks can catalyze economic growth through interconnected financial markets.

The interconnected financial markets that catalyzed economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were driven by ideologies and ideas, as McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56) argue. McCloskey highlights how cultural shifts influenced by rhetoric and ideology, such as the "bourgeois honor" in countries like the Netherlands, France, and eventually Spain, led to new circumstances that facilitated economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge was instrumental in driving economic development during this period. This knowledge was codified and embodied in tacit forms, such as skills and expertise, which were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of knowledge across borders within Europe further underscores its role in fostering economic growth. Mokyr notes that the intellectual movement during this era transcended national boundaries, highlighting the shared cultural and institutional frameworks that facilitated the spread of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The widespread dissemination of ideas and knowledge suggests that the fundamental cause of long-run growth was institutional and deeply rooted in cultural and ideological shifts encouraging innovation and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonialism's institutional changes were a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates had lasting effects on current income per capita, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. This effect is mediated through the persistence of early institutions which, in turn, influence contemporary economic performance. The mechanism involves the correlation between settler mortality and institutional quality: higher mortality rates led to extractive institutions that prioritized colonial interests over local development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions aimed to maximize rents for European colonists. This pattern highlights how historical factors, such as mortality rates, influenced the type of institutions established during colonial rule.

The culture of mistrust in Africa, argued to have been caused by the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27), is a direct result of these institutional changes. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows how the African slave trades led to weakened states and political instability. This effect is mediated by the economic consequences of slavery itself, which created incentives for corruption and undermined state formation. The downstream implication of this mechanism is that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) also notes that slavery's effects include political instability as well as long-term economic consequences such as corruption and underdevelopment. The slave trade thus may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). The institutional legacies of colonialism, which were shaped by historical factors such as mortality rates and the type of institutions established during colonial rule, are critical determinants of economic performance today.

The role of culture in shaping economic institutions is thus overshadowed by the focus on distributional conflicts and inequality, as examined by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4). Both authors share that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie highlights that these conflicts influenced the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds in ways that were not necessarily efficient but rather shaped by power dynamics between different social groups. These elite-advantaging institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality over time. This suggests a broader pattern where pre-existing inequalities shape economic institutions, leading to long-term effects on growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) also note that the specific impact of these institutions can be highly context-dependent (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions underscores the importance of understanding power dynamics within societies to fully grasp how economic growth is achieved over the long run.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) concur, noting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This perspective highlights a key point: economic growth is influenced by distinct institutional evolutions. The historical paths of different regions are shaped by specific environmental responses and technological adaptations (Austin 2008, p.25). These dynamics indicate that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is inherently period-specific, varying across different times and contexts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). Culture does not dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. Instead, it suggests that the interaction between cultural factors and institutional frameworks is more nuanced and context-dependent, with different regions experiencing unique trajectories based on their historical and environmental conditions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide secure property rights and foster trust. The financial revolution in England, which led to the development of new financial instruments and a stable market for public debt, is a clear example of how institutions can drive long-term success (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework facilitated economic growth and played a critical role in establishing political freedom by providing a secure environment for private transactions. The evolution of these institutions was pivotal in enabling the growth of a stable market for public debt, which provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This dynamic illustrates how institutional frameworks can create conditions conducive to economic prosperity and political stability.

This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that cultural factors influence institutional structures and efficiency. The interdependency between individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial, as seen in the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England. These systems were interconnected with labour markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, contributing to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interplay highlights that cultural beliefs and values associated with institutional manifestations can explain economic performance over time.
