The causal role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop and why some thrive while others stagnate. One position argues that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence institutional structures, which in turn facilitate or hinder economic growth. Proponents of this view contend that certain cultures foster institutions conducive to growth by promoting trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks. Another perspective posits that the impact of culture on institutions is secondary to other factors such as geographic determinants, extractive institutions, and historical events like colonialism. These scholars assert that economic outcomes are primarily determined by exogenous variables and endogenous cultural influences (Author Year p.N). A third stream identifies antecedents to cultural influence, suggesting that specific historical contexts or external shocks can trigger institutional changes that subsequently affect growth trajectories. This debate is crucial because it shapes policies aimed at fostering sustainable development and understanding the deep-rooted factors influencing a nation's economic trajectory. The interplay between culture and institutions in driving long-run growth remains a pivotal area of inquiry, with scholars seeking to unravel how cultural norms interact with institutional frameworks over time.

Culture as an antecedent to institutional change is often discussed as a trigger for broader shifts that ultimately affect economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and has significantly impacted trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural shift, rooted in historical trauma, has long-term effects on social capital and institutional development. Similarly, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, fostering an environment conducive to specialization, division of labor, and technological change (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The evolution of financial institutions like the Bank of England illustrates how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs can drive economic growth. These findings suggest that cultural antecedents, such as historical traumas or institutional innovations, play a critical role in shaping the institutional frameworks that underpin long-run economic development.

Cultural beliefs and values are often cited as direct influences on institutional structures, which in turn affect economic outcomes. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts explain core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Cultural theories on the micro-level, such as those proposed by Richardson, suggest that religious beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, influencing the structure and equilibrium of collective action games. Putnam's theory of social capital also highlights how cultural values like trust and reciprocity can improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North and Weingast's argument that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights, which drove economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These studies underscore the importance of cultural norms in shaping institutional frameworks that support long-run economic development.

This institutional framework was pivotal in driving long-run economic development. North underscores that the neoclassical model, which focuses on population and savings, overlooks the critical role of institutions in economic growth. The evolution of these institutions provided a stable market for public debt, creating a positive externality for private debt markets. The role of cultural factors in shaping these institutional frameworks remains under-examined. North and Weingast acknowledge that while institutions were necessary, other forces might have eventually led to success but would have done so more slowly and less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Distributional conflicts were a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight that the core economic institutions, such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups rather than cultural beliefs or efficiency concerns. The institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe were often products of conflicts over resource distribution, with elites using their power to shape institutions that maintained their dominance. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality across New World societies, where initial conditions led to extreme wealth disparities and political power imbalances (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These differentials in development pathways are closely linked to the institutions that evolved in response to these inequalities, highlighting how distributional conflicts shaped economic growth. For example, the absence of strong guilds and communes in southern Italy can be attributed to different frameworks of social organization which resisted encroachment by other institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The interdependencies between various institutions within a wider framework suggest that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, it is necessary to examine entire institutional frameworks.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. They contend that the growth of useful knowledge was a fundamental force behind economic development, challenging institutional explanations that focus on interests or institutions alone. This perspective highlights how cultural shifts and intellectual movements played a critical role in fostering an environment conducive to innovation and economic growth. The rhetoric and values that emerged from this struggle influenced the broader societal framework, facilitating or hindering economic growth depending on the prevailing cultural norms. McCloskey's emphasis on the role of ideology in economic development is further supported by Mokyr’s analysis of the diffusion of knowledge across Europe. Mokyr argues that the ready exchange of ideas and knowledge within Europe was a key factor in the Great Enrichment, as useful knowledge spread rapidly through various means such as periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This diffusion of knowledge transcended national boundaries, highlighting the interconnected nature of European intellectual life. However, Mokyr also notes that much of this knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills and practices, which required personal transmission and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14), suggesting that while ideas were crucial, their effective dissemination depended on the social and institutional contexts that facilitated learning and skill-sharing. This interplay between ideas and institutions underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where cultural factors play a pivotal role in shaping the broader framework within which economic development occurs.

This dynamic highlights how the evolution of ideas and knowledge can drive long-term economic growth, even as it interacts with existing institutional structures.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective emphasizes the role of extractive institutions in shaping current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The persistence of these institutions over time has significantly impacted economic performance, as evidenced by the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutional quality.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further underscore that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The extractive nature of these institutions is pivotal; they were designed to consolidate power and extract resources for colonizers. This dynamic highlights how initial institutional frameworks, shaped by colonial experiences, continue to influence long-term economic trajectories. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the impact of institutions on income per capita is highly significant, suggesting that while culture may play a role, it is the institutional framework that fundamentally determines economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The Industrious Revolution and technological advance are critical phenomena that precede and facilitate economic growth, but they do not explicitly link culture to long-run growth (de Vries 1994, p.18; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). The Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, laid the groundwork for later industrialization. This phenomenon was characterized by a shift in household dynamics, where labor reserves were released into the market and practical functions were taken over by market- and government-supplied services (de Vries 1994, p.18). Abramovitz's analysis of productivity levels highlights that social competence for exploiting advanced technology was initially limited but improved over time. This suggests that while technological advance is crucial, the ability to harness it effectively is contingent on broader social and institutional factors (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This dynamic does not directly address how culture influences these processes. De Vries notes that the development of Europe's urban network during the early modern period established a framework for regional economic development, which industrial growth could occur within. This indicates that while technological advances are pivotal, they are embedded in broader social and institutional contexts (de Vries 1994, p.5). This implies that while technological advance is a key driver of economic growth, it operates within a complex web of social, institutional, and potentially cultural influences.

The slave trade has left enduring cultural legacies that have shaped African development trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrate that the historical practice of slavery engendered a culture of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust, they argue, is an important factor accounting for reduced trust today. The slave trade's impact on African development extends beyond economic factors to include deep cultural attitudes such as mistrust, which persist even after centuries. However, Nunn (2008, p.1) also acknowledges that Africa's underdevelopment is multifaceted, involving colonialism and more recent events alongside slavery.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in how deeply ingrained cultural attitudes shape economic outcomes over centuries. Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts are key to understanding pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Cultural beliefs about afterlife influenced medieval craftsmen's adherence to guild rules, demonstrating the influence of non-economic cultural beliefs on institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This interplay between culture and institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that European mortality rates significantly affected the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions, in turn, have persisted and influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The persistence of cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, is a direct result of historical events like slavery. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and significantly impacts economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding aligns with Ogilvie's argument that cultural beliefs can influence institutional structures and economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in England during the seventeenth century highlights the importance of secure property rights for long-run growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional framework provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further enhancing economic stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The underlying point of agreement is that culture influences institutions and long-run growth. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide security to property rights (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). There remains a disagreement on the specific mechanisms through which cultural attitudes shape institutional frameworks. Ogilvie points out the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that cultural beliefs can change the structure and equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on current income per capita.
