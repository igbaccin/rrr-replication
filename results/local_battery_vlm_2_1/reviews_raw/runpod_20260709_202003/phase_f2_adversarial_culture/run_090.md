The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The intellectual stakes hinge on understanding whether cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic outcomes through institutional frameworks, or if other factors such as extractive institutions, geographic determinants, or historical legacies play a more significant role. One position posits that culture shapes institutions by influencing norms of behaviour and social organization, which in turn facilitate coordinated actions conducive to growth. Another perspective suggests that the impact of cultural beliefs on economic performance is overstated, and that institutional frameworks are primarily determined by exogenous factors such as mortality rates or geographic conditions. Additionally, there are arguments that highlight the dynamic interplay between culture, institutions, and economic development, emphasizing the importance of understanding how these elements interact over time to shape long-run growth.

The debate centres around whether cultural beliefs directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, with some scholars arguing for a strong causal link between culture and institutional frameworks. Others contend that the relationship is more complex, involving a multitude of factors including historical legacies, geographic conditions, and political dynamics. The disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural beliefs interact with other determinants to shape long-run economic growth.

The literature often highlights the interdependencies between institutions within a wider framework. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective suggests that geographic conditions can shape institutional frameworks independently of cultural beliefs, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant of economic growth. However, Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), suggesting that cultural beliefs about reciprocity and social organization may have influenced the development of these institutions. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks highlights the complex relationship between these elements in shaping long-run economic development.

North and his colleagues propose that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they shape the rules and incentives governing economic activity (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Institutions determine the costs of transactions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. North (1989, p.1) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs and enhanced property rights security; these factors were pivotal for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) finding that European settler mortality rates, which influenced institutional development in colonies, have a significant impact on current income per capita. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive rather than growth-promoting; politically powerful groups often design institutions to maximize their rents rather than long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the relationship between institutions and growth is complex, with the specific nature of institutions playing a critical role in determining economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both assert that distributional struggles shape institutions in ways that favor elite interests. Cultural explanations often attribute economic outcomes to broad societal attitudes, such as trust or norms of reciprocity (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie contends that these factors are less influential than the structural power of elites who design institutions to maximize their own rents; this is a pattern evident in pre-industrial Europe and colonial New World societies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illustrate how initial inequality led to institutional frameworks that perpetuated elite advantages, such as policies restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic suggests that institutions are actively shaped by power dynamics and distributional conflicts, not merely reflections of cultural norms.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that historical and institutional factors played significant roles in shaping long-run economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the need to consider dynamic, endogenous processes rather than static geographical determinants. This perspective is complemented by Broadberry and Gupta, who contend that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The great divergence was driven by institutional changes in agricultural investment and labour productivity, which led to varying levels of economic development across regions. This dynamic process underscores how historical institutions influenced long-run growth, with specific mechanisms such as agricultural investment and labour productivity shaping regional economic outcomes. One downstream implication is that the timing and nature of these institutional changes could have profound effects on a region's economic trajectory. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) suggest that higher agricultural labour productivity in Europe did not necessarily lead to abundant grain and low food prices, as labour shifted into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This shift had implications for the distribution of wealth and the structure of economic activity, which could further influence long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). This suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run growth may vary over time.

The literature on African economic performance and institutional reforms highlights that institutional variables are key to explaining development outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) asserts that the significance of institutions in Africa extends beyond colonial rule, necessitating a historical investigation into precolonial and even prehistorical periods. The congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions, as noted by Englebert in Hopkins (2009, p.8), further underscores the legitimacy of governments and their ability to foster development. This congruence enhances institutional effectiveness, which is crucial for inducing growth. The implications are significant: the historical depth of institutions matters, but so does the timing of their analysis.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that has persisted and impacted trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the transatlantic slave trade created long-lasting cultural norms of mistrust, which continue to influence contemporary economic development. In addition, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that the gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant impact on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production in Africa. The persistence of these norms highlights how deeply rooted cultural factors can influence economic outcomes over centuries.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Hornbeck (2010, p.6) both describe specific economic changes in the U.S., such as women's employment patterns and agricultural development, that were driven by exogenous factors. Goldin highlights how shifts in women’s employment were precipitated by increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.8). The 'quiet revolution' in women's employment was a series of abrupt transitions due to changes in household production technology, institutional barriers, and the availability of part-time work (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts in women’s employment patterns were also influenced by social norms. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) shows that farmers on the American Plains used barbed wire fencing to protect their property rights, thereby reducing externalities from livestock damage. He notes that Ellickson (1991) found social norms encouraged ranchers to control cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). These norms played a critical role in shaping agricultural development and economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms by which these changes occurred remain under-examined.

The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is evident through various historical and empirical analyses. Institutions are critical for economic growth; they matter because neoclassical models alone cannot explain it (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast show how the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, including the creation of the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs and secured property rights; both are essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework fostered a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported private debt markets. The interplay between cultural beliefs and economic institutions is further highlighted by Ogilvie's examination of pre-industrial Europe. She argues that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of social capital underscores how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, thereby improving societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The influence of culture on institutions is not limited to Europe. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing that different historical periods and paths complicate straightforward causal relationships (Austin 2008, p.2). He shows how the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous version of geography, where Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is also evident in Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis of African women's contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They demonstrate that gender roles and cultural beliefs significantly impacted the production system, with long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The matrilineal society of Asante highlights how women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth, underscoring the role of cultural norms in economic development.

Acemoglu et al. provide further evidence of the impact of institutions on long-run growth by showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, illustrating how cultural and institutional factors interact over time. The strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions supports this argument (North 1989, p.9).

These findings highlight a shared understanding that institutions and culture are interdependent and critical for long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which these changes occur, particularly whether cultural beliefs directly shape economic institutions or if they operate through broader institutional frameworks. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency by changing the structure and equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Acemoglu et al.'s work on settler mortality emphasizes how exogenous factors like disease environments shape institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of colonial institutions. This shows that cultural beliefs and exogenous factors both play significant roles in shaping institutions, but their relative importance varies across different contexts.
