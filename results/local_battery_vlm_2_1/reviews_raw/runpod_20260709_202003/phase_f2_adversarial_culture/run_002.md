The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centering on whether cultural factors are antecedents to institutional development, or if they operate through other causal pathways. The live disagreement revolves around two primary positions: one arguing that cultural norms and values directly influence the formation of institutions that govern economic activity, while the other posits that institutions themselves are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or colonial history, with culture playing a secondary role. Some scholars contend that trust, derived from historical experiences like the slave trade, is crucial for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), while others emphasize the importance of secure property rights and financial institutions in fostering growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms at play can inform policy decisions aimed at promoting long-term economic prosperity. The interplay between culture and institutions, therefore, is pivotal for grasping the fundamental causes of sustained economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was pivotal in facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. The financial revolution's role in England’s success underscores how institutions can provide a necessary foundation for sustained economic development. North and Weingast acknowledge that while stable public debt markets provided positive externalities for private debt markets, broader economic growth did not immediately follow (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The evolution of these institutions played a crucial role in making possible both economic growth and political freedom, though the process was complex and multifaceted.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions have been a fundamental cause of long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They show this by tracing the effects of European mortality rates on institutional legacies in colonies. The mortality rates influenced whether Europeans could settle and establish institutions, which in turn shaped current economic performance. This argument is supported by a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions. A high correlation between mortality rates faced by soldiers, bishops, and sailors in the colonies and European settlements underscores this effect (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). However, Acemoglu et al. caution that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development; instead, they are often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

This perspective challenges the notion that culture alone dominates institutions in shaping long-run growth because it highlights how institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had persistent effects on economic outcomes. The institutional legacies of colonialism, shaped by factors such as European mortality rates, have significantly impacted current income per capita and economic performance. This dynamic suggests a complex interplay between historical events, institutional design, and long-term economic development.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, challenging interest-only theories (Mokyr 2005, p.56; McCloskey 2015, p.10). This perspective underscores the role of cultural shifts in enabling economic growth. McCloskey's analysis highlights how new rhetoric can create new circumstances that foster long-run development. The ideas that took root during this period represented a profound shift in thinking that drove economic progress and were more than mere institutional changes. Mokyr (2005, p.56) complements this view by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.14). He argues that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by personal transmission and training, which highlights the importance of cultural exchange in disseminating ideas.

McCloskey's perspective on the role of ideas in driving economic change suggests a complex interplay between culture and institutions. McCloskey notes how new rhetoric can create new circumstances, implying cultural shifts can lead to institutional changes (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr further elaborates on this by showing the diffusion of knowledge relied heavily on tacit knowledge and personal interactions in addition to written records. This underscores how culture, through the dissemination of ideas and skills, can fundamentally shape economic outcomes (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implication is that cultural factors, such as the rhetoric and ideologies McCloskey discusses, are not merely passive elements influenced by institutions but active drivers that can initiate institutional changes.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This early modern economic divergence is marked by significant institutional changes that drove long-term economic differences. Broadberry and Gupta point to the role of investment in agriculture, which led to high agricultural labor productivity but did not necessarily result in abundant grain or low food prices. This is because labor shifted out of agriculture into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Pamuk similarly highlights how the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450, driven by greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change in guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.5).

These historical events underscore the importance of institutional changes in driving economic divergence. Pamuk's analysis shows these institutional shifts resulted in labor-saving technological innovations which further propelled economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This dynamic interplay between institutions and technological change suggests that while cultural factors may influence long-run growth through their impact on institutions, the specific mechanisms by which this occurs are complex and multifaceted.

Technological advances and social changes significantly influence economic growth by reshaping productivity levels and institutional dynamics (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; de Vries 1994, p.18). Abramovitz and de Vries both highlight the multifaceted nature of technological progress. Abramovitz discusses how countries' productivity rankings can shift due to institutional changes, which in turn affect their ability to adopt advanced technologies. This dynamic is further illustrated by de Vries's examination of the Industrious Revolution, where increased labor input and new consumption patterns drove economic development. The Industrious Revolution preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution, demonstrating that social changes can lay the groundwork for technological advances (de Vries 1994, p.5). Abramovitz notes that countries' social competence in exploiting advanced methods is crucial for productivity growth. This implies that cultural factors, which influence social competence, play a role in determining how effectively a society can adopt and implement new technologies. The relationship between technological advance and social change is complex, with cultural factors potentially influencing how societies adapt to new technologies.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that Africa's slave trade caused a culture of mistrust which persists to this day (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural deficit is a direct consequence of the institutional upheaval wrought by the slave trades. The transatlantic and intra-African slave trades led to political instability, fragmentation, and weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.27). These outcomes are deeply rooted in historical accounts showing that long-term political instability resulted from slavery. This political instability undermines state centralization and impedes economic development. Recent empirical studies suggest that the depth of experience with state-level institutions correlates positively with real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27). This implies that the slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development is mediated by its effects on institutional development. Cultural factors have a direct and substantial impact on economic outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex. Institutions are critical for economic development and growth (North 1989, p.1). They shape economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs, property rights, and market structures. This influence is evident in both historical and contemporary settings, where cultural factors have significantly impacted institutional development. For example, the slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development is mediated through its effects on trust, which accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on contemporary African economies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). Cultural factors have a direct and substantial impact on economic outcomes. The relationship between culture and institutions is further supported by evidence from Europe, where differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This shows that cultural factors, such as trust and institutional development, are integral to long-run economic growth. The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is seen in the financial revolution of seventeenth-century England, where institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
