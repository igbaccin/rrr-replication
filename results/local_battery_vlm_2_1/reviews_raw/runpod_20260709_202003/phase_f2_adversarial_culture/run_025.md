The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The intellectual stakes are high because understanding this relationship can illuminate why some societies prosper while others stagnate. Two prominent positions exist within this debate. One camp posits that cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic institutions, shaping long-term economic performance. This perspective often focuses on how shared norms and social capital facilitate coordinated actions that promote growth. The opposing camp argues that the impact of culture is indirect at best; instead, they attribute long-run growth to other factors such as secure property rights, favorable contracting institutions, or mortality rates that shaped colonial institutions. These rival explanations emphasize the primacy of institutional frameworks over cultural determinants. This debate underscores a fundamental question: do cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions in ways that drive long-term economic growth? The answer has profound implications for understanding the dynamics of societal development.

Cultural beliefs and norms are often seen as key drivers of economic institutions, which in turn influence long-run growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural theories on the micro-level can explain the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to their decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks are more likely to develop institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. Putnam contends that these cultural beliefs and values, along with their associated institutional manifestations such as strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that cultural factors may have played a more nuanced role in shaping institutional evolution over time, being a static determinant of economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding their impact on long-run growth. Ogilvie highlights the interdependence of various institutions, such as labor markets, credit markets, and family systems, in contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who suggest that favorable contracting institutions may be partially interdependent with property rights institutions. This approach underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks and their role in long-run growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice has been crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, highlighting how institutions provided a large positive externality for the development of both public and private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective is supported by North, who notes that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. These institutional changes were critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

Culture's role as an antecedent to institutions is evident in the significance of social norms and gender roles in shaping economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This perspective aligns with Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Instead, Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production (Austin 2008, p.25). This underscores how cultural factors can shape institutional frameworks and contribute to long-run growth.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). This institutional evolution, particularly the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs. The development of stable public debt markets provided significant positive externalities for private debt markets, further solidifying England's financial foundation (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework facilitated economic growth and supported political freedom. The role of institutions in securing property rights is complex and contingent on broader historical contexts. North argues that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on the specific historical and cultural settings in which they operate (North 1989, p.1). Thus, the impact of these institutions on long-run growth varies across different societies, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic development.

McCloskey and de Vries argue that ideas and ideologies played a pivotal role in driving economic change from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; de Vries 1994, p.18). Their shared claim is that the Industrious Revolution, marked by heightened labor input and new consumption patterns, was a facilitator of the Industrial Revolution. This perspective highlights how cultural shifts in attitudes towards work and innovation fostered economic growth independently of institutional frameworks. McCloskey elaborates on this point by critiquing interest-only theories, asserting that the transformation was driven by a "new rhetoric" which created new circumstances (McCloskey 2015, p.10). The Industrious Revolution exemplifies this dynamic: as de Vries notes, it involved a release of labor reserves to the market and a shift in consumption patterns, indicating a cultural readiness for economic change beyond mere institutional adjustments. This cultural readiness is crucial because it suggests that societal norms and ideologies can create an environment conducive to growth even before formal institutions are fully developed (de Vries 1994, p.18). McCloskey qualifies this by noting that the impact of ideas varied across different societies, with some cultures more receptive to change than others. This variation underscores the complexity of cultural factors in economic development, showing that while ideas can drive growth, their effectiveness is contingent on broader historical and societal contexts (McCloskey 2015, p.9).

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective is supported by a focus on how elite interests shaped institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights that institutions like serfdom and guilds, often viewed as efficient or culturally driven, were actually products of power struggles between elites and commoners. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this idea by showing how institutional frameworks in New World societies, shaped by inequality, favored elites through government policies that restricted competition and maintained social hierarchies. This institutional framework contributed to persistent inequality and limited economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie acknowledges the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may be misleading. This interdependency implies that while distributional conflicts were crucial, the overall institutional environment played a significant role in shaping long-run growth patterns.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who designed them to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that there is a high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the establishment of early institutions, which in turn influenced current institutions. Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and do not necessarily encourage economic development; this is because these institutions are designed to benefit politically powerful groups and foster long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) both challenge simplistic views of economic development by emphasizing dynamic factors that shape institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, and Bryant (2006, p.35) contends that Eurocentric views are essential for understanding economic development. Both scholars highlight the importance of endogenous factors in shaping institutional changes. This dynamic view suggests that institutions evolve through exogenous shocks and internal responses to environmental and social changes. Bryant (2006, p.35) qualifies this by arguing that the European passage to modernity was underpinned by a complex interplay of cultural norms, legal developments, and technological advancements (Bryant 2006, p.4). This implies that while endogenous factors are crucial, they operate within a broader context shaped by long-term cultural and institutional legacies. The implication is that economic growth is not merely a function of current institutions but also deeply influenced by historical trajectories and cultural contexts, which can either facilitate or hinder the adoption of growth-enhancing institutions.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Pamuk (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; Pamuk 2007, p.24) show that technological and structural changes can significantly influence economic growth. These changes, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing in North America and the long-term effects of the Black Death in Europe, demonstrate how innovations and external shocks can transform agricultural practices and labor markets, respectively. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) illustrates that barbed wire enabled farmers to protect their property rights more effectively by mitigating livestock damage from neighboring ranches. Similarly, Pamuk (2007, p.24) highlights the Black Death's role in fostering institutional changes within guilds and promoting labor-saving technological innovations. These developments illustrate how structural shifts can enhance productivity and economic efficiency. These studies suggest that such transformations are contingent upon broader cultural contexts that shape how new technologies or shocks are integrated into existing systems. For instance, Pamuk (2007, p.5) notes that the Black Death initiated a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450. This finding indicates that economic outcomes can vary widely depending on regional responses to technological and structural changes. The implication is that while these shifts are powerful drivers of growth, their impact is mediated through cultural frameworks that determine how societies adapt to new circumstances.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that women have played crucial roles in economic growth throughout history, with significant contributions during pre-colonial, colonial periods in Africa and the U.S. In matrilineal societies like Asante, women were essential for community reproduction, defining wealth transmission through their bloodlines (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic underscores how cultural frameworks of gender relations influenced economic systems: in matrilineal societies, women's roles were integral to economic power structures, as they controlled the inheritance and distribution of wealth. These shifts demonstrate how cultural changes can reshape economic institutions over time. These transformations were not uniform and varied across regions and societal contexts.

The skill premium and standard of living in European economic development do not directly address the fundamental cause of long-run growth (van Zanden 2009, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2). van Zanden (2009, p.24) shows that human capital formation is a determinant of long-term economic growth. This means that differences in the skill premium, which reflect conditions for human capital formation, have affected long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.23) ). The urban-rural wage gap was relatively small for unskilled laborers, and wages reflected agricultural productivity linked to population growth. This means that the skill premium does not directly predict long-term economic performance but may be a downstream effect of other factors such as population growth. The development of silver wages reflected economic conditions in different parts of Europe, indicating that the skill premium is influenced by various factors beyond human capital formation (van Zanden 1999, p.6) ).

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust that has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show how the historical impact of slavery on political instability has created a legacy of mistrust. This mistrust is an abstract social issue with tangible economic implications, particularly in undermining institutional effectiveness. The slave trade's adverse effects on state centralization have weakened institutions (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the historical political instability caused by the slave trades has led to fragmented states with reduced capacity for long-term planning and effective governance. While the legacy of mistrust and weak institutions is profound, it does not operate in isolation from other factors, such as colonialism or recent economic events. The long-term effects of the slave trade on African economies are complex and multifaceted, involving a range of historical and contemporary influences that interact to shape current economic conditions.
