Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. This influence is evident through the transmission of values and norms over time which can affect institutional outcomes. The debate surrounding culture's role in economic development centers on whether it acts as an antecedent to institutional formation or if it operates through other mechanisms, such as distributional conflicts or efficiency considerations. One position argues that cultural beliefs directly shape institutions by influencing collective action and social organization, thereby fostering growth. Another perspective posits that culture indirectly affects institutions through its impact on the distribution of power and resources within societies. The live disagreement lies in whether culture's influence is primarily direct, shaping institutions through norms and values, or indirect, operating through broader socio-economic dynamics. Understanding this debate is crucial for comprehending how cultural factors can be harnessed to promote economic development.

The role of culture as a direct antecedent to institutional formation is supported by evidence showing that cultural beliefs can shape collective action and social organization. For example, Putnam's theory of social capital posits that societies with high levels of trust, reciprocity norms, and horizontal social networks are more likely to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with the idea that cultural values can directly influence institutional outcomes by shaping collective behavior and facilitating coordinated actions. Moreover, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that historical events such as the slave trade have led to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical legacy highlights how past cultural experiences can shape current institutions and economic outcomes.

The indirect influence of culture through broader socio-economic dynamics is evident in studies that examine the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional formation. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that cultural factors can influence institutional outcomes by shaping power dynamics within societies. For instance, the Protestant Reformation led to the decline of guilds in medieval Europe, illustrating how changes in cultural beliefs can alter institutional structures through their impact on distributional conflicts. Furthermore, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This highlights how cultural factors can indirectly influence institutional outcomes by shaping broader socio-economic dynamics.

The relationship between culture and institutions is further complicated by the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. Ogilvie notes that studies recognizing this interdependency suggest that favorable contracting institutions may be partially dependent on favorable property rights institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency implies that cultural factors can influence institutional outcomes through their impact on multiple dimensions of socio-economic life, such as labor markets and credit markets. Moreover, North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This highlights how cultural factors can influence institutional outcomes by shaping broader economic conditions.

Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors can indirectly influence institutional outcomes through their impact on historical events and demographic changes. Furthermore, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This highlights how cultural factors can influence institutional outcomes by shaping power dynamics within societies.

North and Weingast share a view that institutions governing public choice played a critical role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England was pivotal to this development. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by providing a stable environment for both public and private debt markets. This mechanism underscores how secure property rights, underpinned by robust institutions, can drive economic expansion. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note that while other forces might have eventually led to England's success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without the institutional changes (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This suggests that cultural factors, which may influence the pace of institutional change, play a secondary role in long-run economic growth.

This institutional framework was not merely an efficient response to economic conditions but rather a product of power struggles among different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) similarly emphasize the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. They show that in New World societies, initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality, which then shaped institutions to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, demonstrating how institutional arrangements can be skewed by power dynamics rather than driven solely by efficiency concerns. This interdependency means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered to understand how these power dynamics influence long-run economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10); (Mokyr 2005, p.56) posit that ideas and knowledge were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective highlights how intellectual shifts could independently propel economic development, rather than being solely contingent on institutional frameworks. Ideas and ideologies played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes through the dissemination of useful knowledge, which was not confined to written texts but also encompassed tacit skills and embodied practices (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge was crucial for economic development, as it facilitated the diffusion of ideas across borders. This diffusion was enabled by various means such as periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, all of which contributed to the spread of intellectual movements (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Thus, the exchange of ideas not only fostered economic growth but also transformed societal norms and institutions over time. McCloskey's account illustrates how cultural shifts could lead to new ideologies that, in turn, influenced economic policies and practices. For instance, the "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries" like the Netherlands and France experienced significant economic growth due to the emergence of new rhetoric that supported innovation and entrepreneurship (McCloskey 2015, p.9).

The catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and institutional changes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This interplay of technological advancement and social change is evident in the historical patterns of economic development across various regions. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, driven by factors such as foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This growth was not uniform but varied between and within countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Abramovitz highlights that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology during the pre-World War I decades, suggesting that social competence for exploiting advanced methods was still limited (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This limitation implies that while technological advancements are crucial, the ability to implement and integrate these technologies into societal frameworks is equally important. However, Broadberry and Gardner's work shows that newly digitized sources of data have revealed a more nuanced history of economic divergence and convergence in Africa, challenging previous characterizations (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This suggests that our understanding of historical growth patterns can be refined with better data and analytical techniques, offering insights into how technological advancements and social changes interact to drive economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. assert that colonial institutions fundamentally shaped long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional changes introduced by European colonizers were instrumental in determining the economic trajectories of their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth; these institutions did not encourage economic development and were shaped by politically powerful groups who might obtain fewer rents with private property institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, high mortality rates among Europeans in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This effect is evident in the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). However, this mechanism does not imply that all institutions are inherently extractive; rather, it underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and power dynamics that shaped these institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa instilled a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust has been shown to be an important determinant of long-run economic development, as it affects social capital and interpersonal trust within societies. By reducing these types of trust, the legacy of slavery continues to impact current African economies by lowering productivity, reducing investment, and impeding cooperation. This political fragmentation has had long-lasting effects on economic development as it correlates with current levels of corruption, which can deter investment and further hinder growth. However, while Nunn (2008, p.27) identifies colonial boundaries as a proxy for historical conditions (Nunn 2008, p.1), this implies that the mistrust culture may be geographically bounded by these former colonial borders. This raises questions about whether the mistrust culture is uniformly distributed across Africa or if there are regional variations that could affect economic development differently in various parts of the continent.
