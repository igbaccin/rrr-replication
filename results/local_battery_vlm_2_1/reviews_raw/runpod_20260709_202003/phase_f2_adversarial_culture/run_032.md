The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development scholarship. At stake are competing explanations for why some societies thrive economically while others stagnate. One live disagreement centres around whether cultural beliefs and values fundamentally shape the institutional frameworks that drive economic growth, or if other factors such as geographic determinants, extractive institutions, or historical legacies of exploitation play a more decisive role. On one side, scholars argue that cultural norms influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting long-term economic performance. On the other side, researchers contend that institutional frameworks are shaped by exogenous factors like mortality rates in colonies, which then determine current income per capita (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This debate is crucial for understanding the deep-rooted mechanisms that either facilitate or hinder sustained economic development. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks thus represents a critical area of inquiry, one whose resolution could offer profound insights into the fundamental drivers of long-term economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They highlight that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutions created during this period provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets, further bolstering economic stability.

The implications of these institutional changes were profound. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) suggest that even if other forces might have eventually led to England's success under different political regimes, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. This underscores the necessity of institutions in facilitating rapid economic growth and political freedom. The evolution of these institutions actively shaped the environment for sustained development while responding to existing conditions (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These findings are supported by Jeffrey Williamson's work, which provides further evidence for the critical role of institutions in driving long-term economic success.

The institutional framework established during this period had far-reaching effects on England's economy. The secure property rights and reduced transaction costs fostered an environment conducive to investment and innovation. This institutional foundation laid the groundwork for England's industrial revolution and subsequent economic dominance, illustrating how specific institutional changes can have enduring impacts on a nation's economic trajectory. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to cultural factors, emphasizing instead the pivotal role of institutions in shaping long-term growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10)(Mokyr 2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies drove economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Ideas fostered a cultural shift that valued innovation, entrepreneurship, and the accumulation of useful knowledge. In this context, Mokyr highlights how the diffusion of practical knowledge across borders facilitated technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The circulation of ideas through publications, conferences, and personal networks enabled the rapid dissemination of innovations. This process involved a broader cultural embrace of new ideas which shaped economic behaviors and outcomes. For example, McCloskey describes how the "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries" like the Netherlands experienced significant growth due to their openness to new rhetoric and ideas (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr points out that much of this knowledge was tacit, relying on personal transmission and training. This implies that while ideas were crucial, their effective dissemination required social structures that facilitated learning and skill-sharing (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Therefore, the role of culture in fostering economic growth is deeply intertwined with the mechanisms through which ideas are generated, shared, and applied, highlighting how cultural factors can drive long-term economic development.

Acemoglu et al. assert that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These scholars argue that high mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persist today and significantly affect current income per capita. This mechanism is supported by a strong correlation between settler mortality and both early and contemporary institutional measures, demonstrating that colonial-era institutions have enduring impacts on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis highlights the importance of European settlement patterns in shaping these institutions, with high mortality areas more likely to develop extractive systems. This pattern suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had lasting effects on institutional development and economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. caution that these equilibrium institutions may maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implications of this finding are significant: it underscores how the legacy of colonial institutions can shape current economic outcomes, offering a perspective that challenges the primacy of culture in determining long-term economic development.

Ogilvie and other scholars (2007) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) highlight the role of economic institutions in European development, focusing on distributional conflicts and urbanization patterns. They argue that power struggles shaped these institutions. Ogilvie's research shows that guilds in pre-industrial Europe were influenced by non-economic cultural beliefs such as Catholic views on the afterlife (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that economic institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations, suggesting instead that cultural factors can significantly influence their structure and function. The interdependency of these institutions within a broader framework is crucial; for instance, Laslett's analysis of the European family system illustrates how it was interconnected with labor markets, credit systems, and welfare provisions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that economic growth in pre-industrial Europe was the outcome of complex interactions within an entire institutional framework. However, van Zanden (1999, p.2) cautions against overemphasizing the role of institutions alone in explaining economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.2). He argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, indicating that other factors, such as technological advancements or demographic shifts, may also have played significant roles. This nuanced view underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth and development, where cultural influences on institutions are just one piece of a larger puzzle.

The slave trade has had profound and enduring effects on African culture and economy, shaping long-term cultural attitudes such as mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that the slave trade contributed to a pervasive culture of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust persists today and significantly impacts economic development by undermining social capital and cooperation, which are essential for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical legacy of political instability resulting from the slave trades also plays a critical role in Africa's contemporary underdevelopment, as Nunn (2008, p.27) shows. This instability has led to weakened and fragmented states, which hinder effective governance and economic development. Moreover, the adverse effects of slavery on state centralization are linked to modern corruption, further impeding economic progress (Nunn 2008, p.1). The impact of the slave trade varies across Africa; contemporary factors such as malaria transmission, which has remained relatively stable over time, can also influence current conditions and thus moderate the long-term effects of historical events.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both emphasize that historical paths shape economic development through institutions over the long run, but they do not directly address how culture might influence these trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; it does not account for the dynamic, partly endogenous responses to environments (Austin 2008, p.25). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) point out that factor endowments initially led to high living standards but also created extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) . This inequality was reinforced through institutions that advantaged elites. Austin's critique of the 'simple geography' thesis suggests that Africans' responses to their environments, using available technologies, changed over time; however, these changes did not necessarily lead to economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was due to institutions that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework, which favored elites, highlights how cultural factors, such as social norms and power dynamics, can influence long-run growth through their impact on economic institutions. Austin's (2008, p.2) argument about the dynamic responses of Africans to their environments suggests that cultural adaptations could potentially mitigate or exacerbate these institutional effects over time. The role of culture in shaping institutions and, consequently, economic development remains an open question.

The reversal of fortune literature highlights how historical paths influence long-run growth through institutions and inequality. It does not account for the potential role of cultural factors in mediating these relationships.

Culture plays a critical role in shaping institutions that govern long-run economic growth, as evidenced by both direct and indirect mechanisms. Cultural beliefs influence the structure of economic institutions through collective action games, as shown by Richardson's analysis of medieval craftsmen's adherence to guild rules motivated by Catholic beliefs about the afterlife (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these cultural factors can be seen in Putnam’s theory of social capital, where civic cultures characterized by trust and reciprocity foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings underscore how cultural norms can either facilitate or hinder institutional development, thereby influencing long-run growth. The dynamic responses of Africans to their environments, as highlighted by Austin, further illustrate that cultural adaptations can mitigate or exacerbate the effects of institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interaction between culture and institutions is crucial for understanding the complex pathways through which historical paths influence economic development.

The role of culture in mediating institutional effects on growth is also evident in the reversal of fortune literature. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how cultural factors can mediate the relationship between historical paths and long-run growth by shaping the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity. North and Weingast emphasize the importance of secure property rights and stable political institutions in fostering economic growth, demonstrating that cultural beliefs about governance and trust can influence these foundational elements (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These findings are supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and provide the necessary framework for long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of culture in shaping institutions lies in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. Ogilvie argues that the European family system, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency shows how cultural factors can influence multiple aspects of institutional development simultaneously, providing a comprehensive understanding of their role in long-run growth.
