The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious topic among scholars. The stakes are high because the answer shapes our understanding of economic development and the potential for policy intervention to foster growth. Some researchers argue that cultural beliefs influence the structure of economic institutions, which in turn affects economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others contend that cultural factors are secondary to institutional frameworks, which are shaped by broader historical and geographical forces (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The disagreement lies between those who see culture as a primary driver of institutional change and those who view it as a secondary effect. This debate is crucial because it determines whether long-run growth can be attributed to cultural factors or if other mechanisms, such as institutions themselves, are more significant.

She critiques the idea that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, suggesting this explanation oversimplifies the complex interplay between culture and economics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also acknowledges Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that cultural values like trust and reciprocity can improve societal efficiency by facilitating coordinated actions. This view aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon’s findings, which show how historical events such as the slave trade have shaped contemporary cultural norms, including trust. The persistence of these cultural factors underscores their role in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

Ogilvie further discusses the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework, noting that studies often overlook this complexity. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights how Laslett and Solar's work on European family systems and community-based welfare underscores the need to examine entire institutional frameworks. This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize the high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies, European settlements, early measures of institutions, and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Both streams highlight that understanding the role of culture in economic growth requires a nuanced view of how cultural beliefs interact with broader institutional frameworks.

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They contend that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered these secure property rights. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework was pivotal for both economic growth and political freedom as it created positive externalities in the market. For example, the institutions that facilitated a stable market for public debt also supported the development of private debt markets. The implications are significant: even if other forces might have eventually led England to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without these institutional developments (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North acknowledges that while institutions matter for economic growth, their specific nature varies across different contexts.

Acemoglu et al. and Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions are fundamental drivers of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They establish a clear mechanism linking European mortality rates to institutional development in colonies. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions is crucial because they shaped economic outcomes by determining who held political power and what incentives were in place for investment and innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, illustrating how initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to influence economic trajectories today. This highlights a critical downstream implication: while colonial institutions were initially extractive, their persistence and impact on current income per capita underscore the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks.

Idea shifts were the primary drivers of economic change in Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that intellectual movements were pivotal in fostering economic growth. This perspective underscores the role of cultural shifts in driving economic change; ideas that gain traction can transform societal attitudes towards commerce, innovation, and productivity. The 'Great Enrichment' of Europe was an outcome of new rhetoric and ideologies as well as better institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr (2005, p.56) highlights the importance of tacit knowledge, skills and practices embedded in society, and its diffusion through personal communication, training, and international networks. This tacit knowledge, often uncodified, played a crucial role in economic growth by enabling technological advancements and innovative practices (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that technology and costs associated with dissemination influenced the accessibility of codified knowledge, suggesting that while ideas drive growth, their transmission is contingent on material conditions. This implies that cultural shifts, though powerful, require supportive infrastructure to fully realize their economic potential. The interplay between ideas and their material conditions thus shapes long-run economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.2); (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) re-evaluate historical economic theories by emphasizing distributional conflicts over efficiency in explaining pre-industrial European institutions. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin challenges the idea that institutional changes are driven solely by exogenous factors, suggesting instead a more dynamic, partly endogenous process where local responses to environmental conditions play a significant role (Austin 2008, p.25). This implies that cultural beliefs and values, which shape how individuals respond to their environments, can influence the structure of economic institutions. This efficiency results from complex interactions between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks. Ogilvie further elaborates that these institutions are interdependent with other aspects of society such as labour markets, credit markets, and family systems, forming an intricate web that influences economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The role of culture in dominating institutions is intertwined with broader societal structures, suggesting that cultural shifts must align with supportive infrastructure to fully realize their economic potential.

Sokoloff and Engerman's work alongside Broadberry and Gupta (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) examines how the interplay between economic inequality and institutional factors shaped development paths in different regions. The authors identify that societies characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power often developed institutions that advantaged elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in colonial New World societies where the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor initially led to high living standards but also fostered extreme inequality from the outset. These institutional frameworks, which favored elite interests, hindered broader economic growth by limiting opportunities for the majority of the population.

A downstream implication of this pattern emerges when considering the early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia. Before 1800, regions in both continents exhibited similar levels of grain wages but divergent silver wages, reflecting differences in economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This disparity suggests that institutional factors, influenced by pre-existing inequalities, played a crucial role in shaping these divergent paths. Broadberry and Gupta highlight that the prosperity of certain parts of Asia, such as India and China, stemmed from significant investments and high agricultural productivity as well as geographical advantages (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuance underscores that while inequality and institutional factors are pivotal, they interact with other variables like investment and productivity in complex ways. The interplay between economic inequality, institutional frameworks, and development trajectories is intricate and multifaceted, suggesting that cultural shifts must align with supportive infrastructure to fully realize their economic potential.

The slave trade's impact on African development underscores how historical events can shape cultural attitudes such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade instilled a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust persists today and significantly contributes to the reduced levels of trust observed in contemporary Africa, accounting for at least half of the negative effect of the slave trade on current-day trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates that this legacy of mistrust is deeply intertwined with political instability and weakened state structures. This political fragmentation has had profound implications for economic development, as empirical research indicates that a country's history of state development is closely linked to its current economic performance (Bockstette et al. 2002; Chanda & Putterman 2005). However, the relationship between mistrust and economic outcomes is not straightforward: Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that slavery also contributed to modern corruption, suggesting that the cultural legacy of the slave trade extends beyond political instability to encompass broader institutional weaknesses (Nunn 2008, p.1). This highlights how historical events can have multifaceted and enduring impacts on contemporary societal structures.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is fundamental to understanding long-run growth. Cultural beliefs and values influence economic performance by fostering specific institutional frameworks that can either promote or hinder growth. This relationship is evident in the historical context of pre-industrial Europe, where cultural factors played a significant role in shaping core economic institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions were shaped by underlying cultural beliefs as well as economic conditions. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the decline of Catholic beliefs about the afterlife during the Protestant Reformation led to changes in guild structures, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence institutional frameworks and economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This dynamic is further supported by North's analysis, which highlights that institutions matter due to transaction costs arising from positive transaction costs; thus, institutions are crucial for economic growth as they facilitate the specialization and division of labor necessary for technological change and improved productivity (North 1989, p.1).

This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These findings indicate that historical events can have enduring impacts on contemporary societal structures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who show how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This underscores the importance of credible commitment in fostering growth: institutional frameworks that ensure secure property rights are essential for long-term economic development.

This can be addressed by measuring the impact of cultural beliefs on institutional frameworks and economic efficiency using historical data from different regions and time periods.
