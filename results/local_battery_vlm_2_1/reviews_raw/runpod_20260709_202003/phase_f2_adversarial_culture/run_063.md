The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is understanding whether cultural factors are essential drivers that shape economic growth through their influence on institutional frameworks. Two prominent positions exist: one argues that cultural beliefs and values significantly impact the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby influencing economic performance; the other posits that extractive institutions, driven by exogenous factors such as mortality rates, are the primary determinants of long-run growth. The disagreement centers around whether culture operates through institutional frameworks to drive growth or if other factors, independent of cultural influences, dominate this process.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a more compelling explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). She contends that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, influenced the structure of economic institutions like guilds. This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that cultural values like trust and reciprocity facilitate coordinated actions that enhance economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North and Weingast also emphasize the importance of institutions in economic growth, asserting that secure property rights are critical for long-term capital markets and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.9). They argue that the financial revolution in England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, was instrumental in fostering economic growth. This financial revolution is seen as a product of institutional changes that enhanced the time dimension of property rights, reflecting broader political and economic developments (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The significance of these institutions lies in their ability to create a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of private debt markets.

Institutional frameworks are not isolated entities but are interdependent with other systems. Ogilvie highlights how the European family system was interconnected with labor and credit markets, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying individual institutions in isolation may overlook their broader impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. support this idea by showing a high correlation between settler mortality rates, European settlements, and the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their findings underscore that differences in European mortality rates led to the creation of these institutions, highlighting the exogenous factors at play. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a multifaceted issue that involves complex interactions between cultural beliefs, institutional frameworks, and exogenous factors. Understanding these dynamics requires a nuanced approach that recognizes the interdependency of various systems and the influence of both cultural values and external conditions on economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that gender-based colonial codifications significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes by shaping gender roles within African societies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). These codifications established a sexual division of labor that persisted beyond the colonial period, affecting economic productivity and development. For example, in Asante society, women were crucial for community reproduction due to their matrilineal bloodlines, which defined wealth transmission (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This role highlights how cultural constructs can embed long-term economic patterns. Goldin's analysis of U.S. women's employment and education shifts underscores that exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions altered female labor supply and occupational dynamics (Goldin 2006, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts, along with technological advancements in household production and institutional changes promoting part-time work, illustrate how cultural norms and economic opportunities interact to drive long-term growth. However, Goldin also notes that these changes were not linear but rather phased, highlighting the complexity of cultural influences on economic development. This nuanced interaction between cultural beliefs and exogenous factors underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted role of culture in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and van Zanden’s examination of economic development in Europe (Austin 2008, p.2; van Zanden 1999, p.2) highlight that historical patterns of economic growth are complex and multifaceted. Austin argues that the ‘reversal of fortune’ thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while van Zanden shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. These perspectives underscore how difficult it is to attribute long-term growth solely to institutional factors without considering the dynamic interplay of endogenous responses and environmental conditions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further emphasize this complexity by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, suggesting a nuanced relationship between economic development and institutional frameworks (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This highlights how institutions evolve over time in response to various factors, making it challenging to isolate culture as a single determinant of long-run growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) together show that specific technological changes can drive structural economic shifts, but these studies do not directly engage with how culture influences institutions to shape long-run growth. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to protect property rights and reduce livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). These studies highlight how specific technological and structural changes can reshape economic landscapes. However, the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth remains unaddressed.

These structural changes also influenced consumption patterns and labor markets. De Vries (1994, p.18) argues that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption habits, preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.18). This suggests that shifts in economic behavior and technology can drive long-term economic growth. de Vries also notes that these changes varied across Europe, indicating regional variations in institutional frameworks and cultural contexts that influenced how these structural changes played out (de Vries 1994, p.5). Thus, while technological and structural changes are pivotal, the specific mechanisms by which they interact with culture to shape institutions over the long run remain an open question.

Institutional variables play a pivotal role in explaining economic development in Africa, where institutional change is gradual and rooted in historical contexts that extend beyond colonial rule (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the significance of congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions. When these institutions align, they enhance government legitimacy and foster growth-inducing policies. Conversely, low levels of congruence can lead to patrimonial politics, regionalism, centelism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This dynamic is evident in Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) analysis of export production patterns, which were tightly linked to foreign trade and political institutions.

When considering the role of culture in shaping long-run growth, it becomes crucial to recognize that the effectiveness of institutions can be significantly influenced by their alignment with local cultural norms. This is particularly relevant for African economies, where historical institutional frameworks have been shaped by both precolonial and colonial influences. However, this perspective does not directly address how culture itself influences long-run growth in Africa beyond its interaction with institutional variables; it remains an open question whether cultural factors independently drive economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run economic development. These authors posit that European mortality rates in colonies determined whether settlers established extractive institutions, which in turn shaped current income per capita. The mechanism involves differential settlement patterns: high European mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework persisted over time, influencing long-run economic performance. This suggests that while colonial institutions are a critical factor, their impact on economic development can be mediated by domestic political dynamics. The role of culture in shaping these institutions remains an open question, as the focus here is primarily on the exogenous impact of colonial policies.

The slave trades in Africa have had profound adverse effects on economic development, leading to long-term political instability and weakened institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust. This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, which is itself a significant factor in economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development by causing long-term political instability. The persistence of such cultural factors as mistrust suggests a deep-seated impact on institutions that goes beyond the immediate effects of colonialism or more recent events. However, Nunn (2008, p.27) qualifies this by noting that slavery may also be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). This highlights the complex interplay between historical factors and contemporary economic outcomes.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie shows how these conflicts shaped key institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. These institutions arose not from cultural factors or efficiency concerns but from power struggles between different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing that extreme inequality led to institutional arrangements that advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This suggests a pattern where distributional conflicts, rather than cultural factors, are the underlying cause of institutional frameworks that persist over time.

Ogilvie notes that these institutions were interdependent within broader frameworks. For example, the European family system was linked to labor markets and credit markets, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interplay between institutions highlights how distributional conflicts can shape entire economic systems. However, Ogilvie also acknowledges that studying one institution in isolation may miss this interconnectedness (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. The institutions underpinning this financial revolution provided a large positive externality, facilitating economic growth and political freedom. The interplay between secure property rights and financial innovation highlights how institutional frameworks can drive long-run economic growth by fostering a stable environment for investment and development.

Acemoglu et al.'s work also underscores that these effects are part of a broader framework where multiple institutions interact to influence economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
