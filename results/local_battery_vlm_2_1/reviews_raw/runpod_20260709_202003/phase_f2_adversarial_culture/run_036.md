The role of culture in shaping long-run growth through dominating institutions is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding how deeply embedded societal values and norms influence economic trajectories over extended periods. One side of the debate posits that cultural beliefs directly shape institutions, which in turn drive economic growth by fostering efficiency and trust. This perspective asserts that certain cultures encourage institutional frameworks that promote economic development through mechanisms such as social capital and collective action. On the other hand, some scholars argue that long-run growth is primarily driven by exogenous factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns, which shape institutions independently of cultural influences. These competing views highlight the need to disentangle the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic outcomes. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending historical economic trajectories and informing contemporary policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable growth. The debate hinges on whether cultural norms are a fundamental driver of institutional change or merely an ancillary factor in a broader causal chain. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter in economic growth because gains from trade are key to wealth. The development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9) . The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). They note, "Because the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success" (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), the implication is that institutions were necessary for economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their impact can be influenced by exogenous factors such as mortality rates.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) share the view that economic development is a dynamic process shaped by endogenous factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al.'s formulations. This suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of economic development. The mechanism through which this occurs is the adoption of institutions that are favorable for growth in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2). One specific pattern that emerges from this view is the role of African responses to their environments and technologies available at different times (Austin 2008, p.25). However, Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups rather than designed for long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional adoption and its impact on economic development, suggesting that while endogenous factors play a role, the political dynamics of institution-building cannot be overlooked.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Distributional conflicts shape the institutional landscape through their impact on political power dynamics; this process is evident in how elites, influenced by these conflicts, design institutions to maintain their advantage. This dynamic is seen in the development of New World societies, where institutions evolved to favour wealthy and powerful groups who had access to abundant land and natural resources relative to labor (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the institutional frameworks that were established early on; these frameworks restricted competition and consolidated elite power. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the interdependency of these institutions within a wider framework can complicate their impact on economic growth. For instance, the European family system's interdependence with labour and credit markets suggests that studying one institution in isolation may not fully capture its effects. This complexity underscores the need to consider entire institutional frameworks when examining the role of distributional conflicts in shaping long-run growth.

This period saw a convergence of cultural dynamics that facilitated the emergence of modern economic institutions. This perspective challenges the notion that material interests alone can explain long-run economic development.

McCloskey contends that the "Great Enrichment" was not merely a result of institutional changes driven by elite interests, but rather a consequence of a cultural shift that valued commercial activity and technological progress (McCloskey 2015, p.10). The emergence of new ideas about individual rights, property protection, and scientific inquiry created an environment conducive to economic growth. This cultural transformation was not a sudden event but a gradual process shaped by historical contingencies and the interplay between material conditions and ideological changes. Bryant highlights that this transition was enabled by prior developments in technology, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms (Bryant 2006, p.35). The revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent.

The role of ideas and ideologies in economic change is complex. Bryant notes that the European passage to modernity was facilitated by a complex interplay of factors, including civic urbanization, legal developments, technological advancements, and demographic transitions (Bryant 2006, p.4). These factors collectively contributed to a unique cultural milieu that supported economic growth. This suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, they operated within a broader framework of institutional and material conditions. The downstream implication is that the impact of culture on long-run growth is mediated by these interdependent factors. McCloskey's argument about the "Franco-French War" illustrates how cultural struggles can both facilitate and hinder economic development (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This dynamic underscores the need to consider the complex interplay between ideas, institutions, and material conditions when examining the role of culture in long-run growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). This role is crucial for understanding the broader cultural and institutional factors at play. Women's contributions were pivotal in maintaining community structures, particularly in matrilineal societies where their bloodlines defined wealth transmission; this underscores how gender roles intersect with economic development in complex ways (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6; Goldin 2006, p.8). The interplay between colonial constructs and pre-existing social norms shaped long-term dynamics of gender relations. These findings highlight that while cultural factors, such as gender roles, are integral to economic development, they operate within a broader framework of institutional and material conditions. The downstream implication is that the impact of culture on long-run growth is mediated by these interdependent factors.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) (Nunn 2008, p.27) establish that historical events, such as the slave trade, can shape cultural attitudes like mistrust. The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust, which persists today and influences long-term economic outcomes. This mistrust is a direct result of the political instability and weakened states caused by the slave trades; Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave exports were negatively related to state centralization. The cultural legacy of mistrust has far-reaching implications for contemporary Africa, as it affects social capital within communities and economic development. This is because trust is a key factor in fostering cooperation and economic growth. The downstream implication is that historical events can have long-lasting effects on cultural norms, which in turn shape economic outcomes. However, Nunn (2008, p.1) highlights the complexity of this relationship by noting that Africa's history of slavery may also be linked to contemporary corruption, suggesting that the impact of the slave trade extends beyond cultural attitudes and into institutional frameworks. This interplay between culture and institutions underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks is evident in how historical events have shaped contemporary economic outcomes. Trust is a key factor in fostering cooperation and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This trust underpins the development of institutions that facilitate coordinated actions, as seen in Putnam's theory of social capital, which highlights the importance of norms, networks, and reciprocity in enhancing societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evolution of these cultural beliefs and values has been instrumental in creating institutional environments conducive to economic growth. For instance, the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This historical evidence underscores how cultural norms can shape institutions that foster economic development.

The impact of culture on institutional frameworks is further illustrated by the role of distributional conflicts in shaping pre-industrial European economic institutions. Serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were deeply influenced by underlying cultural beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of these institutions within a broader framework suggests that cultural norms can influence the structure and equilibrium of collective action games, thereby affecting economic efficiency. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence from both pre-industrial Europe and colonial contexts shows that cultural norms and beliefs can shape institutional frameworks in ways that have long-lasting effects on economic growth.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is also evident in the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success by providing secure property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical context highlights how cultural norms can influence the development of institutions that support economic prosperity. Furthermore, the significance of cultural beliefs is underscored by the contribution of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production, reflecting the enduring impact of cultural norms on economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from Africa shows that cultural beliefs can shape institutional frameworks in ways that have significant implications for economic growth.

In sum, culture is a fundamental factor in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. This perspective is grounded in the shared finding that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which cultural norms can be influenced by exogenous factors such as mortality rates, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). This debate is settled by historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe and colonial contexts, which shows that cultural beliefs and values have a profound impact on institutional frameworks and economic growth.
