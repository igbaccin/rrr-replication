The role of culture in shaping institutions is central to understanding long-run economic growth, as it influences both the development and effectiveness of these institutions over time. The live disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional change or if they operate through other mechanisms such as efficiency gains or distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions by shaping collective action games and promoting efficiency, while others contend that cultural factors are better understood as part of a broader framework of interdependent institutions. This debate is crucial because it determines whether policy interventions should focus on changing cultural norms directly or addressing institutional frameworks more broadly. Institutions, in turn, govern the incentives and constraints under which economic actors operate, thereby shaping long-term growth trajectories. The interplay between culture and institutions highlights the need to understand how deeply ingrained beliefs and values can either facilitate or hinder economic development through their impact on institutional design and effectiveness. This leads into a consideration of cultural explanations for the persistence of certain institutions over time.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a compelling explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She criticizes the notion that these institutions were driven by efficiency considerations and instead suggests they were shaped by power dynamics. This perspective is complemented by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they determine the costs of transacting, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). North emphasizes that institutions reflect increased security of property rights over time, a dimension crucial to both financial stability and economic growth. This highlights how cultural beliefs can influence institutional frameworks by shaping the incentives and constraints under which economic actors operate, thereby impacting long-term growth trajectories.

The role of culture in shaping institutions extends beyond immediate efficiency considerations. Ogilvie notes that interdependencies among individual institutions within a broader framework are recognized as critical (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These developments underscored the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This period saw the development of a stable market for public debt, which provided a positive externality for the parallel development of a private debt market (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Thus, cultural beliefs can shape institutional frameworks by influencing the incentives and constraints under which economic actors operate, thereby impacting long-term growth trajectories.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further complicated by the interplay between different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The impact of these institutions on economic performance highlights how cultural factors can influence institutional frameworks by shaping the incentives and constraints under which economic actors operate, thereby impacting long-term growth trajectories. Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions underscores how deeply ingrained beliefs and values can either facilitate or hinder economic development through their impact on institutional design and effectiveness.

Akyeampong and Fofack 2014 and Goldin 2006 illustrate how deeply ingrained cultural norms can shape gender relations to influence long-run economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). The role of women in economic growth is particularly evident in the transformation of gender dynamics within societies. Akyeampong and Fofack describe a scenario where African women, despite colonial constructs that marginalized their contributions, remained indispensable to community reproduction in matrilineal societies such as Asante (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic underscores how deeply ingrained cultural beliefs about gender roles can shape economic productivity. Goldin's analysis of U.S. women's employment and education reveals a similar pattern: shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career roles coincided with significant changes in their educational attainment and occupational choices (Goldin 2006, p.8). These transformations were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions, which altered both supply and demand dynamics in the labor market (Goldin 2006, p.3).

The interplay between cultural norms and economic outcomes is further complicated by the persistence of these norms over time. This persistence highlights how historical gender dynamics can continue to influence contemporary economic structures. Goldin's work emphasizes the importance of preconditions such as changes in household technology and institutional barriers, which facilitated women's entry into the labor market (Goldin 2006, p.3). These preconditions are crucial for understanding the broader implications of gender dynamics on economic growth, as they illustrate how cultural shifts can create enabling environments for women's economic participation. The role of women in economic growth thus underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping long-run development trajectories, illustrating how gender norms can either facilitate or hinder economic progress through their impact on institutional frameworks and labor market dynamics.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon assert that long-term effects of the slave trade encompass both economic underdevelopment and deep-seated cultural shifts, such as reduced social cohesion and lower levels of trust. This mistrust has profound implications for institutional development, as it hinders the establishment of effective governance structures and public institutions. For instance, Nunn shows that the historical presence of slave trades is linked to contemporary political instability and weakened state centralization. The legacy of slavery thus manifests in modern-day corruption and weak governance, which are significant barriers to economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27). Recent empirical research has found that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. This suggests that the cultural impact of the slave trade, through its effect on institutional quality, continues to shape Africa’s long-run development trajectory. The persistence of mistrust and weakened institutions underscores how historical events can have enduring effects on contemporary economic outcomes, illustrating the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and growth.

This perspective highlights the importance of cultural shifts and intellectual movements in facilitating growth, rather than focusing solely on institutional frameworks or elite interests. McCloskey emphasizes that the "Great Enrichment" was catalyzed by a new rhetoric that fostered innovation and economic progress. Mokyr complements this view by asserting that the diffusion of useful knowledge was central to economic development during this period. This knowledge, both codified and tacit, circulated widely through various channels such as periodicals, translations, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The widespread dissemination of ideas and skills underscores how cultural factors can enable economic growth by fostering an environment conducive to innovation and learning.

The role of culture in this context is not merely about the transmission of knowledge but also about the creation of a societal ethos that values progress and entrepreneurship. This cultural shift, as McCloskey points out, was instrumental in countries like the Netherlands and Britain, where bourgeois values led to significant economic advancements (McCloskey 2015, p.9). The interplay between cultural attitudes and economic outcomes is further illustrated by Mokyr's observation that tacit knowledge, which is often embedded in skills and practices, played a crucial role. This form of knowledge, while difficult to codify, was essential for technological advancements and industrial progress. However, the effective dissemination of this knowledge required not just technical means but also a societal framework that valued and encouraged its spread.

The cultural factors that facilitated economic growth were not uniformly distributed across Europe. This disparity highlights how cultural norms can either accelerate or impede economic progress, depending on whether they foster an environment of innovation and entrepreneurship or one of stagnation and resistance to change. The enduring impact of these cultural factors suggests that the ideas and ideologies that drive economic growth are deeply rooted in societal values and attitudes.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice is critical: it provided a large positive externality for the development of private debt markets, which further stimulated economic activity. The security of property rights over time is crucial to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself; the financial revolution of seventeenth-century England, including the creation of the Bank of England, exemplifies this dynamic. Institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were necessary for sustained economic growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize short-term rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth. These institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) found that there is a high correlation between settler mortality rates and the type of institutions established in colonies, which in turn affects current income per capita. The persistence of these extractive institutions has significant implications for understanding economic development trajectories among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) observe that these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional change (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the design of equilibrium institutions in former colonies was likely to maximize the rents to European colonists. The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and the mechanisms through which these institutions have influenced long-term development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) examine how distributional conflicts and inequality shape economic institutions rather than focusing on culture as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. Their argument is rooted in the idea that institutions are not merely a product of efficiency but also a result of power dynamics and distributional struggles within societies. Distributional conflicts, where powerful groups seek to maintain their advantages, can lead to institutional frameworks that perpetuate inequality. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where guilds and serfdom were maintained despite their inefficiencies because they served the interests of powerful elites (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This pattern is further complicated by the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework. For instance, the European family system and community-based welfare systems were interconnected with labor and credit markets, influencing economic growth in pre-industrial England (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). However, Sokoloff and Engerman note that while conventional economic factors are important, differences in development are primarily explained by variations in institutions, which in turn are shaped by underlying distributional conflicts. These institutional differences have persistent effects on long-term development trajectories, highlighting the enduring impact of inequality on economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) along with Pamuk (2007, p.24) examine economic performance and structural changes in Europe and Africa, focusing on factors such as foreign trade, export production, and institutional flexibility. They show that the growth of export markets was closely tied to the success of African economies and European regions alike; however, this relationship was not uniform across all countries or time periods. This variability highlights that while foreign trade can be a significant driver of economic development, its impact is contingent on specific local conditions. This suggests that exogenous shocks can induce institutional adaptations that foster economic growth. These structural changes do not directly address how culture influences long-run growth; they point to the role of external factors and institutional responses in shaping economic trajectories.

The Industrious Revolution and agricultural development were pivotal historical processes that laid the groundwork for the Industrial Revolution, with de Vries (1994, p.18) arguing that this period saw significant increases in labor input and new consumption patterns (de Vries 1994, p.18). Hornbeck (2010, p.6) shows that enhanced property rights through barbed wire fencing improved agricultural development on the American Plains by reducing livestock damage and enabling better resource management, illustrating how institutional changes can foster economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This interplay between cultural shifts in labor practices and institutional adaptations in property rights underscores a broader pattern where cultural norms influence the effectiveness of institutions. However, while these studies highlight the role of cultural attitudes towards labor and property, they do not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. The focus on specific mechanisms such as barbed wire fencing or increased labor input does not fully capture the broader cultural dynamics that might underlie institutional changes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They highlight how the institutional evolution in Europe, particularly the political delivery of secure private property rights, played a crucial role in long-term economic growth. This institutionalist approach faces challenges in explaining why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not others. This suggests that cultural factors may have influenced which institutions were adopted and how effectively they functioned. This historical perspective underscores that cultural attitudes towards investment, technology adoption, and labor practices have been pivotal in shaping institutional outcomes. However, Austin (2008, p.2) points out that African responses to environmental challenges, including technological adaptations, also played a significant role in economic development, indicating that endogenous factors influenced by local cultures were at work (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions suggests that cultural norms not only shape institutional frameworks but also determine their effectiveness over the long run. The historical paths of different regions reveal that cultural attitudes towards investment and technological adoption are critical in understanding how institutions contribute to economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.24) and van Zanden (1999, p.2; 2009, p.24); (van Zanden 1999, p.2) together argue that the skill premium is a critical factor in understanding economic performance over the long run. This perspective aligns with new growth theory, which identifies human capital as a key determinant of long-term economic development. The implication is that regions with higher skill premiums are likely to experience greater economic growth due to enhanced productivity and innovation driven by skilled labor (van Zanden 2009, p.24). Van Zanden (1999, p.2) complicates this narrative by highlighting the complex relationship between economic development and changes in living standards across Europe. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) argues that there is no straightforward correlation between GDP per capita growth and improvements in the standard of living from 1500 to 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This nuance suggests that while skill premiums may drive economic growth, the translation of this growth into tangible benefits for the population is not automatic. Instead, it depends on various factors including the distribution of wealth and the effectiveness of institutions in channeling economic gains towards broader societal improvements.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is evident through how cultural norms and beliefs influence institutional frameworks over time. Ogilvie shows that cultural values can directly affect economic institutions, as seen in the impact of Catholic beliefs on medieval guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is further supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they facilitate gains from trade and technological change, which are key underlying features of modern economies (North 1989, p.9). The interplay between culture and institutions highlights how cultural attitudes towards investment and technological adoption are critical in understanding how institutions contribute to economic growth. For example, Putnam argues that 'civic' cultures encourage institutions that favour economic growth and political accountability, explaining the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast underscore this point by demonstrating how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The significance of cultural norms is further illustrated through the lens of social capital, where trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that improve societal efficiency. This perspective is shared by North's assertion that institutions play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The historical evidence from northern Italy supports this view, showing how cultural beliefs and values associated with strong guilds and communes explain superior economic performance over centuries (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This continuity highlights the enduring influence of culture on institutional effectiveness. Austin reinforces this perspective by noting that economists see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2).

The measurement of institutions' impact on economic performance is clear when examining the historical record. Acemoglu et al. show a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting how differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This dynamic underscores how cultural attitudes towards investment and technological adoption are critical in understanding how institutions contribute to economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which cultural norms determine institutional effectiveness. Austin's analysis suggests that African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). This is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack’s finding that women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The historical evidence from northern Italy and the African context shows how cultural norms can either enhance or hinder institutional effectiveness.
