The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within economic and historical scholarship. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional development, or if they are secondary to other determinants such as geography, technology, or political structures. One live disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting efficiency and growth. Another debate revolves around the extent to which institutions themselves are shaped by exogenous cultural theories versus endogenous processes driven by economic and political forces.

One perspective posits that culture plays a direct role in shaping institutions through mechanisms such as social capital and norms of trust and reciprocity. This view suggests that cultural beliefs can influence collective action and institutional design, ultimately impacting economic performance. For example, Putnam's theory of social capital emphasizes how 'civic' cultures characterized by trust and horizontal social networks foster institutions conducive to growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon highlight the enduring effects of historical cultural shocks, such as the slave trade, on contemporary levels of mistrust within societies. Their findings suggest that cultural legacies can persist and significantly influence economic outcomes by shaping institutional environments (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

However, another perspective argues for a more dynamic interaction between culture and institutions, where the latter are not merely products of cultural beliefs but also shape cultural norms over time. This view underscores the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may oversimplify complex historical processes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, North's work on the English financial revolution illustrates how institutional changes, such as the creation of the Bank of England, led to significant reductions in transaction costs and enhanced property rights security. These institutional developments were crucial for fostering economic growth and political stability, demonstrating how institutions can drive long-term economic performance (North 1989, p.9).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the role of exogenous factors such as mortality rates and settler patterns. Acemoglu et al.'s research on European colonies shows that differences in mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions, which in turn affected long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights how initial conditions can shape institutional trajectories, suggesting that cultural factors may interact with exogenous variables to determine institutional development.

The debate surrounding the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is far from settled. While some scholars emphasize the direct influence of cultural beliefs on institutional design and economic performance, others point to the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and exogenous factors. Understanding this dynamic interaction is crucial for comprehending the underlying mechanisms that drive long-term economic development.

Ideas and ideologies were critical in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey (2015, p.10) argues that the cultural struggle for new ideas and ideologies was pivotal in shaping economic growth. This is evident in the shift from traditional, aristocratic values to more bourgeois, entrepreneurial ones: these new ideas fostered a rhetoric that legitimized profit-making activities. The spread of these ideas was not merely a matter of accumulated information combined with interest (McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.56) expands on this by highlighting the role of useful knowledge in economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of tacit and codified knowledge facilitated the Great Enrichment. Mokyr also observes that access to such knowledge depended on written records as well as personal transmission and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This underscores the importance of social networks and institutions in disseminating ideas that could drive economic change. The interplay between cultural shifts and the diffusion of useful knowledge thus created a feedback loop where new ideas generated new circumstances, which in turn fostered further ideological changes. Cultural factors were not just passive reflections of economic conditions; they actively shaped the institutional environment that enabled long-run growth.

However, the significance of these institutions is not universally accepted, as North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30), along with other scholars who share this view (e.g., North 1989), argue that they were crucial for fostering secure property rights. These institutions created a stable environment where economic growth could flourish, as they reduced transaction costs and provided security for long-term investments (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for example, was pivotal in this transformation; it facilitated both public and private debt markets by providing a secure platform for financial transactions. This development underscored how institutions could create large positive externalities that fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). It is important to note that the institutional changes occurred alongside broader societal shifts. While North and Weingast emphasize the role of institutions, other scholars highlight that these institutional developments were contingent on cultural and ideological factors that legitimized and supported them.

Acemoglu et al. show that European colonial settlers established extractive institutions in colonies where they faced high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were designed to maximize the rents accruing to the colonists rather than promote long-run growth, which is a key feature of Acemoglu et al.'s theory of how colonialism shaped economic development. This pattern suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions, shaped by initial settler mortality rates, persists and significantly influences current economic outcomes. They also report that extractive institutions hinder economic development because they are shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritize immediate rents over long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights how the initial conditions set by colonial powers, particularly the mortality rates faced by settlers, played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions that were established. These institutions, in turn, have had lasting effects on economic development, illustrating how historical factors can shape long-run growth trajectories through institutional pathways.

The slave trade had a direct adverse effect on African economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is argued to have persisted over time and continues to hinder contemporary economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused mistrust, which accounts for at least half of its reduced-form effect on trust in Africa today. Nunn argues that it led to long-term political instability and weakened states, which are crucial determinants of current economic performance. This suggests that the historical legacy of the slave trade continues to influence Africa's underdevelopment through enduring cultural and institutional factors. The contemporary effects of this mistrust are further compounded by the prevalence of corruption in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.1). The slave trade may have been one source for precolonial origins of modern corruption, which is itself a significant barrier to economic growth. This highlights how historical factors can shape long-run growth trajectories through both cultural and institutional pathways.

This early divergence was driven by fundamental differences in economic development and institutional change across regions. In northwestern Europe, institutional changes spurred investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This productivity did not translate into lower food prices because labor shifted out of agriculture into industry and services. The Black Death played a pivotal role in this process by initiating long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These changes included declining interest rates, increased mobility, greater flexibility within guilds, and labor-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). One downstream implication of these institutional shifts was a significant increase in economic development in northwestern Europe. This suggests that while the Black Death was a catalyst for economic divergence, it also facilitated structural adjustments that supported long-term growth. The early onset of these economic changes underscores the enduring impact of historical events on long-run economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional explanations for economic growth by arguing that they oversimplify causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutions-first approach neglects the dynamic interplay of endogenous factors, such as distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses distinct historical periods and paths into a singular narrative (Austin 2008, p.25). This oversimplification obscures the complex interplay between institutions and other factors driving economic growth. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) points out that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic interplay is further complicated by cultural beliefs, which can influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency. Austin's critique suggests that the institutional approach must account for these endogenous factors to provide a comprehensive explanation of long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by noting that while cultural beliefs can influence institutions, they are not the sole determinant; the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework also plays a crucial role (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Thus, any explanation of economic growth must consider the complex interplay between institutions, distributional conflicts, and cultural beliefs.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted; it involves understanding how cultural beliefs interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes. Cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This suggests that while cultural beliefs can shape institutions, other factors such as mortality rates also play a crucial role.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He contends that economic growth is determined by cultural beliefs as well as institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience (Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further emphasize the importance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth; they argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for this outcome. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, showing how institutions were necessary for making possible economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This shared finding shows that cultural beliefs interact with broader institutional frameworks to influence long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) provide evidence of the historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as the slave trade causing a culture of mistrust within Africa. This culture persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reflect increased security of property rights, which are critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting the importance of secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). This shows that while cultural beliefs can influence institutions, the security of property rights is also a fundamental determinant of long-run growth.
