The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with some arguing that cultural factors are crucial while others propose rival explanations. The live disagreement revolves around whether cultural beliefs and values fundamentally shape economic institutions and thereby drive long-term economic growth, or if other factors such as geographic conditions, institutional frameworks, or distributional conflicts play a more decisive role. For example, Ogilvie highlights how exogenous cultural theories can influence the structure of economic institutions, while Austin suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Acemoglu et al. identify European mortality rates as a key determinant of institutional development in colonies, which in turn affects current income per capita. These competing views underscore the need to carefully examine the interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth over extended historical periods. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending the deep-rooted factors that influence economic performance on a global scale. Institutions can be understood as the rules of the game, which structure incentives in society (North 1989, p.1). North argues that institutions are fundamental to economic growth because they shape incentives and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). North & Weingast's account of seventeenth-century England illustrates the importance of secure property rights in fostering long-run growth. Their analysis shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, particularly those that emerged during the English financial revolution, was pivotal to this process (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), which in turn enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional development fostered a stable market for public debt, which had positive externalities for private debt markets as well (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The stability provided by these institutions was crucial because it facilitated long-term capital investment and economic growth. North & Weingast's focus on the financial revolution aligns with other scholars who have noted that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War also played a role in shaping these institutions. This historical context underscores how institutional changes can be driven by both internal political dynamics and external pressures such as war. The long-term implications of this institutional evolution were significant, as it supported economic growth and paved the way for political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These findings suggest that while cultural factors may influence economic outcomes, the structural role of institutions in securing property rights and reducing transaction costs is a more immediate determinant of long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by European mortality rates and settlement patterns, which determined the type of institutions imposed on these regions. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) found a strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutional quality, indicating that these colonial-era institutions have persisted and significantly impacted economic performance today. This perspective underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that while extractive institutions may not encourage development, they can be stable and persistent due to the political power dynamics that maintain them.

The mechanism involves the establishment of extractive institutions that prioritize short-term gains over sustainable growth, thereby locking former colonies into a cycle of underdevelopment. This dynamic reveals how historical institutional legacies can override other factors, including culture, in determining economic outcomes.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The central mechanism involves the influence of elites in securing institutional arrangements that favor their interests over those of less powerful groups. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where serfdom, communal structures, and guilds were shaped by power struggles between ruling classes and commoners (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). In the context of colonial societies, greater inequality from the outset led to institutional frameworks that preserved elite advantages. For example, in New World colonies with extreme wealth disparities, elites implemented policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance, thus perpetuating inequality over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

This dynamic has implications for understanding economic growth trajectories across different regions. The persistence of institutions that favor elites can lock societies into pathways of underdevelopment by stifling innovation and limiting opportunities for broader segments of the population. However, Ogilvie cautions against simplistic views of institutional interdependencies, noting that entire frameworks must be considered rather than individual components in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective underscores the complexity of how distributional conflicts shape institutions and their long-term economic impacts.

The slave trade in Africa has been argued to have caused a culture of mistrust that persists and affects long-term economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) identify trust as a key factor in economic development, noting that the slave trade's legacy includes reduced trust levels which continue to impact contemporary African societies. This mistrust hinders cooperation and collective action, essential components for fostering economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates on this point, asserting that the historical political instability caused by the slave trades has led to weakened states with limited capacity to implement effective policies. This political fragmentation creates an environment where corruption thrives (Nunn 2008, p.1), as state institutions struggle to enforce rules and regulations consistently. The persistence of mistrust and institutional weakness forms a vicious cycle, where low levels of trust perpetuate underdevelopment by discouraging investment in public goods and deterring economic initiatives that require collective effort. The impact of mistrust is not uniform across Africa; regional variations in colonial experiences and post-colonial developments qualify this broad pattern (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

Austin and Broadberry and Gupta's work challenges traditional narratives of economic divergence by demonstrating that the differences between Europe and Asia were already pronounced before 1800 (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin argues that African economic development is better understood through a dynamic, endogenous approach. Broadberry and Gupta contend that pre-1800 Asia was economically stagnant compared to Europe. Both authors share the view that the great divergence began much earlier than conventional wisdom suggests, with institutional changes in agriculture driving investment and productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin's emphasis on endogenous responses to environmental challenges shows how African societies adapted to their surroundings using available technologies. This nuanced perspective highlights that while broad patterns of economic divergence can be identified, local contexts and adaptive strategies significantly shape long-term growth trajectories.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Mokyr (2005, p.56) supports this view by highlighting how useful knowledge was generated through tacit and codified forms and shared across borders through personal transmission and training. This diffusion of knowledge was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The growth of useful knowledge thus became a crucial driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that the spread of tacit and technical knowledge was influenced by technological constraints and the payoff to efforts in codification. This qualification suggests that while ideas were central, their transmission and application depended on specific conditions, including technological capabilities and cultural receptiveness to new knowledge.

Culture plays an important role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, as it influences both the development and maintenance of economic frameworks. Ogilvie shows how cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions, such as guilds, and their efficiency, emphasizing that non-economic cultural beliefs can alter the structure and equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they facilitate secure property rights and reduce transaction costs, which are crucial for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.9).

The role of culture in institutional frameworks extends beyond individual institutions to the broader framework within which they operate. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how differences in mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in European colonies, highlighting that these institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows how cultural receptiveness to new knowledge and institutional frameworks can be shaped by historical events and environmental factors, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illuminated by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). North and Weingast provide additional support for this view, showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They illustrate how the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, highlighting the necessary role of institutions in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

There remains disagreement over whether cultural factors alone can account for the persistence of extractive institutions or if other factors, such as technological capabilities, also play a significant role. The specific historical evidence of colonial boundaries and contemporary data on malaria transmission indicates that cultural factors have had a lasting impact on trust and institutional development in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).
