The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue. At stake intellectually is whether cultural factors can fundamentally shape institutions and thereby influence economic growth over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on how to interpret the causal relationships between culture and institutions, with some scholars arguing that cultural beliefs directly influence institutional structures and efficiency, while others posit that institutions are shaped by more immediate economic or political forces, such as distributional conflicts or extractive practices. Some scholars argue that cultural factors, such as trust and social capital, can facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency, thereby fostering economic growth. Some scholars argue that culture's influence on institutional development is frequently exaggerated; tangible factors such as property rights and secure private contracts are crucial for long-run growth. This debate underscores the need to understand how cultural norms interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic outcomes. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional structures remains a critical area of inquiry, particularly as it relates to the enduring effects on economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution of the late 17th century, marked by innovations such as the establishment of the Bank of England (1694), played a pivotal role. This development significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework facilitated both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast's analysis highlights that institutions governing public choice evolved in a way fundamentally necessary for England's economic success.

The Bank of England, initially created to manage public debt, had a transformative impact beyond its original mandate. It fostered a robust market for private debt by providing large positive externalities (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This dual role highlights how institutional changes can have far-reaching effects on both public and private sectors. The financial revolution's success was contingent on the broader institutional context that ensured stable property rights, which in turn supported sustained economic growth.

The implications of these findings extend beyond England's historical experience. They suggest that secure property rights, underpinned by well-designed institutions governing public choice, are essential for long-term economic development. This perspective challenges views that prioritize cultural factors as the primary drivers of economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15)  argue that the 'reversal of fortune' and economic divergence theories primarily discuss the role of institutions and geographical factors in economic development. The 'sophisticated geography' approach, which focuses on endogenous responses to environments, highlights how Africans' technological adaptations shaped their economies over time (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective underscores the importance of dynamic institutional changes that facilitate investment and productivity growth. This pattern aligns with the broader institutional changes observed in north-western Europe. Yet Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) caution that this high agricultural labour productivity did not necessarily translate into low food prices or widespread abundance of grain due to the migration of labor out of agriculture into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuanced view suggests that while institutional changes are crucial, their impact on economic growth can vary significantly depending on regional specificities and labor dynamics.

The centrality of ideas and ideologies in driving economic change from 1600 to 1848 is underlined by McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This period saw a cultural struggle that reshaped economic growth, with ideas about trade-tested betterment and useful knowledge serving as the mainspring of this transformation. The French example illustrates how entrenched ideologies can impede economic growth, despite available information on better institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr emphasizes that the diffusion of useful knowledge within Europe was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, highlighting the role of tacit knowledge in this process (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). This suggests that while formal institutions might shape the flow of information, it is the underlying cultural dynamics, such as the dissemination of ideas and ideologies, that ultimately drive economic growth. McCloskey's focus on the "Franco-French War" exemplifies how cultural struggles can either hinder or facilitate economic progress (McCloskey 2015, p.9). The implication is that the transmission of useful knowledge, whether codified or tacit, is contingent on broader cultural shifts that enable or constrain its application. This underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between ideas and institutions in long-run growth patterns.

The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has enduring effects on contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is tied to the historical political instability and fragmentation caused by the slave trades. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave exports from Africa were associated with a long-term weakening of state institutions, which in turn impeded economic development. Political instability constrained the transmission of useful knowledge such as ideas about governance and institutional building (Nunn 2008, p.1). The cultural legacy of mistrust from the slave trade affected social capital and hindered idea dissemination necessary for economic growth. This suggests that the cultural impact of the slave trade persists through multiple generations. The persistence of mistrust in African societies today underscores how deeply rooted cultural norms can shape long-run economic outcomes, even when direct historical factors are no longer present.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutions play a fundamental role in long-run economic growth. They claim the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. These institutions, shaped by the specific conditions faced by European colonists, have had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that high mortality rates among Europeans in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persist today and significantly affect current income per capita. This pattern highlights how initial institutional frameworks can determine long-term economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that these institutions are not necessarily designed for long-run growth but rather to maximize rents for the colonizers. The implications of this perspective suggest that economic policies aimed at development must consider the historical institutional legacies and their enduring effects on current economic structures, particularly in former colonies.

The impact of colonial institutions is multifaceted: they create a framework that can either foster or hinder growth depending on how they are designed and implemented. This underscores the importance of understanding the institutional framework as a key determinant of long-run economic performance.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective focuses on how conflicts over resource allocation and power dynamics influenced institutional development. For instance, Ogilvie highlights that exogenous cultural theories often fail to account for the complex interplay between institutions and distributional struggles. She contends that such theories oversimplify the role of culture in economic growth by neglecting the underlying power dynamics and conflicts that shape institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this argument by showing how different factor endowments influenced inequality, which in turn shaped institutional frameworks (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality resulted from institutional arrangements favoring elites, not merely cultural beliefs. The persistence of these institutions can be traced to the way they were designed to maintain elite control over resources and power. Ogilvie also recognizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, we must examine entire institutional frameworks to understand their impact on economic growth.

Culture's role in shaping institutions is pivotal for understanding long-run economic growth. Ogilvie demonstrates that cultural beliefs are interdependent with broader institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of institutions designed to maintain elite control over resources and power is evident in the European context, where cultural factors like Catholic beliefs influenced the structure of economic institutions such as guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interplay between culture and institutions highlights how deeply embedded cultural norms can shape economic outcomes. North shows that institutions matter for growth because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further illustrate this by showing how the evolution of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, a key factor in long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions extends beyond Europe. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists today and affects current economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This historical trauma underscores how deeply ingrained cultural norms can be, and their enduring impact on institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The interdependence of culture and institutions is further evidenced by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks evolve over time (Austin 2008, p.25). North argues that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants of growth, is insufficient because it ignores the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.1).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture plays a fundamental role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs are interdependent with broader institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of cultural versus institutional factors in this process. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic interplay between culture and institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the detailed examination of how cultural norms influenced the structure of economic institutions in Europe. This comparative analysis shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules, influencing both institutional design and economic efficiency over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
