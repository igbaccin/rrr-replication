The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake intellectually is understanding whether cultural values and beliefs can shape economic outcomes by influencing institutional frameworks. The debate centres on two primary positions: one argues that cultural factors are integral to shaping institutions that drive long-term economic growth, while the other posits that cultural influences are secondary to other determinants such as geographic or extractive factors. Ogilvie's work critiques the notion of efficiency-driven institutions, suggesting instead that distributional conflicts and exogenous cultural theories provide better explanations for pre-industrial European institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. highlight mortality rates as a factor influencing extractive institutions, which continually impact current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The disagreement hinges on whether cultural factors are primary drivers or merely ancillary influences. For instance, Ogilvie challenges the idea that efficiency is the sole determinant of institutional design, highlighting how cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s focus on mortality rates as exogenous variables shaping institutions. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding long-run economic growth and development strategies, particularly in regions where cultural values and beliefs are deeply embedded.

Institutions are highlighted as a fundamental determinant of economic growth (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. These institutions create enduring effects on income per capita by determining the allocation of resources and the structure of economic interactions. The impact of institutions can be seen through the example of the Bank of England's creation and its subsequent financial innovations; these institutional changes reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Institutions are not always designed for long-term economic development. They often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term gains over sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).. This dynamic illustrates how institutions, vital for economic outcomes, can also be manipulated to serve specific interests and not broad-based prosperity.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work, supported by Hornbeck's analysis (Hornbeck 2010, p.6), demonstrates how institutional changes can create positive externalities that drive market development. For instance, North and Weingast demonstrate the financial revolution in England provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework allowed for the growth of a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated private economic activities. Hornbeck's study on barbed wire fencing on the American Plains illustrates a similar dynamic: when farmers could better protect their property rights, agricultural development flourished (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. Hornbeck's analysis shows that the effectiveness of these institutions depends on context. In some cases, social norms might encourage compliance with property rights even in the absence of strict legal enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This suggests that while institutional changes are crucial for economic growth, their impact can vary based on cultural and social factors.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional struggles rather than efficiency or cultural beliefs. In pre-industrial Europe, serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds emerged as a means to manage conflicts between different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of inequality in New World societies can be traced back to the colonial period when institutions were shaped by elite interests rather than efficiency or cultural beliefs. These institutions then reinforced inequality through various government policies that restricted competition, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality and hindering long-run growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Ogilvie further notes that these institutions are interdependent within a wider framework, suggesting that changes in one institution can have ripple effects throughout the system. This interdependency means that studying individual institutions in isolation may not fully capture their impact on economic growth or inequality. For example, the European family system was intertwined with labor markets and credit markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was similarly linked to land markets and the nuclear family system (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This interdependency underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This mistrust permeated both interpersonal relationships and broader social institutions. The transatlantic slave trade left a legacy involving forced displacement and psychological scars that have endured through generations, influencing contemporary African societies. One specific mechanism through which this mistrust manifests is in reduced levels of trust within communities, with long-term implications for economic development. This disruption of traditional social structures has lasting impacts on how individuals interact and cooperate. For instance, the enslavement process often involved betrayals by local leaders who would sell their own people into slavery to European traders (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical context fostered an environment where trust was difficult to establish or maintain. Consequently, contemporary African societies grapple with lower levels of social capital, which is crucial for economic growth and development. The impact of this mistrust extends beyond interpersonal relationships to affect institutional frameworks as well. For example, the colonial period saw the introduction of new gender roles and production systems that reinforced pre-existing social norms (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This persistence underscores how historical events shape long-term economic outcomes in African societies.

Reevaluating historical causation and Eurocentric views challenges simplistic explanations of economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique is rooted in the dynamic, partly endogenous nature of African responses to their environments, which ultimately shaped economic outcomes. Bryant (2006, p.35) similarly posits that causal connectedness governs every transition from past to present, emphasizing the role of enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms inherited from prior developments. These factors are crucial for understanding phased conversions to industrial modes of production or imperial forms of hegemony. The revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent (Bryant 2006, p.35). This perspective highlights the importance of long-term historical processes in shaping economic development. Bryant (2006, p.35) also notes the complexity of European modernity, stating that any attempt to overturn our understanding of key determinants would be an audacious venture (Bryant 2006, p.4). This complexity suggests that while cultural and social factors are crucial, their interplay with other historical processes must be carefully considered.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) both focus on social and demographic changes influencing economic growth without directly addressing the role of culture in long-run growth. These papers (Goldin 2006, p.8; Pamuk 2007, p.24) share a posture that is adjacent to our topic. They highlight how exogenous factors can drive significant shifts in labor markets and societal structures, which are fundamental to understanding economic development.

Goldin (2006, p.8) identifies several key changes in the early 20th century that altered women's employment patterns in the US, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. These changes created new opportunities for women, transforming their roles in the workforce (Goldin 2006, p.8). This transformation was not merely evolutionary but rather a quiet revolution driven by shifts in social norms and expectations regarding future work (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Pamuk (2007, p.24) similarly shows that exogenous shocks like the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including increased mobility and institutional adaptations. These changes led to technological innovations and altered wage structures across Europe (Goldin 2006, p.3). The demographic shifts following the Black Death, such as increased labor scarcity, led to significant economic restructuring in northwestern Europe by 1450. This period saw a decline in interest rates and skill premiums, alongside the rise of rural industries driven by labor-saving innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.5).

These historical examples illustrate how exogenous changes can catalyze structural transformations that have lasting impacts on economic development. However, while these studies provide valuable insights into the mechanisms driving economic growth through social and demographic shifts, they do not directly engage with the role of cultural factors in these processes.

Cultural factors play a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced medieval craftsmen to follow efficient guild rules and that these 'civic' cultures encouraged institutions favoring economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that features like trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast illustrate how the financial revolution in England led to secure property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9).

This historical evidence demonstrates that cultural beliefs can shape institutions by influencing collective action games. These institutions, in turn, facilitate economic growth through improved efficiency and coordinated actions within societies. The Bank of England's development and the creation of new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, further enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the role of women in African economies, showing that gender roles and cultural beliefs significantly impact economic production systems (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is evident in both pre-industrial Europe and colonial Africa. Cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced guild rules in medieval Europe, while matrilineal societies in Africa saw women as indispensable to community reproduction (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). These cultural factors shaped economic institutions that facilitated growth by reducing transaction costs and promoting coordinated actions within societies.

Cultural factors are thus fundamental to understanding the long-run growth of economies. The shared finding is that institutions matter for economic growth, as North shows (North 1989, p.9), but the precise impact of culture on these institutions remains a subject of debate. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing the dynamic and partly endogenous nature of African responses to their environments (Austin 2008, p.25). This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with environmental conditions to shape economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles this debate is the role of the Bank of England in reducing transaction costs, which facilitated long-run economic growth and demonstrates the impact of institutions on economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). This shows that cultural factors are central to the institutional frameworks that drive economic development.
