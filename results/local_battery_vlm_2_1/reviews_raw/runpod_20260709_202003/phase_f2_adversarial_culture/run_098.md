The role of culture in shaping institutions is central to understanding long-run economic growth, as it influences institutional frameworks that can either foster or hinder development. The debate over the mechanisms through which culture impacts long-term growth involves two key perspectives. One side posits that cultural beliefs and values significantly impact economic outcomes by shaping institutions that promote efficiency and growth. These scholars argue that norms of trust, reciprocity, and social networks facilitate coordinated actions that enhance economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another perspective maintains that the relationship between culture and long-run growth is less direct: institutions are shaped primarily by exogenous factors like geography or disease environments, which in turn influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

A key question arising from this debate concerns the mechanisms through which culture influences institutions and growth. One perspective is that culture shapes institutional frameworks by fostering norms of trust and reciprocity, which in turn support economic efficiency. This perspective suggests that cultural values actively shape institutional structures (Author Year p.N).

In response to these claims, scholars have also examined the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights how power dynamics within societies can lead to institutional arrangements designed to maintain existing power structures (Author Year p.N). The interdependencies between different institutions within a framework further complicate this dynamic, suggesting that the entire institutional system must be considered rather than individual components in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They propose that the financial revolution of 1694, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in this process. This event significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The development of new financial instruments facilitated both public and private debt markets, providing a substantial positive externality for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North (1989, p.1) explains that institutions matter in economic growth because they address the issue of transaction costs, which neoclassical models often overlook. This perspective underscores how institutional arrangements can foster economic development by creating stable and predictable environments for investment and trade.

They argue that even if other factors might have eventually led to success, the institutional changes accelerated and solidified this process. This implies that cultural factors alone are insufficient to explain economic development; instead, it is the interplay between institutions and cultural contexts that drives long-run growth. For instance, North (1989, p.9) highlights how the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, illustrating how institutional changes can be driven by specific historical events and power dynamics. This dynamic suggests that while culture may influence institutional development, it is the institutions themselves that directly shape economic outcomes.

Colonial institutions were pivotal in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that high European mortality rates in colonial settings led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persistently affected current income per capita. The paper uses settler mortality as a proxy for institutional quality, showing strong first-stage relationships between mortality and both early and contemporary institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This highlights how initial conditions set by European colonists, particularly high mortality rates, created enduring institutional legacies that stifled economic development. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, illustrating the extractive nature of colonial institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, it is important to note that while these institutions significantly impacted income per capita, their role in economic development was not uniform across all colonies. For instance, some former colonies experienced institutional changes that fostered industrialization and economic growth, suggesting that while extractive institutions were prevalent, they did not uniformly hinder development. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-run economic outcomes, where the specific historical context and institutional design played crucial roles in shaping economic trajectories.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman assert that distributional conflicts are more influential than cultural factors in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective challenges the notion that culture inherently drives institutional arrangements. Ogilvie posits that distributional conflicts among elites and lower classes were pivotal in forming core European institutions like serfdom and craft guilds. For instance, while Richardson attributes the decline of guilds to Protestant Reformation's impact on Catholic beliefs about the afterlife, Ogilvie counters this with a focus on elite interests. The power dynamics between elites and lower classes influenced institutional design to maintain or challenge existing power structures. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this idea to New World societies, where institutions evolved in ways that maintained inequality. This institutional framework reinforced existing inequalities through policies like restricted competition and limited access to resources. Ogilvie warns that while these distributional conflicts are critical in shaping economic outcomes, they also highlight the need for a holistic understanding of institutional frameworks.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) contend that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic growth from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey's analysis highlights how cultural shifts in rhetoric and ideology fostered new economic circumstances, while Mokyr emphasizes the critical role of knowledge diffusion in enabling growth. The mechanism through which these ideas operated was a shift in rhetoric that legitimized markets and innovation. This rhetorical change created an environment where new economic practices could flourish. McCloskey illustrates this with the example of France, noting how the country's conservative mindset hindered economic growth despite having examples like the Dutch and British to emulate (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr further elaborates on the role of knowledge diffusion (Mokyr 2005, p.56). He argues that the growth of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was a fundamental driver of economic progress. This knowledge moved across borders through various means such as periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. The implication is that while institutions may provide the framework for economic activity, it is the ideas and ideologies that shape how these frameworks are utilized. Mokyr qualifies this by noting that access to knowledge was not just about written records but also depended on personal transmission and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This underscores the importance of cultural factors in facilitating the spread of useful knowledge and, consequently, economic growth. The interplay between culture and institutions thus emerges as a critical factor in long-run economic development.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are contingent on different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta share this view, noting that by 1800, parts of Asia looked more like stagnating regions in southern Europe rather than the developing north-western areas (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This perspective underscores a dynamic interplay between geography, technology, investment, and institutional change. Austin highlights that Africans' responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately reshaped economic development trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). The implication is that cultural adaptations to environmental conditions are pivotal in shaping institutional frameworks and, consequently, long-run growth. However, Broadberry and Gupta caution that high levels of investment in agriculture did not necessarily lead to abundant grain supplies or low food prices (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1), suggesting that the relationship between investment, productivity, and economic development is nuanced and context-specific. This complexity indicates that cultural factors, mediating how institutions are utilized, play a crucial role in determining economic outcomes over the long run.

The slave trade's impact on African development underscores how historical events can shape cultural attitudes of mistrust that persist and influence long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This legacy of mistrust persists as a cultural trait that continues to affect contemporary economic performance. This mistrust, in turn, can impede economic cooperation and development. The persistence of this cultural trait is further exacerbated by political instability and weak state institutions, which are themselves a legacy of the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn shows that "a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance" (Nunn 2008, p.1), suggesting that the institutional fragility stemming from historical events can be a significant barrier to growth. This implies that addressing cultural attitudes and strengthening institutions are crucial for fostering long-term economic development in regions affected by such historical traumas.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply intertwined with the historical development of economic and political structures. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs significantly influence economic institutions, such as guilds and communities (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates has persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), indicating that cultural attitudes can shape institutional frameworks over long periods. The interplay between culture and institutions is further highlighted by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate secure property rights and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast demonstrate how the evolution of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England provided a positive externality for private debt markets, underscoring the role of institutional development in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The historical legacy of cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks is evident in regions affected by traumatic events such as the slave trade. Nunn shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical context is crucial for understanding how cultural traits, exacerbated by political instability and weak institutions, can hinder long-term economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need to consider dynamic, partly endogenous factors (Austin 2008, p.25). Nunn and Wantchekon show how a culture of mistrust developed within Africa due to the slave trade persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural attitudes or institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of long-run growth. North and Weingast show that institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and financial stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). However, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), indicating that cultural beliefs about trust, norms, and networks can also drive institutional development. The historical evidence of financial revolution in England settles this disagreement by showing how the creation of the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and fostered long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9).
