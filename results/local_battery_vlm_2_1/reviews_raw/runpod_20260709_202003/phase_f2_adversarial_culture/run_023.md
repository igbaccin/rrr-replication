The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue within economic historiography and institutional economics. The stakes are high because the answer determines whether cultural factors can be considered enduring determinants of economic development, or if they are merely ephemeral influences on institutions that themselves drive growth. On one side stand scholars who argue that culture shapes institutions by influencing collective action; on another stand those who claim that institutions are shaped primarily by exogenous factors such as mortality rates and colonial settlements; and a third group posits that cultural beliefs can be antecedents to institutional frameworks. The debate hinges on whether institutions are products of culture, shaped by external forces, or both.

The first stream addresses the role of culture in shaping economic institutions through collective action. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts explain core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, communities, and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The influence of cultural beliefs on these institutions is evident in how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules efficiently. This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial; for instance, the European family system contributed to early industrialization by influencing labor markets, credit markets, and welfare systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This stream suggests that culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions through collective action, thereby influencing economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North argues that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North & Weingast note that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This institutional change reflected increased security of property rights over time, which was critical for both capital markets and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast state that this institutional evolution provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt.

The role of institutions in driving economic growth has qualifications. This suggests that although secure property rights and reduced transaction costs were pivotal, they were part of a broader set of factors influencing economic development. The implication is that the institutional framework in seventeenth-century England was an active shaping force in its own right, not merely a passive recipient of cultural influences.

Austin argues that institutions and geographical factors play a pivotal role in economic development, as evidenced by the "reversal of fortune" thesis (Austin 2008, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta reinforce this perspective, noting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, highlighting the significance of institutional changes and investment patterns (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). The dynamic interplay between institutions and geography is particularly evident in the case of southern Indian agriculture, where high levels of investment led to increased productivity. This pattern suggests that endogenous factors, such as institutional changes, can drive economic development independently of exogenous geographical constraints. Austin notes that while geographical factors are not immutable obstacles, they do shape the pathways through which economic growth occurs (Austin 2008, p.25). The implication is that cultural influences may also be subject to these same dynamic interactions with institutions and geography; thus, they might be better understood as part of a broader set of forces shaping long-run growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie highlights how exogenous cultural theories often fail to account for the complex interdependencies within institutional frameworks. Sokoloff and Engerman corroborate this perspective by examining how factor endowments and initial conditions in New World societies led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that reinforced elite power. These institutions restricted competition, thereby influencing long-term development trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) . Ogilvie further elaborates on the interconnectedness of institutions within a broader framework, noting that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. This perspective underscores how distributional conflicts are fundamental in understanding the long-run growth of societies.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the fundamental drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe. They propose that the Great Enrichment was driven by changes in rhetoric and culture, which fostered new attitudes towards commerce and innovation. The cultural struggle for new rhetoric is evident in the shift from aristocratic to bourgeois values, where "Pour la Patrie, les Sciences et la Gloire" (McCloskey 2015, p.9) gave way to a more businesslike approach. This change was institutional and deeply ideological, reflecting a broader cultural transformation that valued innovation and commerce.

Mokyr elaborates on this mechanism by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.56). He posits that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe facilitated the Great Enrichment, as ideas spread rapidly through periodicals, translations, and personal communications. This diffusion included codified knowledge and tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The interplay between codified and tacit knowledge highlights how cultural attitudes towards learning and innovation were crucial in driving economic growth.

The implications of this perspective are profound: the role of culture in shaping institutions is not just a background factor but a primary driver of long-run economic growth. Cultural attitudes, by fostering or hindering the diffusion of useful knowledge, directly influence the trajectory of economic development. Mokyr notes that the diffusion of ideas was not uniform across Europe; national differences in style and emphasis played a role (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This qualification underscores the complexity of cultural influences on economic growth, suggesting that while culture is a fundamental driver, its effects are mediated by local contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. These scholars identify mortality rates among European settlers as a key factor influencing the establishment of institutions in colonies; high mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents rather than foster long-run growth. This mechanism is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) finding that institutional changes, rather than geographic factors, account for a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. also note that the persistence of extractive institutions can be attributed to the reluctance of politically powerful groups to initiate change, as they may not directly benefit from the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and political dynamics in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show how this legacy of mistrust can be attributed to the historical trauma of the slave trade. This mistrust has profound implications for contemporary African societies, as it undermines social cohesion and cooperation, which are essential for economic growth. For instance, the slave trade's impact on state centralization is a key mechanism through which cultural mistrust manifests. Nunn illustrates that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states. This fragmentation has historically hindered Africa's ability to develop strong institutions capable of fostering economic growth. Moreover, the legacy of mistrust extends beyond political instability into broader social dynamics. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that this mistrust can be seen as a source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, which is a significant barrier to economic progress in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.1). The pervasive nature of this mistrust means that it affects individual behaviors and also influences the broader institutional framework, creating an environment where trust-based transactions are less likely. This cultural shift is seen as a rival explanation to broader cultural factors influencing growth and highlights the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary economic development.

The enduring impact of historical events on contemporary economic development highlights the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth. The mistrust that developed within Africa due to the slave trade is an example of how cultural factors can influence institutional frameworks and economic progress (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural shift towards mistrust affects individual behaviors and also influences broader institutional frameworks, creating an environment where trust-based transactions are less likely. These findings complement those on European institutions: Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is recognized by studies of European economic institutions, underscoring the need to look at entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). A shared finding across these sources is that institutions matter in economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s observation on European mortality rates leading to extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The historical evidence of the English financial revolution shows how institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework.
