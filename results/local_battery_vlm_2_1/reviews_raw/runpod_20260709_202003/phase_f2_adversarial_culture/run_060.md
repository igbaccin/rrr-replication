The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding the fundamental causes of economic development and stagnation over centuries. One position posits that cultural beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting efficiency and growth. This view suggests that societies with 'civic' cultures, characterized by trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, are more likely to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another position emphasizes the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating coordinated actions and reducing transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The debate hinges on whether cultural beliefs are exogenous factors that determine institutional structures or if institutions evolve endogenously through complex interactions within broader frameworks. This question is central to comprehending the mechanisms underlying long-run growth and development.

The role of culture in shaping economic institutions has been explored through various lenses, with some scholars arguing that cultural beliefs can directly influence the structure and efficiency of these institutions. For instance, Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe highlights how different cultural frameworks, such as Catholic beliefs about the afterlife, motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can significantly impact economic outcomes by altering the structure and equilibrium of collective action games. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women's contributions to economic growth underscores how gender-based colonial constructs and pre-colonial social norms influenced production systems (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). These findings indicate that cultural beliefs and values can shape institutional frameworks in ways that either facilitate or hinder economic development. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with cultural factors often serving as antecedents that trigger the evolution of institutional structures.

The relationship between culture and long-run growth is further complicated by the interdependencies within broader institutional frameworks. Ogilvie acknowledges the interdependence of individual institutions within a wider framework, arguing that studying one institution in isolation may not capture the full picture (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North and Weingast also highlight how institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for securing property rights and promoting economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These insights suggest that cultural beliefs can influence the evolution of institutional frameworks, which in turn affect long-run growth through mechanisms such as reduced transaction costs and secure property rights. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4), both focus on the role of economic institutions in shaping pre-industrial societies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) focus on how inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power influenced institutional evolution. This distributional conflict framework highlights an important mechanism: institutions are not merely efficient solutions to economic problems but are deeply rooted in the social and political dynamics of their time. The downstream implication is that these institutions can persist over long periods, even if they are not the most economically efficient. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates this persistence through the example of European guilds, which were maintained despite their inefficiencies due to the power of distributional interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). However, Sokoloff and Engerman's perspective qualifies this by highlighting that the persistence of institutions is also influenced by broader factors such as factor endowments and historical contexts. These findings underscore the complexity of institutional change and suggest that cultural factors, while influential, are not the sole determinants of long-run growth.

The divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 challenges views that attribute this gap solely to post-1800 developments (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) assert the early modern great divergence, while van Zanden (1999, p.2) provides a complementary perspective on European economic development. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 resembled the stagnating southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe rather than the developing north-western regions. This divergence was driven by institutional changes in Europe leading to investment in agriculture, which boosted agricultural labor productivity. However, this higher productivity did not result in abundant grain or low food prices because labor shifted from agriculture into industry and services. This divergence suggests that cultural factors, such as institutional changes and investment patterns, played a crucial role in shaping the long-run growth trajectories of Europe and Asia. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women's contributions to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). The literature on the role of women in economic growth and development highlights their substantial contributions, often overlooked by traditional narratives. These contributions are evident in various spheres, including agricultural production, trade, and community building. For instance, in matrilineal societies like Asante, women played a crucial role in defining the transmission of office and wealth through their bloodlines. Goldin's work on U.S. women’s employment and education underscores similar patterns of transformation. These shifts were driven by exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions, which altered both labor supply and social norms. However, these transformations were not uniform across all sectors or regions, highlighting the complex interplay between cultural factors and economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.8). This interplay suggests that while women's roles in economic development are pivotal, they are also subject to broader cultural and institutional frameworks that can either facilitate or hinder their contributions.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The historical legacy of this cultural shift has had profound implications for economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades in Africa caused long-term political instability, which in turn led to weakened and fragmented states. This political fragmentation is crucial because it hinders effective governance and the implementation of policies that could foster economic development. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that this political instability can be linked directly to the adverse effects on state centralization. Specifically, the slave trade disrupted traditional governance structures, making it difficult for African states to consolidate power and resources effectively. The persistence of mistrust is one of the critical factors that hinder economic growth in Africa today.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies and persist today because they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Therefore, the institutional framework introduced during colonial rule continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies.

Institutions are not merely economic structures but are deeply intertwined with cultural beliefs and social norms. These norms can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, as shown by Richardson's argument that Catholic beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of 'social capital', which posits that features like trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency. Putnam argues that societies with high social capital, characterized by civic cultures, encourage institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Furthermore, the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding long-term development. For instance, Laslett's analysis of the European family system highlights its interdependence with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, which collectively contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Cultural beliefs and values are not static; they evolve over time and influence institutional frameworks. The Protestant Reformation's impact on guilds illustrates how shifts in cultural beliefs can alter economic institutions. This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is evident in the historical context of European economic development, where cultural changes drove institutional reforms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Moreover, the evolution of financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England during the English financial revolution exemplify how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).
