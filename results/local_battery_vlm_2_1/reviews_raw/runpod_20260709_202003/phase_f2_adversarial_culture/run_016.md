The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue within economic historiography and institutional economics. At stake intellectually are the mechanisms that drive sustained economic development over centuries. One position argues that cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions by influencing collective action and social norms, thereby fostering or hindering growth. Another position contends that the impact of culture on long-run growth is limited; instead, the key determinants lie in exogenous factors such as geography or institutional frameworks. A third position proposes that extractive institutions, shaped by colonial experiences and high mortality rates, are the primary drivers of economic stagnation. These perspectives conflict over whether cultural beliefs directly influence economic outcomes through institutional channels or if other factors dominate. The debate hinges on understanding how deeply cultural forces penetrate the fabric of economic institutions and growth dynamics.

Cultural theories of economic development emphasize that beliefs and values significantly shape economic institutions, which in turn affect long-run growth. Ogilvie notes that cultural approaches highlight how non-economic beliefs can influence institutional structures and efficiency. For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while Protestant Reformation led to a decline in these guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital suggests that civic cultures, characterized by trust and reciprocity, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. This perspective underscores how cultural beliefs can foster institutional frameworks conducive to long-run economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further illuminated through the interdependencies within broader institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. propose that favorable contracting institutions, which regulate private interactions and property rights, are partially interdependent with other institutions. This interconnectedness suggests that cultural influences on one institution can have ripple effects across the entire framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The creation of the Bank of England exemplifies how secure property rights, fostered by institutional changes, led to a financial revolution that reduced transaction costs and supported long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The interplay between culture and institutions is not limited to Europe. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women's contributions to economic development were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The authors argue that gender-based colonial constructs and the codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for economic production, reflecting how cultural norms can shape institutional frameworks and economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This underscores the pervasive influence of culture on economic institutions across different historical contexts.

The debate over culture's role in dominating institutions extends to the mechanisms through which these influences operate. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests that cultural influences on economic growth are complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. North and Weingast emphasize the critical role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting how institutional changes can create positive externalities for both public and private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This perspective aligns with the idea that cultural beliefs can shape institutions in ways that support long-run economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how cultural forces can drive institutional changes that promote growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al.'s work reveals that European mortality rates played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions established in colonised areas. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists and promote long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, shaped by colonial practices, had enduring economic impacts, as evidenced by the correlation between settler mortality and current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights how initial conditions set by colonial powers can have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al also note that institutions are not static; they change in response to political and economic pressures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic nature of institutions suggests that while initial conditions matter, the long-run impact of colonial institutions on economic development is mediated by subsequent institutional changes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists and significantly impacts contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the legacy of the slave trade has had profound effects on social capital, with contemporary African societies exhibiting lower levels of trust as a direct result. This cultural deficit in trust is a persistent feature stemming from historical events that continues to shape economic interactions and institutional effectiveness. Hopkins argues that this mistrust can be understood within the broader context of Africa's institutional history, where precolonial and colonial experiences have shaped contemporary governance structures. The persistence of mistrust highlights how deeply ingrained cultural norms can hinder economic development by undermining the foundations of cooperation and collective action necessary for growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This dynamic is further complicated by the legacy of colonial rule, which has left enduring marks on African institutions. Hopkins notes that effective institutional reforms must consider these historical legacies, as contemporary governance structures are often incongruent with indigenous institutions, leading to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Addressing the mistrust stemming from the slave trade requires economic reforms as well as cultural and institutional changes that align with Africa's historical context.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective is supported by North's (1989, p.9) emphasis on the role of institutional changes, such as the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights. These developments were pivotal for economic growth.

The evolution of these institutions was an economic phenomenon and also a political one. This interplay between political and economic institutions suggests that institutional reforms must be holistic, addressing both governance structures and economic policies. The effectiveness of these institutions depended on their ability to adapt to changing circumstances.

This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional evolution in driving long-run economic growth, highlighting that cultural factors alone cannot explain economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) contend that distributional conflicts and inequality are crucial in shaping economic institutions. They argue that these factors, along with efficiency considerations, drove the development of core pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds, and influenced the persistence of inequality in New World societies. The institutional design of these systems was shaped by elite interests to maintain their privileges. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) asserts that cultural theories often overlook the role of distributional conflicts by focusing on micro-level efficiency without considering the broader institutional framework. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) add that these institutions reflected underlying power dynamics and resource endowments as well as cultural beliefs. The persistence of inequality in societies with high levels of resource abundance relative to labor was reinforced through government policies that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic highlights how institutional design can be influenced by distributional conflicts, leading to persistent inequalities.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.27) examine various historical factors influencing development but do not directly address how culture shapes long-run growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He highlights that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic suggests that endogenous factors, including cultural adaptations, played a significant role in shaping Africa's development (Austin 2008, p.25). He further argues that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). This implies that the cultural and institutional legacies of historical events, such as the slave trade, have persistent effects on economic outcomes. Nunn also acknowledges that recent empirical studies suggest that Africa's history can explain part of its current underdevelopment. These studies focus on the link between countries' colonial experience and current economic development (Nunn 2008, p.1). This indicates that while historical factors are crucial, they do not fully account for contemporary economic performance, suggesting a more complex interplay of cultural, institutional, and historical factors in shaping long-run growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women's economic contributions, alongside Broadberry and Gupta's examination of the early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), highlights that economic development is influenced by a range of factors, including gender dynamics and regional investment patterns. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate how African women played crucial roles in both pre-colonial and colonial economies, with their contributions often overlooked in traditional narratives. They argue that colonial constructs codified gender roles that had long-term consequences for economic production. Broadberry and Gupta's analysis shows that high levels of investment in agriculture led to increased productivity, but this did not necessarily translate into abundant grain supplies or low food prices. Instead, labor shifted from agriculture to industry and services, indicating a complex interplay between economic factors and labor distribution (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This suggests that the role of culture in shaping long-run growth may be more nuanced than simply being a direct cause; it interacts with broader economic and social structures. Akyeampong and Fofack's focus on gender roles and colonial legacies highlights the importance of understanding how different social groups contribute to economic development. This perspective underscores the need for a more comprehensive approach that considers both historical and contemporary factors in shaping long-run growth, including the complex interplay between cultural norms, gender dynamics, and economic structures.

Institutional frameworks significantly shape long-run growth by influencing investment patterns and labor shifts (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie shows that pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts along with efficiency considerations. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how different social groups contribute to economic development, aligning with Akyeampong and Fofack's focus on gender roles and colonial legacies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The historical evidence from both studies highlights that cultural norms and institutional structures are interdependent, shaping long-run growth trajectories.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is evident in how social capital influences economic performance. Ogilvie discusses Putnam's theory of social capital, where 'civic' cultures characterized by trust and reciprocity encourage institutions that foster growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England, for instance, led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth.

The evolution of public choice institutions also played a crucial role. North and Weingast demonstrate that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which were essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework provided a positive externality for private debt markets, underscoring how cultural norms and institutional structures interact to drive long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains a subject of scholarly debate. Shared finding across the cited sources is that institutions matter: they reduce transaction costs, secure property rights, and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement concerns whether cultural beliefs directly influence institutional structures or whether these beliefs are merely reflective of underlying political and economic dynamics. North and Weingast argue for the latter perspective, showing how financial institutions evolved to address fiscal crises rather than being driven by exogenous cultural factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The historical evidence from England's financial revolution settles this disagreement: it was the creation of secure property rights and reduction in transaction costs that fostered economic growth, not merely cultural beliefs.
