The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with some arguing that cultural factors are crucial in determining the efficacy and persistence of institutions, while others contend that institutional outcomes are primarily shaped by exogenous factors. The debate hinges on whether cultural beliefs and norms significantly influence the formation of institutions that foster economic development or if these institutions are predominantly determined by other forces such as geography, technology, or political dynamics. One position posits that cultural values can lead to the establishment of efficient institutions that promote growth, while another view suggests that institutions are largely shaped by external constraints and historical contingencies. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship could provide insights into why some societies thrive economically while others stagnate, offering potential pathways for policy interventions aimed at fostering economic development. This debate underscores the need to examine how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to produce long-term economic outcomes, setting the stage for a nuanced exploration of the interplay between culture and institutions in driving growth.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions, offering an alternative explanation that emphasizes efficiency over cultural influences. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for key institutions like serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the idea that these institutions were primarily driven by cultural beliefs or values, instead highlighting how power dynamics and resource allocation shaped institutional outcomes. Austin similarly rejects the notion of a simple geography thesis, advocating for a more dynamic view where Africans' responses to their environments played a crucial role in shaping economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). However, this stream does not fully account for the endogenous cultural factors that may have influenced these distributional conflicts and institutional structures, leaving open the question of how cultural beliefs might still play a significant role in shaping institutions. Moreover, Ogilvie acknowledges the interdependency of individual institutions within broader frameworks, suggesting that cultural values could be an underlying factor influencing these complex systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This recognition points to a potential bridge between distributional conflicts and cultural influences, highlighting the need for further investigation into how cultural factors might interact with power dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes.

Ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). The interplay between cultural values and institutional development is evident through the evolution of rhetoric that shaped economic outcomes. McCloskey argues that the "Great Enrichment" was driven by a new rhetoric that fostered innovative circumstances, which in turn influenced subsequent rhetoric and economic growth. This dynamic is particularly notable in countries like the Netherlands and Britain, where cultural shifts enabled the proliferation of ideas conducive to economic development. The Dutch example illustrates how a culture that embraced innovation and trade could drive economic growth. Mokyr further elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He notes that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by international conferences, periodicals, and personal communications, highlighting the interconnectedness of cultural exchange and economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The spread of tacit knowledge, which includes skills and expertise not always codified in written form, played a crucial role in this process. The transmission of ideas was practical, deeply embedded in society's social fabric. This interconnectedness suggests that cultural factors were instrumental in creating an environment where new ideas could flourish and drive economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta show that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). They argue that this divergence was driven by structural changes in Europe. The Black Death initiated these long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These changes included increased mobility, flexibility, and institutional change within guilds, fostering an environment conducive to technological innovation. The scarcity of labor due to the plague drove up wages, incentivizing labor-saving technologies that enhanced productivity (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This dynamic is crucial as it illustrates how exogenous shocks can catalyze structural changes that drive long-term economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta emphasize that while institutional change was pivotal in Europe, it did not necessarily translate into high agricultural labor productivity everywhere. This underscores that the impact of structural changes depends on local conditions and institutions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1).

North and Weingast assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). They argue that the financial revolution in England played a pivotal role in securing long-term success, suggesting that even other forces would have led to slower and less decisive outcomes without these institutional changes. This perspective is supported by Hornbeck's analysis of how barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). The institutions governing public debt in England provided a significant positive externality for private debt markets, facilitating economic growth through secure financial transactions. This dynamic highlights that the evolution of property rights through institutional changes can foster economic activity by ensuring stable and predictable market conditions.

Hornbeck's work also underscores the importance of social norms complementing legal frameworks (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). In modern California, farmers and ranchers rely on social norms to control cattle, indicating that cultural practices can reinforce property rights even when strict legal responsibilities are less stringent. This suggests that while institutional changes are crucial for economic growth, they operate most effectively when supported by a cultural environment that reinforces property rights and market stability.

North argues that institutions are fundamental to economic growth (North 1989, p.1). He and Acemoglu et al. together show that they shape the environment in which markets operate by defining property rights and enforcing contracts (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework reduces transaction costs, facilitating trade and investment, key drivers of economic growth. For example, North highlights how the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering long-term capital markets and economic expansion (North 1989, p.9).

Acemoglu et al. further demonstrate that institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This insight underscores the importance of institutional quality: while institutions are crucial for economic development, their impact depends on whether they foster or hinder productive activities. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often crafted to benefit European colonists, not to stimulate long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the nature of institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, significantly influences their effect on economic performance.

This perspective raises questions about the interplay between institutions and other factors such as culture. It is clear that the impact of institutions on economic growth cannot be fully understood without considering the broader socio-cultural context in which they operate.

The slave trade is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa that has persisted and impacted long-term economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the historical legacy of the slave trade, which decimated African populations and disrupted social structures, has left a lasting imprint on cultural norms, particularly trust. This mistrust, in turn, hinders economic growth by reducing social cohesion and cooperation, both crucial for effective institutional functioning.

Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates that the slave trades led to political instability and weakened states, which are critical determinants of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.1). The disruption caused by the slave trade fragmented African societies, making it difficult to establish strong, centralized institutions. This fragmentation persists today, contributing to Africa's underdevelopment. Thus, the impact of the slave trade on African economic development is part of a broader historical narrative that includes but is not limited to cultural factors.

Ogilvie and Austin argue that distributional conflicts within societies are pivotal in shaping economic institutions, offering an alternative explanation for pre-industrial Europe's institutional framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective emphasizes the role of power dynamics among different social groups. The institutional framework in pre-industrial Europe was thus shaped by these conflicts, which often led to institutions that favored the powerful at the expense of efficiency.

This focus on distributional conflicts is not limited to Europe. Sokoloff and Engerman illustrate a similar dynamic in New World societies, where institutional structures were heavily influenced by the distribution of wealth and power. The paper argues that these institutions, which often restricted competition and maintained inequality, were shaped by the interests of elites who used government policies to secure their advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This historical narrative suggests a broader pattern where economic institutions are not solely determined by efficiency but are deeply rooted in the power dynamics within societies. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores how cultural and distributional factors can have long-lasting effects on economic development, even when they do not align with efficient outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Culture is a fundamental driver of economic institutions that shape long-term growth trajectories. The role of culture in shaping institutions can be seen through the lens of historical and contemporary analyses. Ogilvie shows how distributional conflicts provide an explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores how cultural factors, such as beliefs about trust and reciprocity, influence institutional structures that facilitate or hinder economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These findings align with North's emphasis on the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England highlights how cultural norms can shape economic outcomes by providing a stable market for public debt, which in turn supports private debt markets and overall economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The historical evidence from North and Weingast demonstrates that the development of secure property rights was crucial for long-term economic success. This shows how cultural factors embedded within institutions can drive economic growth over extended periods.

The interplay between culture, institutions, and economic outcomes is further illuminated by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of European colonies. Their work shows that differences in mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This perspective underscores how cultural factors, such as beliefs about trust and reciprocity, influence institutional structures that facilitate or hinder economic growth. These findings align with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that the behavior of older industrial economies can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This highlights how cultural factors embedded within institutions can drive economic growth over extended periods.

The shared finding is that culture plays a critical role in shaping institutions that govern long-term economic outcomes, as North argues that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The precise mechanisms by which culture influences these institutions remain contentious. While Ogilvie emphasizes distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs about efficiency, Acemoglu et al. focus on mortality rates and extractive institutions. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the analysis of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This empirical comparison shows how cultural factors embedded within institutions can drive economic growth over extended periods, providing a comprehensive understanding of the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.
