The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins persistent economic performance across societies. One camp posits that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering growth. Another camp argues that institutional frameworks evolve independently of culture, driven by more material factors such as mortality rates or colonial policies. The debate hinges on whether cultural norms are autonomous forces shaping institutions or epiphenomena of deeper structural determinants.

Some scholars contend that cultural beliefs can significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie criticizes exogenous cultural theories for overlooking distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe, instead focusing on how cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that historical events like the slave trade can shape enduring cultural norms such as mistrust, which in turn affect economic outcomes. This mistrust persists today and accounts for a substantial portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Some scholars highlight material factors as crucial for shaping growth-oriented institutions. North and Weingast highlight how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. also stress that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of culture in economic growth is complex, involving both cultural beliefs and material factors. The debate continues as scholars grapple with how these elements interact to shape institutional frameworks that drive long-run economic performance.

North and Weingast argue that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They contend that the evolution of public choice in seventeenth-century England led to the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. North (1989, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that the neoclassical model's assumption of zero transaction costs overlooks the significance of institutions in facilitating economic development. This perspective underscores how secure property rights, enabled by robust institutional frameworks, are essential for sustained economic growth.

However, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) also acknowledge that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This suggests that while institutions are necessary, they may not be sufficient on their own. The role of culture in shaping institutional frameworks and economic outcomes cannot be overlooked. Economic growth is influenced by a complex interplay between material factors, such as secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, and cultural beliefs that underpin these institutions.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) contend that ideas and ideologies, along with material factors or institutions, drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective highlights the role of cultural shifts in fostering economic growth through changes in rhetoric and knowledge dissemination. McCloskey illustrates this dynamic by noting how the "new rhetoric" in countries like the Netherlands and France created new circumstances, which then reinforced the rhetoric that drove the Great Enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This interplay between rhetoric and material conditions underscores the importance of cultural beliefs in shaping economic outcomes. Mokyr further elaborates on this idea by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as a key driver of economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of knowledge, both codified and tacit, was facilitated through various channels such as periodicals, translations, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This widespread dissemination of ideas enabled nations to overcome institutional barriers and achieve sustained growth. The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus contingent on the broader socio-economic context in which these ideas operate. This suggests that while cultural factors are essential, they must align with material conditions to drive sustained economic development.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They show how variations in European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which in turn shaped current income per capita. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This process involves establishing institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had lasting effects on economic development. This highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-term economic outcomes.

This focus on distributional conflicts shifts the emphasis away from cultural factors as primary drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that the primary drivers of these institutions were distributional struggles rather than cultural influences. These power dynamics among groups led to the formation of institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communities, which in turn impacted economic efficiency. This perspective highlights how the distribution of political and economic power influenced institutional outcomes, suggesting that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by elite interests and intra-elite competition for control over land, labour, and resources. The central claim is that these distributional conflicts are better at explaining the rise of certain institutions than efficiency or cultural factors. When examining the European family system, Laslett's analysis shows how interdependencies between different institutional frameworks, including labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This highlights that economic growth can be facilitated by a complex interplay of institutions rather than being driven solely by cultural factors.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that Africa's slave trades fostered a lasting culture of mistrust which persists today. The slave trade disrupted social structures by removing large segments of the population and instilling fear and suspicion among those left behind. This culture of mistrust, in turn, undermined collective action and weakened institutions, creating a vicious cycle where low trust levels reinforced institutional failure. Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrates that this institutional weakness manifested as political instability and fragmented states, which are detrimental to economic development. The slave trade's impact on African economies was not limited to its effect on trust and institutions; it also had direct consequences for economic activity. For instance, the removal of a significant portion of the population due to the slave trade led to labor shortages, which hindered agricultural production and other economic activities. This shows that while culture plays a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, it is not the sole determinant; structural factors such as labor supply also have a significant impact on growth.

The interplay between technological advancement and social changes is central to driving economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth involves complex dynamics influenced by both exogenous factors and the interplay of technological advance and social change. These factors are driven by institutional changes and investment patterns in agriculture, not straightforwardly by cultural influences. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that high levels of investment in southern Indian agriculture led to elevated productivity during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This investment was driven by significant economic development and low silver wages, not merely geographical factors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Abramovitz notes that social competence for exploiting advanced methods varied significantly across regions and periods, so this pattern does not universally apply. The erratic productivity levels observed in different countries indicate that the gaps between existing and best-practice technology were influenced by both technological advancements and social changes (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Furthermore, this variability suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its impact can be moderated by social factors such as institutional changes and labor mobility. This interplay highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development, where technological advancement and social change are intricately linked but not solely determined by cultural influences.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in how cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions and their efficiency. Cultural theories posit that non-economic beliefs can influence institutional structures and economic performance. For example, Ogilvie shows that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's decline in these beliefs led to a decrease in guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency. In this view, civic cultures that value horizontal social networks encourage institutions favourable for economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The influence of cultural beliefs on institutional frameworks extends beyond individual institutions to entire systems. Ogilvie notes the interdependency within pre-industrial European family systems, which included labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions. This interconnected framework contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Acemoglu et al. similarly observe that favourable contracting institutions are partially interdependent with property rights institutions, reinforcing the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated components (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The persistence of cultural influences on institutions is further illustrated by the long-term effects of historical events and policies. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy highlights how historical experiences shape contemporary social norms and economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman also show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Institutional frameworks are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as demonstrated by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. The evolution of institutions governing public choice during this period was crucial for securing property rights and promoting economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical example underscores how cultural and institutional factors intertwine to drive long-run growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth, as North shows that with positive transaction costs, institutions play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (North 1989, p.1). The precise mechanisms through which culture influences these institutions remain debated. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency, highlighting the role of cultural beliefs and values in shaping institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is provided by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. Their study shows how the evolution of institutions governing public choice led to secure property rights and economic growth, demonstrating the interdependence of cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks in driving long-run economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.24).
