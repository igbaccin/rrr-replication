The role of culture in shaping institutions that underpin long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence institutional frameworks, fostering environments conducive to economic growth. For example, Ogilvie highlights how Catholic beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is complemented by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that features like trust and reciprocity, rooted in civic cultures, encourage institutions that promote growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) . Others contend that the impact of culture on institutions is indirect. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors may influence institutional development through intermediary variables such as health outcomes and economic institutions. The live disagreement centers on whether culture's role is direct or indirect in fostering growth-enhancing institutions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of institutional development, where cultural factors interact with other variables over time to shape economic outcomes. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1) . This view underscores the importance of secure property rights and low transaction costs, which are often influenced by cultural norms and beliefs. Understanding how culture interacts with these factors is crucial for comprehending long-run economic growth.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This dynamic, partly endogenous version of institutional evolution contrasts with the idea that culture dominates institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth. Colonial powers introduced diverse institutional frameworks based on local mortality rates; high mortality led to extractive institutions which persisted and affected current income per capita (Austin 2008, p.25; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutional changes were pivotal in shaping economic outcomes. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial powers to maximize rents, highlights the enduring effects of colonial policies on economic development. However, Austin cautions that the African story suggests a more nuanced interaction between environment and technology, where Africans' responses to their environments also played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts were the primary driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. These conflicts led to the establishment of institutions such as serfdom and guilds, which were designed to manage power dynamics rather than maximize efficiency or align with cultural preferences. This perspective highlights how elites shaped institutions to maintain their dominance over resources and labour, thus influencing long-run growth trajectories. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) shows that the interdependency of economic institutions within a wider framework was crucial for understanding institutional evolution in pre-industrial Europe. The European family system, community-based welfare systems, and other interconnected institutions played significant roles in fostering early industrialization and capitalism. This suggests that the interplay between different institutions created a complex web that influenced economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) caution that while institutional frameworks are important, they must be understood within the broader context of colonial histories and factor endowments. The differential paths of development in Latin America underscore how initial conditions and historical legacies shape institutions over time, thereby affecting long-run growth. This dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional frameworks, and historical contexts reveals that culture alone cannot account for the fundamental causes of long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. By establishing the Bank of England (1694) and developing new financial instruments, transaction costs declined dramatically, reflecting increased security of long-term property rights (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of these institutions provided the necessary conditions for England's transition to sustained economic development. However, the benefits of secure property rights extend beyond financial stability; they also fostered political freedom by balancing power structures within society. North notes that gains from trade are central to economic prosperity, with specialization and division of labor enhancing productivity through technological change and resource allocation (North 1989, p.1). These institutional changes in England illustrate how robust property rights can drive long-term growth, but the implications for other societies remain complex. While secure property rights were essential for England's success, the path to similar outcomes in other contexts may depend on varied historical legacies and initial conditions.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the historical experience of slavery has left enduring effects on social capital, particularly interpersonal trust. This legacy is not confined to economic outcomes but extends to political stability, as Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrates through the link between slave exports and weakened state centralization. The persistence of mistrust can be attributed to the long-term political instability that resulted from the slave trades, which in turn hindered the development of robust institutions (Nunn 2008, p.27). This institutional weakness is a critical factor in understanding Africa's economic underdevelopment, as it directly influences contemporary economic performance. However, Nunn (2008, p.27) cautions against attributing Africa’s current challenges solely to this historical legacy, noting that recent empirical research suggests multiple factors at play (Nunn 2008, p.1).

Broadberry and Gupta (Pamuk 2007, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2) argue that economic development in early modern Europe was characterized by significant divergence between regions, with northwest Europe experiencing notable growth while other parts of the continent lagged behind. This divergence is evident in both urbanization patterns and real wage trends, which reflect broader institutional changes. The role of urban centers such as London (van Zanden 1999, p.6) highlights how economic development varied across Europe. These differences suggest that regional factors, including institutional frameworks and investment patterns, played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Pamuk (2007, p.5; 2007, p.24) notes that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, contributing to greater mobility and flexibility in labor markets and fostering technological innovations. These shifts were not uniform across Europe but were particularly pronounced in northwest Europe, where they led to a significant wage gap by 1450. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15); van Zanden (1999, p.6) caution that while institutional changes and investment patterns are important, they do not fully account for the complex interplay of factors driving economic development. The divergence in Europe underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where cultural and institutional factors interact in ways that are not straightforwardly deterministic.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Hopkins 2009, p.8), together with Hopkins, focus on how institutional reforms and historical economic patterns have influenced African economies. They argue that the legacy of colonial rule and precolonial history significantly shaped current institutions in Africa. The congruence between modern and traditional institutions enhances governmental legitimacy, which in turn fosters development-friendly policies. Historical patterns of export production also played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories across Africa. Broadberry and Gardner highlight that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This finding suggests that the economic performance of African countries was more robust than previously thought, challenging the narrative of stagnation. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional reforms can be tailored to specific historical contexts to promote long-run growth in Africa.

The Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge are distinct phenomena that preceded economic growth (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). The Industrious Revolution involved increased labor input and new consumption patterns that facilitated economic growth. This revolution was evident in Europe's urban network, which connected cities in systems of commercial interaction, establishing a framework for regional economic development where industrial growth could occur (de Vries 1994, p.5). Mokyr further elaborates on the significance of useful knowledge, highlighting that much of this knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills rather than being codified in written form. The transmission of this tacit knowledge depended heavily on personal interactions and training, underscoring the importance of social networks in disseminating economic advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The growth of useful knowledge did not directly address how cultural factors influence institutions as fundamental causes of long-run growth. This highlights that while both phenomena were crucial for economic development, their mechanisms do not directly engage with the role of culture in shaping institutional frameworks. Consequently, understanding the Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge provides valuable context but does not fully elucidate the specific ways in which cultural factors dominate institutions to drive long-term economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women's roles in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods were pivotal, yet these contributions have often been overlooked (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack note that the gendered division of labor established during colonial times has had enduring effects on production systems. Goldin argues that similar transformations occurred in the U.S., where shifts in employment and education patterns began to reshape women’s economic roles starting from the early 20th century. These changes included increased demand for female office workers, growth of educational institutions, and alterations in household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin cautions that these transformations were influenced by various exogenous factors, including institutional changes that made part-time work more accessible. This highlights the complex interplay between cultural norms, economic opportunities, and institutional frameworks in shaping women's roles in economic development.

Culture plays an integral role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth by influencing social norms and values that underpin institutional frameworks. Ogilvie's work highlights how cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance security of property rights, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of these institutions is a product of cultural and social dynamics as well as a response to economic efficiency. For instance, North and Weingast emphasize the role of the financial revolution in England, where institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further evidenced by the impact of colonial constructs on gender roles and production systems in Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting how cultural beliefs and social norms can shape economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This cultural influence extends beyond individual behaviors to the broader institutional framework, as seen in the interdependencies between various institutions such as labor markets, credit markets, and welfare systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in dominating institutions is also evident in the context of European mortality rates and their impact on colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This shows how cultural and environmental factors can interact to shape institutional frameworks that drive long-run economic growth.

Culture's role in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is grounded in the shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9). There remains disagreement over whether cultural beliefs are exogenous or endogenous to institutional development. Ogilvie argues that cultural theories provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency alone, suggesting an exogenous influence of culture on institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). In contrast, Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, implying a more dynamic, endogenous relationship between culture and institutions (Austin 2008, p.25).

The specific historical evidence and empirical comparisons provided in these studies settle this disagreement. North shows how financial institutions in England reduced transaction costs and enhanced property rights security, fostering long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This empirical case demonstrates the endogenous role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutional frameworks that drive economic development. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of European mortality rates and colonial institutions provides a comparative measurement type that highlights how cultural and environmental factors interact to shape institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
