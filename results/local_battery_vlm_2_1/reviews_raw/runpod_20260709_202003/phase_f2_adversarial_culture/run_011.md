Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth by influencing the beliefs and values that underpin societal structures. The debate centers on whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional development or if other variables, such as geography or political structures, hold more explanatory power. One key position argues that cultural beliefs directly influence the efficiency and structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting long-run growth. Another view suggests that while culture may play a role, it interacts with other factors to shape institutions. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for grasping how societies evolve economically over time.

The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks highlights the complexity of economic development. Cultural theories suggest that beliefs about trust, reciprocity, and social networks can foster institutions that promote growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' posits that civic cultures characterized by high levels of trust and horizontal social networks encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. This perspective is supported by historical analyses showing how cultural beliefs influenced the structure and efficiency of guilds in medieval Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The evolution of these institutions, in turn, had significant implications for long-run economic performance.

Cultural factors also interact with broader institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes. For example, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency underscores how cultural beliefs can be embedded within larger institutional structures, influencing their development and impact on growth. The creation of secure property rights, facilitated by cultural norms and institutions, played a crucial role in England's economic growth during the seventeenth century (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This highlights how cultural factors can interact with political and economic institutions to foster long-run growth.

The role of culture extends beyond its direct influence on institutions. Cultural beliefs can also act as antecedents that trigger institutional changes. For example, the gender-based colonial constructs in Africa significantly impacted the dynamics of gender relations, which had long-term consequences for economic production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This suggests that cultural norms and values can shape economic outcomes by influencing the division of labor and resource allocation within societies.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the role of other factors, such as geography and political structures. While cultural beliefs may influence institutional development, they do not operate in isolation. For instance, differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This indicates that while culture may play a role in shaping institutions, it interacts with other factors to determine economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey posits that cultural shifts, particularly the rise of bourgeois values, enabled the Great Enrichment by fostering a new rhetoric that legitimized innovation and entrepreneurship. This cultural transformation was a fundamental driver of institutional change. The spread of useful knowledge, as Mokyr highlights, was crucial for economic growth; this knowledge diffused rapidly within Europe through various channels, including periodicals, translations, and conferences. Tacit knowledge, which is difficult to codify, played a significant role in this process, often transmitted through personal interactions and training. The diffusion of ideas varied across European societies. Mokyr notes that while useful knowledge spread widely within Europe, its adoption varied based on cultural receptivity. For instance, Spain's economic growth was throttled by conservative cultural attitudes despite its early hegemony. This variation underscores how cultural beliefs can either accelerate or impede the adoption of new ideas and technologies, thereby influencing long-run economic outcomes. The role of culture in shaping institutions is thus intricate, with specific ideologies and values facilitating or hindering the diffusion of knowledge and innovation.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) establish that the transatlantic slave trade had profound cultural consequences for Africa, creating enduring legacies of mistrust that continue to influence economic outcomes. The mechanism by which this historical trauma shapes African development is through both economic losses and impacts on social capital and institutional frameworks. The slave trades' destabilizing effects led to weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.27), a condition that Nunn describes as "state antiquity." This political fragmentation has long-term repercussions, as recent empirical research shows that the depth of experience with state-level institutions is positively correlated with real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.1). Consequently, mistrust and weak institutions are deeply intertwined; the slave trade's legacy persists through these institutional channels. Nunn advises against attributing Africa’s underdevelopment solely to this historical trauma, as other factors such as colonialism also play a role. Thus, while the slave trade is a significant antecedent in shaping cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks that impact long-run economic growth, it is part of a broader set of historical and contemporary forces influencing African development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This argument is shared by North (1989, p.1) who contends that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants of economic growth, fails to account for positive transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). Instead, institutions matter significantly in shaping economic outcomes, particularly through their role in securing property rights. The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and subsequent financial innovations dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated a long-term capital market and provided a positive externality for private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of stable public debt markets fostered parallel growth in private debt markets, enhancing overall economic stability and growth. North and Weingast note that while institutions are necessary for economic growth and political freedom, other historical forces can qualify their impact. Thus, while secure property rights are pivotal for economic growth, their full potential is realized through the interplay of robust institutions and favorable historical conditions.

Colonial institutions were pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth across former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. show that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule had profound and lasting effects on economic performance. In particular, they identify a strong correlation between mortality rates faced by European settlers and the types of institutions implemented in those colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than fostering long-term growth, persists today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This persistence is evident in the significant impact of early colonial institutions on current income per capita, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. caution that while these institutions are critical, other factors can qualify their effects. For instance, the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that while colonial institutions laid a foundational role in shaping long-term outcomes, they were not the sole determinant (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency or cultural factors, are crucial in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These scholars show that the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were significantly influenced by power dynamics and inequality. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

These scholars highlight how the distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to institutional frameworks that favored the elite. These interconnected systems contributed to early industrialization and capitalism, underscoring how institutional frameworks shaped by distributional conflicts can drive long-term economic growth. Ogilvie notes that these interdependencies mean institutions cannot be studied in isolation; the entire framework must be considered. This holistic view suggests that while distributional conflicts are pivotal, their effects on long-run growth are mediated through complex institutional interactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of women in economic development is an important aspect that intersects with cultural and institutional factors but does not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight the transformative contributions of African women to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, demonstrating how gender roles shaped by cultural norms significantly influenced the production system. For instance, in matrilineal societies like Asante, women's bloodlines defined wealth transmission and community reproduction, underscoring their indispensable role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Goldin similarly emphasizes the quiet revolution in U.S. women’s employment and education, showing that shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's roles were preconditions for broader economic changes (Goldin 2006, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). The impact of these gender dynamics on long-run growth is complex and mediated through various institutional and cultural factors.

These structural changes in Europe highlight the multifaceted nature of economic development during specific historical periods, driven by a combination of demographic shifts and institutional reforms. Van Zanden argues that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2), a perspective shared by Pamuk who highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes leading to wage gaps across Europe, notably between the northwest and other regions by 1450 (de Vries 1994, p.18). This period saw significant shifts in labor markets, with increased mobility and flexibility, as well as institutional changes within guilds. These conditions fostered an era of technological innovations aimed at saving labor costs (Pamuk 2007, p.24), illustrating how demographic shocks can catalyze economic restructuring. The Industrious Revolution, characterized by heightened labor input and new consumption patterns, further underscores this dynamic. De Vries emphasizes that the Industrious Revolution was a distinct phenomenon preceding industrialization, marked by increased market engagement and urban network development (de Vries 1994, p.5). This period saw Europe's cities connected in systems of commercial interaction, establishing frameworks for regional economic development. de Vries also notes that while this period was not stagnant, it still faced constraints of Malthusian checks on population growth. Thus, European economic development during these periods was a complex interplay of demographic shifts, institutional changes, and technological advancements, all contributing to long-term structural changes that facilitated subsequent industrialization.

The literature on institutional reforms and the economic history of Africa highlights that institutions are central to understanding development and poverty reduction in this context (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Bryant 2006, p.35). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that historical investigation is crucial because current African institutions are products of a past that extends beyond colonial rule. Bryant (2006, p.35) adds that transitions from traditional modes of production to industrial ones require cumulative cultural, institutional, and technological resources. This perspective underscores the importance of long-term institutional development in shaping contemporary economic outcomes. Bryant (2006, p.35) cautions against overly narrow views of causality, suggesting that effective change must consider both immediate processes and deeper historical contexts. The implication is that short-term policy interventions may fall short if they do not align with Africa's deep-seated cultural norms and inherited institutions. This highlights the need for policies that are sensitive to Africa’s unique institutional legacy.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) jointly establish that historical economic phenomena are multifaceted and cannot be reduced to simple causal narratives (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution, investment patterns, and policy experiences across different periods. This perspective is complemented by Broadberry & Gardner (2022), who argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Together, these findings underscore the importance of considering diverse historical contexts when examining long-run growth. Broadberry and Gupta's (2006) analysis demonstrates that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that while institutional factors are crucial, they must be considered within a broader temporal framework that accounts for pre-existing economic conditions. The implication is that understanding long-run growth requires an appreciation of both immediate historical contexts and deeper structural changes over extended periods.

Hornbeck's study of barbed wire fencing and Stern's critique of the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) demonstrate that technological innovations and environmental changes can significantly influence economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; Stern 2004, p.3). Both Hornbeck and Stern highlight how these factors can alter agricultural practices and industrial output, respectively. Barbed wire fencing enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). The EKC hypothesis suggests that economic growth initially increases environmental degradation but eventually leads to improvement as economies shift towards less polluting industries (Stern 2004, p.1). Stern notes that this shift is not guaranteed, however, and may be influenced by changes in industrial output mixes during development phases. This implies that while technological innovations can enhance agricultural productivity, the environmental impact of economic growth remains complex and contingent on broader structural changes in industrial output.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that underlie long-run economic growth. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both highlight the significance of institutional frameworks in economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs and values, such as those promoting social capital, can foster institutions that encourage economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. meanwhile, demonstrate how differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial. Ogilvie cites studies that suggest various institutional systems, such as labour markets, land markets, and credit markets, are interconnected and can collectively promote economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North echoes this by emphasizing that secure property rights, facilitated through institutions like the Bank of England, are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of culture in institutional evolution is further underscored by Austin and Akyeampong and Fofack. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic view of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.2). Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, illustrating how cultural norms can influence economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The creation of stable markets for public debt in England, as detailed by North and Weingast, provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets, further emphasizing the role of institutions in fostering growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The historical evidence shows that culture underpins the evolution of institutions governing economic activity. This is particularly evident in the case of England's financial revolution, which North and Weingast argue was crucial for long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutions leading to this revolution played a necessary role in making possible both economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Meanwhile, Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds, illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The disagreement centers on whether culture or geography is more influential. North and Weingast show that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The evidence from England’s financial revolution illustrates how cultural norms shaped institutions and fostered economic growth. This historical comparison shows that it is culture, not geography, that fundamentally shapes institutions governing long-run growth.
