The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with some arguing that cultural beliefs and values significantly shape economic institutions and thereby influence growth. Others posit that efficiency considerations primarily drive institutional development, while additional perspectives highlight the importance of distributional conflicts or exogenous factors like mortality rates. The central disagreement revolves around whether culture acts as a primary driver of institutional change that fosters long-term economic growth, or if it is merely a secondary factor influenced by other determinants such as geographic conditions, technological advancements, or political structures. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over time and the relative importance of cultural factors in this process. This debate directly impacts how policymakers and economists approach development strategies, as recognizing culture's role could lead to more nuanced and effective interventions. The question remains: does culture fundamentally shape institutions that drive long-run growth, or are other factors more decisive? Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) identify the slave trade as a key factor in shaping African cultural norms, specifically fostering a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, given that trust is a crucial component of social capital and is positively correlated with economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that the adverse effects of slavery on state centralization are consistent with historical accounts of prolonged political instability, which has had lasting impacts on Africa's economic performance. Recent empirical research supports this view, showing a positive correlation between a country’s history of state development and current economic outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn further contends that the legacy of slavery can explain part of Africa's contemporary underdevelopment, suggesting that the slave trade's impact on African institutions was profound. The persistence of this institutional weakness is evident in the high levels of corruption in many African countries today (Nunn 2008, p.1).

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) assert that the institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution. These conflicts resulted in institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The persistence of these unequal institutions is evident in the enduring disparities in wealth and political power observed today. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that "different framework (which may even resist its encroachment), is not unlikely to fail" (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This suggests that institutional frameworks are interdependent, reinforcing the idea that distributional conflicts create a self-perpetuating system of inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) build on this by showing that "the great majority were characterized virtually from the outset by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power" (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implication is that the initial endowments and subsequent distributional conflicts set societies on divergent trajectories of development.

McCloskey and Mokyr share the stance that ideas were pivotal in driving economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey posits that the cultural shift towards embracing new ideas was crucial for economic development. He highlights that countries like Spain, despite being an economic powerhouse in the 16th century, struggled with growth due to conservative resistance to change. This resistance hindered the adoption of new ideas and technologies, which were essential for economic advancement (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr's perspective complements this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge as the primary driver of economic growth. He underscores that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe facilitated economic progress, as tacit knowledge, skills and techniques not always codified in writing, played a significant role. This implicit understanding was transmitted through personal interactions and apprenticeships (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr shows that the movement of both tacit and codified useful knowledge across borders was essential for the Industrial Revolution's success. The implication is that while ideas are crucial for economic growth, their impact depends on the cultural receptiveness and institutional frameworks of societies.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) agree that institutions are crucial for economic growth. Institutions shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity; they determine how efficiently resources are allocated and how effectively property rights are protected, and thus they affect long-term economic performance. The evolution of financial institutions in England, such as the creation of the Bank of England, illustrates this point. This development led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn fostered economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.24) show that these institutions provided a positive externality for both public and private debt markets. These findings underscore how institutional changes can have far-reaching effects on economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide empirical support for the role of institutions in shaping long-term growth, showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita by influencing economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) emphasize that these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may not always prioritize long-run growth, but instead maximize their own rents. This highlights a condition under which the institutional determinants of growth can be compromised when powerful groups design institutions to serve their interests and foster broad-based economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) corroborate that institutions play an important role in determining economic outcomes, showing how colonialism introduced institutional changes that reversed relative incomes among former European colonies. This finding underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions evolve and their impact on long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for colonizers and promote economic development. This observation qualifies the downstream implication that institutional changes always lead to long-run growth: while institutions are a critical determinant of economic performance, their impact depends on who designs them and for what purpose.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for compressing different historical periods and paths, arguing that this approach overlooks the dynamic and endogenous processes at play in economic development. This critique is supported by Bryant (2006, p.35), who contends that revisionist positions dismissing Eurocentric views are analytically incoherent because they ignore the cumulative effects of technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms inherited from the past (Bryant 2006, p.4). These perspectives highlight that economic development is a result of immediate processes but deeply influenced by long-term historical trajectories. This view underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay between institutional evolution, investment, policy experience, and cultural norms in shaping long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). The implication is that while institutions are crucial, their impact on growth varies across different historical contexts, qualifying the notion that institutional changes always lead to long-term economic development in a uniform manner. This nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics suggests that the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth must be examined within specific historical and cultural frameworks.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) together show that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 was marked by significant regional disparities, with the northwest experiencing growth while other areas stagnated. The implications of these findings for understanding long-run growth are significant: the role of culture as a dominant factor in shaping institutions must be understood within this context of uneven development. Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this by noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including greater mobility and institutional reform, which contributed to the wage gap between northwest Europe and other regions (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This suggests that cultural factors may have interacted with these historical shocks to influence institutional development. Thus, examining the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth requires considering both regional economic disparities and the impact of exogenous events like the Black Death.

The new economic history of Africa focuses on institutional reforms as a means to eliminate poverty and argues that historical investigation is crucial for understanding current institutions in the region (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This focus on institutions is grounded in the hypothesis that institutional variables are more significant than other factors in explaining development, highlighting the importance of long-term historical perspectives. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that this approach calls for investigating periods extending beyond colonial rule into precolonial history and even prehistory to understand the gradual nature of institutional change.

One specific mechanism explored by Hopkins (2009, p.8) is the role of indigenous institutions in shaping contemporary economic development. Pierre Englebert, a political scientist cited by Hopkins (2009, p.8), argues that congruence between modern institutions and indigenous ones enhances government legitimacy and fosters growth-promoting institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective underscores the potential for cultural factors, embedded in precolonial institutions, to influence long-run economic outcomes. They also note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting a more nuanced view of Africa's economic history than previously characterized. This indicates that while institutional reforms are crucial, their effectiveness may be contingent on cultural and historical contexts, which can either facilitate or hinder growth.

Technological and agricultural advancements significantly influenced economic development by enhancing productivity and enabling better resource management (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; de Vries 1994, p.18). Hornbeck (2010, p.6) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains improved agricultural productivity by strengthening property rights and reducing livestock damage. One specific mechanism through which these advancements influenced development is the enhancement of resource management capabilities. de Vries (1994, p.18) highlights that this process was not uniform across regions and depended on local conditions and social norms (de Vries 1994, p.5). This suggests that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, their impact is contingent on the cultural and institutional context in which they are implemented. This indicates that cultural factors play a crucial role in shaping the effectiveness of technological advancements.

Akyeampong and Fofack present women as key agents in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). They argue that the role of African women was crucial to community reproduction and wealth transmission, highlighting the importance of matrilineal societies where women's bloodlines defined inheritance. This dynamic underscores how cultural norms shaped economic activities and institutional structures. In matrilineal Akan states in particular, women were indispensable for maintaining societal cohesion through their reproductive roles. Goldin similarly highlights the transformative impact of changes in social norms and expectations on women's employment and education (Goldin 2006, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). The increased demand for female office workers and shifts in household production technology altered labor supply dynamics, illustrating how institutional and technological changes interact with cultural factors to influence economic outcomes. However, Goldin notes that these changes were not linear but rather driven by exogenous factors such as educational expansion and new employment opportunities. This suggests that while cultural norms can facilitate economic development, their interaction with institutional reforms and technological advancements is complex and multifaceted. The role of women in economic development thus intersects with broader cultural and institutional dynamics, influencing long-term growth trajectories through the interplay of gender roles and societal expectations.

Cultural norms play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions that drive long-term growth trajectories. Ogilvie's framework highlights how cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced the structure of guilds and their efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is further supported by Putnam, who argues that 'civic' cultures encourage institutions favouring economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework underscores the complexity of this relationship. Ogilvie acknowledges the interconnectedness of various institutions, such as labour markets, credit markets, and welfare systems, which collectively contribute to economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in shaping institutional frameworks is evident across different historical contexts. North and Weingast demonstrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective aligns with Austin's argument that institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others, reflecting a dynamic interplay between cultural norms and institutional development (Austin 2008, p.25).

The impact of culture on long-term economic outcomes is also evident in the role of women. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This contribution is intertwined with broader cultural and institutional dynamics, as seen in the matrilineal society of Asante, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture significantly influences economic institutions and growth trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The remaining disagreement centres on whether cultural norms are the primary driver or if they interact with other factors such as technological advancements. Austin's work shows that African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This suggests a more dynamic interplay between culture and technology in shaping long-term growth trajectories. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the role of women in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods; Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis of matrilineal societies demonstrates how cultural norms can directly influence institutional frameworks and economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).
