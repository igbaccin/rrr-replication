The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is how to understand the roots of modern economic development and whether cultural factors are integral to institutional formation. The debate centers around two positions: one emphasizes the primacy of exogenous cultural beliefs, positing them as direct drivers of institutional structures; while another focuses on endogenous processes within institutions themselves. This disagreement extends beyond academic circles, influencing policy discussions about fostering growth in developing economies.

One position argues that culture influences economic growth through its impact on institutions. Ogilvie contends that the efficiency of institutions can be shaped by cultural beliefs, such as those related to the afterlife and guild rules, which are rooted in religious doctrines. This perspective is supported by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks foster economic growth. These cultural traits encourage institutions that promote economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Nunn and Wantchekon similarly find evidence for the persistence of cultural norms shaped by historical events, such as the slave trade, which has led to a culture of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust persists today and affects institutional outcomes, including trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The second position emphasizes that institutions themselves shape economic growth through their impact on transaction costs and property rights. North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they reduce the cost of transactions, which is a critical factor in the wealth of nations. The creation of stable markets for public debt, as seen in seventeenth-century England, exemplifies how secure property rights fostered by institutional changes can lead to long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast further illustrate this point by showing that the financial revolution in England was pivotal in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which were essential for both economic growth and political freedom. The institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a necessary role in making possible these outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The interplay between culture and institutions is complex. Culture can influence institutional structures through deeply held beliefs and norms, but institutions also shape cultural values over time. This dynamic relationship underscores the importance of understanding both cultural and institutional factors when studying long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note the financial revolution of England as a critical turning point, with the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development enhanced the security of property rights over time, which was crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.1) explains that institutions matter in economic growth because they facilitate specialization and division of labor, key features of modern economies. These institutions provided a large positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24), which underpinned long-term economic success. North (1989, p.1) also acknowledges that institutions are necessary for rapid and decisive growth but other forces might contribute to economic development at a slower pace.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and knowledge drive economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey (2015, p.10) contends that the emergence of liberal ideals in Northwestern Europe during 1600 to 1848 fostered an environment where innovation thrived, leading to significant economic change. This perspective posits that the Great Enrichment was propelled by new ideologies encouraging entrepreneurial activity and technological advancement.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) elaborates on this dynamic by highlighting how cultural shifts in rhetoric influenced economic outcomes. In France, for example, traditionalist rhetoric stifled growth until more progressive ideas took hold, whereas in countries like the Netherlands and Britain, liberal ideologies facilitated rapid economic development (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr (2005, p.56) complements this view by emphasizing that the diffusion of useful knowledge was a critical driver of economic progress. He notes that during the Enlightenment knowledge spread across Europe through various channels such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This underscores how ideas transcended institutional frameworks to stimulate growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) also points out that much of this knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and practices rather than codified in written texts (Mokyr 2005, p.14), which implies that the transmission of knowledge required personal interaction and training. This qualification highlights the complexity of how ideas drive economic growth, as it necessitates not just the generation of new knowledge but also its effective dissemination through social networks.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus nuanced.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that fundamentally shaped long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were the result of European settlement patterns influenced by mortality rates in colonies. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, which persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita. This mechanism is supported by a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17), highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies. The persistence of these institutions is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often extractive, designed to benefit European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective challenges the primacy of cultural determinants, suggesting instead that the institutional framework established during colonial rule has had a profound and lasting effect on economic development. Understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions requires acknowledging the deep historical roots of these institutions, which were shaped by colonial experiences.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Bryant (2006, p.35) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This pattern is evident in the differences in factor endowments across these societies, which led to extreme inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The resulting institutions were shaped by these initial conditions, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where unequal distributions of resources and power limited opportunities for broad-based economic development. Bryant (2006, p.35) argues that this institutional framework is deeply rooted in the long-term accumulation of technologies, social relations, and cultural norms (Bryant 2006, p.4). This perspective underscores how the cultural context, shaped by historical experiences, influences the evolution of institutions. This highlights a broader implication: while institutions are critical for long-run growth, their effectiveness is contingent on the underlying cultural context, which can either facilitate or hinder economic development.

Distributional conflicts and endogenous factors better explain pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds than exogenous cultural theories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that economic institutions should be understood within the broader framework of their historical context. She highlights that the interdependency between various institutions is crucial for understanding their evolution and impact on economic growth. For example, the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, potentially contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the complex interplay of institutions within a broader framework. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This critique suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific and context-dependent, further emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional frameworks.

The historical impact of the slave trade on African development has profoundly shaped cultural attitudes towards trust and cooperation which in turn have influenced long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the legacy of the transatlantic slave trade has left an enduring imprint on social capital, particularly in fostering a culture of mistrust. This mistrust acts as a barrier to economic development by hindering collective action and reducing the effectiveness of institutions (Nunn 2008, p.27). The mechanism through which this occurs is multifaceted: mistrust erodes social cohesion, making it difficult for communities to engage in collaborative efforts that drive economic growth. Furthermore, mistrust can undermine the legitimacy of state institutions, leading to political instability and weakened governance structures (Nunn 2008, p.1). This political instability further exacerbates economic underdevelopment by creating an environment where long-term planning and investment are challenging.

This legacy of mistrust has also been linked to contemporary issues such as corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). The historical experience of exploitation and betrayal during the slave trade era has fostered deep-seated suspicions about authority figures and institutions. This pervasive mistrust can deter individuals from participating in formal economic activities, opting instead for informal or underground economies where trust is less critical. These informal sectors often lack the regulatory oversight necessary to ensure fair practices and sustainable development. The resulting economic inefficiencies further impede Africa's ability to achieve long-term growth.
