The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake intellectually is the explanatory power of cultural factors versus other determinants such as geography, institutions, and technology. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions or whether these institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors. One position emphasizes that cultural norms influence collective action and institutional efficiency, while another posits that extractive institutions are the result of settler mortality rates and subsequent institutional frameworks. A third perspective argues that the role of women in pre-colonial and colonial Africa significantly impacted long-term economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. Furthermore, there is a debate on whether cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules or if institutions are interdependent within a broader framework. The interplay between these factors remains a critical area of inquiry. This review will explore how different streams of scholarship engage with these questions, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which culture influences economic growth. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights were pivotal in fostering England's economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, notably the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This institutional development reflected an increased security of property rights over time; this dimension was critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The political model outlined by North describes how the exchange of certain rights to Parliament for revenues was instrumental during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, highlighting the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework provided a large positive externality for the parallel development of markets for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Secure property rights are crucial for sustained growth but they alone do not guarantee it.

Ideas and ideologies were central to economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Bryant 2006, p.35; McCloskey 2015, p.9). McCloskey and other scholars have argued that the cultural norms and social relations of a society are crucial for its economic success, as they shape the way individuals interact and make decisions. This perspective emphasizes how ideas influence institutions rather than the reverse; it is not just the institutional framework itself but also the underlying cultural values that drive economic change. For example, McCloskey highlights how the "Franco-French War" in France illustrates a cultural struggle that hindered economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This narrative underscores that the cultural and ideological context can either facilitate or impede economic progress, independent of institutional structures.

Bryant elaborates on this by emphasizing that the revisionist view dismissing Eurocentric perspectives is analytically incoherent because it overlooks the cumulative impact of prior developments (Bryant 2006, p.35). This means that technological innovations, legal reforms, and social changes are all interconnected and build upon each other over time. The cultural norms and historical legacies of a society shape its institutions and economic outcomes in profound ways; these norms and legacies are dynamic, evolving with societal changes. Bryant's argument underscores the importance of understanding the broader context of cultural and ideological shifts when examining long-term economic growth, as these shifts can either facilitate or hinder progress. This dynamic interplay between culture and economics suggests that a society's trajectory is influenced by its evolving ideas and ideologies, not just its static institutions.

Distributional conflicts are posited to better explain core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency or cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the power dynamics between different social groups, as well as the pursuit of economic efficiency. Ogilvie argues that these institutions emerged from struggles over resources and control, with elites shaping policies to maintain their advantage. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality and the establishment of institutions that reinforced elite power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The distributional conflicts approach suggests that the persistence of these institutions can be explained through the interdependency of various institutional frameworks. For instance, Ogilvie notes that the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit systems, and welfare provisions, creating a complex web that influenced economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) This highlights how distributional conflicts not only shaped individual institutions but also their broader framework, which in turn affected long-term economic development. This focus on power dynamics may overlook the nuanced ways cultural beliefs can influence institutional evolution.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism by which colonial institutions impacted economic outcomes is through their enduring effects on property rights and political structures; these institutions created persistent barriers to economic development, as they were not designed to encourage productivity or innovation. Acemoglu et al. highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes and not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective suggests that the long-term effects of colonial institutions are more pronounced in regions where extractive practices were deeply entrenched, leading to persistent economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the impact of these institutions is not uniform; the effectiveness of institutional change depends on the political and economic contexts of the colonized regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This nuanced view underscores how colonial institutions can shape long-term economic trajectories through their influence on property rights and political structures, which in turn affect growth potential.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions, which are central to understanding economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. In this view, the decline in African economies is attributable to a dynamic interplay of endogenous factors and environmental responses, as well as colonial institutions (Austin 2008, p.25). This historical perspective suggests that institutional changes, such as those brought about by colonialism, did not uniformly lead to economic growth. The variability in outcomes highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors like investment patterns and technological adoption. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). This nuanced view underscores how cultural responses to environmental challenges can dominate institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories.

The role of women in economic development intersects with cultural and institutional factors, highlighting how gender dynamics shape long-run growth trajectories (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women were pivotal to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Goldin's findings complement this by illustrating how shifts in women's education, employment, and family dynamics transformed the U.S. economy. These changes were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and technological advancements in household production. The role of women in economic development is deeply rooted in cultural norms and societal expectations, as well as institutional change. For instance, in matrilineal societies like the Akan, women's roles were indispensable to community reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This underscores how cultural beliefs about gender can dominate institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic outcomes. Goldin notes that changes in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career began to shift significantly from the late 1960s onwards, indicating a broader cultural transformation that facilitated women's integration into the workforce (Goldin 2006, p.8). This dynamic interaction between culture and institutions suggests that long-run growth is determined by how institutional changes align with prevailing cultural norms as well as the changes themselves. The interplay of these factors can lead to significant shifts in economic trajectories, as seen in both African and U.S. contexts.

Nunn and Wantchekon's work on the slave trade highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The authors demonstrate that the legacy of the slave trade persists through reduced trust levels, which they attribute to Africa’s history of political instability and fragmented states. This mistrust has a significant impact on contemporary economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that slavery had adverse effects on state centralization, leading to long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). The resulting weakened states are unable to provide the necessary infrastructure and governance required for sustained economic growth. Furthermore, this historical context of mistrust and political instability contributes to modern corruption, as evidenced by recent empirical studies (Nunn 2008, p.1). The legacy of slavery extends to broader social and institutional frameworks beyond its immediate economic impact. The pervasive mistrust and weakened governance structures have created a challenging environment for economic development, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. This is evident in how cultural beliefs influence economic outcomes through the structure of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Ogilvie argues that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's disappearance of these beliefs caused guilds to decline, illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency. Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how cultures characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6), which shows that cultural beliefs and values can explain superior economic performance across regions like northern Italy compared to southern Italy.

Cultural frameworks also influence how different institutional systems interact. Ogilvie notes the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, such as the European family system being linked with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness indicates that the impact of culture on economic growth extends to entire institutional frameworks, not isolated to individual institutions. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show how European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore that cultural factors can shape long-run growth by influencing the development of economic institutions.

The role of culture in fostering secure property rights is crucial for economic growth. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights; this dimension was critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. North and Weingast further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, illustrating how cultural and institutional frameworks interact to drive long-run growth.

This dynamic perspective suggests that African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed economic outcomes. This shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; Austin 2008, p.25) indicates that the impact of culture on economic growth varies across different historical contexts.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the case of England during the seventeenth century. North and Weingast show how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in this period was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This case demonstrates that cultural factors can shape long-run growth by influencing the development of economic institutions, settling the disagreement about whether culture or institutions are more fundamental to economic growth.
