The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and institutional scholarship. At stake is our understanding of how societies evolve economically over centuries, with implications for how we view historical trajectories and contemporary development strategies. Scholars debate whether cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, directly influence institutional structures or are themselves shaped by institutions. This disagreement hinges on the extent to which culture can be seen as an antecedent, a rival cause, or a complementary factor in the development of institutions that drive economic growth.

Some scholars argue that institutions matter significantly in economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) emphasize that secure property rights fostered by institutional evolution were crucial for England's long-run success. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) highlight the role of institutions in accounting for current income differences among former European colonies.

Culture is seen as a significant player in shaping economic outcomes through its influence on trust and social norms. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects current levels of interpersonal trust. This cultural legacy has implications for institutional development and economic performance.

Culture does not always operate directly on institutions; it can also influence growth through its effects on economic behavior. North (1989, p.1; 1989, p.9) asserts that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which in turn facilitate economic growth by enabling specialization and division of labor. This perspective suggests that culture's role might be more nuanced, acting as an antecedent that influences the broader economic environment within which institutions operate.

The interplay between culture and institutions is complex. Scholars argue that institutions can shape cultural norms over time, creating a feedback loop where institutions reinforce certain cultural values while also being influenced by them. This dynamic relationship underscores the importance of understanding how cultural factors interact with institutional structures to drive long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions established during colonial times have had lasting effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Their analysis shows a strong correlation between settler mortality rates and the type of institutions implemented in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persist today. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, have significantly impacted current income per capita. This perspective emphasizes how colonial institutional legacies shape economic development trajectories independent of cultural factors.

The mechanism through which these institutions operate involves a feedback loop where initial conditions influence the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, reinforce economic disparities over time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers implemented institutions based on their mortality rates, which were often high in regions with harsh climates or disease environments. This led to the creation of extractive institutions that prioritized short-term gains for colonists over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions suggests that colonial legacies can override cultural influences, providing a rival explanation for long-run growth patterns.

Acemoglu et al.'s findings indicate that the impact of institutions on economic performance is substantial. Their empirical analysis shows a significant correlation between settler mortality rates and current institutional quality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This relationship underscores how colonial institutions, shaped by initial conditions and cultural norms, have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. also note that these institutions are often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which can hinder long-term growth. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic development, where cultural factors may play a secondary role.

The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic performance today, providing a compelling alternative explanation for long-run growth patterns.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) (Mokyr 2005, p.56) propose that ideas were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. The cultural struggle was a key element in this process, as new rhetoric created new circumstances which then generated further new rhetoric. This dynamic illustrates how ideologies can shape economic outcomes by influencing societal values and attitudes towards innovation and entrepreneurship.

The diffusion of useful knowledge played a crucial role in this process. Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth and this knowledge included tacit knowledge transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). This emphasis on the dissemination of ideas and knowledge underscores the importance of cultural factors in driving long-run growth.

McCloskey notes that the accumulation of information combined with interest does not drive institutional change. Instead, it is the new rhetoric that creates new circumstances, which then generate further new rhetoric (McCloskey 2015, p.10). This suggests that while ideas are crucial, they must be accompanied by a cultural shift that values and promotes economic growth. The transmission of tacit knowledge also depended on personal interactions and training, highlighting the role of social networks in disseminating useful knowledge.

These findings suggest that culture plays a significant role in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth by shaping societal values and attitudes towards innovation and entrepreneurship.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that economic growth is driven by complex interactions between technological advances and social dynamics. This perspective highlights how institutional changes and social competence influence productivity levels and economic development. Abramovitz notes that the adoption of new technologies requires access to knowledge and the social capability to implement them effectively (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Broadberry and Gupta build on this idea by examining the great divergence between Europe and Asia, showing that institutional changes in northwestern Europe led to high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that cultural factors, which shape institutions and social competence, are crucial for economic growth. Abramovitz also acknowledges that these processes can be erratic, with productivity levels sometimes failing to reflect the true gap between existing and best-practice technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). The implication is that while institutional changes and social competence are vital, cultural factors that influence these dynamics must be carefully managed to ensure sustained growth.

This institutional framework was pivotal as it provided a stable market for both public and private debt, thereby enhancing the security of long-term investments. This stability was critical for economic growth and sustaining political freedom.

The financial revolution in England illustrates a specific mechanism through which institutions fostered growth. The secure property rights enabled by these institutions allowed for the development of long-term capital markets, which are essential for sustained economic expansion. This institutional evolution provided a positive externality for the parallel development of private debt markets (North 1989, p.9). This nuance suggests that although institutional changes are vital, they are not the sole drivers of economic development. The interplay between institutions and cultural factors remains complex, with each influencing the other in ways that shape long-run growth trajectories.

The slave trade had profound and enduring effects on African development, shaping cultural attitudes that persist to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon highlight how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust, in turn, has significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes by affecting social capital and interpersonal trust. The historical impact of the slave trades on African development extends beyond cultural attitudes to include political instability and weakened states. These factors have contributed to reduced state centralization and fragmented governance structures. Nunn notes that the adverse effects of slavery are evident in contemporary issues such as corruption, which can be traced back to precolonial origins (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.1). The long-term political instability resulting from the slave trades has hindered Africa's economic performance by impeding state-level institution building. This historical legacy continues to shape current economic underdevelopment in Africa.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. The historical legacy of the slave trade has fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and continues to affect economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural factor is intertwined with political instability and weakened states, contributing to reduced state centralization and fragmented governance structures (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights and economic growth are fostered by institutional development. This period saw the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These developments highlight that institutions can create a positive externality for both public and private debt markets, thereby fostering long-term growth.

The impact of institutions on economic performance is further underscored by the role of European mortality rates in shaping colonial institutions. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This evidence shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are crucial in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.

The long-term effects of these historical legacies are evident in contemporary economic underdevelopment. Institutions matter significantly in economic growth, as neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants fail to account for the role of institutions (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The creation of stable markets for public debt in England exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between the role of culture versus institutions lies in the enduring effects of colonial institutions on current income per capita. This is shown by the high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies, European settlements, early measures of institutions, and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The cultural legacy of mistrust, combined with institutional factors such as extractive policies, has had a lasting impact on Africa's economic performance.
