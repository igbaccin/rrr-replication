The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is central to understanding economic development trajectories across different societies. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are primary drivers that shape institutions and thereby influence economic outcomes, or if other factors such as geographical conditions, political structures, or economic policies play more decisive roles. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions, leading to varying levels of efficiency and growth. Others contend that these cultural explanations are secondary to more immediate determinants like institutional frameworks or exogenous shocks. The debate hinges on whether culture is a foundational element that underpins long-term economic performance or if it operates within a broader set of constraints imposed by other factors.

Cultural beliefs can significantly influence the structure of economic institutions and, consequently, economic efficiency. Ogilvie argues that "non-economic cultural beliefs" can reshape collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by Putnam's theory of 'social capital', which posits that trust, norms, and networks, all rooted in culture, facilitate coordinated actions that improve societal efficiency. These cultural elements are seen as critical in explaining the economic performance differences between regions such as northern and southern Italy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Moreover, Austin notes that institutional evolution is deeply intertwined with historical paths and patterns of investment and policy experience, suggesting a dynamic interplay between culture and institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of culture in shaping institutions should be understood within the broader framework of economic development. North emphasizes that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This view is reinforced by North and Weingast's argument that secure property rights, fostered through institutional changes, were pivotal in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These perspectives suggest that while culture may influence institutions, it does so within a context where institutional frameworks and transaction costs are primary determinants of economic outcomes. Furthermore, the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England highlights how cultural factors interact with broader political and economic structures to drive growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by historical and geographical factors. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how exogenous shocks can shape institutional frameworks, potentially overriding cultural influences. Additionally, Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for reduced trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This indicates that historical events can deeply embed cultural traits that influence long-term economic outcomes, reinforcing the idea that culture operates within a complex web of factors.

This institutional evolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), thereby enhancing the security of property rights. The establishment of a stable market for public debt generated positive externalities, catalyzing the development of private debt markets as well (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital investment and economic growth by ensuring secure property rights over time. This institutional perspective does not dismiss the influence of cultural factors entirely; it suggests that culture may operate within a broader framework where institutions play a decisive role.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutions in societies characterized by high levels of inequality tend to favor elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition. This dynamic is evident in New World societies with greater inequality, where political structures have been shaped to maintain elite advantages. They posit that the historical exploitation of Africa has led to enduring cultural norms that inhibit trust and cooperation within societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of inequality in these regions is thus maintained through institutional arrangements that limit social mobility and economic opportunities for non-elites.

The mechanisms by which inequality perpetuates itself are multifaceted. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how the initial factor endowments, particularly the abundance of land relative to labor, led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These disparities were reinforced through institutions that protected elite interests, such as restrictive property rights and limited access to education. Nunn and Wantchekon further illustrate this by showing how mistrust, rooted in historical exploitation, continues to affect economic development by undermining social capital and reducing the effectiveness of collective action (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring legacy of these institutions is evident in contemporary economic outcomes, where regions with a history of high inequality struggle with persistent poverty and limited economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon qualify this perspective by noting that while mistrust is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of economic development. They emphasize that other historical factors, such as colonial policies and environmental conditions, also play crucial roles (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This qualification underscores the complexity of economic development, where multiple historical and institutional factors interact to shape contemporary outcomes. The persistence of inequality in regions with a history of elite-dominated institutions highlights the need for comprehensive policy interventions that address both cultural and structural barriers to growth.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts between elites and commoners shaped Europe's pre-industrial economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds can be explained by the power struggles between these groups rather than purely efficiency-driven processes. Broadberry and Gupta show that distributional conflicts in Europe were not confined to the elite, but also involved commoners who resisted institutional changes that threatened their interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This dynamic led to a complex interplay of institutions that influenced economic development. One specific mechanism through which these conflicts operated is the resistance to technological change. Elites often opposed innovations that could disrupt existing power structures and redistribute wealth, thus slowing down economic growth. For instance, Ogilvie highlights how guilds resisted new technologies to protect their monopolies on certain crafts (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This pattern of resistance had a downstream implication: it delayed the adoption of labor-saving technologies, which in turn hindered productivity gains. The impact of these distributional conflicts was not uniform across Europe; regional variations in power dynamics and institutional responses led to divergent economic trajectories within the continent.

Historical factors such as slave trades and colonial rule have adversely affected Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Nunn shows that the transatlantic slave trade weakened state structures by causing long-term political instability. This political fragmentation had a direct impact on Africa's ability to develop strong institutions, which in turn impeded economic growth. The slave trades disrupted existing power dynamics and hindered the formation of centralized states, which are crucial for implementing effective policies and maintaining law and order. Hopkins elaborates that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth if they align with indigenous institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This alignment enhances legitimacy and fosters development-oriented policies. When contemporary institutions diverge from historical norms, it creates conditions conducive to corruption and regionalism.

Culture shapes institutions by influencing how societies organize their political and economic systems, which in turn affects long-run growth. This influence is evident in the historical impact of cultural beliefs on the development of economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts, rooted in cultural norms, drove the establishment of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by underlying cultural values and power dynamics within societies. Similarly, North argues that institutions matter because they govern the rules of the game for economic interactions, and their effectiveness is deeply intertwined with cultural norms (North 1989, p.9).

Cultural beliefs also influence trust and social capital, which are crucial for effective institutional functioning. Ogilvie highlights Putnam's theory of social capital, where civic cultures characterized by high levels of trust and reciprocity foster institutions that promote economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting institutional effectiveness and long-run economic performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
