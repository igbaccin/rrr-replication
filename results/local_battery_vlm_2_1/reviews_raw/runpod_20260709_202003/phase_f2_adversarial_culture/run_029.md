Cultural influences on institutions are fundamental to understanding long-run economic growth, as they shape the rules and norms that govern societal interactions. The debate centers around whether culture drives institutional change directly, or if its influence is mediated through other factors such as geography or historical events. A significant disagreement arises between scholars who view culture as a primary determinant of institutional quality and those who see it as a secondary factor influenced by exogenous shocks. Some argue that culture shapes institutions by fostering specific norms and values that either support or hinder economic development, while others contend that institutions evolve independently of cultural factors due to exogenous pressures such as colonial rule or natural disasters. The interplay between these perspectives is crucial for comprehending the enduring impact of cultural heritage on contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth has been extensively studied, with scholars highlighting their importance in shaping long-term development trajectories. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are essential for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This view is complemented by North, who emphasizes the significance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and promoting specialization and division of labor, which are key to technological change and improved productivity (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). These arguments underscore the critical role that institutions play in economic development. The question persists: how do cultural factors influence these institutions over time?

Cultural norms and historical events significantly impact institutional development and economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This cultural legacy has profound implications for contemporary economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack also highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). These insights illustrate how cultural factors can shape institutional development and influence long-run economic trajectories. The mechanisms by which culture exerts this influence are still debated.

This dual impact suggests that the role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth may be overstated, as institutional changes can drive both economic and political development. This dynamic interplay between economics and politics underscores how institutional evolution can be both a cause and consequence of long-run growth.

(2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that European mortality rates played a critical role in shaping the institutions established in colonies, which subsequently influenced long-run economic outcomes. They establish a strong correlation between high settler mortality and extractive institutions, demonstrating that these institutions persist over time and significantly impact current income per capita. This pattern highlights how initial conditions, such as mortality rates, can lock in institutional structures that have lasting economic consequences. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that these extractive institutions may not always be designed to encourage economic development but rather to serve the interests of politically powerful groups. This suggests that culture's role in shaping institutions is complex and intertwined with other factors such as political power dynamics, indicating that cultural influences on long-run growth are interlinked with institutional and political structures.

The new economic history of Africa highlights that historical institutional variables are key to understanding and reforming institutions in order to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new research programme begins with the hypothesis that institutional variables are more significant than other variables in explaining development. This long-term perspective is crucial, as it reveals how contemporary institutions can be congruent with indigenous institutions, which in turn promotes growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Englebert argues that high degrees of congruence enhance the legitimacy of governments and their ability to foster institutions that induce development. The relationship between institutional congruence and economic growth is not simple. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that low levels of congruence create conditions that favour patrimonial politics, which lead to regionalism, clientelism and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting long-run growth depends on their alignment with precolonial structures and the political dynamics they engender.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade had profound impacts on African development by shaping cultural attitudes such as mistrust, which persist to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrates how the slave trades adversely affected Africa's economic development through political instability and weakened state centralization. The slave trade led to long-term political fragmentation, which in turn hindered the establishment of strong institutions that could foster growth (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.1). This historical legacy of mistrust and institutional weakness continues to influence contemporary African economies by making it difficult for governments to implement effective policies and induce development. The slave trade's impact on cultural attitudes and political structures highlights how historical events can have enduring effects on long-run growth, shaping the institutional landscape in ways that persist over centuries.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, a perspective shared by Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These authors posit that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This divergence results from dynamic, partly endogenous responses to environmental challenges as well as geographical factors. For instance, Africans' use of available technologies significantly altered their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). However, this dynamic response does not fully explain the adoption of growth-favorable institutions in some regions and not others. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that while investment in agriculture led to high productivity in both Europe and Asia, the outcomes differed due to varying institutional changes and labor movements (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This highlights that cultural attitudes towards economic activities and institutional adaptations are crucial in understanding long-run growth patterns.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlement patterns, influenced by mortality rates, led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This institutional framework significantly impacted current income per capita, underscoring the enduring effects of historical cultural and institutional dynamics on economic outcomes. Austin supports this view, highlighting that Africans' responses to their environments, including technological adaptations, ultimately altered economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25).

North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional development provided a positive externality for the parallel growth of a market for private debt, illustrating how cultural attitudes towards economic activities can shape institutional adaptations. North further argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for long-term capital markets and overall economic prosperity (North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of culture in shaping economic institutions is further evidenced by Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women's contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They show that gender-based colonial constructs had significant long-term consequences for gender production, reflecting the enduring impact of cultural norms on economic activities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors like mortality rates or by endogenous cultural adaptations. Nunn and Wantchekon show that colonial boundaries, nearly identical to current country boundaries, already control for any effects of colonial rule (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This historical evidence reveals the enduring impact of cultural norms on institutional development, settling the disagreement by demonstrating that both exogenous factors and endogenous cultural adaptations play crucial roles in shaping institutions.
