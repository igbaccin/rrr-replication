The role of culture in shaping institutions is a contentious issue in economic growth literature. At stake is understanding whether cultural beliefs and values are fundamental to long-run growth through their influence on institutional frameworks. One live disagreement concerns the primacy of culture versus other factors such as extractive institutions or factor endowments. For instance, some scholars argue that cultural norms, like trust and reciprocity, facilitate economic growth by fostering efficient collective action (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others contend that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Despite these differing perspectives, there is a shared recognition among scholars that institutions matter for long-run growth. The debate hinges on whether culture fundamentally shapes these institutions or if other factors are more influential. This tension underscores the need to examine how cultural beliefs interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.10); (Mokyr 2005, p.56) propose that ideas and ideologies were fundamental drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey argues that the cultural struggle led to a shift in rhetoric, which in turn shaped new circumstances that fostered growth, while Mokyr emphasizes the role of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective highlights how ideas can transcend national boundaries and institutional frameworks, driving economic development through the diffusion of knowledge. The transmission of tacit knowledge, embedded in skills and practices, played a crucial role in this process (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr's emphasis on codified and tacit knowledge highlights that useful knowledge growth is an institutional phenomenon and deeply cultural. This cultural dimension of knowledge dissemination challenges the notion that institutions alone can drive economic growth, suggesting instead that the interplay between ideas and culture is what truly matters in long-run development. McCloskey's example of the French engineers marching under a banner inscribed with "Pour la Patrie, les Sciences et la Gloire" illustrates how cultural values and ideologies can shape institutional practices and economic outcomes. This dynamic shows that culture's role in shaping institutions involves more than formal rules; it also influences the broader societal ethos driving economic change.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated an environment where gains from trade could be maximized, as specialization and division of labor drove productivity improvements through technological change and better resource allocation (North 1989, p.1). The creation of financial institutions such as the Bank of England exemplifies how structural changes reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs was not merely a financial adjustment; it reflected a broader institutional evolution that ensured the stability necessary for long-term economic growth. The development of these institutions provided a positive externality, encouraging both public and private debt markets to flourish (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These institutions were critical, but their effectiveness relied on the cultural context in which they operated. For instance, the fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War highlights how institutional changes can be driven by broader societal pressures and cultural values, underscoring that economic growth is not solely a function of institutional design but also of the underlying cultural ethos.

The institutional framework established by colonial powers has been identified as a pivotal factor in shaping long-run economic development, particularly through the introduction of extractive institutions that persist over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers introduced institutions tailored to maximize their own rents rather than fostering long-term growth; these institutions were shaped by mortality rates among colonists and the resulting settlement patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The high correlation between settler mortality, early institutional frameworks, and contemporary economic performance underscores the enduring impact of colonial-era policies. The persistence of extractive institutions depends on their initial design and the political dynamics that sustain them. This dynamic suggests that the long-term effects of colonial institutions are not merely a function of their initial form but also of the political and social structures that perpetuate them, which can create conditions where extractive institutions continue to dominate. The implications of this persistence extend beyond economic outcomes, influencing broader societal development trajectories.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional frameworks in New World societies, characterized by greater inequality, restricted competition and perpetuated disparities. Institutions in these societies were shaped by the initial factor endowments that favored elites and led to extreme wealth, human capital, and political power inequalities from the outset. This dynamic was reinforced through government policies that advantaged elites, such as those regulating land distribution and labor markets. These institutions created barriers to entry for new competitors, limiting economic dynamism and innovation. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework can influence economic outcomes. This suggests that inequality in New World societies persisted due to initial conditions and reinforcing effects of interconnected institutional structures. For example, labor market regulations that favored elites could be intertwined with land distribution policies and credit markets, creating a self-reinforcing system that maintained elite dominance and hindered broader economic development. This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequality over long periods, offering a rival explanation to cultural determinants of long-run economic growth.

The dynamics of economic growth and technological change are profoundly influenced by social competence and institutional changes that shape productivity levels (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) posits that economic growth catch-up is intricate; it is driven by exogenous factors and the interplay of technological advancements and social transformations. This perspective challenges simple convergence hypotheses, which assume that economies will naturally converge to similar productivity levels over time. This shift did not result in abundant grain supplies or low food prices because labor moved from agriculture to industry and services.

Crucially, Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that social competence for exploiting advanced technologies was initially limited but improved over time. This suggests that the ability of societies to adapt and utilize new technologies is a critical factor in economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Broadberry and Gupta's analysis complements this by demonstrating how institutional changes can drive productivity gains, even if they do not immediately lead to abundant resources or low prices. The interaction between institutional changes and social competence in driving economic growth underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run development.

The slave trade is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists and affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This effect can be traced through the erosion of state institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade led to long-term political instability, which weakened states across Africa. Nunn (2008, p.27) further illustrates how this historical context is correlated with current economic performance; countries' histories of state development are critical determinants of their present-day GDP levels (Nunn 2008, p.1). The legacy of slavery can thus be seen as a precolonial origin for modern corruption, which in turn undermines the institutional foundations necessary for sustained economic growth. This institutional degradation is an ongoing impediment to Africa's development trajectory, not merely a historical artifact.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is profound and multifaceted. Cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions by influencing collective action games and social organization (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' illustrates how cultural norms of trust and reciprocity foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by the historical context of pre-industrial Europe, where institutional frameworks were interdependent with broader social structures such as labour markets and credit systems. The European family system, for example, was intricately linked to various economic mechanisms that contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interconnected with labour and land markets, furthering economic growth before industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The importance of institutions in economic growth is underscored by North's argument that transaction costs are a critical factor. Institutions matter because they reduce these costs, facilitating economic activity and fostering growth (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can lead to dramatic declines in transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in this regard (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This period saw the development of a stable market for public debt, which provided a positive externality for the parallel growth of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The institutions established during this time played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

The cultural legacy of historical events also plays a significant role. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for reduced trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact is evident in contemporary social capital differences, where historical determinants shape current norms of behavior.

The precise mechanisms by which institutions affect growth are further illuminated by Acemoglu et al.'s findings. Differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic performance.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident across various historical contexts. The shared finding that institutions matter for growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The precise mechanisms by which cultural beliefs shape these institutions are still debated. Nunn and Wantchekon's study shows that historical events such as the slave trade have had lasting effects on contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), while Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutional frameworks established during colonial periods continue to influence economic performance today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of trust levels and income per capita across regions with different historical experiences, such as those affected by the slave trade versus those with different mortality rates. This comparison shows that both cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks play critical roles in shaping long-run growth trajectories.
