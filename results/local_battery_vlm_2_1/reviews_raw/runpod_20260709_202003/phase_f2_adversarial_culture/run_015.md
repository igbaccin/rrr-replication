The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is how we understand the roots of prosperity and stagnation across different societies. One key disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional development or if they play a more ancillary role. Some argue that cultural beliefs shape economic outcomes by influencing institutions, while others emphasize material conditions or geographical determinants as the primary forces shaping institutions. These debates highlight the need to understand how culture interacts with other variables in the complex process of long-run growth.

Some scholars point out that cultural factors can significantly influence institutional frameworks and economic performance. For instance, some have argued that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while Protestant beliefs led to a decline in guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape institutions and thereby affect economic efficiency. Similarly, cultural factors are seen as shaping social capital, which in turn influences institutional development and economic outcomes. For example, societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks tend to have institutions that foster growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view is complemented by the idea that different cultures can lead to varied institutional frameworks, each with its own implications for economic performance. The European family system, for instance, was interdependent with various markets and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Cultural beliefs both shape and are shaped by institutions in a reciprocal relationship. The evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, for example, played a critical role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This suggests that while cultural factors may influence institutional development, they are also influenced by the broader institutional framework. Furthermore, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is also evident in how different societies respond to their environments. Africans' responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic approach suggests that cultural factors evolve in response to changing conditions. Additionally, the role of African women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods highlights how gender roles and cultural beliefs can shape production systems and long-term economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic performance.

Ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change during Europe's Industrial Revolution (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey identifies that the economic transformation in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was primarily driven by a shift in rhetoric, which fostered new circumstances and vice versa. Mokyr reinforces this perspective by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was instrumental in economic growth. He notes that much of the tacit knowledge moved around through personal transmission and training, highlighting the importance of social networks and cultural exchange in disseminating ideas. This process underscores how ideologies, manifested as new rhetorics and knowledge diffusion, created an environment conducive to economic innovation. Mokyr qualifies this by pointing out that access to codified knowledge was also crucial for its application into tacit forms. This suggests a complex interplay between cultural factors, institutional frameworks, and the dissemination of ideas in driving long-term economic growth.

This view is shared by North (1989, p.1), who contends that institutions are pivotal in economic development when transaction costs are positive.

The development of stable markets for public debt provided a significant positive externality for the parallel growth of private debt markets. This institutional setup created a robust financial system that underpinned economic expansion (North 1989, p.1).

North and Weingast qualify this by noting that while institutions were necessary, they alone were not sufficient. The fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War necessitated trade-offs between certain rights and revenues, which in turn shaped the institutional landscape(North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This suggests that economic growth was driven by both the broader political and historical context in which these institutions operated and the institutions themselves.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) find that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The mechanism linking these factors is the differential impact of mortality on settlement patterns; higher mortality discouraged settlement, leading to extractive rather than inclusive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that such equilibrium institutions were designed to maximise rents for European colonists, not to foster long-term economic growth. This suggests that the institutional landscape in colonies was shaped by European interests, which prioritised short-term extraction over sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) show that these extractive institutions had a lasting impact on economic performance, as evidenced by the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions. Thus, the role of culture in dominating institutions is evident in how European interests shaped colonial policies, which in turn influenced long-run growth trajectories.

The focus on mortality rates and settlement patterns highlights the importance of historical contexts in shaping institutional outcomes. This perspective complements North's emphasis on the political and historical context influencing institutional evolution (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The downstream implication is that cultural factors, such as European attitudes towards colonisation and extraction, played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions established in colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s focus on extractive institutions does not directly address how culture itself evolves over time or interacts with institutional change.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4); (Hornbeck 2010, p.6) focus on the institutional impact on inequality and agricultural development. They argue that institutions in New World societies influenced the persistence of inequality by shaping government policies to favor elites. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how differences in factor endowments led to varying degrees of inequality, with many colonies characterized by extreme wealth disparity from their inception (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework privileged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality over time. Hornbeck's analysis of barbed wire fencing underscores the importance of property rights in agricultural development on the American Plains, showing how enhanced protection reduced externalities from livestock damage. The introduction of this technology enabled farmers to better control their land and resources (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). Hornbeck's focus on technological innovation as a driver of agricultural development does not directly address the role of culture in long-run growth.

The influence of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is key to understanding pre-industrial Europe's economy. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Pamuk 2007, p.24) both highlight that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. This perspective challenges cultural theories that attribute institutional outcomes to exogenous factors like beliefs about the afterlife or social capital. Pamuk's work on the Black Death underscores how demographic shocks can alter economic structures by increasing labour scarcity and prompting technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This dynamic is crucial for understanding long-run growth because it illustrates how shifts in power dynamics, driven by factors like disease and mortality rates, can reshape institutions. Ogilvie warns that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation may fail to capture the broader institutional context (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This suggests that while distributional conflicts are pivotal, they must be examined within the complex web of interacting institutions to fully grasp their impact on long-run growth.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia began before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) share this view. They argue that the economic development of Europe and Asia during the early modern period was more similar than previously thought, with both regions experiencing stagnation in their agricultural sectors and a lack of significant technological advancements. This divergence was not primarily driven by differences in institutions or technology but rather by changes in demographic patterns and labor movements. The shift from agriculture to industry and services in Europe led to higher productivity in the latter sector (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This higher productivity did not translate into lower food prices because of the movement of labor out of agriculture. The economic development of Europe was thus characterized by a complex interplay of factors including population growth, institutional changes, and technological advancements.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade had profound implications for African economic development by fostering cultural attitudes such as mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust was a persistent cultural trait as well as an immediate consequence of the trade. Nunn argues that this mistrust has long-term economic impacts, including weakened and fragmented states due to political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). The presence of malaria transmission, as noted by Nunn and Wantchekon, acts as a proxy for historical conditions in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This factor likely played a role in shaping the cultural landscape, reinforcing mistrust and hindering economic development. These findings suggest that the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond immediate economic losses, creating enduring cultural attitudes that continue to influence contemporary African economies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) both highlight the significant contributions of women to economic growth and development in their respective contexts. Akyeampong and Fofack illustrate this by noting how women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth in matrilineal societies, emphasizing their indispensable role in community reproduction. This dynamic is further supported by Goldin, who observes that changes in social norms concerning women's family and career expectations began to alter around the late 1960s and early 1970s (Goldin 2006, p.8). These shifts were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers, growth of educational institutions, and technological advancements in household production. Goldin (2006, p.8) also points out that these changes were not uniform across all sectors; barriers to the employment of married women persisted despite advances in other areas (Goldin 2006, p.3). Consequently, the economic contributions of women are evident but remain contingent on broader institutional and social frameworks.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach: Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective highlights the importance of endogenous factors and local adaptations in shaping economic development. Bryant (2006, p.35) further underscores that effective causality is governed by a causal connectedness that spans longer-term processes, emphasizing the role of accumulated technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms (Bryant 2006, p.35). This view challenges simplistic interpretations of economic growth by highlighting the complex interplay between these factors. Bryant (2006, p.35) also notes that any attempt to overturn our reigning comprehension of the key determinants in the European trajectory would be an audacious venture, suggesting that while endogenous factors are crucial, they must be considered within a broader historical and cultural context (Bryant 2006, p.4).

The Industrious Revolution, marked by heightened labor input and evolving consumption habits, laid crucial groundwork for technological advances that facilitated subsequent industrial growth (de Vries 1994 (de Vries 1994, p.18); Abramovitz 1986 (Abramovitz 1986, p.21)). This period saw significant changes in household behavior, with families allocating more time to market-oriented activities and less to subsistence farming. De Vries describes how increased labor input, coupled with new consumption patterns, transformed urban economies before the onset of industrialization (de Vries 1994 (de Vries 1994, p.5)). This shift involved profound social changes that reshaped the fabric of societies as well as economic growth. Abramovitz elucidates this process by highlighting how technological advances and social competence for exploiting new methods were key drivers, with productivity levels becoming more stable over time (Abramovitz 1986 (Abramovitz 1986, p.12)). This process was not uniform across all regions; the dynamics of technological advance and social change varied significantly. This variability suggests that the Industrious Revolution's impact on long-run growth was contingent on both societal capacity to adapt and integrate these changes and technological advances.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth by influencing both economic and political structures. Cultural beliefs and values can significantly impact the efficiency of economies by altering collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Ogilvie's analysis shows how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs influence institutional frameworks and economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed in North’s argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reflect increased security of property rights, which are critical for both capital markets and long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further underscored by the role of social capital. Putnam's theory highlights how features such as trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, thereby improving societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural approach aligns with North’s assertion that secure property rights are essential for economic growth, emphasizing the importance of institutions in fostering a stable environment for economic activity (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also crucial. North and Weingast demonstrate how the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets, highlighting the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework was essential for creating a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the growth of private markets.

Cultural beliefs and values influence political structures as well as economic institutions. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was pivotal for long-term capital markets and economic growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illustrated by the impact of African women on economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of gender roles in shaping economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This underscores how cultural beliefs about gender can influence institutional frameworks and economic growth.

The relationship between culture and institutions is complex and multifaceted. While cultural beliefs and values shape economic and political structures, these institutions in turn influence long-run growth by fostering stable environments for economic activity. The precise impact of culture on institutions varies across regions and historical periods (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The underlying point of agreement is that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The remaining disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs directly influence institutional frameworks or if this relationship is mediated by other factors such as economic efficiency. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency, highlighting the role of non-economic cultural beliefs in shaping collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast show how institutional changes, such as the financial revolution, led to secure property rights and facilitated economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the role of the Bank of England in facilitating both public and private debt markets. This institution played a critical role in England's long-run success by providing a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of private markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The measurement type used here is transaction costs and property rights security, showing how institutional changes directly impact economic growth.
