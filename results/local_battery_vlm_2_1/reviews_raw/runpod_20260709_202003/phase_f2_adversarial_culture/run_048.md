The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and institutional theory. At stake is understanding whether cultural factors provide the fundamental cause for variations in economic development trajectories across societies. One side argues that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions, which in turn affect efficiency and growth. This perspective posits that certain cultures foster institutions conducive to economic progress by promoting trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks. The opposing view contends that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita, suggesting that geographic factors and mortality rates are more decisive than cultural influences. The debate hinges on whether cultural frameworks or exogenous factors like geography and mortality are the primary drivers of institutional development and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlight how institutions play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom, but Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17) emphasize exogenous factors such as mortality rates faced by settlers in colonies.

The dynamic interplay between culture and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that Max Weber was right to highlight the influence of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, while Richardson contended that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules. This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can significantly impact the structure and efficiency of economic institutions by altering the dynamics of collective action games. Austin (2008, p.25) shares this view, emphasizing how Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. The idea is that cultural frameworks shape institutional development in ways that can either promote or hinder economic growth.

Cultural beliefs and values are not the only factors influencing institutional frameworks; geographical and historical contexts also play significant roles. This perspective suggests that exogenous factors like geography and mortality rates are more decisive than cultural influences in shaping institutional development. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) argue that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would ultimately have led to success, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. The institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The impact of culture on institutional frameworks is not limited to European contexts; it also applies to African societies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) highlight how women remained indispensable to the reproduction of communities in Asante, where their bloodlines defined the transmission of both office and wealth. This suggests that cultural beliefs about gender roles significantly influenced economic development in pre-colonial Africa. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that interdependencies between individual institutions within a wider framework are crucial for understanding institutional development. The European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism.

North (1989, p.1) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight institutions as a fundamental determinant of economic growth. Institutions are critical because they structure incentives in ways that foster productivity improvements through specialization, division of labor, and technological change. This institutional framework emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and low transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), which are conducive to long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) caution that even equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth. This implies that the institutional framework's impact on economic development is not uniform and can vary based on the interests of those in power. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes underscores their significance as a determinant of long-run growth, even if their effects are mediated by political dynamics.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The evolution of these institutions enabled the development of stable markets for both public and private debt, which in turn facilitated long-term capital accumulation and investment. This institutional framework provided a significant positive externality by creating a secure environment for financial transactions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that institutions governing wealth distribution and political power play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes. They emphasize how variations in institutional frameworks can lead to persistent inequality, influencing long-term development trajectories. This underscores the critical role of institutions in determining both immediate economic conditions and long-run growth.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a profound adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical phenomenon is posited to have created lasting cultural and institutional legacies that continue to influence African economies today. The slave trade disrupted the formation of strong, centralized states by introducing political instability. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades caused long-term political instability which resulted in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This fragmentation made it difficult for Africa to develop robust institutions capable of fostering economic growth. The absence of centralised state structures hindered the establishment of effective governance, property rights, and other institutional frameworks essential for sustained development. Furthermore, Nunn (2008, p.27) suggests that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). This corruption further erodes trust within societies, creating a vicious cycle where mistrust impedes economic cooperation and growth. This mistrust is detrimental to economic development as it undermines social capital, a critical resource for economic growth. The legacy of slavery in Africa thus offers an alternative explanation for underdevelopment, one that does not hinge on cultural influence over institutions but rather on the direct and lasting impact of historical events.

The role of ideas and ideologies in shaping economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 is profound (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). The shift towards bourgeois values and the liberalization of economic thought were pivotal. This intellectual transformation fostered an environment where innovation and entrepreneurial activity flourished, driving long-term economic growth. McCloskey's analysis shows that this cultural shift was a fundamental driver of institutional changes (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr further elaborates that the diffusion of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was crucial for economic development during this period. The movement of ideas across borders facilitated by scientific journals, international conferences, and personal communications underscores the importance of a shared intellectual framework in promoting growth (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr also notes that the transmission of knowledge depended heavily on personal interactions and training, indicating that while ideas are powerful drivers, their practical application requires social mechanisms to disseminate them effectively. This interplay between ideological shifts and the dissemination of knowledge suggests that cultural factors play a critical role in economic growth by shaping both the intellectual environment and the practical implementation of innovative ideas.

Pamuk and Broadberry and Gupta share that historical events like the Black Death and urban development played significant roles in shaping economic performance (Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Pamuk's analysis shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes. The plague led to a decline in interest rates, increased labor mobility, flexibility, and institutional change within guilds, and fostered the rise of rural industries (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These developments collectively contributed to an era marked by labor-saving technological innovations. The prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 resembled the stagnating areas of Europe more than they did the developing north-western European regions. This suggests that while historical events like the Black Death and urban development were pivotal, their impact was mediated by regional specificities. Pamuk's findings indicate that these structural changes had already begun to shape economic outcomes by 1450, suggesting a deeper historical context for the Great Divergence (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This implies that cultural factors, though not explicitly addressed in this literature, may have played an underlying role in how these events unfolded and were interpreted within different regions.

Distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs shaped economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and Africa. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) show that the roots of these institutions lay in power struggles over resources, which were influenced by cultural norms. In Europe, distributional conflicts led to the establishment of core economic institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, which Ogilvie argues are better understood through power dynamics than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions emerged solely from a desire for economic growth. The enduring legacy of these norms suggests that cultural factors can act as a mechanism through which distributional conflicts shape institutions. This implies that while distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs are crucial, they operate within a complex web of interconnected factors that collectively influence economic growth over the long run.

Economic transformations and property rights have driven significant societal changes, such as shifts in women's employment and agricultural development (Goldin 2006, p.8; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). Goldin illustrates how exogenous factors, including increased demand for female office workers and advances in household production technology, catalyzed women’s entry into the labor force. This transformation involved a series of interlinked changes that altered social norms and expectations regarding women's roles. This technological innovation reduced externalities from livestock damage, highlighting the role of property rights in economic development.

The impact of these transformations on long-run growth is nuanced. These preconditions suggest a complex interplay between institutional changes and cultural norms, where one facilitates the other. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) further qualifies this by showing how social norms can sometimes supplant strict legal responsibilities, as seen in Ellickson’s analysis of modern California county ranchers. This dynamic indicates that while property rights are crucial, their effectiveness is mediated through cultural and social norms. The interplay between economic transformations, property rights, and cultural factors thus shapes long-run growth in intricate ways.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) critique simplistic views of causation in economic development by showing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric perspectives are empirically suspect (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin illustrates this by critiquing Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's work for compressing different historical periods and paths. In particular, Austin argues that the 'simple geography' thesis is flawed because it ignores dynamic responses of African societies to their environments. Bryant further supports this critique by emphasising the interconnectedness of historical transitions, arguing that even revolutionary transformations rely on accumulated resources and opportunities from prior developments (Bryant 2006, p.35). This perspective underscores the importance of long-term institutional evolution and cultural norms in shaping economic trajectories. Bryant cautions against oversimplifying these dynamics, noting that effective causality is often mediated by a complex interplay of factors over extended periods (Bryant 2006, p.4).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency by altering collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Austin supports this view by emphasizing the interconnectedness of historical transitions and the importance of long-term institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.25). North further underscores that institutions matter in economic growth because they reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The interplay between culture and institutions is also evident in the work of North and Weingast. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This highlights how cultural norms and institutional frameworks can shape economic trajectories over extended periods.

Moreover, Akyeampong and Fofack illustrate how African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their work shows that gender roles and production systems reflect the sexual division of labor established during these historical periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This underscores the importance of cultural norms in shaping economic development, even in contexts where traditional views might marginalize certain contributions.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter for long-run growth because they shape economic outcomes through secure property rights and efficient markets. North shows that transaction costs are critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise mechanisms by which cultural norms influence institutional frameworks remain a subject of debate. Austin argues that effective causality is often mediated by a complex interplay of factors over extended periods (Austin 2008, p.25).
