The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars of economic history and development. At stake are the fundamental mechanisms underlying sustained economic progress and the relative importance of cultural versus institutional factors. Some scholars contend that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby impacting economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with theories emphasizing the significance of social capital and civic cultures in fostering growth- oriented institutions. Other researchers propose a dynamic interplay between geography, institutions, and culture, indicating that cultural factors may be less deterministic than previously thought (Austin 2008, p.25). The debate hinges on whether cultural elements are primary drivers or secondary influences within broader institutional frameworks.

The relationship between cultural beliefs and the efficiency of economic institutions is central to understanding long-run growth. Cultural theories posit that specific beliefs can motivate economic behavior and shape institutional structures. For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife have been linked to the rules governing guilds in medieval Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while the decline of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation is associated with the weakening of guilds. This cultural shift illustrates how non-economic beliefs can influence both the structure and equilibrium of collective action games within economic institutions, thereby affecting overall efficiency. Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital underscores that societies characterized by high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks tend to have institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these cultural values over time can explain variations in economic performance across regions, as seen in the comparative economic histories of northern and southern Italy. This perspective indicates that cultural beliefs play a direct role in shaping institutional frameworks that support long-run growth.

The interdependence between individual institutions within broader frameworks is another critical aspect of how culture influences long-run growth. Cultural values and beliefs often interact with other economic institutions, creating complex systems that can either facilitate or hinder growth. For example, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnected institutional framework is hypothesized to have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. These examples highlight how cultural values can shape multiple interconnected institutions, creating a framework that supports long-run economic development. The interdependence of these institutions shows that cultural factors are embedded within broader institutional structures, influencing their overall efficiency and impact on growth.

The dynamic interplay between culture, geography, and institutions further complicates the role of cultural beliefs in long-run growth. Cultural values are influenced by geographic conditions and historical contexts. For instance, differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, illustrating how cultural beliefs can interact with geographic factors to shape institutional outcomes. Similarly, the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This historical trauma has long-term implications for economic development, as trust is a critical component of social capital that supports institutional efficiency. The dynamic relationship between cultural beliefs, geographic conditions, and institutions underscores the multifaceted nature of cultural influence on long-run growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) share the stance that ideas are key drivers of economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey argues that new cultural norms, such as "bourgeois virtues" like honesty and hard work, were essential to the Industrial Revolution. These ideas transformed societal attitudes towards commerce and industry, fostering an environment where entrepreneurship flourished and innovation was rewarded. This cultural shift was a driving force behind institutional changes. Mokyr reinforces this idea by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He notes that much of this knowledge was tacit, transmitted through personal interactions and apprenticeships (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This implies that cultural factors, such as openness to new ideas and willingness to share knowledge, played a crucial role in facilitating the diffusion of useful knowledge across societies. However, Mokyr also points out that the diffusion of knowledge was not uniform; national differences did exist but were secondary to the broader intellectual movement (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The dynamic interplay between cultural norms and the dissemination of ideas highlights how culture can shape economic growth through its influence on the generation and spread of useful knowledge. This interplay underscores the importance of cultural factors in fostering an environment conducive to long-run economic development.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) together with van Zanden's (1999, p.6) research on European economic development, highlight the significant divergence in economic trajectories between Europe and Asia before 1800. The variation in silver wages across different parts of Europe reflected broader economic developments and investment patterns (van Zanden 1999, p.6), suggesting that institutional changes played a critical role. This highlights how regional disparities within Europe influenced overall economic performance, setting the stage for later industrial development.

This pattern suggests that while certain economic indicators were comparable, the purchasing power and overall economic development differed significantly. The divergence in silver wages points to deeper structural differences between European and Asian economies, which could be influenced by cultural factors affecting investment patterns and institutional changes.

This divergence underscores the importance of understanding regional variations within Europe and their implications for long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that these institutions are key drivers of economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights the role of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The legacy of colonial rule thus left a lasting impact on institutional frameworks that continue to influence economic trajectories. Austin suggests that this focus oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths. For example, the 'reversal of fortune' thesis may not fully account for endogenous responses within African societies, where local adaptations to environmental conditions played a significant role in shaping development outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). The shared finding of the colonial institutions stream is that the extractive nature of these institutions shaped economic growth by prioritizing short-term gains over long-run development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency or cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The central claim is that these institutions were shaped by power struggles between different social groups. In the context of serfdom and guilds, for example, Ogilvie suggests that these institutions emerged as a result of conflicts over resource distribution. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's argument that differential factor endowments across societies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the evolution of institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutional frameworks were deeply influenced by the power dynamics within societies. The implication is that economic growth was significantly influenced by the distributional conflicts inherent in societal structures. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the power dynamics within societies when examining long-run economic development.

Institutions shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), thereby influencing long-run growth. North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are crucial for economic growth. The mechanism through which institutions operate involves securing property rights and fostering a stable market for public debt, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate in their analysis of seventeenth-century England. North and Weingast highlight the role of secure property rights in economic growth, while Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-term development. This suggests that the specific type of institution, extractive or inclusive, plays a critical role in determining whether an economy grows or stagnates.

Nunn and Wantchekon's work on the slave trade in Africa shows that historical events can shape cultural attitudes such as mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). These authors argue that the slave trade left a lasting impact on African societies by fostering a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day. The mechanism through which this occurs involves the disruption of social cohesion and the erosion of trust within communities. This mistrust, in turn, affects long-term economic and social outcomes by hindering cooperation and collective action. The slave trades caused political instability and weakened states, which further exacerbated the negative effects of mistrust on development. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is multifaceted, encompassing both cultural attitudes and institutional weaknesses. The historical impact of the slave trade on African development underscores how deeply rooted cultural norms can influence long-term economic outcomes.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth by influencing norms and behaviors within societies. The historical legacy of the slave trade, for instance, has left enduring cultural impacts on African development, including mistrust and weakened state institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural mistrust, in turn, hinders cooperation and collective action necessary for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The interdependence of cultural norms and institutional frameworks is evident in how the slave trade's legacy has affected both cultural attitudes and institutional weaknesses. Shared findings on the impact of the slave trade on African development underscore that deeply rooted cultural norms can significantly influence long-term economic outcomes.

The role of culture in shaping institutions extends beyond Africa, as shown by Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts are key to understanding core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The influence of cultural beliefs on institutional structures is exemplified by the impact of Catholic and Protestant beliefs on guilds during medieval times. This dynamic highlights how non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency through collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital illustrates that cultural values such as trust and norms of reciprocity foster institutions conducive to growth and political accountability (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical evidence from the slave trade in Africa and the institutional evolution in Europe shows that culture influences long-term economic outcomes by shaping institutions. The legacy of the slave trade, for example, has left a lasting impact on African societies through mistrust and weakened state institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). European societies developed institutions such as guilds that were influenced by cultural norms and beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27), which facilitated economic growth. The precise historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on African development settles the disagreement about whether culture or institutions are more fundamental to long-run growth.
