The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and institutional theory. The live disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional change that underpins economic growth, or if they are secondary to other determinants such as geography, technology, or political dynamics. One position argues that culture shapes institutions by influencing collective action and social norms, thereby fostering or hindering economic development. Another position posits that culture is a less significant factor compared to exogenous shocks, such as mortality rates or technological advancements. A third view suggests that cultural beliefs are endogenous to institutional frameworks, evolving in response to broader economic and political structures. The stakes intellectually involve understanding the mechanisms through which societies achieve sustained economic growth, with implications for policy-making and development strategies. This debate underscores the need to disentangle the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and long-run growth.

A stream of research examines how cultural beliefs influence economic institutions and efficiency. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by underlying power dynamics. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on the impact of mortality rates on institutional development in colonies, highlighting how exogenous factors can shape institutions that persist over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial, as noted by Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. This implies that studying one institution in isolation may not capture the full picture of how cultural beliefs and social norms interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The role of culture in shaping institutions is intertwined with broader economic and political structures, suggesting a complex interplay.

Ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey and Mokyr argue that cultural shifts in ideas catalyzed growth. This perspective is evident in McCloskey's critique of interest-only theories, where he highlights that institutional change was driven by accumulated information combined with interest, a process that was far from mere calculation (McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr further elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge, which was codified and embodied in tacit forms such as skills and know-how. This tacit knowledge moved about through personal transmission and training, underscoring the importance of cultural mechanisms in disseminating economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr qualifies this by noting that while national differences existed within the West, the broader intellectual movement was more fundamental to the Great Enrichment (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This suggests that cultural beliefs and social norms were instrumental in driving economic growth, operating through a complex interplay with institutional frameworks.

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1)(North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was essential to the English financial revolution that facilitated economic progress. The evolution of institutions in England thus played a necessary role in making possible both economic growth and political freedom, underscoring the significance of institutional frameworks in driving long-run economic success.

This narrative is nuanced by the specific historical context in which these institutions operated. North (1989, p.9) highlights that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This suggests that while institutions were crucial, their effectiveness was contingent on specific historical and political conditions. These conditions included the presence of a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the development of private debt markets, a pattern that underscores the interconnected nature of institutional evolution and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The interplay between fiscal crises, institutional reforms, and economic outcomes illustrates how institutional frameworks can drive long-run growth through secure property rights and reduced transaction costs. This dynamic interaction highlights the complex role of institutions in shaping economic development over time.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. He notes that the "main interest" of Acemoglu et al.'s work is its contribution to overcoming limitations in the new institutionalist economic historiography associated with North, which emphasized secure private property rights as key to long-term growth (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues for a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of what Acemoglu et al. call a 'sophisticated geography' approach (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective highlights the role of Africans' responses to their environments and available technologies in shaping economic development. The African story suggests that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies (Austin 2008, p.25; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These changes account for a significant fraction of current income differences, but Acemoglu et al. note that "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the institutional frameworks established during colonialism were not necessarily conducive to sustainable economic development in the long run.

Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This institutional inequality can restrict competition and contribute to the persistence of unequal power structures. One specific mechanism by which this occurs is through the creation of policies that favor elite interests at the expense of broader economic development. For instance, in societies where elites hold significant political power, they may implement regulations that limit market access for new entrants, thereby protecting their own economic positions. This dynamic can lead to a self-reinforcing cycle where institutions designed to maintain inequality perpetuate the very conditions that hinder long-run growth. The interplay between institutions and cultural beliefs adds another layer of complexity. These cultural beliefs and values may encourage or discourage certain institutional arrangements, thereby affecting economic performance over the long run. This suggests that while institutional inequality is a significant factor in shaping development paths, it operates within a broader framework where culture plays an important role.

Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women's economic contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8) highlights the significant role of gender in shaping economic development. Women were indispensable to community reproduction in matrilineal societies like Asante, where their bloodlines defined wealth transmission. This aligns with Goldin's argument that changes in women’s employment and education significantly altered family dynamics and career expectations (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.3). The quiet revolution in the U.S. which transformed women's roles in the workforce, was driven by shifts in social norms and educational opportunities. Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis suggests that colonial constructs and the codification of gender roles have had long-term consequences for gender production in Africa.

The early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) alongside van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that the economic gap between these regions predates the Industrial Revolution. This divergence is attributed to geographical factors and significant investments in agriculture, leading to productivity gains in regions like southern India (van Zanden 1999, p.6). Institutional changes in Europe facilitated investment in agriculture, which in turn enhanced agricultural labor productivity. This did not result in lower food prices or an abundance of grain because labor transitioned from agriculture into industry and services. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) notes that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living during this period.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.1) argue that the transatlantic slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa. This culture persists today, impacting contemporary social capital. The slave trade disrupted social structures and undermined political institutions, leading to fragmented states and long-term political instability. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that this political fragmentation hindered economic growth by creating conditions for corruption and weak governance. The persistence of mistrust in African societies can be traced to this history. This suggests a direct link between historical events and present-day social norms.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Institutions are critical to economic performance because they define the rules of the game which structure incentives and constrain choices (North 1989, p.1). For instance, North and Weingast demonstrate how the evolution of public debt markets in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and thus facilitated economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Ogilvie's study on pre-industrial Europe further supports this by highlighting that cultural beliefs can influence the structure and efficiency of institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The role of culture is evident in how it shapes social norms, which in turn affect institutional frameworks. Putnam’s theory of 'social capital' illustrates how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions necessary for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The relationship between culture and institutions is dynamic and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlement patterns influenced by mortality rates, have persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This persistence underscores the enduring impact of historical cultural factors on institutional development. Austin’s critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further emphasizes that African economic trajectories are deeply intertwined with their own responses to environmental challenges and technological availability, reflecting a dynamic interplay between culture and institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.25). The historical evidence from Africa reveals that women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the gendered dimensions of cultural influences on institutional development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is settled by comparing the empirical findings of Acemoglu et al. with those of Austin. The former show that differential mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Meanwhile, Austin demonstrates that African economic trajectories are deeply intertwined with their own responses to environmental challenges and technological availability (Austin 2008, p.25). This comparison settles the disagreement by showing that the enduring impact of historical cultural factors on institutional development can be measured through persistent differences in income per capita.
