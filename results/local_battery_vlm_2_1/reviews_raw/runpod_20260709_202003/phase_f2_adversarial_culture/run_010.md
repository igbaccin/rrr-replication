The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The extent to which cultural factors can be seen as fundamental causes of economic development is at stake, along with their role as epiphenomena of underlying structural forces. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs and values directly influence institutional frameworks, thereby fostering or hindering growth. One position argues that culture shapes institutions by motivating collective action and determining the efficiency of economic systems. Another position contends that differences in mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita. A third view posits that cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline. This review will explore how culture interacts with institutions to drive long-run growth.

The relationship between culture and institutional frameworks is complex and multifaceted. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efficient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. North similarly emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that they matter because neoclassical models assume zero transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). This view underscores how institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Institutional frameworks are interdependent with other economic systems. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, entire institutional frameworks must be examined. North and Weingast further illustrate this point by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would ultimately have led to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. Institutions thus played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

The cultural underpinnings of institutional development are further elucidated by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This cultural shift has persisted and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This perspective complements studies that consider historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as those linking social capital within Italy to the independence of cities during the eleventh to fourteenth centuries (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The importance of trust and its origins are thus integral to understanding how cultural factors influence institutional development and long-run growth.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was a critical driver of economic growth itself, in addition to being a response to pre-existing cultural factors. North and Weingast underscore that even if other forces might have led to eventual success, institutions played a necessary role in accelerating and solidifying this process. North and Weingast's analysis highlights how institutions can create self-reinforcing mechanisms for economic development, independent of cultural factors.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions fundamentally shaped long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Specifically, the mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies determined the type of institutional framework implemented. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists. These institutions were directly influenced by the settler mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This dynamic highlights how institutional changes, driven by colonial experiences, became a pivotal factor in determining the economic trajectories of former colonies.

Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that these extractive institutions persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. also note that the equilibrium institutions established by colonial powers were designed to maximize rents for the colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, their impact on economic development was contingent on the specific goals and interests of the colonizing powers.

Thus, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is challenged by the enduring effects of colonial institutions, which were shaped by settler mortality rates and designed to maximize short-term gains rather than fostering sustainable economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. They propose a mechanism where economic growth is driven by new cultural norms and ethical values, such as the bourgeois virtues of hard work, thrift, and innovation. McCloskey argues that these ideas created a new rhetoric that changed circumstances, which in turn reinforced new ideas (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr highlights that "useful knowledge" was central to this process, with both explicit codified knowledge and tacit knowledge being crucial for economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr also notes that the diffusion of useful knowledge was facilitated by international communication and collaboration within Europe. This implies that while cultural norms and values were important, they did not operate in isolation from other factors such as institutional frameworks and technological advancements. Therefore, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is nuanced, with ideas and knowledge interacting with broader socio-economic conditions to drive economic change.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than cultural factors for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These conflicts are rooted in the power dynamics between different social groups, which shape institutional frameworks to serve their interests. Ogilvie shows how serfdom and guilds were outcomes of struggles between landlords, peasants, and artisans over resources and control. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The institutional frameworks that emerged from these distributional conflicts served to maintain the status quo and protect elite interests. Ogilvie further notes that these institutions are interdependent within a broader framework, meaning that changes in one institution can have ripple effects throughout the system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness suggests that institutional evolution is a complex process driven by underlying power dynamics. Culture interacts with these distributional conflicts to shape institutions over time.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) discuss the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia as distinct historical paths. These paths are marked by different institutional evolutions, which were shaped by a complex interplay of factors including technological adoption and investment patterns. This suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their impact is mediated by various endogenous processes.

Austin (2008, p.2) emphasises the dynamic nature of Africa's response to its environment, highlighting how Africans used available technologies to adapt to their surroundings (Austin 2008, p.25). Similarly, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that institutional change in Europe led to significant agricultural investment, which in turn drove high labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This pattern underscores the role of endogenous factors in shaping economic trajectories. These authors acknowledge the importance of institutions but do not directly address how culture might dominate these institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) also discusses the limitations of the 'new institutionalist' approach, noting its difficulty in quantitatively testing the relationship between institutions and economic growth (Austin 2008, p.25). This limitation highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how culture interacts with institutions over time. This points to the need for further research on the role of culture in shaping institutions and economic outcomes over the long run.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting trust levels and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27)  assert that this mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experience of slavery. This culture of mistrust has significant implications for economic outcomes, as it affects the ability of communities to cooperate and engage in collective action. The slave trade also led to political instability and weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) identifies that this long-term political instability was a direct result of the slave trades, which caused fragmented states and hindered economic development. This historical context is crucial for understanding modern corruption levels in Africa, as it suggests that the legacy of slavery continues to shape contemporary institutions (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) also notes that this history can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, as it influences collective action and trust within societies. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon's finding that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and affects contemporary institutional structures (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of these cultural norms highlights their enduring impact on economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that cultural factors have dynamic effects over time (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast extend this by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional framework facilitated the development of financial instruments that dramatically declined transaction costs, underscoring the importance of cultural and institutional factors in long-run growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus well-supported by evidence. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth by enabling secure property rights and fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Acemoglu et al. identify mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies as a significant determinant of current institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of historical factors on institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence of European settlement patterns and their correlation with current institutions settles this disagreement, demonstrating that both cultural beliefs and historical contexts shape institutions that drive long-run growth.
