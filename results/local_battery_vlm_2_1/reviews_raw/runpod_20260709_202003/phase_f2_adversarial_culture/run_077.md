The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the literature on economic development. At stake is whether cultural factors can fundamentally influence economic trajectories over centuries by shaping institutional frameworks. The disagreement centers around two key positions: one emphasizing the primacy of institutions, which are often shaped by culture, and another highlighting geographical or exogenous determinants that shape institutions independently from cultural influences. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they define the framework for economic interactions (North 1989, p.1). However, Nunn and Wantchekon point to a different causal mechanism: the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa which persists today and affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The extent to which cultural factors can fundamentally shape institutions over long periods and thereby influence economic growth remains an open question.

Institutions are highlighted as a fundamental determinant of economic growth, shared by North (1989, p.1) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48). This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping economic interactions and outcomes. Acemoglu et al. establish a causal link between settler mortality rates and the persistence of extractive institutions, which significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions are shaped by historical factors such as European settlement patterns and mortality rates, which in turn influence economic performance through their impact on property rights and transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Acemoglu et al. assert that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth; they can be extractive and inhibit development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the institutional framework established during colonialism has lasting effects on economic outcomes, reinforcing the idea that institutions are a critical determinant of growth. The historical legacy of these institutions continues to shape contemporary economic performance by influencing factors such as property rights and transaction costs, thereby underscoring their fundamental role in long-run growth dynamics.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate that the financial revolution in England provided a large positive externality, facilitating the development of both public and private debt markets. However, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) note that differences in institutions alone cannot fully explain varying paths of development; the underlying causes of these institutional differences must also be considered. This indicates that while secure property rights are a necessary condition for economic growth, they may not be sufficient without complementary factors (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Culture's role in shaping institutions and economic growth is far more complex than simplistic causal narratives suggest (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique underscores a broader issue: the difficulty in quantifying institutional change over long periods, which complicates our understanding of how institutions evolve to promote growth (Austin 2008, p.25). The African case study shows that local responses to environmental conditions and technological availability are crucial factors that influence economic development over time. Bryant's work builds on this by emphasizing that a causal connectedness governs every transition from past to present, with enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms playing pivotal roles (Bryant 2006, p.4). Without these inherited elements, transitions to industrial modes of production or imperial forms of hegemony would be impossible. These insights reveal the importance of long-term cultural and institutional dynamics in shaping economic outcomes, even when immediate factors like natural resources are available. This perspective underscores that understanding long-run growth requires a nuanced view of how culture dominates institutions over extended periods, rather than relying on short-term or geographically deterministic explanations.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) assert that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa. They argue that this cultural shift has had lasting effects on African development by eroding trust, which is crucial for economic growth. Trust enables cooperation and reduces transaction costs, facilitating market exchange and investment. The slave trade disrupted social networks and created an environment where people were less willing to cooperate with strangers due to the high risk of exploitation or betrayal. This mistrust has persisted over generations, influencing contemporary behavior and attitudes toward institutions and governance. Nunn (2008, p.27; 2008, p.1) argues that this history can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment. The slave trade led to political instability and weakened states, which in turn hindered economic development. This institutional weakness is a key factor in understanding the long-term effects of the slave trade on African growth. However,  Nunn (2008, p.27; 2008, p.1) also notes that this is not the sole determinant; other historical factors such as colonialism and more recent events have played significant roles.

The role of women in economic development is an important aspect that intersects with cultural and institutional factors but does not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women played indispensable roles in the reproduction of communities, particularly in matrilineal societies such as Asante, where their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic underscores how gender roles and cultural norms influenced economic structures, with long-term consequences for production systems. Goldin (2006, p.8) complements this perspective by showing that shifts in women's employment and education in the U.S. were driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes altered female labor supply and household production technology, illustrating how institutional and cultural factors can interact to shape economic development.

Goldin (2006, p.8) notes that the transformation of women's roles was not merely evolutionary but involved significant shifts in social norms and expectations regarding family and career. This quiet revolution in women's employment and education had profound implications for long-run growth, as it altered the dynamics of labor supply and household production. However, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains complex. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional changes highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how these factors influence economic development over time.

The new economic history of Africa emphasizes institutional reforms as key to alleviating poverty, but it does not directly address how culture might dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The hypothesis that institutional variables are more significant than other factors in explaining development is central to this approach. The role of cultural congruence with indigenous institutions becomes crucial in promoting growth; high degrees of congruence enhance government legitimacy and foster development-inducing institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). However, the effectiveness of these institutional reforms is contingent on their alignment with existing cultural norms. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) highlight that while institutional change can drive investment and agricultural productivity in Europe, similar outcomes in Asia are influenced by high levels of investment but do not necessarily lead to abundant grain supplies or low food prices (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuanced understanding suggests that the interaction between cultural norms and institutional changes is pivotal for long-run growth. The implications of this relationship extend beyond economic policies alone; they necessitate a holistic approach that integrates cultural sensitivity into institutional reforms, ensuring that these reforms are not only economically sound but also culturally congruent.

Culture plays an essential role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. The enduring impact of cultural norms on economic outcomes is evident in the historical context of colonial settlements and their institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that high settler mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persistently affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This finding aligns with North's argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1).

The cultural dimensions of institutional change are further illuminated by the role of women in African economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This perspective is complemented by North's discussion on the financial revolution in England, where institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The implications of this relationship extend beyond economic policies alone; they necessitate a holistic approach that integrates cultural sensitivity into institutional reforms, ensuring that these reforms are not only economically sound but also culturally congruent. This approach is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon's work, which shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The historical evidence of colonial settlements and their institutional legacies, as well as the role of women in African economic development, provide a nuanced understanding of how cultural norms shape institutions that drive long-run growth.
