The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue within economic scholarship. At stake is the extent to which cultural values and beliefs shape institutional frameworks that either facilitate or hinder economic development over extended periods. One position posits that cultural norms directly influence economic outcomes by fostering trust, reciprocity, and social capital, thereby enhancing institutional efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view suggests that societies with 'civic' cultures, those characterized by altruism, horizontal networks, and strong community ties, are more likely to develop institutions conducive to growth. Another position contends that the impact of culture on economic growth is mediated through other factors such as property rights and contracting institutions, which are themselves shaped by historical and geographical determinants (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective emphasizes the interdependence of various institutional frameworks and argues that cultural influences must be understood within a broader context of economic and political conditions. The live disagreement revolves around whether culture operates directly on growth through shaping institutional efficiency or indirectly via its influence on other institutional variables.

A central debate in this area is the extent to which cultural values and beliefs shape institutions that, in turn, drive long-run growth. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial European economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. She notes that exogenous cultural theories, such as those proposed by Max Weber and Putnam, often overlook the complex interdependencies between different institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates, which significantly affected current income per capita. They highlight that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of these institutions and that these institutions persisted over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This suggests that cultural factors may interact with geographical determinants to shape institutional frameworks that influence economic growth.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the dynamic interplay between culture, historical contexts, and institutional evolution. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that a more nuanced understanding of institutional change requires considering how cultural beliefs and values evolve over time in response to various factors, including technological advancements and political developments. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they shape the costs of transacting, which in turn affect productivity and resource allocation (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments are cited as examples of institutional changes that reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth. These historical examples underscore how cultural values and beliefs can interact with broader economic and political conditions to shape institutions that drive long-run growth.

Another stream focuses on the role of gender in shaping economic institutions and growth. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). They argue that gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production, suggesting that cultural norms around gender can influence economic outcomes. This perspective is complemented by Nunn and Wantchekon's work on the slave trade and its impact on African culture. They contend that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that historical events can shape cultural norms, which in turn influence institutional frameworks and economic growth. The enduring impact of these cultural norms underscores their role as fundamental causes of long-run growth.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the role of external factors such as geography and mortality rates. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This perspective suggests that cultural values and beliefs interact with geographical determinants to shape institutional frameworks that influence economic growth. The enduring impact of these institutions underscores the importance of understanding how external factors can shape cultural norms and institutional evolution over time.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the legacy of the slave trade has left deep imprints on African institutions, which have been slow to adapt. The authors note that trust is an important determinant of economic development and argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day. This finding underscores the enduring influence of historical events on present-day economic conditions in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

They argue that secure property rights are a key factor in fostering long-run growth, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage investment.

This mechanism is evident in North's (1989, p.1) analysis of the English financial revolution, which led to the creation of new financial instruments and reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The development of institutions like the Bank of England provided a stable market for public debt, which positively impacted private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.1). This institutional framework facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights and lowering transaction costs. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that these institutions were crucial in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), which in turn drove long-run economic success.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) build on this by showing how extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were aimed at benefiting the politically powerful groups that controlled them. This highlights a significant downstream implication: the type of institutions established during colonial rule can have enduring effects on economic development.

These distributional conflicts and inequality, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), reveal how power dynamics within societies can either foster or hinder economic growth. They argue that these unequal institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) . This dynamic is crucial because it highlights how institutional frameworks can be shaped by pre-existing inequalities, which in turn reinforce those inequalities over time. Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this perspective by noting that external shocks, such as the Black Death, could initiate long-term structural changes that altered wage gaps and economic development trajectories (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This suggests that while distributional conflicts and inequality are significant drivers of institutional change, exogenous factors can also play a substantial role in shaping economic institutions.

This indicates that while both regions had similar agricultural productivity, the purchasing power over tradable goods and services was notably different. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) adds a layer to this argument by highlighting that economic development in Europe did not necessarily translate into improved standards of living (van Zanden 1999, p.6). The divergence is further nuanced by the role of institutional changes in north-western Europe, which drove investment in agriculture and subsequently increased agricultural labour productivity. This mechanism underscores how institutional frameworks can influence economic outcomes. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) qualifies this perspective, noting that while there was continuous growth in GDP per capita in some parts of Europe, this did not uniformly improve the standard of living for all (van Zanden 1999, p.6). The downstream implication is that economic development and institutional changes do not always lead to widespread improvements in living standards.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10); (Hornbeck 2010, p.6) examine how agricultural development in Africa was closely tied to the dynamics of export production and foreign trade. This relationship is evident in the varying growth patterns across different regions, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The mechanisms that underpin this connection illustrate a broader pattern: when agricultural productivity is enhanced, it often correlates with increased economic activity driven by external markets. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) qualifies this perspective by showing how social norms can significantly influence the effectiveness of property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This suggests that while institutional frameworks are crucial, cultural factors also play a pivotal role in shaping agricultural development and, by extension, long-run economic growth. The downstream implication is that understanding the interplay between institutions and culture is essential for comprehending the diverse trajectories of economic performance across different regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) show that women have played significant roles in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The cultural norms and social structures of the time not only facilitated but also necessitated women's active participation in economic activities. For instance, in matrilineal societies like Asante, women were essential to the reproduction of communities as their bloodlines defined wealth transmission (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic was not confined to Africa; similar transformations occurred in the U.S. where shifts in education and technology altered female labor supply.

Goldin (2006, p.8) identifies several exogenous changes that drove these transformations, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. These factors created new opportunities for women, altering their roles within households and societies at large. The implications of these changes are profound: they challenge the notion that cultural norms are static or immutable. Instead, they reveal how external forces can catalyze shifts in social structures (Goldin 2006, p.8). These transformations did not occur uniformly across regions or cultures; cultural differences shaped how women's roles evolved.

These norms, though altered by colonialism, persisted and continue to influence contemporary economic dynamics.

The cultural foundation of institutions plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing both the creation and evolution of these institutions. The argument that culture shapes institutions is supported by Ogilvie's discussion on how pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions were influenced by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, reflect underlying cultural values and beliefs that persist through time, shaping the institutional landscape. The persistence of these institutions suggests a deep-rooted influence of culture on long-run growth. This is further supported by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). Institutions create a framework within which economic activities occur, and cultural beliefs shape how these frameworks are structured and maintained.

Moreover, the role of culture in shaping institutions is evident in the historical evolution of gender roles and their impact on economic dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that pre-colonial social norms significantly influenced gender relations (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6), which in turn shaped economic production systems. These cultural norms continued to influence long-run growth by maintaining certain institutional structures, such as the sexual division of labor established during colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The persistence of these norms shows how culture can dominate institutions over extended periods, influencing economic trajectories.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. They argue that the evolution of secure property rights was crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This secure framework allowed for the development of stable markets for both public and private debt, which in turn facilitated long-run economic growth. The cultural context, including beliefs about property rights and financial transactions, played a significant role in shaping these institutions.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture fundamentally shapes institutions, which then drive long-term economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). However, the precise mechanisms by which this occurs remain debated, with some scholars emphasizing the direct influence of cultural beliefs on institutional frameworks and others focusing on the indirect effects through distributional conflicts. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is found in the detailed analysis of pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These institutions, shaped by underlying cultural values, provide a clear example of how culture can dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.
