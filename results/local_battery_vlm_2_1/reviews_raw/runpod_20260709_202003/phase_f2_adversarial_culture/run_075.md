The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue that engages multiple disciplines. At stake is understanding why some societies thrive economically over centuries while others stagnate. One position argues that cultural values and beliefs directly shape economic institutions, which in turn drive growth. This perspective posits that deeply ingrained cultural norms influence how institutions are structured and function, thereby determining long-term economic performance. Another view contends that institutions themselves are the primary drivers of growth, with culture playing a secondary or even negligible role. Proponents of this stance assert that the structure and enforcement of property rights, contractual arrangements, and governance mechanisms are what truly matter for sustained economic development. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are autonomous forces shaping institutional frameworks or merely epiphenomena of deeper structural dynamics. This question is central to unraveling the complex interplay between culture and institutions in fostering long-term economic growth. How does the relationship between culture, institutions, and growth unfold over time? Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This argument is anchored in the role of financial innovation as a key driver of economic development, with the creation of the Bank of England being a pivotal institutional reform. The emergence of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of long-term property rights (North 1989, p.9). This reduction was a product of the fiscal crises that necessitated the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The institutional changes spurred by these financial innovations had far-reaching implications, creating a large and positive externality for the development of both public and private debt markets. These developments were further reinforced by the growth of a stable market for public debt, which facilitated parallel advancements in private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This dynamic highlights how institutional reforms can catalyze economic growth through enhanced financial stability and reduced transaction costs. This qualification underscores that while institutions play a necessary role in economic growth, they operate within a complex interplay of factors.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey's argument challenges interest-only theories by emphasizing the role of cultural shifts in enabling economic growth. This is illustrated by the emergence of a new rhetoric that fostered innovation and entrepreneurship, particularly in countries like the Netherlands, which embraced bourgeois values and rejected aristocratic traditions (McCloskey 2015, p.10). The dissemination of knowledge was also critical; Mokyr highlights how useful knowledge spread throughout Europe through various channels such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This diffusion of ideas facilitated technological advancements and economic growth. However, Mokyr notes that much of this knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and practices rather than codified texts (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The transmission of useful knowledge required written materials as well as personal interactions and training. This tacit dimension underscores the importance of cultural and social networks in the spread of economic growth-enhancing ideas. The interplay between explicit and tacit knowledge suggests that while institutions may provide the framework for economic activity, it is the cultural environment that fosters the generation and dissemination of innovative ideas.

Acemoglu et al. and Austin both posit that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). These authors argue that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which then persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. A specific mechanism for this relationship is that mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies correlated strongly with the presence of European settlements; these settlements in turn shaped early institutions, which continued to influence economic outcomes today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This suggests a direct link between colonial institutions and long-term economic development. However, Austin qualifies this perspective by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). Thus, while institutional changes are crucial, their impact on economic growth is mediated by complex historical dynamics that extend beyond simple correlations with mortality rates. Therefore, understanding long-run growth requires considering not only the initial institutional frameworks but also the evolving responses of local populations to these institutions over time.

Ogilvie's work (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) and that of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) together argue that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie highlights how these conflicts led to the establishment of institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communal structures, which were not solely driven by efficiency but rather by the power dynamics among different social groups. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial factor endowments and institutional frameworks created extreme inequality from the outset. Both works underscore that these institutions, whether in Europe or the Americas, were fundamentally shaped by the interests of elites who sought to maintain their power through various government policies. These policies often restricted competition and perpetuated inequalities, thereby influencing long-term economic trajectories. Ogilvie further elaborates on this by noting that the interdependencies among different institutions within a broader framework are crucial for understanding economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). However, this perspective is nuanced by Sokoloff and Engerman's observation that differences in institutional frameworks across societies can be attributed to varied initial conditions and historical trajectories. This suggests that while distributional conflicts played a significant role, the specific outcomes were contingent on local contexts and power dynamics.

The literature on economic growth and technological advance highlights various factors influencing long-term economic performance, including institutional changes and social dynamics, without explicitly linking these to cultural determinants (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Abramovitz (1986, p.12) observes that the exploitation of advanced technologies is contingent upon social competence, suggesting that societal factors play a crucial role in economic growth. These institutional frameworks are not merely static structures but dynamic entities that evolve over time, influencing a nation's ability to harness technological advancements. Broadberry and Gardner point out that African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that local contexts significantly impact economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This historical perspective underscores how institutional changes can either facilitate or hinder technological advance and economic growth. However, Abramovitz notes that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, highlighting the complexity of the catch-up process in economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). The interplay between institutions and societal factors is crucial for understanding long-term economic performance, but it remains an under-explored area in relation to cultural determinants.

The slave trade's impact on African economic development underscores how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon contend that the slave trade not only depleted Africa’s population but also fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust. This mistrust persists to this day, significantly hindering contemporary economic growth by making it difficult for individuals to engage in cooperative ventures. The mechanism through which trust impacts economic outcomes is multifaceted: it influences investment decisions, business partnerships, and the effectiveness of public institutions. This political fragmentation further exacerbated mistrust by undermining the development of strong, centralized governance structures capable of enforcing contracts and providing public goods. The legacy of this historical mistrust is evident in contemporary Africa, where it continues to impede economic development by creating barriers to social capital and cooperative behavior. Consequently, understanding the deep-rooted cultural impacts of the slave trade is crucial for addressing present-day economic challenges in Africa.

The role of culture in shaping dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence economic institutions by altering collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, she notes that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's decline in these beliefs led to the weakening of guilds.

This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on how extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial, as Ogilvie highlights that the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27), all of which contributed to early industrialization. This interconnectedness underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping economic growth through institutional frameworks.

North emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by pointing out that the neoclassical model holds only under zero transaction costs, but with positive transaction costs, institutions are pivotal (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

North and Weingast further illustrate how the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution played a crucial role by providing a large positive externality for private debt markets, highlighting that even if other forces would have led to success, they would have done so more slowly (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The shared finding is that culture plays a significant role in determining the efficiency of institutions and their impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.25).

Nunn and Wantchekon's work complements this perspective by showing how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact is evident in contemporary Africa, where historical mistrust continues to impede economic development by creating barriers to social capital and cooperative behavior.

Culture's role in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident through several mechanisms. The shared finding across these studies is that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.25). There remains a disagreement over whether the impact of culture on institutions is direct or mediated through other factors such as transaction costs (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The historical evidence of the financial revolution in England shows that cultural shifts, such as those brought about by the Protestant Reformation, directly influenced institutional changes and economic growth. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by demonstrating how cultural changes can directly alter institutions without necessitating mediation through transaction costs.
