The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether cultural beliefs and values are fundamental to the creation of efficient institutions that foster sustained economic development. One position holds that cultural factors, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, directly influence institutional frameworks and thereby promote economic efficiency. This view posits that societies with strong social capital, characterized by high levels of trust and horizontal networks, tend to develop institutions conducive to growth. Another perspective argues that the impact of culture on institutions is limited and that other factors, such as geographic endowments or political stability, play a more significant role in determining long-term economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are antecedent to institutional development or if they operate through mechanisms that reinforce existing institutional structures. Understanding this relationship is crucial for identifying the drivers of economic growth and informing policies aimed at fostering sustainable development. The interplay between culture, institutions, and economic performance has been explored in various contexts, from pre-industrial Europe to contemporary Africa, revealing complex interactions that challenge simplistic explanations. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights. The evolution of these institutions was pivotal to the development of stable markets for both public and private debt. This institutional framework, which emerged from the English financial revolution, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), thereby enhancing economic growth. The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. By facilitating both public and private lending, these institutions created an environment conducive to sustained economic expansion.

This institutional framework played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. Jeffrey Williamson's recent work provides further support for this thesis, highlighting the broader implications of secure property rights and stable financial markets on economic development. The institutions that evolved in seventeenth-century England fostered economic growth and laid the groundwork for political stability, which is essential for sustained development.

North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that the neoclassical model, which focuses on population and savings as principal determinants of growth, fails to account for the critical role of institutions (North 1989, p.1). This perspective underscores that economic growth is deeply influenced by both demographic factors and the institutional environment. The evolution of these institutions in England highlights how secure property rights and stable financial markets can drive long-term economic prosperity.

This suggests that the institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were economically significant and politically transformative. The institutions that evolved during this period continue to influence modern economies by providing a model for secure property rights and stable financial markets.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). Ideas drove economic growth by shaping societal norms and values. For instance, McCloskey highlights how new rhetoric fosters new circumstances, which in turn generate further new rhetoric, creating a feedback loop that facilitates economic enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr reinforces this idea by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in economic growth; he points out that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated through various means such as periodicals and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This implies that the spread of ideas and knowledge across societies can significantly influence economic development. However, Mokyr also notes that much of the useful knowledge during this period was tacit, embedded in skills and practices rather than codified in written form (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This qualification suggests that while ideas are crucial, their tangible impact on growth depends on how effectively they are transmitted and applied within societies.

Distributional conflicts are a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that the structure of economic institutions is fundamentally shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups. These institutions were shaped by both economic conditions and struggles for power among elites and lower classes. This perspective challenges the notion that cultural factors alone can explain long-run growth patterns. Ogilvie notes that these institutions are interdependent within a broader framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The European family system, for example, was interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, all of which contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts shape individual institutions, the overall institutional framework is crucial for understanding economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this point by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This highlights that the institutional frameworks shaped by distributional conflicts can have lasting effects on economic development.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The mechanism is straightforward: Europeans were more likely to settle in areas where mortality was low because they could survive and flourish there. These settlements created institutional frameworks that favored long-term economic development. However, where mortality rates were high, European settlers established extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for the colonizers rather than promote sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern is evident in the persistence of these institutions over time, which Acemoglu et al. argue significantly influence current income per capita by shaping economic performance and industrialization trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These findings suggest that the institutional legacies of colonialism are a significant factor in explaining long-run economic outcomes.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The thesis suggests that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, challenging the notion that this divide began with industrialization. Broadberry and Gupta support this view, highlighting that the prosperous regions of Asia during this period were more similar to stagnating parts of Europe than developing north-western European economies (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This perspective underscores a dynamic, endogenous process where institutional changes in agriculture led to high labour productivity, which did not necessarily translate into abundant grain or low food prices. Austin's analysis reveals that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, suggesting significant variation in outcomes over time (Austin 2008, p.25). This variability implies that the impact of institutions on long-run growth cannot be universally applied across different historical contexts, but rather must be understood within specific temporal frameworks. Thus, the dynamic nature of institutional evolution and its context-dependent effects challenge simplistic explanations of economic divergence.

The impact of the slave trade on African development highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes such as mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) find that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today. This mistrust is a significant determinant of long-term economic outcomes, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This political instability, in turn, contributes to current economic underdevelopment, as recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.27) suggests that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. The relationship between slave exports and state centralization is complex (Nunn 2008, p.1). The impact of these historical events on contemporary African development underscores the importance of understanding how cultural attitudes shaped by past experiences can influence long-term economic trajectories.
