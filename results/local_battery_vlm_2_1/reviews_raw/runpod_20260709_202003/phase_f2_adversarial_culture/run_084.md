The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can help explain persistent economic disparities between societies over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are primary drivers that directly shape institutions and thereby influence economic growth, or if they are secondary to other determinants such as geography, institutional frameworks, or historical events. On one hand, some researchers argue that culture plays a crucial role in shaping the institutional environment, which in turn affects long-run economic performance. Others posit that cultural influences are less direct, often mediated by other factors like colonial institutions or mortality rates, which have enduring effects on economic outcomes. A third perspective suggests that cultural beliefs and values can be antecedents to institutional frameworks, influencing how these frameworks develop and operate. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with each potentially reinforcing the other in a feedback loop that shapes long-run growth trajectories.

Culture's influence on institutions is evident in historical contexts where cultural norms have directly impacted economic outcomes. For example, Ogilvie highlights how different cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, thereby influencing economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high levels of trust and reciprocity tend to have institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability. Putnam's work suggests that cultural beliefs and values can shape institutional frameworks that support long-term development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). However, the role of culture is not always straightforward; it can be intertwined with other factors such as interdependencies within broader institutional systems. For instance, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This view is complemented by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize the importance of contracting and property rights institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Their research shows that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). These findings suggest that while cultural factors may influence institutional development, they are often part of a broader set of determinants that include geographical and historical elements.

Ideas and ideologies were key drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Mokyr 2005, p.56; McCloskey 2015, p.10). McCloskey argues that the emergence of new ideas about liberty, innovation, and progress reshaped societal values, fostering a cultural environment conducive to economic growth. This intellectual shift was a catalyst for institutional changes; it influenced how people thought about their interests and the institutions they created. Mokyr underscores this point by emphasizing that useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of ideas and knowledge across Europe facilitated economic development, as innovations spread rapidly through international conferences, translations, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The cultural struggle in France exemplified how entrenched ideologies could impede or facilitate economic progress; even though France had a history of conservative resistance to change, it eventually embraced new rhetoric that fostered growth. Mokyr notes the importance of tacit knowledge, which was often transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This highlights that while ideas and ideologies are crucial, their practical application requires institutional frameworks that support the dissemination and utilization of both codified and tacit knowledge.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework was pivotal in creating an environment where long-term capital markets could thrive, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The development of new financial instruments, such as the creation of the Bank of England (1694), led to a significant decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflected increased security in property rights, essential for both a stable capital market and sustained economic growth.

The implications of these institutional changes were profound. By providing a secure environment for long-term investments, the institutions established during this period fostered an atmosphere conducive to economic development. North (1989, p.1) highlights that the gains from trade, specialization, and division of labor are key underlying features of modern economies, but these benefits require stable institutions to materialize. This suggests that while cultural factors may influence economic development, it is the institutional environment that ultimately determines the pace and sustainability of growth.

The role of institutions in fostering economic growth has its nuances. North (1989, p.9) notes that the creation of stable markets for public debt provided a positive externality for the parallel development of private debt markets. This indicates that while secure property rights are essential, the broader institutional framework, including financial innovations and market regulations, plays a critical role in economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in England ensured secure property rights and created an ecosystem where both public and private sectors could thrive, underscoring the multifaceted nature of institutional contributions to long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions are a fundamental cause of long-run economic development, shaping the trajectory of growth in former colonies. This institutional legacy is rooted in the mortality rates faced by European colonizers, which determined the type of institutions established. High mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists rather than fostering long-term growth. The mechanism linking colonial institutions to economic development is about the enduring impact of institutional frameworks set during colonization. Acemoglu et al.'s findings illustrate that these institutions, shaped by European colonizers' interests, often prioritized extractive practices over inclusive ones (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

This perspective highlights a critical pattern: the type of institutions established during colonial rule directly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. For instance, where mortality rates were high, colonists implemented extractive institutions that hindered long-term economic development. This institutional framework was designed to maximize rents for European settlers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The enduring legacy of these institutions is evident in the current economic performance of these regions. Acemoglu et al.'s research underscores that the initial conditions set by colonial powers, particularly the institutional frameworks they established, have lasting effects on economic development. This variability in institutional outcomes suggests that cultural factors may also play a role in shaping long-run growth, albeit through the lens of institutional frameworks established during colonial rule.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) jointly argue that the root cause of long-run growth lies in how distributional conflicts shape institutional frameworks, not merely through cultural influences. This perspective is evident in their shared focus on how power dynamics between social groups lead to specific institutional outcomes. This perspective is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) who show that in New World societies, initial inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites. These institutions restricted competition and perpetuated inequality through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), illustrating how institutional frameworks can be molded by distributional conflicts as well as cultural factors. This nuanced view acknowledges that although distributional conflicts are crucial, they operate within a complex web of interconnected institutions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) discuss the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia. They highlight that these discussions often simplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, failing to capture the complex interplay between institutions, culture, and economic growth. One specific mechanism is how the evolution of institutions can be shaped by both endogenous factors and exogenous shocks. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that African responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, ultimately altered their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic process suggests that institutional change is an outcome of cultural adaptations and technological advancements. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further illustrate this by showing how investment in agriculture led to high productivity in southern India, driven by significant levels of investment (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuanced understanding underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutional frameworks over time, which in turn influences long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that Africa's slave trade has led to a persistent culture of mistrust, which in turn reduces economic development. The slave trade disrupted traditional political structures and weakened states across Africa, leading to long-term instability that persists today. This historical disruption created a legacy of institutional fragility that continues to impede economic growth in the region (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn further shows how this institutional weakness is directly linked to modern corruption levels. By weakening state institutions and fostering mistrust, the slave trade has left a lasting imprint on Africa's development trajectory. This historical legacy of institutional fragility continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, creating a persistent barrier to long-term growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Cultural beliefs and values influence institutional frameworks and their efficiency. Ogilvie argues that cultural factors like Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, which in turn affected economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by North's assertion that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance property rights security (North 1989, p.9).

The impact of culture on institutional development is further illustrated by the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural dimension of institutional fragility is also highlighted by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The interplay between culture and institutions is not limited to historical contexts; it extends to contemporary economic performance. Austin points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a more dynamic understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic approach is crucial for understanding how cultural factors continue to shape institutions and economic growth.

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is shown by the shared finding that institutions matter: institutions are interdependent with cultural beliefs, which influence their efficiency and effectiveness (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The degree to which this relationship holds across different historical contexts remains a point of contention. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25), while North and Weingast show that the financial revolution in England played a critical role in its long-run success, highlighting the necessary role of institutions in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the fact that the English financial revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This illustrates how institutions play a crucial role in fostering long-run economic growth.
