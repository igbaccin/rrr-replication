Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions and thereby influencing long-run economic growth. The debate centers on whether cultural factors are fundamental drivers of institutional development or if they are ancillary to other determinants such as geography, technology, or exogenous shocks. Some scholars argue that culture directly influences the efficiency and structure of economic institutions, while others contend that cultural beliefs are merely epiphenomenal to deeper structural forces. The live disagreement hinges on whether cultural factors are the primary cause of institutional outcomes that foster growth, or if they are secondary to other causal mechanisms. This question is not merely academic; it has profound implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary development strategies.

Culture's direct influence on economic institutions has been a subject of extensive debate. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial European institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, challenging the notion that cultural beliefs about afterlife influenced guilds' structure. Putnam's theory of social capital posits that cultural values such as trust and norms of reciprocity facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency. These cultural beliefs, according to Putnam, underlie institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast complement this view by highlighting how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The role of culture is further complicated by its interaction with other institutional frameworks. Ogilvie notes that European family systems, community-based welfare systems, and interlinked institutional frameworks contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. This interdependency suggests cultural factors are embedded within broader institutional contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North similarly emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that they matter significantly under conditions of positive transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).

The relationship between culture and long-run growth is also influenced by exogenous shocks and historical events. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors may be overshadowed by more immediate and impactful historical events. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further complicates this picture by arguing that the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous version of geographical influences on economic development (Austin 2008, p.25).

The interplay between culture and institutions is not unilateral; cultural factors can also be antecedents to institutional development. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This perspective underscores how historical events can shape cultural norms, which in turn influence institutional outcomes. The enduring impact of these cultural norms highlights the long-term effects of historical antecedents on contemporary economic development.

The slave trade's impact on African development is multifaceted, with cultural attitudes being one of its most significant legacies. Mistrust persists as a factor influencing contemporary societal dynamics (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that this culture of mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, underscoring the deep and lasting impact of historical events on cultural norms. The slave trade's legacy is evident in the political instability it caused, leading to weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) elaborates that this political instability has contributed to long-term economic underdevelopment, demonstrating how historical antecedents can shape institutional outcomes. The persistent mistrust and political instability are intertwined, creating a vicious cycle where cultural attitudes reinforce institutional weaknesses. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between culture and institutions in shaping long-run growth.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). This institutional framework facilitated a financial revolution marked by the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This financial stability enabled long-term capital investment and technological change, key factors in modern economies' wealth creation. The secure property rights and reduced transaction costs fostered by these institutions were pivotal for economic growth and political freedom. North notes that the gains from trade are contingent on the presence of effective institutions (North 1989, p.1). This institutional framework enables long-term capital markets, which are critical for sustained economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are posited to better explain core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency or cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie argues that powerful groups shaped early modern European institutions, with purely economic efficiencies playing a lesser role. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who emphasize that differences in institutions are rooted in distributional conflicts that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts led to policies restricting competition, which in turn sustained inequality. Distributional conflicts were embedded within complex institutional frameworks. Ogilvie also acknowledges these frameworks could resist external influences, complicating the application of cultural or efficiency-based explanations. The interplay between institutions and power dynamics reveals that political and social contexts influenced economic growth alongside efficient market structures.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas drive economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). Ideas and ideologies fostered innovation, enabling the diffusion of useful knowledge. This perspective underscores how the spread of ideas and knowledge transformed economies in Europe. McCloskey highlights that economic change was propelled by a new rhetoric that created new circumstances, emphasizing the role of cultural shifts in driving growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr elaborates on this by explaining that useful knowledge grew through personal training and transmission in addition to written records (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The interplay between ideas and knowledge dissemination suggests that economic growth is deeply intertwined with cultural factors, as the diffusion of knowledge across Europe facilitated innovation. This highlights a nuanced relationship between ideas, knowledge dissemination, and economic growth, where cultural factors play a critical role in facilitating the spread of innovation.

(2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions are a fundamental determinant of long-run economic outcomes, with the extractive nature of these institutions significantly influencing current income per capita. This institutional framework persisted over time, creating a lasting impact on economic development. The extractive nature of these institutions shapes economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutional legacies on contemporary economic development, challenging cultural explanations by highlighting the primacy of institutional factors in determining long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) highlight that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia reflect different historical paths and institutional evolutions. These authors underscore a dynamic, endogenous process in which environmental responses and technological choices play crucial roles in shaping economic development. The African story, for example, illustrates how Africans adapted to their environments using available technologies, suggesting that institutions evolved in response to these adaptations (Austin 2008, p.25). This perspective aligns with the view that institutional change often precedes investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity and subsequent economic development. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that this higher productivity did not necessarily lead to abundant grain or low food prices in developing parts of Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The implications are significant: economic growth is contingent on how institutions adapt to environmental challenges, technological advancements, and investment patterns.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial. For instance, the European family system was interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).
