The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars, particularly when examining how cultural beliefs influence economic performance and institutional development. The intellectual stakes involve understanding whether cultural factors can explain differences in economic outcomes across regions and historical periods. This debate pits those who argue that cultural values shape institutions to promote economic growth against those who contend that institutions are primarily driven by other factors such as geographical endowments or political structures. While some scholars emphasize the importance of cultural beliefs in fostering trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, all of which facilitate coordinated actions and economic efficiency, others posit that extractive institutions established due to high mortality rates in colonies have a more significant impact on current income per capita. This disagreement highlights the need to explore how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

This historical mistrust, shaped by events such as the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27), illustrates how cultural attitudes can persist across generations and influence long-term outcomes. The slave trade disrupted African societies. It caused political instability and weakened states, as Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrates with data showing a negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization. This disruption has long-term effects on economic development: Bockstette et al. (2002) find that countries with a longer history of state-level institutions have higher real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27). The institutional damage from the slave trade is not only political but also cultural, as Nunn (2008, p.1) indicates when he states that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. The mistrust and lack of social capital resulting from these historical events can hinder economic growth by reducing cooperation and investment in public goods. However, the effects are not uniform across Africa: Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.15) control for contemporary data on malaria transmission to approximate historical conditions. This suggests that while the slave trade had a significant impact on African development, other factors such as disease environments also played a role.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs. This development reflects an increased security of long-term property rights, which is critical for both a robust capital market and sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutions that facilitated this transformation provided significant positive externalities for private debt markets, further enhancing economic stability and growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) note how the evolution of these institutions allowed England to develop a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported private financial operations. However, the institutional framework alone does not guarantee sustained growth; other factors such as political stability and effective governance are also essential. This historical context highlights that while institutions are crucial, they must be supported by a broader set of economic and political conditions to foster long-term growth. The role of credible commitment in underpinning growth is also evident here; the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England provided a framework where long-term commitments could be reliably enforced.

This divergence underscores that differences in urban development, real wages, and institutional changes were key drivers of economic growth before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the prosperous parts of Asia during this period resembled the stagnating regions of Europe more than they did the developing northwest. Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this perspective by noting that structural changes initiated by the Black Death, such as increased mobility and labour-saving technological innovations, contributed to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This early divergence suggests that long-term economic development in Europe was influenced by both institutional changes and demographic shifts.

This highlights that institutional variables are pivotal in explaining development, as seen in the new economic history of Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is shared by scholars who emphasize the role of historical institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) posits that understanding current African institutions requires delving into precolonial and even prehistoric times, as these past contexts have shaped present-day institutional frameworks. This long-term view underscores the gradual nature of institutional change, a perspective that is crucial for comprehending Africa's economic trajectory.

This focus on institutions does not fully address how cultural factors might influence growth dynamics. Hopkins (2009, p.8) acknowledges the significance of congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions in promoting effective governance and development. This suggests that culture, through its influence on institutional legitimacy and effectiveness, plays a role in long-term economic performance. This highlights the interconnectedness of cultural, institutional, and economic factors in shaping African economies.

This finding suggests that historical economic conditions were more robust than often portrayed, indicating a potential for sustained growth when cultural and institutional factors align favourably. It also underscores the complexity of African economies where multiple variables interact to shape long-term development outcomes.

This examination reveals how institutions in New World societies advantaged elites through various government policies, contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Bryant 2006, p.35). They argue that European colonies in the New World were characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from their outset. This inequality was reinforced by institutions that restricted competition and favored elite interests. The institutional framework in these societies evolved to maintain the status quo, hindering economic development and perpetuating social disparities. Bryant (2006, p.4) extends this perspective by noting that the enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms from prior developments were crucial for industrial modes of production or imperial hegemony. This suggests that the institutional advantages for elites in New World societies were not just a matter of immediate policy decisions but were deeply rooted in historical legacies. Bryant (2006, p.4) also underscores that these legacies did not operate in isolation; they interacted with broader cultural and social structures which shaped the trajectory of economic growth. This interaction highlights the complex interplay between institutions and culture in determining long-run economic outcomes.

de Vries (1994, p.18); (Hornbeck 2010, p.6) examine how agricultural developments can enhance economic growth through increased labor input and technological advancements. De Vries argues that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by heightened labor productivity and new consumption patterns, laid the groundwork for subsequent industrial growth. This phenomenon involved a significant shift in societal norms and economic behaviors, where households released more labor to the market and became increasingly reliant on market- and government-supplied services (de Vries 1994, p.18). Hornbeck extends this idea by showing how technological advancements such as barbed wire fencing enabled farmers to protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation facilitated a more efficient use of land resources, thereby enhancing agricultural productivity (Hornbeck 2010, p.6).

Hornbeck's work (Hornbeck 2010, p.6) highlights the importance of social norms in facilitating economic growth by encouraging ranchers to control cattle and manage their property effectively. These norms complement legal responsibilities and create an environment where farmers can operate more efficiently. This underscores how cultural factors, such as adherence to social norms, can support technological advancements in agriculture. The mechanisms do not directly address the role of culture in shaping long-run growth. The influence of culture on economic outcomes is often indirect and intertwined with broader institutional frameworks that are shaped by historical legacies (de Vries 1994, p.5).

This overlooked role underscores how cultural factors can both facilitate and hinder long-run development, depending on historical legacies and institutional frameworks (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) underscore the importance of African women's labor in community reproduction, noting that matrilineal societies like Asante relied heavily on women's bloodlines for transmitting wealth and office. Goldin (2006, p.8) highlights a similar dynamic in the U.S. where shifts in household technology and educational institutions altered female labor supply and demand, leading to significant changes in employment patterns. These transformations involved exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational opportunities (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes did not occur uniformly across all sectors or regions. The quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. for instance, was a gradual process influenced by various factors including social norms and expectations regarding future work. This nuanced view of economic growth underscores how cultural factors can both facilitate and hinder long-run development, depending on historical legacies and institutional frameworks.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. The Great Enrichment began during this period was driven by a cultural shift that valued innovation and economic growth. McCloskey illustrates this through the "Franco-French War, " a cultural struggle in France where new ideas clashed with traditional ones (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Even though France had experienced significant economic growth, conservative forces often hindered further development due to resistance to new ideologies. Countries like the Netherlands and Britain embraced these new ideas more readily leading to sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) supports this by noting that the diffusion of useful knowledge was a key driver of economic growth during this period. This knowledge included tacit knowledge which was transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The widespread dissemination of knowledge across Europe facilitated the Great Enrichment, as ideas about innovation and economic progress spread rapidly despite national differences. Mokyr (2005, p.56) also highlights that this process was not uniform; while some nations embraced these new ideas more readily than others the overall trend was towards greater economic growth driven by a shared intellectual movement.

Austin and Ogilvie share a critique of institutional explanations for economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They argue that simplistic views of causation fail to account for dynamic, endogenous processes. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical paths by compressing different periods; this perspective is echoed by Ogilvie's critique of exogenous cultural theories, which she argues can obscure the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Ogilvie highlights how distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin's analysis of Africa's dynamic responses to environmental challenges further illustrates that endogenous factors played a significant role in shaping development paths; these responses involved the active use of available technologies. Ogilvie qualifies this perspective by noting that institutions are interdependent and must be studied within their broader frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how endogenous processes interact with institutional frameworks over time; it is about the dynamic interplay between cultural beliefs distributional conflicts and institutional evolution.

Abramovitz and Stern jointly assert that economic growth is influenced by technological advances and social change (Stern 2004, p.3; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). These factors shape the output mix of economies over time. Early development stages often see a shift from agriculture to heavy industry, which can increase emissions due to higher pollution intensities in industrial sectors. As economies mature there is a transition toward services and lighter manufacturing which have lower emissions per unit of output (Stern 2004, p.3). This dynamic interplay between technological progress and social change suggests that the environmental impact of economic growth follows a complex trajectory shaped by institutional frameworks and cultural beliefs. Abramovitz notes that productivity levels are erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, highlighting the importance of social competence in exploiting advanced methods (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This underscores how cultural factors, such as education and social norms, can influence an economy's ability to adopt new technologies efficiently. Stern qualifies this perspective by noting that the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) hypothesis, which posits that economic growth initially increases pollution but eventually reduces it, has a flimsy statistical foundation (Stern 2004, p.1). This critique suggests that while technological advances and social change can mitigate environmental impacts, the relationship between economic growth and environmental degradation is more nuanced than simple convergence models suggest.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions were shaped by European mortality rates and had lasting effects on economic outcomes in former colonies, establishing them as fundamental drivers of long-run growth. The institutions were determinants of economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial powers established extractive institutions to maximize their rents rather than promoting long-run growth in colonies. This institutional design, influenced by mortality rates and political power dynamics, led to persistent economic disparities between former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) show that these institutional changes significantly impacted the surge of industrialization among formerly poor colonies, thereby accounting for current income differences.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident through various frameworks that emphasize institutional design and cultural beliefs. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) highlight that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, showing how the English financial revolution significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered a stable market for public debt. This development was pivotal in England's long-run success, demonstrating that institutions play a necessary role in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Similarly, North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter because they facilitate gains from trade, which are key to the wealth of nations.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illuminated by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who contends that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency. For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to a decline in these guilds due to changing cultural beliefs. This shows how non-economic cultural beliefs shape institutional structures and economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin (2008, p.25) builds on this by arguing that Africans' responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, ultimately changed their economic paths. These responses were dynamic and partly endogenous, highlighting the importance of cultural adaptations in shaping long-term economic development.

The impact of culture on institutions is also evident in the context of African economic history. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that colonial constructs and gender roles significantly affected production systems and had long-term consequences for gender relations. This demonstrates how cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks interact to shape economic outcomes over time, as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs continue to influence contemporary economic structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). In addition, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) reveal that settler mortality rates influenced the establishment of institutions in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita. This highlights how historical factors, including cultural adaptations and institutional design, continue to shape long-term economic growth.
