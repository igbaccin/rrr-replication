The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is whether cultural beliefs and values fundamentally shape institutional frameworks that drive economic growth over extended periods, or if such beliefs are merely epiphenomenal to broader structural dynamics. The disagreement pits those who see culture as a primary force shaping institutions against those who view it as secondary to other factors like geography, demography, or political economy. Some scholars emphasize the deep historical roots of cultural norms, arguing that these norms influence economic outcomes by shaping institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others posit that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous shocks and strategic interactions among elites, with culture playing a more passive role. This disagreement hinges on whether cultural beliefs are seen as endogenous to institutional change or as an independent variable driving it.

The debate extends to how institutions themselves are conceptualized: whether they should be understood as complex frameworks that interact in nuanced ways (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) or discrete entities whose impact can be studied independently. For instance, some scholars argue that the interdependencies between different institutional elements within a broader framework make it essential to study these frameworks holistically rather than focusing on individual institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This holistic approach suggests that cultural norms are embedded in and reinforced by various interconnected institutional practices. By contrast, other research emphasizes the role of exogenous shocks like European mortality rates in shaping colonial institutions, suggesting a more direct causal pathway from environmental factors to economic outcomes rather than through culture (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how different factors interact to shape long-term economic growth.

Cultural beliefs and values are seen as driving forces behind the formation of institutions that support economic growth. For example, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how cultural norms like trust, reciprocity, and horizontal networks can facilitate coordinated actions that enhance efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that societies with "civic" cultures, those characterized by strong social capital, are more likely to develop institutions that foster economic growth. Such institutions include robust guilds and communes, which have been linked to superior economic performance in regions like northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This emphasis on cultural norms as drivers of institutional change contrasts with views that focus on the role of political and strategic interactions among elites.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for understanding how secure property rights and economic growth are fostered. For instance, North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England played a critical role in securing property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England reduced transaction costs, providing a positive externality for both public and private debt markets. The significance of these institutions lies in their ability to create stable frameworks that support long-term economic development.

Cultural norms also interact with broader historical processes to shape economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which suggests that colonial institutions significantly affected current income per capita, oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective emphasizes the dynamic nature of institutional change and the importance of understanding how cultural norms interact with broader historical processes to shape economic outcomes. By focusing on the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and historical context, scholars can gain a more nuanced understanding of how long-term growth is achieved.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) propose that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. A key mechanism through which these changes operated was the establishment of extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita by shaping the economic trajectories of colonies (Austin 2008, p.25). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous relationship between geography and economic development. This perspective challenges the notion that cultural beliefs dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. This variation underscores the complexity of institutional change and its effects on economic growth.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic growth (de Vries 1994, p.18; McCloskey 2015, p.10). They propose that the Industrious Revolution was a critical precursor to the Industrial Revolution. This period saw increased labor input and new consumption patterns, facilitating subsequent industrial growth (de Vries 1994, p.5). The shared finding is that ideas and ideologies were not merely passive reflections of economic conditions but active drivers of change. McCloskey emphasizes that the cultural shifts in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were instrumental in fostering a new rhetoric that, in turn, created new circumstances conducive to economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr complements this view by highlighting how the diffusion of useful knowledge across Europe was facilitated by international conferences and personal communications, underscoring the role of tacit knowledge in driving economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.56; Mokyr 2005, p.14). This perspective challenges views that focus on culture as the fundamental cause. However, Mokyr also notes that the codification of knowledge varied based on technology and costs, implying that while ideas were crucial, their implementation depended on practical considerations.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight that distributional conflicts are crucial in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. These scholars argue that economic institutions were not merely shaped by efficiency considerations but were heavily influenced by the interests of dominant groups seeking to maintain their power and wealth. The community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England, according to Solar, was interdependent with labour markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system; this distinctive framework may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, societies characterized by extreme wealth disparities often implemented government policies that restricted competition. These policies favoured elites at the expense of broader economic growth. This perspective underscores how institutional frameworks, shaped by distributional conflicts, can persist over time, influencing long-run economic trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution marked by the creation of the Bank of England (1694) and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated both long-term capital markets and broader economic growth. North and Weingast's work underscores that these institutions provided a large positive externality for developing stable public debt markets, which in turn supported private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution allowed England to achieve rapid and decisive growth by reducing the risks associated with long-term investments. This institutional shift was not merely an isolated event but part of a broader political model that traded certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, reflecting a strategic response to fiscal crises. North's observation nuances this narrative: economic growth is intrinsically linked to gains from trade, specialization, and division of labor (North 1989, p.1). This perspective highlights the multifaceted role of institutions in fostering economic development beyond just secure property rights.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This culture of mistrust is a legacy of the historical trauma inflicted by the transatlantic slave trade, which not only decimated African populations but also eroded social cohesion and institutional development. Nunn (2008, p.27) explains that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political fragmentation is a critical antecedent to long-run growth as it undermines the state's capacity to enforce property rights, provide public goods, and foster an environment conducive to economic development. The adverse effects of the slave trade on state centralization are evident through the historical accounts that document how African states were left in disarray by the slave trades. The impact of the slave trade on trust and institutional development varies across Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the effects of the slave trade vary significantly depending on local conditions such as disease environments, which influenced the intensity and duration of the slave trades (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The malaria transmission index, for example, provides a close approximation to historical conditions, suggesting that areas with higher malaria prevalence experienced different dynamics of the slave trade.

Broadberry and Gupta's work (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) establishes that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. The economic development of north-western Europe, marked by institutional change leading to high agricultural labor productivity, did not result in abundant grain or low food prices because labor shifted from agriculture into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This pattern is shared finding (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1; van Zanden 1999, p.6), which shows that economic development and changes in the standard of living are complexly related. Pamuk's work (Pamuk 2007, p.5) suggests a different mechanism for divergence within Europe itself: the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. These long-term effects included declining interest rates, increased labor mobility, and technological innovations driven by scarcity and high labor costs (Pamuk 2007, p.24). The implication is that exogenous shocks like pandemics can catalyze institutional changes that drive economic divergence. Van Zanden's work qualifies this mechanism by showing that there was no clear relationship between economic development and the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) argue that institutional reforms in Africa have been shaped by historical economic patterns, with both colonial and precolonial histories playing crucial roles. This suggests that understanding the role of culture in long-run growth requires examining how historical institutional changes have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner show that export production patterns, driven by foreign trade, were crucial for income levels in many Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century (Broadberry&Gardner_2022). However, Hopkins notes that the effectiveness of these institutions in promoting growth depends on their congruence with indigenous institutions; high degrees of congruence enhance legitimacy and development, while low congruence fosters regionalism and corruption. This implies that cultural factors, such as precolonial ethnic fragmentation, can mediate the impact of institutional reforms on economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) highlight that women's economic contributions significantly shaped growth in both pre-colonial African societies and the U.S. challenging views that marginalize their role. Women were indispensable to community reproduction in Asante, with matrilineal bloodlines defining wealth transmission. This dynamic was driven by the dual international trading networks of the trans-Sahara and the trans-Atlantic, where women's roles remained crucial despite the competition over resources such as gold and land. This transformation was a quiet revolution driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) note that women's roles were deeply entrenched in the social fabric of Asante society, where matrilineal systems ensured their economic contributions were vital to community survival. This cultural embedding of women’s economic roles suggests that institutional changes alone may not suffice for long-term growth without considering the cultural context in which these institutions operate.

The skill premium is an important predictor of long-term economic performance (Bryant 2006, p.35; van Zanden 2009, p.24). Bryant (2006, p.35) argues that understanding the transition to industrial modes of production requires acknowledging the cumulative effects of prior developments, including cultural norms and social relations. This perspective aligns with van Zanden's (2009) analysis of human capital formation as a key determinant of long-term economic growth. Van Zanden shows that the skill premium reflects conditions for human capital formation, which in turn influence economic growth. However, cultural norms and social relations are not merely background conditions but active forces shaping economic outcomes. For instance, van Zanden's discussion of unskilled laborers in Japan, India, and Indonesia highlights how urban-rural wage gaps and agricultural productivity are interconnected with demographic changes. This interplay suggests that cultural factors, such as the organization of labor and agrarian systems, can significantly influence the skill premium and, consequently, long-term economic performance. This perspective underscores the importance of considering how cultural factors interact with institutional changes to drive long-term growth, even if these interactions are not explicitly modeled in studies focused on the skill premium.

Cultural factors play an integral role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows how cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency by altering collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This interplay between culture and institutions is crucial for understanding long-term economic performance. For instance, North argues that institutions matter significantly in economic growth due to their role in reducing transaction costs, a perspective supported by his work on the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice, as highlighted by North and Weingast, was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This period saw significant institutional changes that provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the importance of considering different historical periods and paths when examining economic growth. He emphasizes that African responses to their environments changed over time, making use of various technologies when available (Austin 2008, p.25).

The role of women in economic development is another critical aspect of cultural influence on institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods by maintaining essential roles in community reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This contribution challenges views that marginalize the role of women in economic development, showing that cultural beliefs and gender roles are integral to understanding long-term economic performance.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions matter for long-run growth. The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which cultural factors, such as social norms and collective action games, influence institutional changes that drive economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The historical evidence of women's roles in African economic development and the impact of European mortality rates on colonial institutions settles this debate. These cases show how cultural beliefs and gender roles shape institutions, reinforcing the idea that culture is a fundamental cause of long-term growth.
