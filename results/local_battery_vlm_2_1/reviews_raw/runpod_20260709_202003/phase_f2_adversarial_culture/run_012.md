The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether the roots of prosperity lie in cultural values and beliefs, which influence institutional frameworks over centuries, or if other factors are more decisive. One position emphasizes the direct impact of cultural norms on institutional efficiency. This view suggests that deeply ingrained societal beliefs can shape collective action problems and economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another position argues for a more indirect role: cultural values interact with broader institutional frameworks to create environments conducive to growth, but these frameworks are themselves influenced by other factors, such as distributional conflicts or the evolution of public choice institutions. This debate is significant because it informs how we understand and address economic development challenges today.

This conflict is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that European mortality rates shaped colonial institutions, which in turn affected long-run economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The implication is that cultural factors are less important than the specific historical conditions that led to the establishment of certain institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts drove the institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2011). Serfdom, guilds, and communities were shaped by power dynamics and collective action problems, suggesting a more nuanced role for culture in determining economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The question of whether cultural factors are a primary or secondary influence on institutional development remains unresolved.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize that the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This institutional development enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.1) further elaborates that this secure environment allowed for specialization, division of labor, and technological change, all key features of modern economies (North 1989, p.1). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large positive externality for the parallel development of a private debt market. This institutional framework was pivotal in creating an environment conducive to economic expansion (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) warn that these institutions were necessary for growth but other underlying forces may have been needed as well.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies, along with the growth of useful knowledge, were pivotal drivers of economic change. These scholars contend that the evolution of economic thought and the dissemination of practical knowledge played a significant role in shaping the economic landscape of Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. A key mechanism here is the interplay between cultural narratives and technological advancement. For instance, McCloskey highlights how cultural shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, facilitated new economic ideologies that promoted growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This cultural transformation altered societal attitudes towards commerce and created an environment where innovation thrived. He notes that while national differences existed, the broader European intellectual movement ensured that innovations were widely shared across borders. This sharing of knowledge fostered a collective advance in technological capabilities, which in turn drove economic growth. Mokyr notes that this process was not uniform; tacit knowledge, which is often embodied in skills and practices, played a significant role (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The transmission of such knowledge required personal interactions and training, highlighting the importance of social networks in facilitating economic progress. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of cultural influences on economic growth, suggesting that while ideas and ideologies were foundational, their impact was mediated through practical knowledge and social dynamics. Cultural factors inspired new economic paradigms and shaped the mechanisms through which these paradigms were implemented and sustained. The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus deeply intertwined with the dissemination and application of useful knowledge.

Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and that of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) suggests that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes were driven by the mortality rates faced by early European settlers, which influenced the types of institutions established in these colonies. High mortality rates led to the creation of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework persisted over time, significantly impacting current income levels. Austin (2008, p.25) qualifies this perspective by highlighting the dynamic nature of African responses to their environments and suggesting that endogenous factors also played a role in shaping economic outcomes. This nuanced view acknowledges the complexity of institutional evolution and the interplay between exogenous shocks and local adaptations. Therefore, while colonial institutions had a profound impact on long-run growth, the story is more complex than simple causality, involving both external impositions and internal responses.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) assert that Africa's slave trades created deep-seated cultural norms of mistrust. This mistrust is a persistent feature that continues to shape contemporary African societies. The mechanism by which the slave trade fostered mistrust involves the erosion of social capital and the disruption of traditional community structures, leading to long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). These disruptions weakened state centralization, creating fragmented states that struggle with governance and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.1).

This perspective underscores how the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond immediate economic impacts into the realm of cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) notes that while the slave trades had profound effects, they are not the sole determinant of Africa's current underdevelopment. Other historical factors, including colonialism and more recent events, also play significant roles in shaping Africa's economic trajectory.

Distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key factor in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality, offering an alternative explanation to cultural determinants of long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The persistence of these institutions is primarily due to the entrenched interests of powerful groups who benefit from maintaining the status quo. For instance, the craft guilds and merchant guilds in medieval Europe were not merely efficient organizational forms; they were also mechanisms for controlling competition and preserving elite privileges. This perspective suggests that the legacy of distributional conflicts can be as enduring as cultural legacies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that individual institutions within a wider framework are interdependent; studying one institution in isolation is insufficient and we must look at entire institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This nuance underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need to consider multiple factors when analyzing long-run growth.

Culture plays an integral role in shaping institutions that govern long-run growth by influencing economic behavior and institutional frameworks. Institutions such as guilds have been shown to be deeply influenced by cultural beliefs; for example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these institutions reflects a broader pattern where culture shapes economic outcomes through its influence on institutional structures. The evolution of financial instruments and institutions such as the Bank of England illustrates how secure property rights, fostered by cultural norms and political agreements, can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This interplay between culture and institutions is not limited to Europe; in Africa, the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting current levels of trust and economic behavior (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The role of culture in long-run growth is further complicated by the interdependency of individual institutions within wider frameworks. This interdependency means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, entire institutional frameworks must be considered to understand their impact on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets and credit systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need for a holistic approach when analyzing long-run growth. The historical evidence shows that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in both European and African contexts, where cultural beliefs have shaped institutional frameworks that persist to this day. Cultural theories show how non-economic beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency by changing the equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). In Africa, the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This historical evidence and empirical comparison settle the disagreement by demonstrating how cultural legacies can shape institutions over centuries.
