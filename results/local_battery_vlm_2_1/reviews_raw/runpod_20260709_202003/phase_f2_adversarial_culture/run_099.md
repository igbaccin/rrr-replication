The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The debate revolves around the extent to which cultural factors shape economic institutions and, consequently, influence economic performance over the long term. One camp posits that cultural beliefs and values are crucial in determining the efficiency and structure of economic institutions, thereby driving growth. This perspective emphasizes that societies with certain cultural traits, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, tend to develop institutions that foster economic growth. The opposing view argues that other factors, such as geographical conditions or institutional frameworks, play a more significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. These scholars contend that the impact of culture on institutions is often overstated; instead, they highlight the importance of exogenous variables like mortality rates and extractive institutions. This debate underscores the need to understand how cultural influences interact with other determinants of growth, as it has implications for policy-making and development strategies. The resolution of this dispute requires a nuanced examination of historical evidence and theoretical frameworks that can account for the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic performance. The institutional evolution in England during the 17th century was pivotal for securing property rights and driving economic growth, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1). They contend that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, led to a dramatic reduction in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This decline in transaction costs underpinned the security of property rights over time, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutions that emerged during this period provided a significant externality for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24), further bolstering economic activity. North's broader argument underscores that economic growth hinges on the institutional framework that governs public choice, emphasizing that secure property rights are a cornerstone of sustained economic development. The financial revolution in England exemplifies how institutions can facilitate economic growth by enhancing the security and efficiency of markets. This perspective contrasts with cultural explanations for economic growth by highlighting the primacy of institutional factors over cultural determinants. Institutions are critical; however, their effectiveness depends on the broader political and economic context in which they operate.

Distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by power struggles rather than efficiency considerations. The community, craft guild, and merchant guild were formed to consolidate elite control over resources. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's account of how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality reinforced the dominance of elites through government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie notes that these distributional conflicts are interdependent within a broader institutional framework. This interdependence means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, we must examine entire institutional frameworks to understand their collective impact on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Bryant 2006, p.35; McCloskey 2015, p.9). This view is shared by Bryant, who emphasizes the importance of cultural norms as a fundamental aspect of societal transformation. Cultural norms actively drive economic outcomes through their influence on individual behaviors and institutional frameworks according to McCloskey and Bryant (2006, p.35); (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This perspective suggests a mechanism where cultural shifts foster a new rhetoric, which in turn creates favorable circumstances for economic growth. For example, McCloskey notes the role of a new rhetoric in countries like Spain, where conservative reasons initially throttled economic growth but eventually gave way to a more dynamic environment (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This dynamic is underpinned by a broader cultural shift that includes ideas, ideologies, changes in social structure, and technological advancements. Bryant highlights the interconnectedness of these factors, asserting that without the enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms, transitions to industrial modes of production would be impossible (Bryant 2006, p.35). McCloskey's emphasis on ideas and ideologies raises questions about the specific pathways through which these cultural shifts translate into economic change. This suggests that while ideas and ideologies are crucial, they operate within a complex web of interdependent elements that collectively drive economic growth.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies influenced the type of institutions established there (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, in turn, have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The relationship between settler mortality and institutional quality is robust, as shown by a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This finding underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic performance. The role of institutions in long-run growth involves their evolution over time as well as their initial establishment. Acemoglu et al. highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions initially shaped economic outcomes, their extractive nature has had lasting effects on economic development trajectories. The persistence of these institutions underscores the complex interplay between historical factors and contemporary economic conditions.

Nunn and Wantchekon's research demonstrates that the slave trade has had profound effects on African development by shaping cultural attitudes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade resulted in a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust has persisted over centuries, influencing long-term economic outcomes. The historical context of slavery and its impact on political instability is well-documented: the slave trades caused long-term political instability that weakened and fragmented states(Nunn 2008, p.1; Nunn 2008, p.27). This fragmentation impeded state centralization, which is a critical determinant of current economic performance. Thus, the legacy of the slave trade continues to affect contemporary African economies through its enduring impact on cultural attitudes such as mistrust and political instability.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women played crucial roles in African economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). The contribution of women is particularly evident in matrilineal societies like the Akan, where women's bloodlines were integral to the transmission of office and wealth. This dynamic underscores how cultural norms and gender roles can significantly influence economic development. These impacts are evident in the enduring sexual division of labor, which continues to reflect the structures established during the colonial period. The role of women in economic growth is further substantiated by Goldin's findings on the quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation was driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and shifts in household production technology, which altered female labor supply and made part-time work more accessible. These factors collectively highlight how cultural attitudes towards gender roles can shape economic institutions over time.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) show that technological and structural changes have significantly influenced economic growth over time (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; Pamuk 2007, p.24). Hornbeck's analysis of the American Plains reveals how the introduction of barbed wire fencing transformed agricultural development by strengthening property rights and mitigating livestock damage. This innovation enabled farmers to better control their resources, a pattern that echoes Ellickson’s (1991) findings in modern California where social norms also encouraged ranchers to manage cattle effectively (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). Pamuk's examination of the Black Death similarly highlights how demographic shifts and labor scarcity drove technological innovations and institutional changes. The plague brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and adjustments within guilds, contributing to the rise of rural industries and labor-saving technologies (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These structural changes collectively illustrate how exogenous shocks can trigger enduring economic transformations. However, Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this by noting that these changes were not isolated events but part of a broader historical context that shaped long-term economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations underscores the complex dynamics through which structural changes influence economic growth over extended periods.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both highlight that the divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800 (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This earlier divergence is rooted in institutional evolution, investment patterns, and policy experiences. These factors shaped economic development across regions by influencing how resources were allocated and utilized. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was driven by significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that African responses to their environments also played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes through technology adoption (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interaction between endogenous factors and environmental responses suggests that local adaptations were pivotal in determining long-term economic trajectories. The precise mechanisms by which these adaptations influenced growth remain complex and multifaceted.

These findings underscore a crucial aspect of long-run economic development: the importance of endogenous factors such as institutional changes and investment patterns. These elements actively shape the trajectory of economic growth over time.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is complexly intertwined with institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework has been recognized by some studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness suggests that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting efficiency and growth. For instance, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' highlights how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutional evolution and economic development are deeply intertwined with cultural factors. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This highlights how cultural norms, such as trust and reciprocity, can shape institutional frameworks that support long-term economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding underscores how exogenous factors can interact with cultural norms to shape institutional outcomes over time.

The precise mechanisms by which these adaptations influenced growth remain complex and multifaceted. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what they call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This interplay between exogenous factors and cultural responses suggests that local adaptations were pivotal in determining long-term economic trajectories. The African story, for example, illustrates how Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the institutional landscape, making use of various technologies when available (Austin 2008, p.25).
