The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional evolution and thereby economic growth, or merely secondary influences. One position argues that cultural beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions, affecting efficiency and growth. Ogilvie examines this perspective through the lens of pre-industrial Europe, noting that cultural theories often explain the rise and fall of institutions like guilds by linking them to broader social norms and values (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). A related but distinct question is how institutions themselves can be interdependent within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may overlook critical dynamics. This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional frameworks and their impact on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Meanwhile, another position posits that extractive institutions established during colonial periods have persistently affected current income per capita, emphasizing exogenous factors like mortality rates as key determinants (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The debate over the role of culture in institutional development is further complicated by rival explanations that propose alternative causal mechanisms. For instance, some scholars argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous approach to understanding economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. This perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and low transaction costs in fostering long-term economic development (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9).

The relationship between culture and institutional development is further complicated by antecedents that trigger cultural changes, leading to shifts in institutions. For example, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This perspective highlights how historical events can shape cultural norms, which in turn influence institutional frameworks. The interplay between these factors underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional development and its impact on long-run growth, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of how culture interacts with institutions to drive economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) assert that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The establishment of a stable market for public debt by the Bank of England also facilitated the development of private debt markets, reinforcing economic stability.

However, the implications of these institutions extend beyond immediate economic benefits: they also underpinned political freedom, as secure property rights created an environment conducive to both economic and political stability. This dual impact highlights how institutional changes can have far-reaching effects on societal structures. North's argument that "there are no institutions in the neoclassical world" underscores the necessity of considering institutions when examining long-run growth (North 1989, p.1). Historical evidence from seventeenth-century England demonstrates that the evolution of these institutions was an active response to fiscal crises and political pressures, shaping both economic outcomes and societal norms. This interplay between institutions and economic development suggests that cultural factors alone cannot fully account for long-term growth patterns, as the institutional framework provides the necessary conditions for sustained prosperity (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the proliferation of ideas and ideologies is fundamentally driven by economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that the spread of useful knowledge was pivotal in fostering economic development. Useful knowledge, encompassing both codified and tacit forms, facilitated technological advancements and improved productivity. Mokyr highlights that much of this knowledge circulated through personal transmission and training, underscoring the importance of social networks and educational systems in diffusing innovative ideas (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This mechanism suggests that cultural factors, which influence the generation and dissemination of useful knowledge, play a crucial role in long-run growth. McCloskey notes that economic growth involves a complex interplay between interests, institutions, and ideologies (McCloskey 2015, p.9). The spread of ideas was influenced by cultural norms and societal values, which shaped the rhetoric around economic change. This dynamic interplay indicates that while culture can drive the generation and adoption of new ideas, it operates within a broader institutional framework that supports or hinders these processes. Culture thus acts as a catalyst for economic growth by fostering an environment conducive to the creation and dissemination of useful knowledge, but its effects are mediated through institutional structures.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) assert that colonialism's institutional changes are fundamental drivers of long-run economic outcomes. The key mechanism is the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates among European settlers. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, influenced economic performance by determining the nature of property rights and political power structures. The extractive institutions, which often involved centralized control and limited private property protections, were not conducive to fostering long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutions can evolve over time in response to changes in political dynamics and economic conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Distributional conflicts are central to shaping economic institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue. The former identifies distributional conflicts as the key driver behind the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. These institutions arose from power struggles among different social groups. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the guild system was shaped by the interests of craftsmen in maintaining their monopolies and controlling competition (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) similarly observe that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. They argue that differences in factor endowments led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective challenges efficiency-based explanations by highlighting how institutional outcomes reflect the balance of power among different groups. This emphasis on distributional conflicts suggests that cultural beliefs can interact with these conflicts to shape institutions in complex ways.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and related studies highlight the complex interplay between historical paths, institutions, and economic performance in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) provide a nuanced view of African economic history, showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance and underscores the importance of local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). Broadberry and Gardner note that these factors influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production across various African countries. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and related studies emphasize the need for a more comprehensive understanding of Africa's economic history, one that integrates cultural dimensions to fully explain long-run growth patterns.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); Nunn (2008, p.27) assert that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust that persists today and shapes long-term economic outcomes. This mistrust is an enduring legacy that continues to influence contemporary economic behavior and social interactions. Nunn shows that this long-term political instability has hindered state centralization, a crucial factor in fostering economic growth. The fragmented states resulting from the slave trade's legacy have struggled to implement effective governance and economic policies, perpetuating underdevelopment. This dynamic is further complicated by recent empirical studies linking Africa's history of corruption to its precolonial experience with slavery (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn (2008, p.27) suggests that these historical factors contribute to the current state of underdevelopment in Africa. The legacy of the slave trade thus provides a critical lens through which to understand the enduring impact of cultural attitudes on economic development, highlighting how mistrust and political instability continue to shape African economies today.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Institutions are critical for economic development because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.1; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This is evident from the historical case of England, where the establishment of secure property rights through financial innovations like the Bank of England led to significant economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The role of cultural attitudes in institutional development is further highlighted by the impact of the slave trade on African economies. The legacy of the slave trade has fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and influences contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical trauma has shaped institutions in ways that perpetuate underdevelopment, as seen in the enduring effects of political instability and corruption (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The interdependency of cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding long-run growth. Cultural values such as trust and reciprocity can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital illustrates how civic cultures in northern Italy contributed to superior economic performance compared to southern Italy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical experience with slavery provides a critical lens through which to understand the enduring impact of cultural attitudes on economic development. This dynamic is further complicated by empirical studies linking Africa's history of corruption to its precolonial experience with slavery, highlighting how mistrust and political instability continue to shape African economies today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical determinants of trust are pivotal in shaping current institutional frameworks.

The precise role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains a subject of debate. While it is agreed that institutions matter for economic growth, there is disagreement over whether cultural attitudes or exogenous factors like geography and resources play a more significant role (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from Africa shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by demonstrating the enduring impact of cultural attitudes on institutional development.
