The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth has been hotly debated among scholars, with differing views on whether cultural factors are antecedent to institutional development or if they operate through other mechanisms. Some researchers argue that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, leading to divergent paths of economic growth. Cultural theories propose that certain beliefs can motivate individuals to follow efficient rules, while others contend that cultural norms of trust and reciprocity facilitate coordinated actions that enhance economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). However, rival explanations suggest that the establishment of extractive or inclusive institutions is driven by factors such as mortality rates and settler populations rather than culture. Furthermore, some scholars identify antecedent factors like the slave trade, which shaped cultural norms of mistrust that persist today. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional development or if they are secondary to other historical and economic forces. This tension underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how culture interacts with institutions over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast, along with North (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1), argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of private debt markets. The implications are significant: while other forces might have ultimately led to England's success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without the institutional changes. This perspective underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, even if cultural factors are also at play.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey's work underscores how cultural shifts, particularly the rise of bourgeois values and rhetoric, fostered an environment conducive to innovation and economic growth. Mokyr highlights the role of useful knowledge, which institutions produce but broader cultural attitudes towards learning and experimentation also shape. The diffusion of this knowledge across Europe was facilitated by personal communications, international conferences, and periodicals, highlighting how culture enabled the spread of ideas that drove economic change. Mokyr further elaborates on the importance of tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships, underscoring the embeddedness of cultural practices in the dissemination of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This dynamic interplay between ideas, knowledge, and cultural practices illustrates how culture can shape long-run growth trajectories, independent of institutional frameworks. McCloskey's work suggests that this process is economic yet deeply intertwined with social and political changes (McCloskey 2015, p.10).

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlement patterns, influenced by mortality rates, led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly impacted current income per capita. The mechanism underlying this process is that the differential mortality rates faced by Europeans determined their ability to settle and establish institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This exogeneity of settler mortality creates a robust natural experiment for estimating the impact of institutions on economic performance, with strong first-stage relationships between settler mortality and current institutions. Further, Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers designed these institutions to maximize their own rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights a critical pattern: the persistence of extractive institutions is driven by the interests of the colonial powers. However, this perspective is qualified by the recognition that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development even if they are designed for rent extraction; instead, they can be shaped by politically powerful groups who fear losing their power with institutional change (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key factor in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the persistence of pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be better explained by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.4) analysis, which shows that in New World societies with greater inequality, elites used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their advantages. The mechanism here is clear: distributional conflict shapes institutions because powerful groups design them to preserve their own status while blocking those of lower rungs from gaining political or economic power. One downstream implication of this mechanism is the persistence of inequality, as these institutions are designed to perpetuate the existing power structures rather than promote widespread growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that interdependencies among institutions complicate their evolution and impact. Institutions do not function in isolation; instead, they form complex frameworks where changes in one area can have ripple effects throughout the system. This implies that even when distributional conflicts drive institutional design, the broader framework of interconnected institutions may resist or modify these changes, potentially mitigating some of the negative impacts on growth over time.

The impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic development highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists today and acts as a significant barrier to economic development by impeding cooperation and collaboration, both essential for growth (Nunn 2008, p.1). The persistence of this mistrust has contributed to political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the political instability caused by the slave trades has resulted in a negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization. This historical legacy continues to shape African economies today.

The literature on complexity in economic growth processes (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2) highlights how technological advance and social change are multifaceted and dynamic, shaping long-run growth trajectories. This perspective challenges simple convergence hypotheses, which often overlook the intricate interplay between institutional frameworks and technological progress. He notes that while secure private property rights are crucial, the adoption of such institutions varies across countries due to complex endogenous factors.

Austin (2008, p.2) further argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al. This complexity is evident in how social competence for exploiting advanced technologies has evolved over time. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) explains that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, and social competence for exploiting these methods was still limited, particularly in the earlier years and in the more recent latecomers. As the pre-World War I decades wore on, however, both these qualifying circumstances became less important, indicating a gradual but steady improvement in social competence.

The implications of this complexity are significant for understanding long-run growth. The multifaceted nature of technological advance and social change suggests that cultural factors, which influence institutional frameworks and social competence, play a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced approach to studying economic growth, one that considers the dynamic interplay between technology, institutions, and culture.

The role of culture in shaping institutional frameworks is pivotal for understanding long-run growth. Ogilvie and North both agree that institutions matter; however, they diverge on the specifics of how cultural factors influence these institutions. Ogilvie emphasizes distributional conflicts as key drivers of pre-industrial European economic institutions, noting that exogenous cultural theories can significantly impact collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife influenced medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, which in turn affected economic efficiency. North, on the other hand, focuses on the creation of secure property rights as a critical institutional development for long-run growth (North 1989, p.9). The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, exemplifies how institutions can reduce transaction costs and enhance economic growth.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illuminated by the work of Acemoglu et al. and Austin. Both scholars highlight that differences in European mortality rates influenced the types of institutions established in colonies, which have persisted to this day (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical cultural factors on institutional development and economic performance. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25), suggesting a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of geographical determinants. The African story illustrates how Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories.

The specific mechanisms through which culture influences institutions are also evident in the work of Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. North and Weingast similarly demonstrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England, provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt. This institutional framework was essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are pivotal for long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The precise remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of cultural factors versus exogenous shocks in shaping these institutions. Ogilvie shows how Catholic beliefs influenced guild structures and economic efficiency, while Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust which persists to this day (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the role of cultural factors in shaping institutions. For example, Ogilvie's analysis of how Catholic beliefs influenced guild rules provides a clear example of how cultural factors can shape economic institutions, while Nunn and Wantchekon's work on the slave trade shows how cultural legacies can have long-lasting effects on trust and economic development.
