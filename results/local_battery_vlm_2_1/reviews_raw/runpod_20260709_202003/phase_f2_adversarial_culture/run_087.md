Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth, yet scholars disagree on how and to what extent cultural factors influence institutional development and economic performance. One position posits that culture is a fundamental cause of economic growth through its impact on institutions; another views distributional conflicts as more crucial. This debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the interplay between culture, institutions, and long-run growth. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs can directly influence institutional structures and efficiency. Some scholars argue that distributional conflicts offer a better explanation for core economic institutions (Author Year). The stakes are high: resolving this disagreement could clarify how societies achieve sustained economic development.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is evident in various historical contexts. Ogilvie argues that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to their decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of geography affecting economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would ultimately have led to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional development provided a large and positive externality for the parallel growth of a market for private debt. Furthermore, North contends that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that gains from trade are key to the wealth of nations (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9).

The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial for understanding long-run growth. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system was interdependent with labor markets and credit markets, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective underscores the importance of studying entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further illustrated by the impact of gender-based colonial constructs on economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This highlights how cultural beliefs and social norms can influence institutional structures and economic outcomes, particularly in contexts where gender roles are deeply ingrained.

The relationship between culture, institutions, and long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Culture influences institutional development through its impact on social norms and values; distributional conflicts also shape core economic institutions. Understanding these dynamics requires a nuanced approach that recognizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the legacy of the transatlantic slave trade has left an enduring mark on African societies, fostering a deep-seated mistrust among individuals. This cultural shift persists as a force shaping contemporary social dynamics. The slave trade disrupted traditional community structures, leading to a pervasive sense of insecurity and distrust that continues to influence interpersonal relationships. This mistrust is particularly pronounced in regions historically affected by the slave trade, where it has become ingrained in the social fabric. Akyeampong and Fofack illustrate how colonial constructs and codification of gender roles during this period also had long-term consequences for gender production, reflecting the sticky nature of pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. These enduring effects underscore the profound impact of historical events on contemporary societal structures and economic outcomes. The persistence of mistrust highlights the deep-rooted nature of cultural beliefs and their ability to shape institutional development over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that European mortality rates during colonization significantly influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which have persisted and affected current income per capita. These scholars identify a strong correlation between settler mortality and institutional outcomes; high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not long-run economic growth (Author Year). This dynamic is evident in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasis on equilibrium institutions, which may be extractive even if they do not promote development. These institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize their own interests over broader economic benefits. The persistence of these extractive institutions, driven by the legacy of colonial practices, has profound implications for contemporary economic outcomes in former colonies. This historical institutional framework challenges the notion that cultural factors alone determine long-run growth, as it underscores the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on current economic performance.

McCloskey (2015, p.10); (Mokyr 2005, p.56) jointly assert that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. These ideas shaped the prevailing rhetoric and influenced societal values, which in turn fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. McCloskey's work highlights how cultural shifts, particularly the rise of bourgeois values, created new circumstances that promoted economic enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This cultural transformation drove changes in institutions and policies to align with emerging ideologies.

Mokyr further elaborates on this idea by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe, facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, was crucial for economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This diffusion process underscores how ideas and ideologies transcended national boundaries, creating a shared intellectual movement that propelled economic growth across Europe.

Mokyr also notes the importance of tacit knowledge in this process. Tacit knowledge, which is not explicitly codified but embedded in skills and practices, played a significant role in the economic advancements of the time (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This form of knowledge required personal transmission and training, highlighting the interconnectedness of cultural factors and economic outcomes.

The interplay between ideas, ideologies, and tacit knowledge suggests that cultural factors were fundamental to the economic growth experienced in Northwestern Europe during this period. The diffusion of useful knowledge and the role of tacit knowledge indicate that cultural elements were integral to the economic transformations, challenging the notion that institutions alone drive long-run growth.

This perspective on culture's role in shaping institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth underscores the complexity of economic development, where cultural factors actively shape institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). While these perspectives often focus on institutional factors as the primary drivers of economic development, they tend to overlook the complex interplay between culture and institutions. This dynamic process is not limited to Africa; it extends to other regions as well. Bryant (2006, p.35) emphasizes that effective causality in economic development is governed by a causal connectedness that encompasses enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms inherited from the past (Bryant 2006, p.4). The 'sophisticated geography' approach advanced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) must be understood as an ongoing process influenced by these factors, not a static environmental obstacle to economic development. This perspective highlights that cultural elements are integral to the institutional frameworks that drive long-run growth. For example, the European trajectory to modernity was shaped by a multitude of factors including civic urbanization, legal-juridical developments, productive innovations, and scientific advancements (Bryant 2006, p.4). These factors collectively contributed to the secularizing dynamic that refashioned both the institutional orders of Europe and the mentalities of its inhabitants. Bryant (2006, p.35) cautions against dismissing Eurocentric views as empirically suspect or analytically incoherent. The revisionist position, which seeks to undermine these views, risks explanatory distortions by restricting effective causality to shorter-term and immediate processes (Bryant 2006, p.35). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth, acknowledging the complex interplay between cultural factors and institutional frameworks.

This perspective highlights the role of institutional evolution in shaping long-run economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of private debt markets. The evolution of these institutions provided a positive externality that fostered economic growth by ensuring secure property rights over time. The implications of this institutional framework extend beyond immediate economic gains. North (1989, p.1) points out that the neoclassical model, which focuses on population and savings as principal determinants of growth, overlooks the critical role of institutions in a world with positive transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). This perspective underscores the necessity of institutional frameworks for sustained economic development, suggesting that cultural factors alone are insufficient to drive long-run growth. The institutional evolution in England illustrates how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs can create an environment conducive to economic prosperity.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) jointly establish that distributional conflicts among elites shape economic institutions. They argue that these institutions reflect the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain their status. One specific mechanism through which this occurs is the manipulation of property rights and governance structures to favor elite interests. In pre-industrial Europe, for example, guilds and serfdom systems were maintained because they benefited ruling classes who controlled these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This pattern of institutional design perpetuates inequality by restricting competition and limiting opportunities for broader economic participation. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle where elites use their power to shape institutions that, in turn, sustain their dominance. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how this dynamic played out in the New World, where extreme wealth disparities led to institutional frameworks that entrenched inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how cultural norms interact with economic institutions to create enduring patterns of growth and stagnation across different societies.

This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings highlight how cultural norms can interact with economic institutions to create enduring patterns of growth or stagnation.
