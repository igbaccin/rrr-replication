The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually are the relative importance of technological progress versus institutional frameworks in driving sustained economic development. One live disagreement involves the primacy of technological innovation as the driver of growth versus the foundational role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining that growth. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they enable efficient use of innovations, while others emphasize that technological advancement itself is the primary catalyst for growth. The crux lies in understanding how these factors interact to produce long-term economic outcomes.

The interaction between technological progress and institutional development is a key aspect of economic growth. Technological advancements require complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr argues that without institutional improvements, even significant technological progress can be frittered away by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective aligns with Kuznets' view that advancing technology necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Both scholars highlight the importance of institutions in harnessing technological progress to drive economic growth. The interplay between these factors suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by robust institutions to achieve sustained growth.

The role of institutions is further underscored by their impact on transaction costs and market exchanges. North posits that effective property rights and enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This aligns with Hornbeck's findings that technological improvements in agriculture, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The efficiency gains from reduced transaction costs underscore the importance of institutions in enabling economic activities. Together, these studies indicate that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to efficient market exchanges.

Institutional frameworks also influence the dynamics of technological progress and social change. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The interplay between technological progress and social change highlights the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, thereby driving sustained economic development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions provides additional insights into the dynamics of institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p. 7) argues that pre-industrial European institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts and efficient solutions to transaction problems. This perspective suggests that the evolution of institutions is influenced by power dynamics and conflicts over resources. Institutions are shaped by underlying social and political forces. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for comprehending how institutions influence long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a pivotal moment where political institutions were restructured to enforce property rights more effectively. This process involved establishing rules as well as ensuring their enforcement and adaptation. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that credible commitment mechanisms were essential for maintaining these institutions over time; they argue that the regime's commitment to property rights improved the state’s ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The institutional changes post-Glorious Revolution illustrate how political stability and secure property rights can create a conducive environment for economic growth. This dynamic highlights the importance of institutions not just as regulators but as active participants in shaping long-term economic outcomes, underscoring that sustained growth requires both the establishment and effective enforcement of property rights.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that European mortality rates during colonialism were crucial in shaping institutions and long-run economic outcomes. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. These institutions, shaped by the political power dynamics of the time, were not conducive to economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. This mechanism highlights how initial colonial conditions can have lasting effects on institutional quality and economic performance. However, Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) emphasize that institutions are not static; they evolve over time and can be influenced by various factors, including political changes and economic pressures. This dynamic nature of institutions suggests that while initial conditions matter, there is potential for institutional change that could alter long-run economic outcomes. Thus, the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth underscores the importance of understanding historical contexts in shaping current economic performance.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7) demonstrate that institutions designed to protect property rights can facilitate economic development. Hornbeck demonstrates how the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enabled farmers to better safeguard their land, significantly enhancing agricultural productivity by reducing externalities from livestock damage. This institutional change allowed for more efficient use of land and resources, directly contributing to economic growth. Ogilvie complements this finding by showing that pre-industrial European institutions like merchant guilds provided security and contract enforcement as "club goods". These institutions mitigated information asymmetries and principal-agent problems in long-distance trade, fostering economic efficiency. Ogilvie notes that the efficiency of these institutions can vary with changing conditions and over time; they must adapt to maintain their effectiveness. This highlights a nuance in institutional design: while strong property rights and contract enforcement are beneficial, the institutions supporting them must be flexible enough to evolve with economic changes. Long-run economic growth requires robust initial institutions as well as adaptive ones capable of responding to new challenges and opportunities.

This adaptability is crucial for sustaining economic development over the long term; it ensures that institutions remain effective despite changing economic landscapes.

Technological advance and social change are pivotal in shaping economic growth independently of institutional frameworks (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Komlos 1998, p.8). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) highlights that technological progress can accelerate capital stock growth by reducing the relative price of capital goods. This dynamic is amplified when technological opportunities encourage modernization, which in turn boosts productivity and aggregate output. Komlos (1998, p.8) complements this view by showing how economic growth during the Industrial Revolution was accompanied by changes in consumption patterns due to rising relative nutrient prices. These shifts reflect broader adjustments in economic behavior driven by technological and social changes. This implies that while technological advance can drive economic growth, its impact is moderated by social and demographic factors, highlighting the complex interplay between technology, society, and economic development. The implication is that long-run economic growth must account for these dynamics to ensure sustained progress.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) argue that by 1800, the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway. They show that urban development and productivity differences were significant by this period, with advanced parts of India and China resembling stagnating southern, central, and eastern European regions rather than the developing northwestern European ones. The authors posit that the link between urbanization and economic development is strengthened by external economies of scale associated with urbanization as well as localization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This pattern suggests that the spatial concentration of diverse industries conferred benefits that could not be realized in protoindustrial settings, highlighting the importance of urban infrastructure and specialized information flows. However, Pamuk (2007, p.22) notes that technological changes driven by capital-intensive industries also played a crucial role (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Technological advancements were concentrated in urban areas, where guilds produced new technologies rather than rural industries. This implies that the divergence was not only due to urban development but also to technological innovations that required significant capital investment, further differentiating regions based on their economic and institutional capabilities. The implication is that long-run economic growth must account for these dynamics to ensure sustained progress.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share a perspective that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This insight offers an alternative to the notion that institutions are crucial for long-run economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) specifically highlights that technological progress, enabled by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, is a key driver of economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets (1973, p.10) supports this view by noting that modern economic growth is closely tied to the mass application of technological innovations, many of which are based on recent scientific discoveries. This feedback loop between science and technology propels economic advancement. Mokyr (2005, p. 54) also acknowledges a role for institutions in sustaining economic improvement. Without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, the benefits of such progress could be limited or even reversed by rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that while knowledge accumulation is pivotal, institutions play a supporting role in ensuring the sustainability and efficiency of economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly highlights that mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with further progress in science and technology (Kuznets 1973, p.4), suggesting that institutions are crucial for harnessing these advances effectively. This interplay between technological progress and institutional development underscores the importance of a supportive institutional framework in sustaining economic growth.

The dynamics of technological advance and social change also influence the catch-up process in economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that the same technological opportunity permitting rapid progress by modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This indicates that institutions facilitating technological adoption and capital accumulation are essential for long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further supports this view by arguing that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-run growth because they facilitate the adoption of new technologies and reduce transaction costs. The disagreement lies in whether distributional conflicts or efficiency concerns primarily drive institutional arrangements.
