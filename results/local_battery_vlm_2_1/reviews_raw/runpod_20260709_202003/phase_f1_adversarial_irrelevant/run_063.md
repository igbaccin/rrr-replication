The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of understanding economic development. At stake is the identification of key factors driving sustained economic progress and the mechanisms through which they operate. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of technological innovation as the mainspring of economic growth, often viewing institutions as secondary or complementary, and those who argue that institutions are fundamental drivers of economic outcomes, shaping the conditions under which technological advancements can be effectively utilized. Understanding whether institutional frameworks are critical in facilitating long-run economic growth or merely supportive of other primary factors is essential for designing effective policies aimed at fostering sustained development.

Institutions play a crucial role in complementing and sustaining technological progress. Mokyr notes that without institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be limited by rent-seeking behaviors, which erode economic improvements over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is shared by Kuznets, who argues that economic growth driven by technology necessitates corresponding institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Institutions facilitate the coordination and enforcement mechanisms required for technological progress to translate into sustained economic development. The interplay between technological innovation and institutional frameworks highlights the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to long-run economic growth.

The significance of institutions is further underscored by their role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing market exchanges. North argues that political institutions specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This view aligns with Hornbeck's findings on the impact of barbed wire fencing, which enhanced agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). These examples illustrate how institutions can directly influence economic outcomes by lowering barriers to efficient resource allocation and market transactions.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The political factors underpinning this development were pivotal in creating a stable environment that allowed markets to flourish; the regime's commitment to enforce rules governing these exchanges ensured that investments would yield returns without fear of expropriation, thereby fostering long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.7) maintain that institutions complement reputation-building strategies by providing constraints when reputation alone fails to prevent reneging on agreements. This view underscores the importance of political institutions in enhancing the efficacy of economic transactions; they act as a safeguard against opportunistic behavior, thereby ensuring that economic actors can engage in long-term planning and investment with greater confidence. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.5) posits that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs like land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, are insufficient to explain economic growth. These models overlook the critical role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs; without a robust institutional framework, even societies with abundant resources may struggle to achieve significant economic development. As specialization and division of labor increase, the volume of market exchanges grows, making it essential for institutions to reduce costs per transaction and ensure smooth economic operations.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The authors argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. also show that there is a strong correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies and the presence of European settlers, early measures of institutions, and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These findings highlight that the institutions established during colonial times were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run economic growth. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping institutions during pre-industrial Europe due to power struggles rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that various economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, and merchant guilds were products of these conflicts. She shows that the Mesta, a guild-like association of shepherds and sheep-owners in pre-modern Spain, has been reinterpreted as an efficient solution to property rights and resource access problems, rather than merely blocking agricultural improvements (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This interpretation underscores how institutions can emerge from distributional conflicts. Domar (1970, p.7) contends that slavery or serfdom arises when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, highlighting a specific economic condition under which these institutions emerge (Domar 1970, p.7). However, Domar also notes that technological progress and increased capital use do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor, qualifying the downstream implications of efficiency-driven institutional change (Domar 1970, p.6).

The persistence of these institutions had a profound impact on economic development.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) jointly examine how technological changes and historical events influenced economic development. Hornbeck's study on the introduction of barbed wire fencing demonstrates that technological improvements in agriculture enhanced property rights protection and reduced externalities from livestock damage, facilitating agricultural development on the American Plains. This finding is complemented by Pamuk’s analysis of the Black Death, which highlights how the pandemic initiated long-term structural changes leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. However, Hornbeck qualifies this mechanism by noting that direct technological effects do not solely drive the main results; rather, increased protection from others' cattle plays a significant role (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This suggests that while technological changes can foster economic growth, their impact is often mediated by institutional factors such as property rights and externalities management. Pamuk's analysis of the Black Death further illustrates this point, showing how technological advancements in capital-intensive industries emerged during the late medieval centuries, driven by increased costs of soldiers and firearms (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This interplay between technology and economic development underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when examining long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr highlights the accumulation of useful knowledge as a key factor in driving economic growth. This process often involves recombining existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets further elaborates on this dynamic by noting that technological advancements are closely tied to specific needs and conditions of the originating country, illustrating how necessity drives invention (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This interplay between technological progress and economic growth suggests that while institutions may play a supporting role, the fundamental engine of growth lies in the accumulation and application of knowledge. Institutions can mitigate coordination failures and prevent rent-seeking behavior that might otherwise erode economic gains. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the long-term benefits of technological advancements, even if their primary role is supportive rather than foundational.

Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating economic growth by addressing coordination failures and preventing rent-seeking behavior that could erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54; North 1989, p.6). Mokyr's argument highlights the necessity of institutional progress to complement technological advancements, ensuring sustainable economic improvement. North further elaborates on this point, emphasizing that institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms, which are essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth. This synergistic relationship between technology and institutions is evident in Kuznets' work, where he notes the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional adjustments for efficient use (North 1989, p.5). Institutions thus serve as a critical complement to technological progress, enabling long-term economic benefits through coordinated efforts and reduced transaction costs.

The role of institutions extends beyond merely facilitating growth; they also shape distributional outcomes and influence the nature of economic activities. Ogilvie provides a detailed examination of pre-industrial European institutions, arguing that these structures were designed primarily to address distributional conflicts rather than purely efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly impacted the relative incomes of former colonies. The efficiency-driven approach to understanding institutions is thus insufficient; it must be complemented by an appreciation for how institutions address power dynamics and distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The historical evidence from various studies shows that institutions have a profound impact on long-run economic growth, particularly through their role in mitigating coordination failures and preventing rent-seeking behavior. Hornbeck's analysis of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains demonstrates how institutional changes can enhance agricultural development by protecting property rights (North 1989, p.6). This historical example underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating technological advancements and ensuring sustainable economic progress. The precise measurement of these effects, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al., shows that institutions play a significant role in accounting for current income differences among former colonies (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical evidence settles the disagreement on how institutions shape long-run growth by providing concrete examples of institutional changes driving economic development.
