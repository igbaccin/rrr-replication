The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, as it addresses whether the trajectory of economies can be influenced by deliberate institutional design. The debate hinges on whether institutions are merely passive reflections of economic outcomes or active drivers that facilitate technological progress and market efficiency. Two prominent positions exist in this dispute: one emphasizes institutions' role in fostering growth through efficient allocation of resources and reduction of transaction costs, while the other views these institutions as responses to underlying distributional conflicts rather than catalysts for growth. The interplay between technology and institutions is a crucial aspect of this debate. Institutions can both facilitate and hinder technological progress by shaping incentives and providing frameworks for innovation. Understanding this dynamic is essential for grasping how economies evolve over time. Kuznets argues that economic growth requires not only technological advancements but also institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize these innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North 1989, p.5). This interplay between technology and institutions sets the stage for exploring how different scholars have approached this relationship, and whether their theories on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth align or diverge.

North and Weingast argue that institutional evolution governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They contend that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial. This dynamic facilitated market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, which in turn stimulated economic activity. The institutional framework established during this period created an environment where economic transactions could occur with greater efficiency and lower risks, thereby promoting long-run growth. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on the significance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting that neoclassical models overlook the critical role of institutions in determining societal wealth (North 1989, p.5). This perspective underscores how institutional structures can enhance economic performance by fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further explain that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing their efficacy in preventing reneging on agreements (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). They caution that while institutions are essential, they must be appropriately chosen to effectively mitigate incentive problems. This qualification suggests that the design and implementation of institutions play a critical role in determining their impact on economic growth, emphasizing the need for careful consideration in institutional development. The interplay between secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is fundamental to understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. explain this phenomenon through the lens of politically powerful groups, which may resist institutional change if it threatens their political power or does not directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic is evident in colonies where high mortality rates led to extractive institutions that persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This mechanism underscores how initial institutional designs can have lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. caution that while institutions are crucial, their impact is contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they were established (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Temin argues that economic growth dynamics are fundamentally shaped by labor allocation and technological advance (Temin 2002, p.4; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Abramovitz's work also emphasizes the role of technological progress in driving economic growth. For instance, Abramovitz explains how technological advances can reduce the price of capital goods relative to labor, thereby accelerating the growth of the capital stock. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how economies catch up; it suggests that countries with lower initial productivity levels might experience faster growth due to the adoption and diffusion of advanced technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Temin's analysis focuses on Europe’s post-WWII Golden Age and shows that this period was characterized by a significant disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium, driven by technological shifts and social changes, played a key role in the unusually rapid economic growth observed during this era (Temin 2002, p.3). However, Temin also notes that while new growth theories have provided valuable extensions to traditional models, they often come at the cost of complexity, which can obscure the underlying mechanisms driving growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.30) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, are key in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe highlights how merchant guilds were initially efficient providers of security and contract enforcement but evolved into inefficient cartels due to shifting power dynamics among merchants. This evolution was driven by distributional conflicts as merchant elites sought to maintain their positions and control over resources. Hornbeck extends this perspective by examining the role of technological change in agriculture, noting that while barbed wire fencing enhanced property rights protection on the American Plains, its impact was more nuanced than just improving efficiency. The introduction of barbed wire significantly reduced externalities from livestock damage, thereby facilitating agricultural development. However, Hornbeck's findings also suggest that the direct technological improvements of barbed wire were not the primary drivers of this development; instead, the increased protection from others' cattle played a crucial role (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This indicates that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is deeply intertwined with the broader social and political context in which they operate. The distributional conflicts that shape these institutions can either enhance or hinder their efficiency, depending on the specific circumstances and power dynamics at play.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological progress drives economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr emphasizes the accumulation of useful knowledge as the primary engine of economic growth. This process is not merely linear but also involves coordination failures that require institutional progress to sustain economic improvements. Without such complementary institutional advancements, rent-seeking behaviors could erode the gains from technological progress. Kuznets' analysis supports this view by highlighting how mass application of technological innovations requires further scientific advances and institutional adjustments. These adjustments are crucial for efficiently utilizing new technologies and ensuring sustained growth. The interplay between technology and institutions is thus essential; technological progress alone cannot guarantee long-term economic growth without supportive institutions that facilitate its effective use.

Mokyr also notes that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This means that new inventions frequently build upon prior knowledge and the understanding of current technologies. Technological advancements are thus deeply interlinked with the broader knowledge base, suggesting that institutions play a critical role in fostering an environment where such knowledge can be effectively shared and utilized.

This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the accumulation and application of useful knowledge. The effective utilization of new technologies requires not only scientific advancements but also the right institutional framework to support their widespread adoption.

The role of institutions in promoting economic growth is therefore multifaceted, encompassing both the direct facilitation of technological progress and the broader social and political context that shapes these institutions.
