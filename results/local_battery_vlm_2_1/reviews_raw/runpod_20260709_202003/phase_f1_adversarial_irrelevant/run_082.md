The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economics and economic history because it addresses the fundamental mechanisms driving societal prosperity. At stake intellectually is understanding how different institutional frameworks facilitate or hinder sustained economic development over centuries. The live disagreement revolves around two main positions: one emphasizes the primacy of political and legal institutions in securing property rights and fostering growth, while another focuses on technological innovations and factor endowments as primary drivers. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or exogenous factors that independently shape economic outcomes.

A central argument is that political institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes by ensuring credible commitments and secure property rights. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This perspective highlights how political stability and institutional reliability are essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. North posits that the development of efficient markets and a reliable medium of exchange is contingent upon the establishment of property rights that allow individuals to engage confidently in transactions with strangers (North 1989, p.2). This view underscores the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic efficiency.

The debate extends to the role of technological innovations and factor endowments. Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that other factors, such as institutional changes, were more critical (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to technological advancements or resource availability. Austin supports this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Institutions play a pivotal role in determining long-term economic trajectories, more so than technological progress or resource endowments alone (Author Year p.N).

Their work highlights the importance of political institutions in enforcing rules and commitments, which are essential for economic development.

The institutional framework, as North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) assert, complements reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints that prevent reneging on agreements. This is particularly relevant in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to ensure compliance. For instance, institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. By doing so, they enhance the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others even when personal knowledge or reciprocal exchange relationships are absent.

The ability of institutions to adapt to changing circumstances influences their effectiveness in fostering economic growth. This adaptability ensures that institutions remain relevant and effective over time, thereby sustaining long-term economic development. Institutions that fail to adapt may become obsolete or counterproductive, hindering economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding long-run economic growth, with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo and feared losing their influence through institutional change (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic is evident in colonies where high mortality rates among Europeans led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The persistence of these institutions highlights their design to maximize short-term gains for colonists.

Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both focus on economic development in Europe without explicitly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth. Van Zanden argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, while Komlos demonstrates that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. The two studies jointly suggest that economic development did not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in standards of living; instead, it was marked by a divergence between aggregate economic performance and the actual living conditions of the population. This pattern highlights how economic growth can be decoupled from enhancements in well-being (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Van Zanden's analysis further indicates that total factor productivity could generate long-term growth without significant improvements in living standards, suggesting that structural changes within the economy might not directly benefit the broader population. Pamuk (2007, p.8) extends this point by showing how urban-rural linkages can weaken, leading to persistent differences in wages and living standards between regions. Komlos's findings (Komlos 1998, p.8) demonstrate that relative price effects and shifts in consumption patterns also play a crucial role in determining food consumption and nutritional outcomes, which are key components of living standards. These studies collectively illustrate how economic development can lead to complex and sometimes counterintuitive changes in the standard of living.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) together with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) examine the Industrial Revolution and economic divergence by focusing on technological barriers and international price arbitrage. They argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, driven significantly by differences in labour productivity. This mechanism implies that Asian economies faced significant constraints in their ability to compete with European economies. Broadberry and Gupta further note that wages were equalized between sectors within each economy, influencing the price of non-tradable commodities, which depended negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This qualification suggests a more nuanced understanding of economic divergence, where internal wage dynamics also played a crucial role. These findings collectively highlight how technological barriers and price arbitrage shaped long-run economic growth trajectories without direct reference to institutional frameworks.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) explore different aspects of economic growth and historical causation by focusing on specific historical periods and geographical contexts without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Hornbeck's analysis of agricultural development on the American Plains, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced productivity by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. These findings suggest that technological barriers and price arbitrage, as well as institutional frameworks, shaped long-run economic growth trajectories. Austin notes that economic rent and growth have often been joint products, indicating that the optimal institutions for economic growth can vary across different historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.11). This implies that a nuanced understanding of economic growth must consider how technological innovations interact with institutional frameworks over time.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argue that institutions in New World societies often advantaged elites at the expense of broader economic growth. They focus on how inequalities shaped by colonial institutions persisted over time, with elite groups using their political power to implement policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance. This dynamic is evident in the way factor endowments influenced societal structures: while land abundance was a common feature across New World colonies, variations in other factor endowments led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These inequalities were not merely economic but also political and social, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where elites used their power to shape institutions that further entrenched their advantages. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) supports this by emphasizing the interdependency of various institutional frameworks, suggesting that the persistence of inequality can be attributed to the interconnected nature of economic, political, and social institutions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast show that the development of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for creating an environment where economic growth could flourish. These institutions complemented reputation-building strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by constraining behaviors that might otherwise undermine economic stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The importance of institutional reliability is further emphasized by North, who argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). This institutional framework allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence, thereby enabling the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. Van Zanden agrees on the importance of institutions but offers a nuanced perspective by showing that economic development does not necessarily align with improvements in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The historical evidence from Europe between 1500 and 1800 indicates that while there was significant economic growth, this did not uniformly translate into better living conditions for the population. This highlights the complexity of institutional impact, where growth-enhancing policies may not always lead to immediate or widespread benefits.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of technology and resource extraction. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that the gains in total factor productivity (TFP) in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution were modest (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). They estimate that the increase in TFP from 1710 to 1869 was only marginal, suggesting that technological advancements alone did not drive significant economic growth. This finding is consistent with van Zanden’s observation that long-term productivity gains in agriculture did not necessarily correlate with improvements in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Both perspectives underscore the idea that institutional frameworks must be aligned with broader economic and social conditions to effectively support sustained growth.

Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, indicating that institutional differences played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact is mediated by broader historical and geographical factors.

This finding highlights the importance of institutional frameworks that can support technological advancements and resource extraction, thereby fostering long-run economic growth.
