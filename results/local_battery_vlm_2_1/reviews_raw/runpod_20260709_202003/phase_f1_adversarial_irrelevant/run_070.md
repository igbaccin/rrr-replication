The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, as it addresses how societies evolve and prosper over extended periods. At stake intellectually is the understanding of the mechanisms that drive sustained economic development, which can inform policy and theoretical frameworks alike. Institutions are the rules of the game for society (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). One live disagreement revolves around whether institutions primarily facilitate growth through efficiency improvements or by resolving distributional conflicts. A second debate hinges on the relative importance of technological progress versus institutional factors in driving economic growth. The former view emphasizes that technical advancements, such as those during the Industrial Revolution, are the primary drivers of growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.6; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.5). The latter perspective suggests that institutions play a crucial role in enabling and sustaining technological progress by providing the necessary framework for innovation and economic activity (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Kuznets similarly highlights the importance of advancing technology but adds that institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while technological progress is a key driver, its benefits can only be fully realized within an appropriate institutional framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The literature on technological and social factors in economic growth emphasizes the role of dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that the catch-up process is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and technological progress, suggesting a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. This interplay between technology and social factors suggests that while technological progress is a key driver, its benefits can only be fully realized within an appropriate institutional framework.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) also highlights that technological progress alone may not be sufficient for sustainable economic improvement without complementary institutional progress. He argues that coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors can erode the gains from technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while social change and technological advance are crucial, they must be supported by effective institutions to ensure long-term economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) further qualifies this by noting that the growth of productivity makes for an increase in aggregate output, but circumstances making for an acceleration in the growth of the capital stock also fall its chronological age (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This implies that the dynamics of technological advance and social change are not isolated phenomena.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for the efficient use of innovations, which is relevant to economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that technological advancements are often tailored to the specific needs of the countries where they originate, implying that these innovations may not be readily transferable to other contexts without adaptation. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that can facilitate the absorption and application of foreign technologies. This suggests that the evolution of institutions governing public choice can significantly influence the efficiency with which innovations are utilized. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also note that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, indicating that effective institutional frameworks must work in tandem with other social norms to maximize their impact (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This interplay between institutions and social norms is essential for sustaining long-term economic growth.

Hornbeck finds that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, leading to increased investment in agriculture and improved productivity. The technological change brought about by barbed wire was about the enhanced protection it offered against cattle trespassing from neighboring farms as well as direct improvements. Hornbeck notes that this protective function drives the main results (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This underscores the critical role of property rights in fostering economic development, as secure property rights encourage long-term investment and efficient resource use. Pamuk highlights that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, they often require complementary institutional changes to fully realize their potential (Pamuk 2007, p.22; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This suggests that the impact of barbed wire on agricultural development was likely amplified by other institutional factors that supported its adoption and use.

The Industrial Revolution's economic growth did not universally improve population health, as Komlos and Clark and Jacks (Komlos 1998, p.8; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1) show. This paradoxical outcome is rooted in insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment; the former is a consequence of technological stagnation in agriculture and diminishing returns to labor in food production. The latter arises from the substitution of industrial commodities for food products, driven by rising relative nutrient prices. One downstream implication is that economic growth does not necessarily lead to better health outcomes if it is not accompanied by improvements in nutrition and income distribution. This point is further illustrated by the fact that during this period, the life expectancy gap between upper- and lower-income groups widened (Komlos 1998, p.8). This suggests that while institutions can facilitate economic growth by fostering technological innovation and resource allocation, they may not always translate into improved well-being for the broader population.

Distributional conflicts are the primary drivers shaping key economic structures in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) jointly emphasize that efficiency cannot be the sole explanatory factor; distributional conflicts provide a more robust explanation for institutions like serfdom, guilds, and merchant associations. Ogilvie's work highlights the misconception of viewing these institutions as mere inefficiencies or cartels, instead suggesting they were shaped by power dynamics and conflict resolution mechanisms among different social groups. Domar adds that land-to-labor ratios and labor scarcity play a crucial role in the emergence of slavery or serfdom, indicating that economic structures are about managing resource constraints and social tensions as well as efficiency. Domar's analysis also notes the complexities involved in comparing the profitability of free versus slave labor, suggesting that technological progress and capital use do not straightforwardly reduce the profitability of slavery (Domar 1970, p.7; Domar 1970, p.6). This nuanced view implies that while institutions may adapt to changing economic conditions, their underlying structures are deeply rooted in historical distributional conflicts.

Acemoglu et al.'s work (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) highlights that colonial institutions shaped the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. The institutional changes introduced by European powers were designed to extract resources and maximize rents for colonizers, often at the expense of long-term growth in the colonies. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that high mortality rates among Europeans in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions, characterized by poor property rights and limited economic opportunities for locals, persisted over time and significantly influenced current income levels. These variations suggest that while extractive institutions generally hinder growth, specific historical and geographical factors can modulate their long-term effects.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profound, as they create frameworks that either facilitate or hinder technological progress and resource allocation. Institutions shape the economic landscape by influencing investment decisions and technological adoption (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). For example, the merchant guilds provided security and contract enforcement for early trade networks, illustrating how institutional structures can foster long-term economic stability (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Similarly, Abramovitz highlights that technological opportunities encourage growth in capital stocks, demonstrating that institutions that support modernization accelerate economic development (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

Moreover, Kuznets argues that the efficiency of modern economic growth hinges on the integration of technological innovations and institutional adjustments. This integration is crucial for harnessing the full potential of scientific advancements, which in turn drive further technological progress and economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The interplay between technological innovation and institutional frameworks is evident in historical contexts where technological advancements were closely tied to specific needs, such as during the Industrial Revolution in England. This historical evidence underscores that institutions play a pivotal role in directing technological progress towards economically beneficial outcomes. For example, Kuznets notes that the mass application of technical innovations, which constitute much of modern economic growth, is closely connected with further scientific advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform; specific historical and geographical factors can modulate their long-term effects. The variations in European settlement patterns and mortality rates created exogenous differences in institutional development, illustrating how different contexts shape institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This nuanced understanding is further supported by Mokyr's argument that technological progress depends on the growth of useful knowledge, which requires complementary institutional progress to be sustainable. Without such institutional support, economic improvements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The specific historical evidence of the Mesta in Spain, a guild-like association of shepherds and sheep-owners, provides a clear example of how institutions can adapt to changing conditions. Initially vilified for blocking agricultural improvements, the Mesta has been reinterpreted as an efficient solution to property rights and resource access issues (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This historical case study shows that institutions evolve in response to economic needs, illustrating their dynamic role in shaping long-run growth.

The precise remaining disagreement lies in how much institutional progress is driven by technological advancements versus other factors. Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe highlights that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions, suggesting that efficiency may not always be the primary motivation (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective contrasts with Abramovitz’s emphasis on the role of technological opportunity in driving modernization and capital stock growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4), but both acknowledge the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is seen in the case of the Mesta, where the institution's efficiency was directly tied to addressing property rights and resource access issues, showing how institutions adapt to technological and economic needs (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).
