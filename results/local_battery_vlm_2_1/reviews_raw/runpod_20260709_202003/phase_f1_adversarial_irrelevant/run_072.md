The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that addresses how societal structures and rules influence sustained economic development over extended periods. At stake intellectually is understanding whether institutions are mere facilitators of economic processes or active drivers that can propel or hinder growth. The live disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions, as opposed to technological progress alone, fundamentally determine economic trajectories. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks are essential for harnessing technological advancements and ensuring their sustainable application, while others posit that the primary driver of long-run economic growth is technological innovation itself. Additionally, there is a debate over whether institutions primarily serve efficiency or distributional conflicts. This review will explore these positions by examining how different streams of scholarship approach the role of institutions in economic growth.

The relationship between technology and institutional progress highlights their interdependence in driving economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological advancements are crucial for economic development, but he also notes that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of these innovations can be eroded by rent-seeking behavior shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly argues that while technological progress is a key driver, it requires institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use of new technologies. This perspective underscores that institutions play a critical role in translating technological advancements into sustained economic growth by providing the necessary frameworks for implementation and enforcement. The interplay between technology and institutions suggests that neither can function optimally without the other.

The role of institutions as facilitators of market exchanges is another key aspect of their influence on economic growth. North (1989, p.6) argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, thus driving economic growth shared finding (North 1989, p.6; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This view aligns with the idea that institutions create an environment where economic activities can thrive by ensuring that transactions are secure and predictable. In this context, institutions act as enablers of economic development by providing the necessary infrastructure for market operations.

The perspective on institutions as drivers of efficiency is somewhat nuanced when considering their role in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) challenges the notion that core economic institutions were primarily driven by efficiency, arguing instead that they often served to manage distributional conflicts shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This interpretation suggests that institutions can be shaped by power dynamics and the need to allocate resources equitably as well as considerations of economic efficiency. The focus on distributional conflicts adds a layer of complexity to the role of institutions, highlighting that they are influenced by broader social and political factors.

Institutions can also play a direct role in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on technological progress and its diffusion. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) posits that backwardness creates an opportunity for modernization in both embodied and disembodied technology, suggesting that institutions can facilitate the adoption of new technologies by reducing barriers to innovation shared finding (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective underscores how institutions can act as catalysts for economic growth by promoting technological advancement and ensuring its widespread application.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. They posit that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering these conditions, which in turn drove economic development. This perspective is supported by North's analysis of transaction costs in growing economies (North 1989, p.6). He argues that as economies grow, the volume of transactions increases due to specialization and division of labor, making the efficient enforcement of property rights essential for reducing per-transaction costs. Consequently, the development of political institutions that enforce these rights is fundamental for sustaining economic growth.

This institutional framework complements reputation-building strategies by providing additional constraints where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). However, North (1989, p.6) qualifies this by noting that neoclassical theories, which focus on population and savings as primary determinants of economic growth, oversimplify the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes (North 1989, p.5). This qualification underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks interact with other economic factors to drive long-run growth.

The role of political institutions in enforcing property rights and reducing transaction costs highlights their critical function in fostering an environment conducive to economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mortality rates faced by Europeans determined the type of institutional frameworks established in their colonies; high mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents rather than long-run growth. This pattern was driven by the incentives of European colonizers who were reluctant to invest in institutions that would foster development if they did not directly benefit from them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic outcomes.

Ogilvie and Domar highlight how institutions in pre-industrial Europe emerged from power struggles as well as efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped institutions like serfdom and guilds. These institutions arose due to the balance of power among different social groups. Domar shows how slavery or serfdom emerged when land was abundant relative to labor driven by economic incentives as well as efficiency considerations alone. This perspective underscores that institutions are products of societal power dynamics and resource availability. The implications of this dynamic extend beyond Europe: the profitability of slave labor in traditional societies can be influenced by technological progress and capital accumulation (Domar 1970, p.7). Domar notes that predicting the relative profitability of free versus slave labor is complex depending on various factors including the marginal product of labor and subsistence levels. This complexity suggests that while economic conditions drive institutional emergence, their long-run effects on growth are contingent on multiple interacting variables.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that technological advancements can significantly enhance economic development by reinforcing property rights. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains exemplifies this dynamic, as it enabled farmers to better protect their property and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological change not only improved agricultural productivity but also facilitated the emergence of more robust property rights institutions. These improvements were not merely technological; they also involved social and institutional changes that allowed for more effective enforcement of property rights. The broader implications of this dynamic extend beyond agriculture: the protection of property rights can stimulate economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. Technological advancements that enhance property protection can thus catalyze broader economic development by fostering the conditions necessary for sustained growth.

Economic growth and social change are interconnected processes that shape long-term economic development (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) highlights the role of technological advancements in driving productivity growth. This process involves the adoption of new technologies as well as the substitution of labor with capital-intensive methods, as Pamuk (2007, p.22) elaborates on. The rise in wages relative to rents and the price of capital led to a shift towards more capital-intensive industries (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This dynamic is further supported by the emergence of new, more capital-intensive industries during the late medieval centuries, as Pamuk notes. However, while technological advancements can spur growth, they also create conditions that may exacerbate distributional conflicts and lead to social changes that affect economic outcomes.

Mokyr's argument that technological advancement is the primary driver of long-run economic growth is substantiated by his observation that invention often involves recombining existing techniques. This accumulation and recombination of knowledge can lead to significant technological leaps, as seen during the Industrial Revolution. Kuznets similarly highlights the role of technology in economic growth. He identifies a feedback loop where mass application of technical innovations, many based on recent scientific discoveries, provides positive reinforcement for further advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is crucial institutional progress is also necessary to ensure sustainability and prevent rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic improvements. This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks underscores the complex nature of long-run economic growth, where both knowledge accumulation and institutional support are essential for sustained development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their capacity to facilitate and sustain technological progress. Institutions create the framework within which technological advancements can be effectively implemented and scaled. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that without institutional progress complementing technological advances, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) builds on this idea by noting that mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with further scientific progress, which in turn provides a positive feedback loop for additional advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay shows how institutions support but also amplify the effects of technological change.

Institutions also play a critical role in managing the distributional conflicts that arise from economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds were responses to these conflicts as well as efficient solutions to transaction costs (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective aligns with Abramovitz's (1986, p.4) view that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and social change. The dynamics of technological advance are deeply intertwined with institutional frameworks, which manage conflicts and ensure that the benefits of innovation are widely distributed (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). North (1989, p.6) emphasizes that institutions matter because they specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms, reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.5).

The specific historical evidence from Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains. This technological innovation enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, illustrating how institutions that support technological progress can drive long-run economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The measurement type used in Hornbeck (2010, p.30), namely the impact of barbed wire on agricultural productivity provides a clear example of how institutional frameworks can amplify the effects of technological change. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement between Kuznets's focus on scientific progress and Ogilvie's emphasis on distributional conflicts by showing that both factors are crucial in different contexts.
