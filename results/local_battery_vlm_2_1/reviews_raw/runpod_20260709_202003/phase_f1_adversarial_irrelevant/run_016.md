The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature. At stake are fundamental questions about the drivers of prosperity and poverty: are institutions mere facilitators of growth driven by other factors, such as technological progress or geographic endowments? Or do they actively shape economic trajectories through their own dynamics? Institutions can be understood as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions, and self-imposed codes of conduct). One position is that institutions are crucial for growth because they reduce uncertainty, promote investment, and facilitate transactions. Another view posits that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent on other factors, such as the level of technological development or the presence of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for specific groups rather than promoting long-run growth. These conflicting views underscore the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how different institutional arrangements influence economic outcomes over the long run. Kuznets's observation that economic growth requires both technological progress and institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations is particularly relevant (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

This coordination failure recognition highlights the necessity for institutions to facilitate secure property rights as argued by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They claim that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering this environment, leading to sustained economic development. North (1989, p.5) further argues that neoclassical theories, which focus on population and savings as principal determinants of economic output, are misleading. These theories fail to account for the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) explain that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints when reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view underscores the endogenous role of political institutions in improving the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). As a result, secure property rights foster economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. North (1989, p.5) recognizes that institutions govern more than just economic exchange; they also dictate how rules are enforced and changed, which is vital for long-term economic stability.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions established by European settlers played a pivotal role in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. The institutions introduced during the colonial period were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a pattern consistent with extractive institutions that concentrate power and wealth in the hands of a few. This institutional design was influenced by factors such as mortality rates faced by Europeans, which determined the feasibility of settlement. For instance, high mortality rates in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions highlights how initial colonial policies can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also note that institutional changes can play a crucial role in reversing relative incomes among former colonies, suggesting that institutional reforms can mitigate the long-term impact of colonial legacies. The interplay between extractive institutions and economic performance underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutional frameworks shape contemporary economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.30) argue that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were primarily shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's analysis of merchant guilds reveals that these institutions, initially seen as efficient providers of security and contract enforcement, were actually driven by the interests of powerful elites who used them to control markets and maintain their status. This perspective challenges the notion that such institutions were inherently beneficial for economic growth. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provides a complementary view by examining the impact of barbed wire fencing on agricultural development in the American Plains, showing how technological changes can enhance property rights protection, which is crucial for economic efficiency. However, this improvement in property rights was not driven solely by efficiency concerns but also by the need to mitigate externalities from livestock damage.

The distributional conflicts highlighted by Ogilvie and Hornbeck underscore a broader pattern: economic institutions often emerge as solutions to specific problems faced by powerful groups within society. This dynamic is evident in the case of merchant guilds, where the primary beneficiaries were not necessarily the broader economy but rather the elites who controlled these institutions. The introduction of barbed wire fencing similarly illustrates how technological advancements can alter the landscape of property rights and economic interactions, but their implementation is often motivated by immediate practical needs rather than long-term efficiency goals.

The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions that arise from distributional conflicts may not always align with broader economic efficiency, leading to potential inefficiencies in resource allocation and economic development. This insight suggests that designing and implementing economic institutions should consider both their immediate benefits and long-term impact on economic growth and social equity.

Economic growth is driven by advancements in technology and knowledge, which require complementary institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that technological progress is closely tied to the specific needs of the country where it originates. This implies that institutions must be adaptable to harness technology effectively for economic growth. Mokyr's (2005) analysis shows that technological advancements often depend on existing knowledge and techniques, suggesting that effective institutions can facilitate the recombination and application of knowledge to drive innovation. Kuznets points out that not all innovations are universally applicable; therefore, institutions must foster an environment where local needs inform technological development. This local adaptation is crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth. Mokyr's (2005) observation that institutional progress complements technological advancement highlights the importance of policies that address coordination failures and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors. These mechanisms ensure that the benefits of technological progress are not eroded by inefficient institutions, thus supporting sustainable economic improvement.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Pamuk 2007, p.22) focus on how historical events and initial conditions shaped economic development. They show that the Black Death precipitated long-term structural changes across Europe by 1450, which created a wage gap between the northwest region and the rest of Europe. Pamuk (2007, p.22) elaborates that these changes were not merely demographic; they fundamentally altered the economic landscape. As wages rose relative to rents and capital costs, there was a notable substitution of land and capital for labor across the continent (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This shift highlights how historical events can drive technological innovation by altering economic incentives. Pamuk (2007, p.22) further notes that urban guilds played a pivotal role in producing new technology during this period (Pamuk 2007, p.23), suggesting that the institutional framework of medieval Europe facilitated these advancements despite the initial shock of the Black Death. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that such historical events also shaped institutions in ways that could perpetuate inequality, as seen in New World societies where elites benefited from government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional entrenchment of inequality underscores how historical factors can influence long-term economic trajectories through the institutional channels they create.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring sustainable economic improvement. Mokyr's work highlights how institutional progress complements technological advancements, preventing the erosion of gains from rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This is supported by Kuznets' argument that economic growth driven by technology requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The interplay between technological change and institutions is crucial, as seen in the Industrial Revolution, where technological progress was sustained through effective property rights enforcement. These findings underscore that institutions are not merely passive frameworks but active facilitators of long-term economic development.

The role of institutions extends beyond mere efficiency to include distributional conflicts and political power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups can stifle long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is echoed by North, who contends that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.5). These views converge on the idea that institutional structures influence economic outcomes by shaping the distribution of power and resources. Ogilvie further complicates this narrative by arguing that pre-industrial European institutions were shaped more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

The historical evidence from colonialism provides a stark illustration of how institutions can perpetuate inequality. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This aligns with North's assertion that efficient property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The specific historical evidence of colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists settles the disagreement. These institutions, which were extractive and did not encourage long-run development, provide a clear example of how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth.
