The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within economic scholarship. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can help explain why some economies thrive while others stagnate. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions drive economic growth and the mechanisms through which they do so. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs, enhancing property rights enforcement, and fostering technological progress shared finding (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Others contend that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, suggesting that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The debate over institutional efficiency versus rent-seeking highlights a broader question: do institutions primarily enable or constrain economic growth? Kuznets and North offer complementary perspectives on this issue. Kuznets emphasizes that economic growth requires not only technological advancements but also the development of efficient institutions to facilitate their implementation shared finding (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North 1989, p.6). Without effective institutional frameworks, even significant technological progress might be unsustainable, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains over time. North builds on this by arguing that institutions matter because they shape the incentives and constraints within which economic actors operate (North 1989, p.5).

This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the relative incomes of former colonies shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North 1989, p.6). Their analysis underscores the importance of institutions in determining long-run economic outcomes, suggesting that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonizers hindered growth. This highlights a critical point: institutions can be both enablers and constraints on economic growth, depending on their design and the interests they serve.

Extending this idea, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The role of mortality rates is that they influenced the type of institutions established in these regions. This dynamic was driven by the need to quickly exploit resources before succumbing to disease; thus, institutions were designed to facilitate extraction and transfer wealth back to Europe. Acemoglu et al. highlight that this institutional framework persisted over time, locking former colonies into a trajectory of underdevelopment. The persistence of these institutions is crucial because it underscores how initial conditions set by colonial powers can have enduring effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise mechanism through which these institutions persist involves complex and multifactorial dynamics. Acemoglu et al. suggest that the persistence of these institutions may be due to a lack of political will or incentives for change among elites who benefit from the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern reveals how historical institutional legacies can constrain contemporary economic development, shaping long-run growth trajectories in profound ways.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the invention of barbed wire fencing in the late 19th century significantly boosted agricultural development on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This innovation enabled farmers to better safeguard their property rights and mitigate externalities from livestock damage, illustrating how specific institutional changes can drive economic growth. The barbed wire fencing allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively, reducing disputes over grazing rights and preventing cattle from damaging crops. However, Hornbeck notes that the technological improvements brought by barbed wire were not the primary driver of agricultural expansion; rather, the enhanced protection of property rights was key (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). For instance, the Black Death initiated wage increases relative to rents and capital prices, leading to a substitution of land and capital for labor across Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This shift highlights how institutional changes can alter economic structures over time. However, Pamuk qualifies this by noting that while technological advancements were significant during the late medieval centuries, they did not drive economic growth in isolation but rather as part of a broader institutional framework (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This underscores the interconnected nature of institutions and technology in shaping long-run economic development.

Building on this technological dimension, Ogilvie's examination of pre-industrial Europe reveals that economic institutions such as serfdom and craft guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). These institutions reflected deeper social and political dynamics beyond mere responses to economic obstacles. For example, Ogilvie's analysis of merchant guilds highlights how these entities evolved from efficient providers of security and contract enforcement in the tenth and eleventh centuries to more complex structures by the twelfth to fifteenth centuries (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This evolution suggests that institutional arrangements were influenced by changing socio-economic conditions and power dynamics rather than a singular focus on efficiency. However, Domar also notes that technological progress and capital accumulation do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor (Domar 1970, p.6), implying that the persistence of such institutions could be influenced by factors beyond simple economic efficiency. This nuanced understanding underscores how institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe were multifaceted, shaped by a complex interplay of economic, social, and political forces.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) both highlight the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping economic growth; however, they do not directly address long-run economic growth as an outcome. To understand how these institutions functioned, it is crucial to examine the mechanisms that supported their development and maintenance. These political institutions facilitate market exchanges by specifying property rights and providing increasingly effective enforcement, which results in falling costs per transaction as the volume of exchanges multiplies through specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast elaborate on how these institutions complement reputation-building strategies (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). They improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view suggests that political institutions play an endogenous role in mitigating incentive problems, particularly those related to expropriation and the maintenance of bargains with constituents. Institutions governing public choice are crucial for ensuring that the state can maintain its part of bargains by restricting its ex post behavior. These mechanisms underscore how institutions shape economic growth through their impact on transaction costs and the enforcement of property rights, which in turn influence market development and efficiency.

In a related vein, Mokyr and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective challenges the centrality of institutions in long-run economic development. He highlights that technological progress often relies on recombining existing techniques and analogical thinking, which can alert inventors to new opportunities without the need for institutional intervention. This view suggests that the growth of useful knowledge can occur independently of institutional frameworks, as long as there is a steady accumulation of technical innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets further elaborates on this by noting that technological progress is closely linked to scientific advancements and the mass application of innovations, which in turn drive economic growth. However, Kuznets also acknowledges that there might be a limited stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries, implying that institutional adjustments could play a role in adapting technological progress to specific contexts.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological innovations and managing distributional conflicts. Kuznets acknowledges that while technological progress is the main driver of modern economic growth, institutions are crucial for adapting these innovations to specific contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, for instance, significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).

Moreover, institutions influence how technological progress translates into economic growth across different regions. Mokyr highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This view is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors. This underscores the importance of institutions in determining economic outcomes over the long run.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the distributional conflicts they manage. Ogilvie argues that many core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were designed to address these conflicts rather than simply enhance efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective aligns with Kuznets' observation that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries might be limited, implying that institutional adjustments are necessary for adapting technological progress to specific contexts (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement is found in the comparative analysis of colonial institutions introduced by Acemoglu et al. This work shows that colonies with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists experienced lower long-run growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Kuznets 1973, p.10).
