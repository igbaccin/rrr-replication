Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how resources are allocated and how incentives are structured within societies. The live disagreement centres on the specific mechanisms through which institutions exert this influence, with some scholars emphasising the importance of secure property rights and credible commitment from political institutions (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) while others focus on broader socio-economic factors that can either facilitate or hinder growth. This debate is crucial because it informs our understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice has been identified as a critical factor in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This credible commitment underpins growth by providing long-term predictability, which encourages investment and innovation. The role of institutions in mitigating incentive problems is also highlighted, as they complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints on the state's behavior. This institutional framework improved the efficacy of the reputation mechanism, ensuring that bargains between the state and its constituents were maintained (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Austin further elaborates on this by noting that economic rent and growth are often joint products, influenced by the specific historical context and institutions in place (Austin 2008, p.1). This interplay between political stability and economic performance underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in driving long-term economic development.

Building on this interplay, North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework improved the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints on state behavior, ensuring that bargains between the state and its constituents were maintained. These institutions supplemented reputation-building strategies, especially when reputation alone could not prevent reneging. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) support the importance of institutional changes by highlighting how differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the long-term impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic development. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) found that institutions played a crucial role in economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, they also noted that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the specific design of institutions matters significantly in determining their impact on economic outcomes.

Expanding on this idea, North (1989, p.6) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) demonstrate that institutional structures play a crucial role in reducing uncertainties and shaping economic outcomes by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement. This efficiency in property rights enforcement enabled long-distance trade by securing transactions between strangers. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) also notes that these institutions were part of a broader framework and could fail if they did not align with the wider institutional structure (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further support this view by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This suggests that while efficient property rights are crucial for economic growth, the broader institutional framework must also support these efficiencies to ensure sustained development.

Despite this emphasis on institutions, Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both discuss economic development and changes in the standard of living without explicitly addressing institutions as a causal factor. They share the view that conventional views on early modern economic growth may overlook critical dimensions of human well-being. Komlos supports this perspective by showing that despite aggregate economic gains during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.13). This suggests that while economic growth is a necessary condition for improving living standards, it is not sufficient on its own. The mechanisms through which economic development translates into improved welfare are complex and multifaceted, involving factors such as income distribution and nutritional intake. Van Zanden's work highlights the importance of considering long-term productivity trends, which may not align with short-term improvements in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Komlos qualifies this by emphasizing that even during periods of economic growth, disparities in nutrition can lead to divergent outcomes for different social groups (Komlos 1998, p.8). This underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how economic development impacts various aspects of human well-being.

Extending this analysis, Lindert and Williamson show that technological advancements have historically been a prime source of secular inequality trends (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). This is because uneven productivity growth across sectors can exacerbate income disparities. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Bryant 2006, p.29; Kuznets 1973, p.1), while Bryant highlights that the lack of structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors in China limited its economic dynamism, contrasting with European experiences where technological imbalance fostered growth-enhancing articulations. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes. However, Kuznets' argument is qualified by Lindert and Williamson's observation that magnitudes matter (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.29): early technological forces may not always be strong enough to outweigh other factors such as human capital accumulation or the redistribution effects of Engel’s Law, leading to a more nuanced understanding of inequality trends during economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not directly addressed by the structural changes highlighted in Temin and Williamson's shared finding (Temin 2002, p.2; Williamson 1996, p.25). Both authors present a significant mechanism where economic growth is driven by factors other than institutional frameworks. The business cycle can produce rapid growth over short periods, such as a year or two (Temin 2002, p.3; Pamuk 2007, p.8). Temin's analysis of post-WWII Europe and Williamson's examination of global price shocks both illustrate how structural changes in labor allocation and commodity prices can drive economic growth independently of institutional dynamics. This perspective is particularly relevant for understanding periods of rapid economic expansion, such as the Golden Age of European economies after World War II.

One downstream implication of this mechanism is that structural shifts can lead to significant wage gaps between regions, as seen in Pamuk's analysis (Pamuk 2007, p.8; Pamuk 2007, p.15). The Black Death in Europe initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence resulted from demographic shifts and labor shortages that altered economic dynamics. These findings suggest that while institutions play a crucial role, other structural changes can also significantly influence long-run economic outcomes.

Environmental and technological factors significantly influence long-run economic growth by altering productivity and resource management practices. Stern (2004, p.3) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) both argue that improvements in technology and resource management can lead to increased productivity and economic development. Specifically, Stern's analysis of the environmental Kuznets curve highlights how technological advancements can reduce emissions per unit of output, even if this is not the intended consequence. This mechanism involves changes in total factor productivity and emissions-specific process improvements, which collectively enhance efficiency (Stern 2004, p.3). Similarly, Hornbeck demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, leading to a significant increase in agricultural productivity. Whatley's examination of the gun-slave cycle shows that while technology can drive economic activity, its impact depends on social processes and intertemporal production links. The influx of gunpowder technology in Africa increased slave exports by enhancing the productivity of resources devoted to slaving, illustrating how technological factors can interact with social dynamics to shape economic outcomes (Whatley 2018, p.2). This interaction underscores that while technology and resource management are crucial drivers of growth, their effects are mediated by broader socio-economic conditions.

Hopkins's examination of Africa’s economic history shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent on their ability to adapt to changing development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is shared by other scholars who highlight diverse historical factors, including slavery and colonial policies, as key influences on African economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). Nunn's analysis of the slave trades in Africa demonstrates that these institutions hindered economic growth and had long-lasting negative impacts on societal development. Akyeampong and Fofack’s work underscores that gender roles played a significant part in shaping African economies during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women's contributions to agricultural production and resource allocation were crucial for economic growth, even as their control over certain crops was increasingly limited by men (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This nuanced view of gender dynamics within institutional frameworks suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth varies based on social structures and historical contexts. Hopkins notes that while institutions can be enduring, they are also adaptable, emphasizing the need for policy reforms to align these institutions with current development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This adaptive capacity underscores the dynamic nature of institutional influence on economic growth, highlighting that effective policymaking must consider both the historical legacy and contemporary requirements of institutional frameworks.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) examine various historical factors contributing to economic divergence and industrial development but do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, focusing instead on technological barriers and extraction costs in coal mining. Broadberry and Gupta similarly highlight the importance of labour productivity in both tradable and non-tradable sectors, noting that lower silver wages in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector. This implies that institutional factors that influenced labour productivity were crucial for economic divergence between Europe and Asia long before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, noting that institutions favoring economic growth in one context might not be optimal in another. This nuanced view underscores the need for a more contextual understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.11). The shared finding is that while technological advancements and labour productivity are key drivers of economic divergence, institutional frameworks play a significant role in mediating these effects across different historical contexts.
