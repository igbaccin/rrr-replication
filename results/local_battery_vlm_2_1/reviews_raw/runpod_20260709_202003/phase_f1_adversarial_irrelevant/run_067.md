The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars of development and economics. The disagreement centers on the extent to which institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over extended periods. Some argue that institutions are pivotal in determining long-term economic performance by fostering secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs. Others contend that institutions alone do not account for growth, suggesting instead that factors such as population dynamics or savings rates play a more significant role.

Institutions can be defined broadly as the rules of the game in a society, which shape economic behavior by providing incentives for individuals to engage in productive activities. The debate over their importance is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and development strategies. If institutions are indeed crucial for growth, then policies aimed at strengthening institutional frameworks could be pivotal in fostering sustainable economic development.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice was critical for securing property rights and promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who assert that institutional changes introduced during colonialism were instrumental in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies. The reversal can be attributed to the design of equilibrium institutions that maximized rents for European colonists and fostered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This view underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over extended periods.

The role of institutions is further highlighted by North's argument that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks. Efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are necessary to facilitate market exchanges, thereby lowering transaction costs (North 1989, p.2). This perspective is aligned with Broadberry and Gupta's analysis, which posits that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. The price of non-tradable commodities in Asia depended negatively on labor productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labor productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The evidence suggests that institutions played a crucial role in facilitating market exchanges by providing reliable property rights.

The causal mechanism through which institutions influence growth is complex. Institutions can improve the efficacy of reputation mechanisms by acting as constraints where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging, thereby fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective underscores that institutions are rules governing economic exchange and encompass political factors underpinning their enforcement and change.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The European settlers established institutions tailored to maximize their own rents and foster long-term growth in the colonies, which led to extractive institutions that persist today. These institutions were designed to benefit the colonizers, often at the expense of local populations, and were shaped by factors such as mortality rates faced by Europeans in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, high European mortality rates in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. This highlights how the institutional framework imposed during colonial rule continues to impact economic performance long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further substantiate this by demonstrating that these institutions played a critical role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, thereby accounting for significant current income differences. It is important to note that while extractive institutions initially hindered economic development, they also created pathways for future institutional change and economic growth, as seen in some former colonies today. The legacy of colonial institutions thus underscores the enduring impact of historical policies on contemporary economic outcomes, shaping the long-run trajectory of economic development in these regions.

Distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) shared finding (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective challenges efficiency-based explanations that portray these institutions as solutions to specific problems. For example, merchant guilds in the twelfth to fifteenth centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7), a pattern that is also seen in other pre-modern institutions such as the Mesta in Spain or the noble feud system. This efficiency was often contingent on distributional conflicts between different social groups. For instance, the merchant guilds' efficiency was intertwined with their ability to enforce contracts and property rights in long-distance trade, a mechanism that Sokoloff & Engerman (2000) note was deeply influenced by existing power structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This highlights how distributional conflicts could lead to institutional frameworks that, while efficient under certain conditions, were fundamentally shaped by underlying social dynamics. Ogilvie's analysis further underscores the importance of studying these institutions within their broader framework, as they are interdependent with other economic and social factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.27), a view that Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) aligns with in their examination of New World societies, where institutional differences were deeply rooted in initial factor endowments and power dynamics. Ogilvie also cautions that the efficiency approach can sometimes obscure the true nature of these institutions by focusing too narrowly on their economic functions and not their social origins.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26), along with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26), re-evaluate historical economic drivers by shifting focus away from institutions as the key determinant of long-run economic growth. They argue that coal, a crucial energy source, played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution. This perspective challenges traditional views that emphasize institutional factors. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that total factor productivity gains in coal mining were modest during the Industrial Revolution era. The authors estimate upper- and lower-bound increases in productivity, revealing only slight improvements despite significant cumulative output (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) build on this by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. They attribute this to differences in labor productivity across sectors and institutional frameworks. This economic divergence was driven by productivity differentials, not just institutional factors. Broadberry and Gupta highlight the importance of labor productivity, Clark and Jacks (2007, p.31) note that landowners' expectations for coal prices remained modest, indicating a more complex interplay between resource availability and economic growth. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intertwined with broader economic factors such as energy resources and labor productivity.

van Zanden (1999, p.2); (Komlos 1998, p.13) jointly establish that the relationship between economic growth and improvements in living standards is complex and non-linear. They argue that despite periods of significant economic development in Europe from 1500 to 1800, these gains did not uniformly translate into enhanced biological well-being for the population. This finding is grounded in a specific mechanism: the disconnect between aggregate economic growth and the distribution of its benefits. Van Zanden highlights how optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth often overlook the nuances of productivity increases and real wage trends. Komlos illustrates that during the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic expansion, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests a critical pattern: economic growth does not inherently improve living standards without equitable distribution of resources. Komlos qualifies this by noting that relative price effects also play a significant role in shaping consumption patterns. The increasing cost of nutrients relative to industrial goods influenced dietary choices, further complicating the relationship between economic growth and biological well-being. This underscores the importance of considering both income distribution and relative prices when assessing the impact of economic growth on living standards.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and reduce transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth. They argue that these institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework allowed for the development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement, which are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North further elaborates that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement, thereby lowering costs per transaction as the economy grows (North 1989, p.6). This institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others even in complex interdependent situations. The establishment of such a set of property rights enables individuals to engage in transactions with those they do not personally know, fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.2).

The importance of institutions is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al., who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with North's view that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may not initiate institutional change if they do not directly benefit from the resulting economic gains. The results of Acemoglu et al.'s study support the view that institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This underscores the significance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they determine the distribution of resources and the incentives for economic activity.

The historical evidence from Broadberry and Gupta further supports the idea that institutional factors were critical in the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. They show that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, which was influenced by institutional factors such as property rights and enforcement mechanisms (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth. The price of non-tradable commodities also depended negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector, highlighting the role of institutions in determining economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This evidence from Broadberry and Gupta, combined with the findings of Acemoglu et al., demonstrates that institutions are a key factor in shaping long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). There remains disagreement over the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al. emphasize the extractive nature of equilibrium institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from Broadberry and Gupta settles this disagreement by showing that institutional factors were critical in the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. Their analysis of silver wages and labour productivity in tradable and non-tradable sectors provides a clear measurement type that demonstrates the role of institutions in determining economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).
