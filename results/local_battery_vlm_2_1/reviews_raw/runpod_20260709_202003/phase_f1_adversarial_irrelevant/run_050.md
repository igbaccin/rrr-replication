The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, as it directly addresses how societies can achieve sustained prosperity. At stake is the understanding of the underlying mechanisms that drive economic development over extended periods. Institutions are often seen as the rules of the game that structure economic interactions, but there is a live disagreement between those who view institutions as critical drivers of growth and those who attribute growth primarily to other factors such as natural resources or geographic advantages.

One key aspect of this debate involves how institutions can foster secure property rights and economic stability. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints on behavior, thereby improving the efficacy of long-term economic interactions (North and Weingast 1989, p.7) This perspective is supported by North's broader argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, countering neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). Institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for sustaining economic growth. The development of efficient property rights allows individuals to engage confidently in complex interdependent situations, thereby enabling the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2).

These arguments align with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a significant role in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups often maximize rents and highlight the complex interplay between political power and economic development. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how institutional frameworks can either hinder or support economic growth over extended periods.

The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth cannot be understood without considering their impact on productivity. Clark and Jacks provide a detailed analysis of the coal industry during the Industrial Revolution, showing that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output despite technological barriers (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This suggests that institutional factors, such as secure property rights and efficient market exchanges, were critical in enabling sustained economic growth during this period. Broadberry and Gupta further support this view by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with labor productivity playing a key role (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This divergence underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth, as efficient property rights and market exchanges are essential for achieving high levels of productivity.

North and Weingast identify political factors underpinning economic growth including rules governing economic exchange and institutions enforcing these rules. North elaborates on this by highlighting that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges. North also notes that institutional structures will not evolve if they cannot reduce uncertainties associated with such situations (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). This suggests that while institutions are essential for long-run economic growth, their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt and respond to changing economic conditions.

Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth, which Acemoglu et al. note can occur when powerful groups fear losing political power through institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). A key mechanism here is the impact of European mortality rates on settlement patterns and subsequent institution-building; high mortality rates led to extractive institutions that persisted over time. This persistence of colonial-era institutions has significantly affected current income per capita, demonstrating how historical institutional changes can have long-lasting economic consequences. This highlights a nuanced view of institutional change where political dynamics play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the economic institutions of pre-industrial societies were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are relevant to long-run economic growth by highlighting alternative drivers of institutional change. The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, have been traditionally interpreted through an efficiency lens, with scholars emphasizing their role in solving specific transactional problems. This view overlooks broader social and political contexts shaping these institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) shows that merchant guilds provided essential security and contract enforcement as 'club goods'. This highlights how distributional conflicts among different societal groups influenced the formation of these institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to New World societies, where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutional frameworks that favored elites. These societies developed government policies that restricted competition, perpetuating inequality and hindering economic growth. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the long-run effects of institutions on economic development. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may overlook its broader impact.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14); (Komlos 1998, p.13) argue that the Industrial Revolution's economic growth masked significant declines in biological well-being. The latter finding suggests that the technological advancements did not translate into substantial improvements in living standards for the broader population. This is because even though total factor productivity increased only marginally during this period (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31), it was accompanied by a rise in relative nutrient prices and a shift in consumption patterns towards cheaper, less nutritious foods. Komlos (1998, p.13) further notes that life expectancy diverged between upper- and lower-income groups due to these changes (Komlos 1998, p.8). This divergence underscores how economic growth during the Industrial Revolution failed to translate into widespread improvements in health and well-being for all segments of society, particularly those at the lower end of the income spectrum.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (2008, p.11) focus on historical interpretations of the Great Divergence and the reversal of fortune thesis, examining specific periods and paths of economic development. They argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This challenge to the notion of a late 19th-century takeoff highlights the role of long-term structural factors in shaping economic growth. One key mechanism is labour productivity: Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) demonstrate that relatively low silver wages in Asia reflected lower productivity in the tradable goods sector, while Bryant (2006, p.28) shows how agricultural practices in England led to significant gains in per capita productivity and rising discretionary incomes. These findings underscore the importance of agricultural productivity and labour allocation in driving economic growth. Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies this by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products indicating that the institutions favouring growth in one context may not be optimal in others; this suggests that the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex and historically contingent.

The literature on African economic development highlights diverse historical factors, including slavery, colonial policies, and gender roles (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that the new economic history of Africa has drawn attention from development specialists by highlighting how institutions shape investment incentives. This perspective is complemented by Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014) finding that women significantly contributed to economic growth, despite their roles being marginalized in some historical accounts (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn provides a specific mechanism by which the slave trades adversely affected economic development: domestic warfare and raiding disrupted social cohesion and reduced incentives for productive investments. This is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014) observation that colonial policies often appropriated resources, limiting local control over production (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.18). Nunn (2008, p.3) also notes that institutions can change over time suggesting that the negative impacts of historical factors are not immutable. This implies that institutional reforms could potentially harness underutilized resources, such as women’s labor, to drive further economic development.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that facilitate market exchanges and secure property rights. North and Weingast argue that credible commitments underpin growth because they enable the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Institutions foster economic growth through their role in reducing transaction costs, which are critical for sustaining complex interdependent societies (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The evolution of market-enhancing institutions is evident in the development of capital markets and the enforcement of rules governing public choice. This process is crucial because it allows for the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, thereby supporting long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth varies across different historical contexts. The divergent paths of Europe and Asia illustrate how institutional changes can lead to varied outcomes. Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between these regions was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This divergence highlights the importance of considering the specific institutional frameworks that foster economic growth in different historical periods. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the development of efficient product and factor markets, as well as a reliable medium of exchange.
