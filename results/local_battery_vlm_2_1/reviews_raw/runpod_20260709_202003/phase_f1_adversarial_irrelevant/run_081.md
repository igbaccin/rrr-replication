The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of understanding why some economies thrive while others stagnate over extended periods. The debate centers around whether institutions are merely a reflection of deeper economic forces or if they actively shape economic outcomes through their own mechanisms. On one side, some scholars argue that institutions matter fundamentally in determining long-run growth trajectories, often emphasizing credible commitments and efficient property rights. Others posit that technological progress is the primary driver, with institutions playing a secondary role in facilitating but not driving economic growth. Another perspective posits that distributional conflicts are pivotal for understanding core economic institutions (Author Year p.N). The interplay between these views underscores the complexity of institutional effects on long-run economic growth. Institutions may facilitate or hinder technological progress through their influence on property rights, contract enforcement, and market exchanges, thereby shaping long-term economic performance.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both highlight the significance of technological change in driving economic growth. Abramovitz argues that catch-up processes are complex and influenced by technological advancement, which encourages capital stock growth and reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets similarly emphasizes that modern economic growth is closely linked to technological innovations, which require institutional adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The interplay between technological progress and institutions suggests that while technology may initiate growth, institutions are crucial in sustaining it. Institutional progress complements technological advancements by addressing coordination failures and preventing rent-seeking behaviors that could erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

North (1989, p.6) posits that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, is misleading because it overlooks the role of institutions. North argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). This perspective aligns with Kuznets' view on the need for institutional adjustments to harness technological innovations effectively. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) presents an alternative view, arguing that distributional conflicts are pivotal for understanding core economic institutions. Ogilvie suggests that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped more by power dynamics and conflict resolution mechanisms (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This indicates that while institutions may facilitate growth, their formation and evolution are deeply influenced by societal power structures and conflicts.

The role of technology in economic growth is further complicated by the interdependence between technological progress and institutional frameworks. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological progress often depends on existing knowledge and analogical thinking, which requires a supportive institutional environment (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provides an example of how technological change in agriculture, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, enhanced property rights and reduced externalities, thereby fostering agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This interplay between technology and institutions underscores that while technological innovations can drive growth, their impact is mediated by institutional factors.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that secure property rights are central to long-run economic growth because institutions governing public choice facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 illustrates this dynamic; the political changes it wrought ensured that the state would adhere to its commitments, thereby fostering a stable environment for investment and innovation (North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.6) shows how these institutions enhance market efficiency by lowering transaction costs per exchange. As economies grow more specialized, markets expand, increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) complement this by showing how institutions can improve the efficacy of reputation mechanisms. They do so by constraining the state's ex post behaviour. This constraint is crucial in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging on commitments (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These political institutions thus become indispensable for maintaining long-term economic growth, as they underpin the credibility of property rights and other contractual arrangements, ensuring that economic actors can expect their investments to be secure over time.

Distributional conflicts led to institutional arrangements that were designed to allocate resources and power among different groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutions was the creation of guilds, which served as tools for elites to maintain their dominance. These guilds, such as merchant and craft guilds, were designed to restrict competition and maintain the status quo (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate on this by showing that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that perpetuate existing power structures. These institutions also had downstream implications for economic growth. For instance, the persistence of inequality in New World societies with large slave plantations meant that a significant portion of the population was not incentivised to invest in human capital or engage in productive activities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This dynamic underscores how distributional conflicts can shape institutions in ways that hinder long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. and others (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers determined the type of institutions they established. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, while low mortality rates facilitated more inclusive ones. In regions where Europeans could not settle due to high disease prevalence, they implemented institutions designed to extract resources with minimal investment in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions persisted over time and had lasting effects on economic growth. This pattern of institutional development highlights how initial colonial experiences shaped long-term economic trajectories.

These findings underscore the importance of historical context in understanding contemporary economic disparities. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies with high European mortality rates illustrates how initial conditions can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provides empirical support for this claim, demonstrating that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth and industrialization. This institutional path dependence means that the economic performance of these regions continues to be influenced by the historical legacies of colonial rule. The persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies suggests that overcoming these historical barriers will require significant institutional reforms aimed at fostering inclusive economic development.

Historical events and technological changes have significantly shaped economic development by influencing agricultural practices and labor markets. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22) demonstrate that specific innovations and catastrophic events can drive long-term structural changes in economies. Similarly, Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death led to a wage gap between Northwest Europe and other regions by 1450 due to increased labor costs and substitution of land for labour. This shift in labor markets involved technological innovations driven by urban guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.22). Pamuk (2007, p.22) acknowledges that the emergence of more capital-intensive industries during this period also played a role, suggesting that while technological changes and historical events are pivotal, their impact is nuanced by broader economic transformations. The implications extend to understanding how such shifts can alter the trajectory of regional economies, highlighting the complex interplay between technology, labor, and long-term economic development.

These shifts in economic development underscore the multifaceted nature of growth, involving factors such as technological advance and social change, as highlighted by Komlos (1998, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4). For instance, the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance. This multifaceted nature is evident during periods of modernization when rapid progress permits an increase in capital stock through returns to modernization itself. Technological progress also broadens the horizon for scale-dependent technological advancements and increases aggregate output. During the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment; this was exacerbated by rising nutrient prices relative to industrial commodities (Komlos 1998, p.8).

The broader implications of these dynamics are significant. Technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture lagged behind those in industry, contributing to the increased price of food relative to textiles. These shifts highlight how technological advancements and economic dynamics can alter consumption patterns and well-being, even during periods of overall growth. The impact of these changes on long-term economic development underscores the complex interplay between technology, labor, and broader economic transformations.

This process requires complementary institutional progress to mitigate rent-seeking behaviors and ensure long-term benefits (Mokyr 2005, p.54), as Mokyr notes. The sustainability of economic growth hinges on this interplay: technological advancements alone are insufficient without supportive institutions that facilitate efficient use and distribution of innovations.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping the dynamics of economic growth by influencing how technological progress is integrated into society. Abramovitz identifies that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex, influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby enhancing overall productivity (North 1989, p.6), as North argues. This institutional framework ensures that technological advancements are effectively utilized, contributing to long-term economic benefits.

The historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe illustrates the importance of institutions in managing distributional conflicts and ensuring efficient use of resources. Ogilvie highlights how merchant guilds and other pre-modern institutions provided security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions, despite their perceived inefficiencies, played a critical role in maintaining economic stability and facilitating growth. Similarly, Hornbeck demonstrates that technological innovations like barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth. North shows that institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.5). However, the precise role of institutions remains a subject of debate: while Ogilvie emphasizes the distributional conflicts managed by institutions, Mokyr highlights the necessity of institutional progress to complement technological advancements. The historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe and the agricultural developments on the American Plains settles this disagreement by showing that institutions facilitate efficient use of resources and manage distributional conflicts, thereby contributing to long-term economic growth.
