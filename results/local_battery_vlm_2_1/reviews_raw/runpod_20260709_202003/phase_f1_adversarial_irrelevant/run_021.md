The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, or if they merely facilitate processes initiated by other factors. One live disagreement centers on the efficacy of institutions in fostering economic growth: some scholars argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) while others emphasize distributional conflicts and institutional interdependencies as key factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Understanding this debate is crucial for grasping how historical contexts influence contemporary economic policies. The evolution of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6), a perspective shared by North and Austin, who both highlight the importance of institutions in economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that credible commitment is essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They posit that appropriately chosen institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7), suggesting that institutional changes at critical historical junctures, such as the Glorious Revolution, were pivotal. This perspective is echoed by Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). They emphasize that the relatively low silver wage in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that institutional factors played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. who argue that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the complex relationship between institutional design and economic performance, where institutions can either facilitate or hinder growth depending on their specific characteristics. This suggests that the efficiency approach to understanding institutional development may overlook the role of power dynamics and conflict in shaping economic outcomes. Institutions are active agents in the economic growth process, influenced by historical contexts and power structures.

This institutional stability is crucial because it complements reputation-building and punishment strategies, which alone may be insufficient to prevent reneging. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by highlighting that the evolution of complex social frameworks depends on institutions capable of reducing uncertainties associated with interdependence (North 1989, p.2). These institutional requirements include developing efficient markets and a reliable medium of exchange. The establishment of such property rights allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even those they do not know personally. This is vital for realizing productivity gains from impersonal exchange. The extent to which institutions can reduce uncertainties also depends on their ability to facilitate market exchanges effectively.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The authors highlight that the extractive nature of these institutions was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a pattern that persisted over time. Acemoglu et al. further demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of these extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). High mortality rates in colonies deterred European settlement, leading to the implementation of institutions that facilitated resource extraction rather than fostering local development. This institutional legacy had a profound impact on the economic performance of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and industrialization. The persistence of these institutions over time explains current income disparities among countries (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive even if they do not encourage economic development because they are shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings underscore the critical role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic trajectories, with implications for understanding current global income inequalities.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (2008, p.11) show that historical economic divergences between regions can be understood through the lens of institutional factors. They argue that labour productivity in tradable sectors was influenced by both domestic institutions and international arbitrage (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Bryant 2006, p.29).

Bryant (2006, p.29) further elaborates on this mechanism by highlighting how institutional differences led to divergent economic paths in Europe and China. In Europe, agricultural productivity gains released labour for industrial employment, fostering urban growth and economies of scale. This structural differentiation was absent in China, where peasants concentrated handicrafts within households, limiting the scope for industrial expansion (Bryant 2006, p.28). The demographic realities of land scarcity and abundant cheap labour in China constrained technological adoption and capital accumulation, which were crucial for sustained economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).

This institutional divergence had profound implications for long-run economic trajectories. In Europe, the release of labour from agriculture into manufacturing sectors drove industrialization and urbanization, creating a virtuous cycle of productivity gains and income growth. In China, however, the lack of structural differentiation between agricultural and non-agricultural sectors hindered similar transformations. This historical institutional divergence helps explain why certain regions experienced sustained economic growth while others stagnated.

The implications of these findings extend beyond historical analyses. They underscore the enduring importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over the long run. The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence productivity, labour allocation, and technological adoption are critical for understanding both past divergences and contemporary development challenges.

Hornbeck examines how technological advancements, such as barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). These findings are supported by Whatley's analysis of gunpowder imports increasing slave exports through intertemporal production links and social processes, supporting the gun-slave cycle hypothesis (Whatley 2018, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This technological advancement in agriculture allowed for more efficient land use and increased productivity, which in turn fostered economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire fencing protected farmland from livestock damage and also facilitated the enclosure of previously open ranges. This shift enabled farmers to invest more confidently in their lands, leading to improved agricultural practices and higher yields. Whatley shows that technological advancements can have complex and unintended consequences, as seen in the case of gunpowder imports and slave exports (Whatley 2018, p.15). The increased productivity from these technologies did not always translate into sustained economic growth but instead had downstream implications for social structures and labor markets.

The literature on African economic development highlights diverse historical factors including slavery, colonial policies, and gender roles without directly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms by emphasizing the role of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment. Nunn demonstrates that Africa's involvement in slave production, driven by domestic warfare and raiding, had detrimental impacts on subsequent economic development. This suggests that the disruption caused by slavery hindered immediate productivity and also eroded long-term institutional stability necessary for sustained growth. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The contributions of women highlight the importance of gendered institutions in shaping economic outcomes. By understanding these historical factors, it becomes clear that the institutional framework set by slavery and colonial policies had a profound impact on Africa's long-run economic trajectory.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argue that pre-industrial societies' institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These scholars highlight how different institutional frameworks in New World societies led to varying levels of inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power was present from the outset in many New World economies due to their unique factor endowments. This inequality persisted over time, influencing the development of institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) further illustrates this point by showing how merchant guilds, initially efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as club goods, evolved into less efficient cartels over time. The implications of this institutional framework are profound; the interdependencies between different institutions mean that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient for understanding its impact on long-run economic outcomes.

This evolution restricted competition and limited economic growth, leading to the entrenchment of inequality through various government policies. This interplay between political power and institutional design underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks.
