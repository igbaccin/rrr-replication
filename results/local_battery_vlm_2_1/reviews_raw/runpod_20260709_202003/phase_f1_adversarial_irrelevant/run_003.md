The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a fundamental question that lies at the heart of economic history and development economics. Understanding this relationship is crucial for grasping how societies transition from stagnation to sustained prosperity. The central debate revolves around whether institutional frameworks are primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or initial endowments, play more significant roles. One position emphasizes that institutions create the necessary conditions for economic development by providing stable property rights and reducing transaction costs (van Zanden 1999, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Another view posits that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing growth; other positions argue that the role of institutions is overstated, with geographies or initial endowments being more pivotal. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic processes or exogenous factors that shape economic trajectories independently (van Zanden 1999, p.2). To understand this relationship fully, it is necessary to explore how institutions influence long-run growth through various mechanisms and to assess the empirical evidence supporting these claims.

The role of political institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth has been highlighted by North and Weingast. The Glorious Revolution is a pivotal example, where institutional changes led to more secure property rights and reduced expropriation risks, thereby encouraging investment and economic activity. This perspective underscores the importance of credible commitments by political institutions to enforce property rights and maintain stable economic environments (North and Weingast 1989, p.7; van Zanden 1999, p.2). North elaborates on this by asserting that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2).

The empirical evidence supports the view that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of institutions on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is further supported by Broadberry and Gupta, who contend that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, suggesting that institutional differences played a significant role in this divergence (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23; Austin 2008, p.11).

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They show that European mortality rates shaped the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and influence current income per capita significantly, persisted. The mechanism is straightforward: where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions to exploit resources quickly and return home rather than invest in productive, growth-oriented institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This pattern highlights that the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. note that these extractive institutions were designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists and had unintended consequences on long-term development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The enduring impact of these colonial institutions suggests that understanding the specific historical context and institutional legacies is crucial for comprehending contemporary economic disparities.

Building on this idea, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This institutional framework was shaped by factor endowments which led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. The specific focus on how these extremely different environments may have led to societies with very different degrees of inequality is crucial in understanding the long-run economic growth trajectories. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This mechanism highlights how institutional changes can directly impact productivity and economic development. The persistence of inequality suggests that institutional changes may enhance productivity in specific sectors but do not necessarily address underlying structural inequalities. Hornbeck's analysis of crop productivity increases underscores the potential for institutional innovations to drive economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These gains must be considered within the broader context of societal inequality, which can limit the widespread benefits of such developments.

The evolution of political institutions during the 17th century England was crucial for establishing credible commitments to enforce rules governing economic exchange, which in turn facilitated market development and economic growth. This institutional framework complements mechanisms such as reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing their efficacy by acting as constraints on reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). By improving the state's ability to uphold its bargains with constituents, these institutions create an environment conducive to long-term economic stability.

The importance of institutional reliability is underscored by North's emphasis on reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations. This reliability allows for confidence in outcomes that are increasingly remote from personal knowledge, enabling individuals to engage in impersonal exchange and realize productivity gains (North 1989, p.2). However, the development of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms requires a medium of exchange with reliable features, which further supports the institutional framework necessary for economic growth.

In contrast to this view, Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) examine the role of institutions in historical economic divergence by emphasizing that institutional frameworks are not uniform across space and time. They argue that the institutional settings that fostered growth in some contexts may have been suboptimal in others, highlighting that institutions must be understood within their specific historical and geographical contexts. This approach underscores how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes through their impact on productivity and economic enterprise.

Austin (2008, p.11) illustrates this point by focusing on the Atlantic slave trade, noting that "the exchange-value product of Africans' labour in Africa was lower than it was in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken" (Austin 2008, p.11). This disparity suggests that institutions governing labor and economic activity significantly influenced productivity levels. They argue that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflects lower productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that institutions play a critical role in determining economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.11) extends this analysis by noting that colonial regimes often supported African economic enterprise, which further influenced growth trajectories (Austin 2008, p.1). This support highlights how institutional changes can drive economic growth by fostering productivity and enterprise.

This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional effects, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their influence is contingent on various factors and cannot be isolated from broader historical and geographical contexts.

The literature on African economic development and institutions shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is significant. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.3) argue that historical events and institutional reforms have profoundly influenced Africa's economic trajectory (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). Institutions, particularly those that provide incentives for investment, are crucial for development. These institutions can be long-standing but not immutable; thus, the task of policy is to adapt them to present needs. Nunn illustrates this with his findings on how Africa's slave trades had adverse effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.16). This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their impact can be both enduring and malleable. Hopkins also notes the role of East Asian experiences in informing African institutional reforms, highlighting that 'good' institutions matter for providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is significant because it underscores the need for context-specific policy adaptations.

This literature does not directly address how institutions are irrelevant to long-run economic growth. Nunn's work on slavery and institutional impact shows that the production of slaves had negative impacts on subsequent development (Nunn 2008, p.3). This implies that while institutions can be adapted, their historical legacy can have lasting effects on economic outcomes. Therefore, understanding the specific historical context in which institutions operate is essential for crafting effective policies.

Therefore, Komlos and van Zanden highlight that improvements in aggregate purchasing power did not necessarily translate into better biological well-being or higher life expectancy for all income groups (Komlos 1998, p.13; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This is because economic growth during the Industrial Revolution was often accompanied by poor childhood nourishment, which led to a decline in biological well-being despite overall economic progress (Komlos 1998, p.13). This pattern of declining living standards was not universal across Europe. Pamuk observes that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.8). This suggests that regional variations in economic development and institutional frameworks played a crucial role in determining how economic growth affected living standards across different parts of Europe. This was particularly evident in the divergence of life expectancies between upper and lower income groups, where relative price effects influenced consumption patterns, leading to a substitution of industrial commodities for food products (Pamuk 2007, p.8). Van Zanden argues that the relationship between economic development and changes in living standards is complex and not always straightforward. This complexity was evident in France's agricultural sector, which experienced long-term growth in total factor productivity despite stagnationist interpretations of early modern economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Therefore, understanding regional variations and specific institutional contexts is essential for comprehending the impact of economic growth on living standards.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering economic growth, highlighting how credible commitment underpins market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework reduced uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing individuals to engage confidently in impersonal exchanges (North 1989, p.2).

Institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains from specialization and division of labor. North shows that the development of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms lowers transaction costs per exchange, although the aggregate volume of transactions increases with economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Broadberry and Gupta extend this perspective by demonstrating how institutional differences between Asia and Europe contributed to the great divergence in economic trajectories before 1800. Their analysis reveals that lower productivity in Asia's tradable goods sector reflected institutional constraints, which hindered long-term economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).

Acemoglu et al. further elucidate the role of institutions by examining how colonialism introduced extractive institutions that maximized rents for European colonists and did not promote long-run growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective underscores the importance of considering institutional contexts when evaluating economic development, as different historical periods and paths can lead to varying outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11).

The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth: credible commitment underpins market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.2). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. North shows how institutional reliability reduces uncertainties, facilitating complex exchanges and economic specialization (North 1989, p.6), whereas Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions can hinder long-term growth by maximizing rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Comparing the silver wage rates between Asia and Europe settles this disagreement, as Broadberry and Gupta show how institutional differences contributed to the great divergence in economic trajectories before 1800. The relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected lower productivity in the tradable goods sector due to institutional constraints (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).
