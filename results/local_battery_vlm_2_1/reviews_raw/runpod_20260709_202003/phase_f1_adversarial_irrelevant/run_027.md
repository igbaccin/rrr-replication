The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake intellectually is understanding whether institutional frameworks can drive sustained economic performance over centuries, or if other factors such as geography, technology, or culture are more decisive. A live disagreement exists between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that while institutions matter, they are not the sole determinant. Some researchers emphasize that specific types of institutions, particularly those that ensure secure property rights and enforce contracts, are crucial for fostering economic development. Others point to the complexity of institutional change and the interplay between different factors, suggesting that institutions alone cannot fully explain long-run growth patterns.

Institutional frameworks can drive sustained economic performance over centuries by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North argues that as economies grow more complex, efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms become essential for maintaining confidence in impersonal exchanges (North 1989, p.2). This perspective is complemented by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The development of reliable institutions allows individuals to engage in transactions with strangers, enabling specialization and division of labor. This institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby facilitating long-run economic growth.

The role of specific types of institutions in promoting economic development can be understood through the lens of property rights and contractual enforcement. North highlights that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This aligns with van Zanden's observation that institutions guaranteeing fair execution of contracts, such as guilds in western Europe, played a beneficial role in economic development by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 2009, p.27). These institutions also faced criticism for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades, which could balance their positive effects. This tension underscores the complex nature of institutional impacts on economic growth.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between different factors, including geography, technology, and culture. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups may serve to extract rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective is supported by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Institutions that favored economic growth in certain contexts may not have been optimal in others, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional impacts on long-run economic performance.

The complexity of institutional change and its interplay with other factors suggest that institutions alone cannot fully explain long-run growth patterns. Austin notes that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the kinds of institutions that favored growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.11; Austin 2008, p.1). This complexity is further illustrated by Clark and Jacks's analysis of coal mining during the Industrial Revolution. Their findings suggest that total factor productivity gains in coal mining were modest, indicating that technological barriers and extraction costs played a significant role in economic performance (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

This institutional framework governed economic exchange and ensured that these rules were enforced and adaptable. The importance of institutional reliability extends beyond economic exchange to encompass broader social frameworks that reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependencies. This is particularly relevant as economies grow more specialized and interconnected. This highlights the necessity for institutions to be reliable and efficient enough to manage increased transaction volumes without stifling economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies by shaping long-run growth patterns through persistent institutional frameworks. These institutions were often extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists, promoting local development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrates this by showing how differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of these extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persistently affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2).

The mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced long-run economic growth involved the persistence of early institutional frameworks. This persistence highlights how initial colonial policies set trajectories that were difficult to alter. This implies that the legacies of colonial institutions continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes by maintaining institutional frameworks that may not be conducive to sustained growth.

Extending this analysis to pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts underpinned the core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). She contends that these institutions were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over resources and power. The community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild are all examples of institutions that emerged from these conflicts. These distributional disputes led to the creation of institutional frameworks that served to protect the interests of dominant groups, driven by efficiency considerations. This perspective suggests that the institutions themselves were efficient solutions to economic problems and deeply influenced by the power dynamics and social struggles of their time. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to their ability to maintain the status quo, ensuring that the powerful retained control over resources.

For example, merchant guilds in the early medieval period were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement but later evolved into monopolistic entities. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this analysis by showing how unequal distributions of wealth and power shaped institutional development in New World societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They argue that institutions in these societies were structured to advantage elites, thereby perpetuating inequality. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the underlying social dynamics that shape institutions, as these dynamics can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. These studies collectively illustrate how distributional conflicts shaped institutional frameworks and influenced their evolution over time.

This institutional evolution is further illuminated by Clark and Jacks along with Broadberry and Gupta who re-evaluate historical economic growth theories by focusing on specific factors such as coal extraction costs and international price arbitrage (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). The interplay between technological barriers and productivity gains is the focus here, not the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This finding underscores that technological advancements and resource management played a crucial role in economic growth, alongside institutional frameworks alone. They argue that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, highlighting how economic factors beyond institutions drove historical economic disparities. This perspective is further supported by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11). Austin argues that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are best understood through a disaggregated view of factor endowments, including aspects of the natural environment. This qualification suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex than simple causal models might imply. The interplay between technological advancements, resource management, and environmental factors therefore emerges as a critical component in understanding long-run economic growth.

Despite these advancements, the Industrial Revolution did not equate to improved biological well-being for the population as economic growth failed to translate into sufficient income gains and adequate childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.13). Komlos (1998, p.13) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) share a finding that the benefits of economic expansion were unevenly distributed, leading to health disparities. This disparity arose from a combination of insufficient income growth for the lower classes and poor nutrition, particularly among children (Komlos 1998, p.13). This dual role of institutions in both facilitating and hindering growth underscores the complex interplay between economic structures and human well-being.

The impact of income distribution on health outcomes during industrialization highlights a critical dimension of long-run economic growth. The relative price effect, where the cost of nutrients increased due to slower technological change in agriculture compared to industry, further exacerbated the issue by altering consumption patterns (Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift led consumers to substitute cheaper carbohydrates for protein-rich foods, which had detrimental effects on nutritional intake and overall health. Thus, the economic growth experienced during the Industrial Revolution did not universally improve living standards, particularly for those in lower income brackets. The nuanced relationship between economic indicators such as GDP per capita and well-being measures like life expectancy reveals that a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth must account for distributional effects and nutritional outcomes.

This disparity is further explored by Hornbeck's work on barbed wire fencing, which highlights the role of technological innovation in enhancing property rights protection, significantly impacting agricultural development in the American Plains. For example, from 1880 to 1890 average productivity across all six crops increased by 23.4% more in counties with less woodland (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This finding suggests that technological advances can bolster economic growth by improving resource management and reducing externalities, such as livestock damage. Whatley's study on gunpowder imports further illustrates how technology can influence economic activities through intertemporal production links and social processes (Whatley 2018, p.15). These studies collectively demonstrate the potential of technology to drive economic change in specific sectors. These studies do not directly address the open question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing and enforcing rules that govern economic activities and property rights. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework supported market development and ensured that these markets functioned efficiently over time, promoting sustained economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta reinforce this idea by demonstrating how institutional changes can affect productivity and wages, which are critical determinants of economic performance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).

Moreover, the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs is evident in North's work, which highlights that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are essential for lowering costs per transaction in a growing economy. This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges and encourages specialization and division of labor, both of which drive economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further illustrate how institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The relationship between institutions and technological change is also significant. Institutions that support innovation and the adoption of new technologies can enhance productivity and resource management, thereby driving economic growth. Clark and Jacks provide evidence that technological advances in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution were modest, suggesting that institutional factors may have limited the impact of these technological changes (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This points to the importance of institutions in leveraging technological progress for economic growth.

The reversal of fortune thesis, as critiqued by Austin, underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic development. Institutions that favour economic growth in one historical context may not be optimal in another, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other factors over time (Austin 2008, p.1). Acemoglu et al. show that institutions introduced by colonialism played a crucial role in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies, reinforcing the idea that institutional changes can have long-lasting effects on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the detailed analysis of institutional frameworks and their impacts on productivity and wages. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta's examination of labor productivity in tradable goods sectors shows how institutional changes can lead to significant differences in economic performance between regions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Similarly, North and Weingast’s focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth highlights the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
