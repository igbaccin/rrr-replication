The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. This topic intersects with debates on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography or natural resources. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are endogenous to economic growth or serve as exogenous determinants that drive it. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks, including property rights and governance structures, are crucial for fostering economic development by reducing uncertainty and transaction costs. Others posit that institutions alone cannot account for long-run economic growth without considering the interplay with other factors such as technological change and cultural norms. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at promoting sustainable economic development.

North and Weingast underscore the importance of political institutions in securing property rights, noting that credible commitments by regimes enhance market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is complemented by North's broader argument that efficient property rights and their enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The evolution of such institutions allows for the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, which in turn supports long-run economic growth.

They argue that political institutions play a critical role in enforcing rules and maintaining commitments, which in turn support market development.

The credibility of institutions in maintaining commitments is further highlighted by North's broader argument about the role of property rights. Institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability allows individuals to engage in impersonal exchanges with confidence, realizing productivity gains from specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). North underscores that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial for long-run economic growth. The emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is essential for reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. share that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly influenced current income per capita and long-run economic growth of former colonies  (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their research shows that power dynamics between colonists and indigenous populations influenced these institutions, frequently resulting in extractive institutions. For example, Europeans faced high mortality rates in certain colonies, resulting in the formation of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. These institutions persist over time due to powerful groups resisting changes that could jeopardize their political power or economic gains . Acemoglu et al. further demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to varied institutional outcomes, which influenced long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The particular nature of colonial institutions plays a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition by favoring the interests of powerful groups, thereby contributing to persistent inequality. The specific mechanism through which this occurred was the concentration of political power and economic resources among a small elite. This concentration allowed these groups to shape institutions that protected their interests at the expense of broader economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For example, in societies where land and natural resources were abundant relative to labor, the initial conditions often led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. These disparities then reinforced institutional arrangements that perpetuated inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) ). The downstream implication is that without institutional changes that redistribute power more equitably, the persistence of inequality can stifle long-term economic development. This dynamic highlights how initial conditions and elite-controlled institutions can lock in patterns of inequality over extended periods.

Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11; Pamuk 2007, p.8). The Black Death initiated structural changes leading to wage gaps between regions. Pamuk contends that these long-term structural changes led to a significant wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, demonstrating how historical events can reshape economic trajectories over centuries (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This perspective challenges views that attribute regional economic divergence solely to later developments. Komlos argues that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). This suggests that even when institutions support economic growth, distributional issues can undermine broader improvements in welfare. Thus, while historical events and institutional frameworks significantly influence long-term economic development, their impacts are mediated by complex social and economic dynamics that can produce varied outcomes across different contexts.

Historical economic drivers are often reassessed by examining factors beyond institutions. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) along with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26), both re-evaluate historical economic drivers by emphasizing the roles of coal and early economic divergences over institutional determinants in shaping long-run economic growth. Clark and Jacks focus on the productivity gains in coal mining, arguing that extraction costs did not rise significantly with cumulative output during the Industrial Revolution. They estimate modest gains in total factor productivity (TFP) in coal mining from 1710 to 1869, suggesting that technological barriers were not a significant driver of economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, highlighting differences in labour productivity in tradable and non-tradable sectors as key drivers of this divergence. This perspective challenges the notion that institutional factors alone can explain long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta's argument about pre-1800 divergences underscores the role of productivity differentials between Europe and Asia, with lower silver wages in Asia reflecting low productivity in tradable goods sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This suggests that early economic divergences were driven by fundamental differences in productivity and labour markets. These findings suggest that while institutions may play a role in shaping economic growth, other factors such as technological advancements and productivity gains are equally important. For example, Clark and Jacks (2007, p.31) argue that the assumption of large unexploited coal reserves in the northeast indicates modest gains in productivity, further supporting the idea that technological barriers were not a significant driver of economic growth during the Industrial Revolution. This reassessment highlights the need to consider multiple factors when examining long-run economic growth, including the role of natural resources and early economic divergences.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms explores the impact of historical events and policies on contemporary development, including Bryant (2006, p.29) and Hopkins (2009, p.8). This is despite not directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Bryant 2006, p.29; Hopkins 2009, p.8). One mechanism through which institutions shape economic outcomes is by influencing investment incentives, as good institutions provide essential support for development by stimulating investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Bryant (2006, p.29) highlights the differences in agricultural productivity between Europe and China, noting that European agriculture released labor for industrial employment while Chinese agriculture remained tied to subsistence farming. This structural differentiation fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country in Europe, which were absent in China (Bryant 2006, p.29). Nunn (2008, p.3) further qualifies this by showing that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding had negative impacts on subsequent development. These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes and suggest that historical legacies continue to influence contemporary development trajectories.

The role of institutions is further complicated by their adaptability. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that while institutions can be long-standing, they are not immutable; policy tasks involve adapting inappropriate institutions to present development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This implies that the impact of institutions on economic growth is dynamic and contingent on policy interventions, which can mitigate or exacerbate historical legacies.

Institutions facilitate economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges and property rights enforcement. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is because institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in ensuring the regime was committed to rules, which underpinned capital markets' success (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Institutions also enhance transaction efficiency by reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependencies. North explains how reliable institutional structures allow individuals to engage confidently in remote exchanges without personal knowledge of counterparts (North 1989, p.2). This framework is essential for realizing productivity gains from specialization and division of labor, which are key drivers of economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

Institutions also play a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes by influencing the distribution of economic rents. Acemoglu et al. show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can stifle long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in colonial contexts, where institutions were often structured to benefit European colonists rather than promote local growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin's analysis of the Atlantic slave trade highlights how institutional frameworks can impact labor productivity and economic outcomes by influencing the exchange value of labor across different regions (Austin 2008, p.11).

The adaptability of institutions further complicates their role in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions are not immutable; they can be adapted to present development needs, which implies that their impact on economic growth is dynamic and contingent on policy interventions. Clark and Jacks demonstrate how the evolution of coal mining productivity was influenced by institutional adaptations over time (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This adaptability suggests that historical legacies continue to influence contemporary development trajectories, even as institutions evolve to meet new challenges.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further elucidated by their impact on labor productivity and economic rents. Broadberry and Gupta show how differences in labor productivity between tradable and non-tradable sectors can affect economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This dynamic is influenced by institutional frameworks that govern property rights and market exchanges, highlighting the interconnected nature of institutions and economic growth. The evidence shows that institutions matter for long-run economic growth because they shape the distribution of economic rents and influence labor productivity across different sectors.

The historical legacies of institutions continue to influence contemporary development trajectories, as shown by North's analysis of how institutional reliability is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.2). This is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had long-lasting effects on economic development in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the detailed analysis of institutional changes over time, such as those documented by Clark and Jacks in coal mining productivity gains during the Industrial Revolution. These findings underscore the enduring impact of institutions on long-run economic growth, demonstrating how institutional frameworks shape economic outcomes through their influence on property rights, market exchanges, and labor productivity.
