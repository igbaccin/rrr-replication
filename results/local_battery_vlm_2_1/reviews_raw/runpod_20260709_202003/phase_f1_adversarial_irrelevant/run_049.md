The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable development and alleviating poverty. Institutions, formal rules and informal norms that shape human interactions, can either facilitate or hinder economic progress by influencing the allocation of resources, incentivizing investment, and determining the distribution of power. One school of thought emphasizes institutions' central role in promoting growth through secure property rights and efficient governance structures. Other scholars, however, argue that institutions are merely one factor among many, with geopolitical, cultural, or resource factors playing equally significant roles. The debate hinges on whether institutions operate as a primary driver of economic growth or as a secondary influence modulated by other determinants.

One stream of research highlights the critical role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for establishing secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework, they contend, underpinned the development of markets by ensuring that rules were enforced and could be changed only through established procedures. Broadberry and Gupta support this view by showing how the great divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, suggesting that institutions played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes long before industrialization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The institutional reliability enabled individuals to engage in complex transactions with confidence, thereby facilitating market exchanges. This perspective underscores the importance of credible commitments and stable governance structures in promoting long-run economic growth.

Another stream focuses on the broader impact of institutions on economic development, emphasizing their role in shaping the overall economic environment. North posits that institutions are essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.2). This institutional framework allows for the specialization and division of labor, which in turn drives productivity gains. Acemoglu et al. extend this argument by demonstrating how colonial institutions introduced by European powers influenced the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrate the lasting impact of institutional legacies on economic development. The persistence of these institutional frameworks underscores their role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation. Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that other factors, including institutional changes, were more critical (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This perspective aligns with van Zanden's contention that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards in Europe between 1500 and 1800. Van Zanden critiques optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This stream suggests that institutions must adapt to technological changes and broader economic shifts to sustain long-run growth.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They demonstrate how the Glorious Revolution led to institutional changes that improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, including not expropriating wealth. This commitment was essential for long-run economic growth because it provided a stable environment for investment and market exchanges. The development of efficient markets and a reliable medium of exchange creates confidence in dealings with unknown parties, facilitating complex interdependent situations. North emphasizes that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with growing specialization (North 1989, p.2). This means that even as the network of interdependence widens due to increased specialization, individuals can have confidence in outcomes that are increasingly remote from their personal knowledge. The ability to realize productivity gains through impersonal exchange relies on a set of property rights that allow for secure transactions even among strangers.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional frameworks established by colonial powers were pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific institutions introduced during colonialism had profound impacts on current income per capita and economic growth; these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-term development. This mechanism is rooted in the political dynamics of the time, where powerful groups shaped institutions to secure their interests. Acemoglu et al. highlight that mortality rates faced by Europeans influenced settlement patterns, which in turn determined institutional structures. For example, high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were maintained even after independence and significantly affected current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring legacy of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that these institutions are not static; over time, they can evolve in response to changing political and economic conditions. These findings imply that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced examination of historical institutional changes and their lasting effects on economic performance.

Sokoloff and Engerman, alongside Hornbeck, show that institutions in societies with greater inequality restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This is because the economic elites of these societies established rules that favored their interests. For instance, they secured property rights for themselves while limiting access to resources for others. The resulting lack of competition stifled innovation and prevented the efficient allocation of resources. These institutions were particularly effective at maintaining inequality by perpetuating a system where the wealthy remained in control. This process was about more than just economics; it was deeply rooted in political power dynamics, which ensured those in control maintained their position over time.

These institutional arrangements had significant implications for development trajectories: they created barriers to entry for new competitors and limited opportunities for social mobility (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). By restricting access to resources and opportunities, these institutions ensured that wealth remained concentrated among a small elite. This concentration of power further reinforced the existing inequalities, as those in control could use their influence to maintain the status quo. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights how deeply entrenched inequality can be, and how difficult it is to overcome without fundamental changes to the underlying institutional framework.

Van Zanden and Komlos both share a finding that economic development does not necessarily translate to improvements in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Van Zanden's work highlights the disconnect between economic growth and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, challenging optimistic views on early modern economic growth. He notes that while some scholars argue for significant productivity gains during this period, these claims often overlook declining real wages and the long-term stagnation experienced by many Europeans. Komlos's research further illustrates this disconnect in the context of the Industrial Revolution, showing that despite overall economic growth, biological well-being actually declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This pattern suggests that economic development does not automatically lead to better living conditions; rather, it depends on how wealth is distributed and whether basic needs are met. The divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower-income groups during this period underscores the importance of distributional factors. Komlos also points out that relative price effects played a crucial role in shaping consumption patterns (Komlos 1998, p.8). For example, the rising cost of nutrients relative to industrial goods led consumers to substitute carbohydrates for protein-rich foods, which had significant implications for nutritional outcomes. This observation highlights how economic changes can have complex and unintended consequences on living standards.

The Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Pamuk 2007, p.8). This divergence was driven by structural changes in wages and productivity, initiated by events such as the Black Death. Arbitrage ensured a single international price for tradable goods, measured in silver, which led to lower silver wages in Asia due to low productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This mechanism highlights how productivity differences between regions can significantly influence economic outcomes. Pamuk (2007, p.8) builds on this by showing that the Black Death triggered long-term structural changes, including a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450. These changes were driven by severe labour shortages which led to a decline in the skill premium and encouraged human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This process varied across Europe; urban-rural linkages differed significantly, and large wage differences persisted in some countries (Pamuk 2007, p.8). The implication is that while structural changes and productivity differences played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes, the impact of these factors was mediated by regional specificities.

Coal and distributional conflicts played significant roles in shaping economic development during the Industrial Revolution (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) argue that coal did not drive the Industrial Revolution as extensively as previously thought. They contend that total factor productivity gains in coal mining were modest, with extraction costs rising only slightly despite increased output. This finding challenges the notion of coal being a pivotal driver of economic growth during this period. She argues that guilds and other institutions were shaped by power dynamics rather than purely by their contribution to economic efficiency.

The implications of these findings are substantial for understanding the broader impact on economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) extends this perspective by noting that the efficiency approach to institutions often overlooks the complex interdependencies within broader institutional frameworks. This insight underscores that the role of coal and distributional conflicts in economic development is part of a larger, interconnected system where multiple factors interact to shape outcomes.

Distributional conflicts, such as those between different social classes or regions, can significantly alter the trajectory of economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the adoption of new technologies. This perspective suggests that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view that considers not just their direct impact on productivity but also their broader social and political context.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs and promoting market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Institutions that effectively specify and enforce property rights facilitate the development of complex social frameworks necessary for economic growth. By providing reliable mechanisms for market transactions, they enable individuals to engage in impersonal exchange with confidence, even when dealing with unfamiliar parties (North 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Institutional reliability is crucial for economic growth because it mitigates uncertainties associated with interdependent social situations (North 1989, p.6). This reliability allows for the realization of productivity gains from increased specialization and division of labor. When institutions are reliable, they enable individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, which is essential for maintaining long-term economic growth. The development of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms is a key factor in reducing transaction costs and promoting market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). This highlights the importance of political institutions that enforce rules governing economic exchange.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize their own interests over long-term economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.7; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). These groups can design institutions to maximize rents for themselves rather than promoting broad-based economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), which can lead to extractive institutions that hinder economic progress. This dynamic is evident in the context of former European colonies, where colonial institutions were often designed to benefit European colonists at the expense of long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with differences in productivity and wages reflecting underlying institutional differences. In Europe, secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms facilitated market exchanges and economic growth, while in Asia, institutions that did not prioritize these factors limited economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of coal as a driver of the Industrial Revolution offers further insights into how technological progress interacts with institutional frameworks. Institutions governing resource extraction and allocation played a crucial role in shaping the trajectory of economic growth during this period. The modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining highlight that technological advancements alone are insufficient for sustained economic growth; they must be supported by institutions that facilitate efficient resource use (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie. In a recent book Hoffmann (1996) showed that the traditional agriculture of France was able to generate long term growth of total factor productivity between 1450 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Institutions that support technological innovation and efficient resource use are essential for long-term economic growth.

The historical evidence from seventeenth-century England shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The Glorious Revolution marked a significant institutional change that enhanced the reliability of political institutions, thereby promoting economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This period illustrates how institutional changes can complement reputation-building mechanisms to improve the efficacy of market exchanges. The institutional framework established during this time provided a stable environment for economic growth by ensuring that property rights were secure and contracts were enforceable.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the analysis of total factor productivity gains in coal mining and the development of efficient property rights. The modest gains in total factor productivity during the Industrial Revolution, coupled with the significant institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, demonstrate that reliable institutions are essential for sustained economic growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; van Zanden 1999, p.2). These findings show that while technological advancements are important, they must be supported by institutions that facilitate efficient resource use and secure property rights.
