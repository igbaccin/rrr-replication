The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the dynamics that drive prosperity and development over extended periods. This question matters because it addresses how societies can harness their resources effectively to sustain economic progress. At its core, there exists a live disagreement about whether technological innovation alone drives growth or if institutional frameworks play an equally crucial role. On one side of this debate are scholars who emphasize the primacy of technological change, viewing institutions as secondary factors that facilitate but do not fundamentally drive growth. On the other hand, proponents of institutional primacy argue that effective institutions are indispensable for translating technological potential into sustained economic outcomes. This debate is pivotal because it determines whether policy should focus on fostering innovation or on building robust institutional frameworks to support long-term development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a critical area of inquiry, as understanding this dynamic can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable development and prosperity. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. By fostering secure property rights, these institutions create an environment conducive to market exchanges and economic development. The evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England exemplifies this dynamic. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight how the Glorious Revolution led to institutional changes that enhanced the credibility of commitments made by the state (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This credibility is crucial for maintaining trust and ensuring that constituents do not face expropriation, thereby encouraging investment and economic growth. The political institutions that emerged during this period acted as constraints on the state's behavior, improving its ability to uphold bargains with constituents. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This reduction in transaction costs is essential for economic growth, as it allows for increased specialization and division of labor, leading to a higher volume of exchanges. The institutional framework established during this period supported economic growth and laid the groundwork for sustainable development by ensuring that property rights were secure and enforceable.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional frameworks established during the colonial period were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a pattern evident across diverse colonial contexts. This extractive nature of institutions was driven by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, who implemented systems that secured their control over resources and labor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The differential mortality rates faced by Europeans in various colonies further shaped these institutions: high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as seen in many African and Latin American colonies. These institutions persisted over time due to the entrenched interests of local elites who benefited from maintaining the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights that the persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had a lasting impact on economic performance, contributing significantly to current income disparities among former colonies. However, this does not mean all European settlements resulted in extractive institutions; where Europeans could settle and establish institutions more conducive to long-run growth, as in parts of North America and Australia, the economic outcomes were markedly different (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The legacy of colonial institutions thus underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7)  argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.30), with this perspective challenging the notion of these institutions as efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. Ogilvie's critique extends to merchant guilds, which have been traditionally viewed as inefficient cartels but are re-evaluated in her work as effective solutions for security and contract enforcement. This reinterpretation is extended by Hornbeck (2010, p.30) who shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The mechanism through which these institutions operate is by addressing distributional conflicts, which in turn shapes economic outcomes. This perspective prompts questions about the conditions under which such institutions are effective. This implies that while distributional conflicts play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions, the outcomes are contingent on the specific historical and socio-economic conditions.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Temin 2002, p.4) both argue that technological advance is crucial for economic growth. They emphasize that the catch-up process involves complex dynamics beyond simple convergence to a steady state. Abramovitz notes that rapid progress through modernization encourages capital stock growth, driven by returns to modernization and reduced relative prices of capital goods (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This mechanism underscores how technological opportunities can accelerate economic development. Temin builds on this idea by highlighting the role of labor allocation shifts from agriculture to industry in driving unusually rapid post-WWII European growth (Temin 2002, p.3). Abramovitz also points out that backwardness itself carries an opportunity for modernization in both embodied and disembodied technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This implies that the catch-up process involves adopting new technologies and leveraging existing ones more efficiently.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are both essential for economic growth because they help harness innovations efficiently (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) highlights that technological advancements are often tailored to the specific needs of their country of origin and argues that for countries aiming to catch up, even basic science may not directly address their unique requirements. This suggests that while technological innovation is vital, its benefits are contingent upon supportive institutions that facilitate efficient resource allocation and prevent the dissipation of economic rents. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates on this interdependence by noting that mass application of technical innovations, many based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop for additional technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by pointing out that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking. This implies that while new inventions are crucial, they build upon a foundation of pre-existing knowledge and institutional frameworks that enable their effective deployment.
