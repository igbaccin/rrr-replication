Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by structuring incentives and constraining behavior, but there is a live disagreement about how this influence operates. One position holds that institutions facilitating secure property rights and credible commitments are essential for fostering economic growth. This view emphasizes the role of political institutions in enforcing rules and ensuring that they are not arbitrarily changed, thereby reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment. Another perspective posits that institutions influence economic growth based on historical context and cannot be universally applied across different periods or regions. This position underscores the complexity of institutional effects, suggesting that their influence varies depending on specific economic and social conditions. A third stream suggests that institutions mediate other factors such as technology or natural resources, which ultimately determine economic growth. The stakes here are high: understanding the role of institutions in economic growth has implications for policy-making and development strategies, influencing how societies can best foster sustainable prosperity.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by providing a framework that supports secure property rights and credible commitments. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules, as well as how they may be changed. This perspective highlights the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic development by ensuring that property rights are secure. North (1989, p.2) further elaborates on this idea by emphasizing that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This view aligns with Broadberry and Gupta’s analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800, where differences in productivity and wages reflect underlying institutional frameworks (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The evolution of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms is crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.6), demonstrating that institutions matter significantly in economic growth.

The institutional framework provided by the Glorious Revolution was pivotal in establishing a credible commitment mechanism, which ensured that rulers would adhere to agreed-upon rules and not expropriate wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This credibility fostered market development by reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth.

The evolution of these institutions was a direct response to the need for reducing transaction costs and uncertainties inherent in growing economies. This institutional development allowed for the emergence of markets that could support economic growth by providing a reliable framework for transactions. Consequently, this institutional reliability was instrumental in fostering long-run economic development by creating an environment where individuals could confidently engage in market activities.

The implications of these institutional changes are profound. The establishment of reliable property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms directly influenced the development of markets, which in turn drove economic growth. Furthermore, the credible commitment provided by these institutions reduced the uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby enabling individuals to engage in impersonal exchange without fear of expropriation. This institutional reliability was essential for long-run economic growth as it created a stable environment conducive to market development and economic prosperity.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that institutions introduced during the colonial period are crucial in determining long-run economic growth, with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not foster long-term development. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over broader economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, colonies with high mortality rates among Europeans tended to develop extractive institutions because settlers feared losing political power if institutional changes occurred (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic highlights how the initial conditions set by colonial powers profoundly influenced the trajectory of economic development in these regions. The persistence of these institutions over time suggests that their impact on current income per capita is a deep-seated structural influence. The extractive nature of these institutions means they are designed to benefit the ruling elite rather than promote widespread economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional design has lasting implications for economic performance, as it creates barriers to innovation and productivity gains that are essential for sustained growth. This correlation underscores how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic outcomes.

Building on this perspective, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Hornbeck 2010, p.34), along with Hornbeck, show that institutions in societies with greater inequality often restrict competition. This institutional framework can be traced to the early stages of colonial development, where factor endowments and initial conditions led to extreme inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power. This institutional design, which favored elites, restricted competition and innovation, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. Hornbeck's study also qualifies this pattern by showing that while barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development, it did not entirely eliminate externalities from livestock damage (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that while institutional improvements can drive productivity gains, they may not fully address all challenges associated with inequality and economic development.

The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during the early modern period is complex and not straightforwardly positive (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Both van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) explore this dynamic. One key mechanism linking these two aspects is the distribution of income gains across different social groups. Komlos (1998, p.13) argues that while aggregate measures of purchasing power may indicate growth during the Industrial Revolution, they do not capture the decline in biological well-being experienced by many due to poor childhood nourishment. This divergence suggests that economic development did not uniformly translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. The optimists among economic historians have argued for a long-term growth in total factor productivity. Komlos (1998, p.13) qualifies this pattern by highlighting that despite overall economic growth, the life expectancy gap between upper and lower income groups widened during this period (Komlos 1998, p.8). This implies that while institutions may drive economic growth, their impact on living standards can be unevenly distributed.

This complexity is further explored by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (2008, p.11), who critique various theses on economic development by examining the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. They argue that this gap was driven by differences in labour productivity within the tradable goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Bryant 2006, p.29). In Europe, structural differentiation between agriculture and industry fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country. This economic arrangement allowed for economies of scale, increased demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production, and enhanced capital accumulation. The agricultural revolution in Europe saw a shift towards larger farming units that combined grain production with livestock rearing. This improved diets, increased productivity through the substitution of animal labour for human labour, and enhanced soil fertility (Bryant 2006, p.28). Chinese peasants were increasingly driven into a crops-only pattern, leading to the reverse substitution of human for animal labour power. The demographic realities of mounting land scarcity and a surfeit of cheap labour decisively governed all economic considerations, even to the point of resisting the spread of laboursaving technologies (Bryant 2006, p.29). This divergence in agricultural practices and economic structures highlights how different institutional frameworks can lead to varying paths of economic development.

This historical context is further enriched by Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) (Pamuk 2007, p.8), who argue that long-term economic growth is significantly influenced by specific historical economic factors, such as the role of coal during the Industrial Revolution. Clark and Jacks's analysis reveals that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, contrary to some prevailing views. The Black Death created severe labour shortages, which in turn influenced human capital formation. This dynamic was further complicated by the decline in interest rates across Europe following the pandemic (Pamuk 2007, p.15).

The economic factors that Clark and Jacks highlight suggest that coal mining productivity gains were modest at best during the Industrial Revolution era from 1710 to 1869. These findings indicate that technological barriers and extraction costs did not significantly impede coal production, challenging the notion of a resource-driven industrial boom (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This perspective underscores how specific economic factors can shape long-term growth trajectories, even when institutional frameworks are held constant.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underpinned by their ability to foster secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North and Weingast show that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice was crucial for securing property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework enabled England's economic growth, as evidenced by capital markets' success. These findings align with North’s broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7).

Institutions also play a critical role in addressing the challenges posed by technological barriers and extraction costs. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that despite modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining during 1710 to 1869, institutions were instrumental in managing these economic factors (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This is supported by Broadberry and Gupta’s analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. Their work highlights how institutional frameworks influenced labor productivity and wage disparities, underscoring that economic growth is driven by technological advancements as well as by institutional structures (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).

Moreover, institutions shape long-term growth trajectories by influencing urban-rural wage disparities and sectoral linkages. While Pamuk's historical context provides insights into these economic shifts, van Zanden’s analysis of Europe between 1500 and 1800 reveals that economic development did not always translate to improved standards of living (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This discrepancy underscores the importance of institutions in mediating the relationship between economic growth and broader societal benefits.

The remaining disagreement centers on whether institutions alone can drive economic growth or if other factors, such as technological advancements and resource availability, play a more significant role. North shows that institutions are essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks (North 1989, p.2). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11). To settle this disagreement, empirical comparisons of institutional reliability across regions with similar resource endowments are necessary. These comparisons reveal that regions with more reliable institutions experience higher economic growth rates, even when technological advancements and resource availability are held constant.
