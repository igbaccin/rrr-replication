The central question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is pivotal in understanding historical and contemporary economic trajectories. At stake are competing explanations for why some regions thrive while others stagnate. The debate centers on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, or technology. One key position emphasizes that institutional frameworks, such as property rights and governance structures, that facilitate secure transactions and economic stability are crucial drivers of growth. This view is contested by those who argue that long-run growth is primarily driven by exogenous factors like geographic endowments or technological advancements, which institutions merely complement.

This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to promote sustained economic development. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn stimulated economic growth. This institutional evolution was about establishing rules and creating mechanisms to enforce them reliably. North and Weingast emphasize the role of political institutions in enhancing the state's credibility, ensuring that it upholds its commitments (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The Glorious Revolution is a case study showing how restrictions on ex post behavior can improve the state’s reliability in maintaining agreements with constituents (North 1989, p.2).

North extends this argument by highlighting the importance of institutional reliability in reducing uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks. He argues that as economies grow and become more specialized, the need for reliable institutions increases to facilitate impersonal exchanges and ensure confidence in remote transactions (North 1989, p.6). North's analysis underscores how efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for realizing productivity gains from specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.2).

The role of reputation-building strategies must not be overlooked. These strategies complement institutional frameworks by providing additional layers of accountability and trust (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This interplay between institutions and reputation mechanisms ensures that economic transactions are conducted with a high degree of reliability, even in the absence of personal knowledge or ongoing exchange relationships.

The evolution of political institutions thus creates a feedback loop where secure property rights enhance economic growth, which in turn necessitates more robust institutional frameworks to manage increased complexity. This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks facilitate and sustain long-run economic development by adapting to changing economic conditions and ensuring that transactions remain efficient and trustworthy.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have been pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, emphasizing that these specific historical institutions are more critical than general ones. The institutional frameworks established during colonial periods were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic development. These institutions persisted post-colonization and significantly influenced current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to their design, which was tailored to benefit European colonists rather than foster long-term economic development. This is further corroborated by the high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies and the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The impact of these colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform across all regions. Acemoglu et al (2002) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) note that while institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of some former colonies, this effect varies significantly depending on local conditions and the specific nature of the colonial institutions imposed. This variation underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality have historically favored elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by shaping property rights in ways that benefited those already in power. This example underscores how institutional frameworks that protect property rights can lead to more efficient use of resources and increased productivity, even in environments characterized by high levels of inequality. This variation highlights the complexity of institutional impacts on long-run economic development, suggesting that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their design and how they interact with local factors and pre-existing social structures.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) share a finding that economic development does not necessarily lead to improvements in the standard of living. Van Zanden critiques optimistic interpretations of early modern European growth, noting that while some scholars argue for long-term productivity gains in agriculture and a process of 'modern economic growth' in specific regions like the Dutch economy between 1580 and 1670, these views often overlook the complexities of real wage declines and the varying experiences across different socioeconomic groups. Meanwhile, Komlos focuses on the Industrial Revolution, highlighting that despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in outcomes suggests that economic development alone does not guarantee improved living standards; other factors such as income distribution and nutritional access play crucial roles. Komlos further qualifies this relationship by noting that relative price effects also mattered: during the onset of modern economic growth, the relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns (Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift in consumption highlights how changes in relative prices can mitigate some of the benefits of economic growth, affecting biological well-being and overall living standards.

Historical events and structural changes significantly influenced economic divergences long before modern industrialization. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Pamuk (2007, p.8) argue that significant divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway by 1800 due to differences in labour productivity across tradable goods sectors. The price of non-tradable commodities depended on the relative productivity of these sectors, with higher silver wages reflecting greater productivity in tradable sectors. This mechanism suggests that preindustrial economic divergences were driven by underlying structural factors rather than industrialization alone.

Pamuk (2007, p.8) further supports this view, noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes leading to wage gaps between northwest Europe and other regions by 1450. The decline in the skill premium due to severe labour shortages encouraged human capital formation, highlighting how demographic shocks can reshape economic structures over centuries. However, Pamuk (2007, p.15) also notes that these effects could be complex, with varying impacts on different regions depending on factors such as urban-rural linkages and population recovery.

The interplay between structural changes and economic divergences underscores the importance of historical context in understanding long-run economic growth. The Black Death's impact on wage gaps illustrates how demographic events can trigger lasting economic transformations, while Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) show that productivity differences in tradable goods sectors influenced relative prices and wages. These findings suggest that preindustrial structural changes played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views debate the historical and geographical determinants of economic development (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). These approaches argue that pre-existing conditions in Africa and Asia influenced their economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the low labour productivity in Sub-Saharan Africa can be understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11). The scarcity of labour relative to cultivable land and capital is a key factor in understanding the economic conditions that led to the Atlantic slave trade. Bryant (2006, p.29) highlights how differing agricultural patterns influenced socio-economic structures. In Europe, the productivity revolution in agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, fostering urban growth and economies of scale (Bryant 2006, p.29). This dynamic contrasts with China's pattern of concentrating handicrafts within peasant households, which limited structural differentiation between rural and urban sectors. The demographic realities of land scarcity and cheap labour in China governed economic considerations, resisting the spread of laboursaving technologies. These differences in agricultural patterns and socio-economic structures had profound implications for long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.11) acknowledges that colonial regimes supported African economic enterprise to extract economic rent (Austin 2008, p.1). This suggests that institutions favoring economic growth were context-specific, highlighting the need for nuanced historical analysis when considering the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing property rights and market development. North and Weingast show that secure property rights are essential for fostering economic growth, as they enable individuals to invest with confidence (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This is supported by Austin's analysis of colonial regimes, which highlights how institutions favoring economic growth were context-specific and influenced by the need to extract economic rent (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, with institutional differences playing a significant role in this process. The establishment of efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, as North argues (van Zanden 1999, p.2; North 1989, p.2). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and effective market functioning. This is seen in the case of early modern Europe, where institutional changes supported economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes is further illustrated by the experience of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective is supported by van Zanden's analysis of economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800, which shows that institutions played a crucial role in generating long-term productivity gains. The case of France, where traditional agriculture was able to generate long-term growth of total factor productivity between 1450 and 1800, highlights the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This is consistent with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not promote long-run growth. The historical evidence from France shows that institutions favoring economic growth were context-specific and influenced by the need to extract economic rent, as seen in the case of colonial regimes supporting African economic enterprise (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Austin 2008, p.11).
