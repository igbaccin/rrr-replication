The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One key disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate efficient use of resources or serve as tools for distributing power and wealth within society. Institutions are seen by some scholars as mechanisms to reduce transaction costs, enhance property rights, and foster innovation (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). Some scholars posit that institutions frequently arise from distributional conflicts and may perpetuate existing power structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Some studies focus on the role of technological progress as a driver of economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while others emphasize the importance of institutional adjustments to harness this progress effectively. The resolution of these debates has profound implications for understanding historical economic development and for designing policies aimed at fostering sustained growth in contemporary economies. This is particularly evident when considering how different institutions have influenced agricultural advancements by protecting property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).

Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that high mortality rates led Europeans to establish extractive institutions in these colonies designed to maximize rents for colonists. This institutional framework was shaped by the political and economic interests of European colonizers, who sought to secure their positions and extract resources efficiently. The persistence of these institutions highlights how historical factors can have enduring effects on economic development. The role of technological progress in mitigating or exacerbating these institutional legacies is an open question.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Domar (1970, p.7) show that technological advancements in property protection can significantly enhance economic development by strengthening institutions that safeguard land rights. Barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to better secure their land from livestock damage, thus reducing externalities and fostering agricultural growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). Barbed wire's introduction was a critical institutional change enhancing farmers' ability to protect their property rights. However, the direct technological effects of barbed wire were not the primary driver of these results; instead, it was the institutional change that provided stronger property rights protections. Domar (1970, p.7) further qualifies this by noting that the profitability of labor systems can be influenced by technological progress and capital accumulation, which in turn affects the economic viability of different labor arrangements (Domar 1970, p.7; Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements like barbed wire can enhance property rights and spur development, their impact is contingent on broader economic conditions and institutional frameworks. The interplay between technology and institutions highlights how specific innovations can reinforce existing economic structures or catalyze new ones, depending on the prevailing conditions.

Building on this, Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. Ogilvie contends that merchant guilds and other pre-modern institutions, such as the Mesta in Spain, have been misinterpreted as efficient solutions to transactional obstacles when they should be understood through the lens of power dynamics among elites. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, where institutions favored elites and perpetuated inequality. They note that countries like Brazil and those in the West Indies were dominated by large plantations and slave populations, which led to extreme wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional framework restricted competition and reinforced elite control, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of inequality. Ogilvie notes that while these institutions might have been inefficient from an economic standpoint, they were highly effective in maintaining the status quo for elites (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding long-run economic growth. If distributional conflicts drove institutional development, then policies aimed at promoting economic growth must consider power dynamics and elite interests. This suggests that reforming institutions to foster growth requires addressing underlying social inequalities and the political structures that sustain them. The persistence of inequality in many societies today underscores the enduring influence of these historical institutional frameworks (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).

North's work (North 1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) focus on institutional changes introduced by colonialism but do not directly address long-run economic growth as an outcome. The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is pivotal for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs in a growing economy. This institutional framework fosters specialization and division of labor, thereby increasing the volume of exchanges and reducing per-transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Moreover, secure property rights are crucial for economic development. Credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that rules governing economic exchange are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon processes. This commitment is vital because it complements reputation-building mechanisms, acting as a constraint when reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism did not always lead to long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share that technological progress is pivotal for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10)(Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr highlights the interdependence of technological and institutional progress, noting that without complementary institutional changes, technological advancements risk being undermined by rent-seeking behavior. This dynamic means that while technological progress drives growth, institutions play a crucial role in sustaining it. Both authors underscore the importance of technological progress in fostering economic growth, but Mokyr's focus on coordination failures suggests an additional layer where institutions ensure that technological gains are not eroded over time.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) identifies specific mechanisms through which technological progress occurs, such as the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking. These processes highlight how knowledge accumulation fuels innovation, driving economic growth. Mokyr acknowledges that this process is not linear; it depends on institutional frameworks that support sustained technological advancement. Kuznets (1973, p.10) extends this idea by noting that technological innovations are often tailored to the specific needs of their country of origin, implying that the broader applicability of these innovations requires adaptation through institutions. This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver, institutional frameworks are essential for harnessing its full potential. Kuznets also points out that mass application of technical innovations provides a positive feedback loop, further driving technological and scientific advancements.

Institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.6) argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective challenges neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as primary determinants, showing instead that institutions matter significantly (North 1989, p.5). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further support this by demonstrating that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The historical evidence of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains illustrates how institutions can enhance agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that this technological innovation significantly improved agricultural outcomes by enabling better protection of property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This case underscores the broader point that institutions are essential for harnessing the benefits of technological progress, as seen in the positive feedback loop between technological innovations and scientific advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. Institutions facilitate the coordination necessary for sustaining economic improvements and drive technological progress through efficient property rights enforcement (North 1989, p.6). The specific historical evidence from barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, combined with the broader applicability of technological innovations across different countries, settles the disagreement over whether institutions or technology is the primary driver. Institutions are essential for harnessing the full potential of technological progress, as shown by the positive feedback loop between mass application of technical innovations and further scientific advancements (Ogilvie 2007, p.6; North 1989, p.5).
