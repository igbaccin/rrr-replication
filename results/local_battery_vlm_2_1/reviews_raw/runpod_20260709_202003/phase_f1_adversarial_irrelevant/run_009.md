The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether institutions are pivotal drivers of economic progress or merely secondary factors. The disagreement hinges on whether the institutional framework shapes technological advance and social change or if these factors operate independently, with institutions being mere byproducts of economic development. Some argue that institutions facilitate economic growth through credible commitment mechanisms and efficient property rights enforcement (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). Some scholars argue that distributional conflicts are primary drivers behind core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. For instance, if institutions are indeed critical, then policy interventions should focus on institutional reforms to enhance property rights and reduce transaction costs. If distributional conflicts drive policy, then addressing income inequality might be more effective.

Institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide enforcement reduce transaction costs per exchange. This reduction is crucial for fostering a growing economy, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. Institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds have been interpreted as efficient solutions to various obstacles in pre-modern economies. These institutions can also reflect underlying power dynamics and distributional struggles. This dual role of institutions, both facilitating economic efficiency and managing social conflicts, highlights their complex interplay with long-run economic growth.

Institutions are crucial for technological progress but not the sole determinant; they interact with other factors such as capital accumulation and labor productivity (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Technological innovations drive modern economic growth, and institutions facilitate the efficient use of these innovations. Mass application of technological innovations, based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that further advances technology. This interaction between institutions and technological progress underscores their interdependent relationship in shaping long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers introduced different institutional frameworks depending on the mortality conditions they encountered. This pattern was driven by the settlers' reluctance or inability to establish permanent settlements, leading to institutional frameworks that prioritized immediate gains. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had lasting effects on economic performance, as evidenced by current income disparities between former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. note that while institutions are crucial, other factors such as the initial conditions and subsequent historical events mediate their impact. This nuance underscores the complex interplay between institutional legacies and contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) focus on institutional changes introduced by colonialism but do not directly address long-run economic growth as an outcome. They share the view that institutions are central to facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political institutions play a critical role in enforcing property rights and maintaining commitments, which is essential for sustained growth. North (1989, p.6) argues this is because a society's output depends on more than just inputs: the institutional framework governing these inputs matters enormously (North 1989, p.5). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) caution that institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms. They highlight that political institutions can improve the efficacy of reputation by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging, such as preventing expropriation (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This nuance underscores how institutional changes can influence economic growth through multiple pathways, including securing property rights and enhancing market efficiency.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) (Domar 1970, p.7) both focus on specific historical institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds. They examine how these institutions shaped economic outcomes without directly addressing the long-term impact of these institutions on growth. Serfdom, for instance, is analysed through its relationship with land scarcity and labor supply rather than its direct effect on sustained economic development. Ogilvie highlights that the efficiency of merchant guilds varied across different periods, suggesting that their role in facilitating transactions was not uniform over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This variability underscores how institutional arrangements can evolve to meet changing economic conditions, even if they do not directly promote long-term growth. This perspective is nuanced by the fact that technological progress and capital accumulation can influence the profitability of slave versus free labor, adding complexity to the relationship between institutional arrangements and economic outcomes. Domar notes that in traditional societies without significant technological advancements or capital accumulation, the end of slavery becomes more certain as populations grow (Domar 1970, p.7). This insight suggests that long-term economic changes, such as population growth, can alter the viability of specific institutions over time. The downstream implication is that while these institutional arrangements may not directly drive long-run economic growth, they are subject to broader economic forces that can reshape them in ways that indirectly influence development trajectories.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8) note that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved biological well-being for all segments of society. The complexities of economic dynamics during the Industrial Revolution meant that while aggregate output increased, significant portions of the population experienced declining health due to inadequate income gains and poor nourishment. Komlos highlights that this economic growth did not uniformly benefit all social classes; it led to a divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups (Komlos 1998, p.8). This disparity is further exacerbated by relative price effects, where the increased cost of nutrients relative to other goods forces consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns, often at the expense of nutritional quality. Komlos observes that during this period, the relative price of food increased significantly, leading to a substitution of cheaper carbohydrates for more nutritious protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). These shifts in consumption patterns, driven by economic changes, had profound implications for public health and biological well-being. This points to a broader pattern where economic growth can lead to differential outcomes across social strata, influenced by the distribution of income gains and relative price changes.

Technological progress often builds on existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel solutions (Mokyr 2005, p.12; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This process underscores how technological advancements can spur economic growth through the efficient use of innovations that respond to specific needs.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) also notes that while technological progress can be more linear and irreversible, institutional progress is far less so. This highlights a crucial role for institutions in sustaining long-term economic growth, even if they are not the primary drivers. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further supports this idea by emphasizing that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. The positive feedback loop between scientific discoveries and technological applications suggests that while technology may drive initial growth, institutions are necessary for harnessing these innovations effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technological progress and institutional support is essential for understanding the complex dynamics of long-run economic growth.

Institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements by reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. North shows that effective property rights and enforcement mechanisms lower costs per transaction, which is crucial for a growing economy (North 1989, p.6). This aligns with Kuznets' observation that institutions facilitate the mass application of technological innovations, which in turn drives economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Moreover, institutions are necessary for coordinating technological progress. Mokyr highlights coordination failures that can impede sustainable economic improvement without complementary institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This institutional support is essential for harnessing the full potential of technological advancements and ensuring long-term economic sustainability.

The role of institutions extends beyond merely facilitating technological progress; they also shape the dynamics of economic growth through their impact on social change. Abramovitz argues that technological opportunity encourages rapid growth of the capital stock, which in turn accelerates modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic is further influenced by institutional factors that determine how resources are allocated and utilized within an economy.

Institutions also play a critical role in addressing distributional conflicts and ensuring economic efficiency. Ogilvie challenges the traditional view of pre-industrial institutions as inefficient cartels, instead arguing that they often provided necessary security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve to meet changing economic conditions.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional support is complex but essential for long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the need for institutions that facilitate technological advancements and ensure equitable distribution of resources.

The historical evidence from former European colonies provides a clear example of how institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly impacted economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that these institutional factors account for a substantial fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This empirical comparison underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, settling any remaining disagreement about their importance.
