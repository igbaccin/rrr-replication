The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of economic development debates. At stake are fundamental understandings of how societies progress and why some economies thrive while others stagnate. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973 (Kuznets 1973, p.4), Abramovitz 1986 (Abramovitz 1986, p.4)) or if they are themselves the driving force behind economic growth, a position that challenges neoclassical theories emphasizing population and savings as principal determinants. (North 1989 (North 1989, p.5)). The debate extends to the nature of institutions: some scholars argue that distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns shape core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.7)), while others highlight the importance of institutional progress in sustaining technological advancements. This tension underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of this interplay is crucial for comprehending long-term economic development. Institutions are active agents that can either propel or hinder economic progress.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. This effect is mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, which were designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that these institutions were not conducive to economic development because they were crafted by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over broader economic gains. This historical legacy has had lasting impacts on economic performance, with regions that experienced high mortality during colonization still bearing the institutional scars today. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further underscore the role of institutions in the process of economic growth and industrialization, suggesting that the institutional framework established during colonial times continues to influence current income differences. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions affect economic outcomes remain complex and multifaceted.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) both assert that the complexity of institutional effects on economic outcomes can be attributed to distributional conflicts within pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) challenges the prevailing view of merchant guilds as inefficient cartels, arguing instead that early merchant guilds were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as club goods. This perspective is shared by Domar (1970, p.7), who posits that slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, suggesting a different set of economic conditions for their emergence. A key mechanism in this distributional conflict is the political power of elites, which shapes institutions to maintain their advantages. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) provide evidence that New World societies with greater inequality saw institutions advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth may be less about efficiency and more about maintaining power structures. However, this does not negate the broader impact of institutions on economic outcomes; rather, it highlights the complexity of the relationship between distributional conflicts and institutional design. The political power of elites can shape institutions to maintain their advantages, but these institutions also have downstream effects on economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) together with North (1989, p.6) (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), focus on institutional changes introduced by colonialism but do not directly address long-run economic growth as an outcome. They highlight the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, particularly through their role in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms should, according to North (1989, p.6), lead to a decrease in costs per transaction while increasing the overall volume of exchanges. This institutional framework is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as evidenced by the success of capital markets during this period. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that these institutions complement reputation-building strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). However, while these institutional changes are significant, they do not necessarily guarantee long-run economic growth. The effectiveness of these institutions in promoting growth depends on various factors, including the degree to which the regime is committed to and bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This commitment is crucial for maintaining the stability and predictability necessary for sustained economic development.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) highlight that specific historical events and technological changes played pivotal roles in shaping regional economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck's study of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains illustrates how technological innovations could enhance agricultural productivity by securing property rights and mitigating livestock damage, which in turn fostered economic growth. This phenomenon was about technology and the institutional framework that allowed for the effective use of new tools. The introduction of barbed wire enabled farmers to better protect their investments and reduce externalities, demonstrating how technological change can interact with institutions to drive economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Similarly, Pamuk's analysis of the Black Death in Europe shows that demographic shocks could lead to long-term structural changes, such as increased wages relative to rents. This wage gap facilitated a substitution of land and capital for labor, driving technological innovations particularly within urban guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Temin (2002, p.4) further underscores this by noting how economic growth can be driven by disequilibria in labor allocation between sectors, highlighting the importance of structural changes in propelling long-run economic development (Temin 2002, p.4).

The interaction between technological change and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding regional economic development. Hornbeck's findings on barbed wire illustrate that while technology can drive productivity gains, its effectiveness depends on the institutional context that supports property rights and reduces externalities. This interplay is further supported by Pamuk's analysis of the Black Death, which shows how demographic shifts can alter economic structures, leading to technological advancements (Pamuk 2007, p.22). However, Temin qualifies this perspective by emphasizing that rapid growth periods, such as Europe's post-WWII Golden Age, are often driven by broader structural changes in labor allocation rather than isolated technological or institutional factors alone (Temin 2002, p.3). This suggests that while specific historical events and technologies can spur regional economic development, their impact is mediated through complex interactions with broader economic structures.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are fundamental drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Komlos 1998, p.8). Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological improvements often rely on existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process is about individual genius and the collective knowledge base and institutional support. Komlos (1998, p.8) adds that while technological advancements spur growth, they do not always translate into immediate improvements in biological well-being; relative price effects and nutritional deficiencies can offset these gains. This is evident in the divergence of life expectancy between upper and lower income groups during the Industrial Revolution (Komlos 1998, p.8). Despite rapid economic growth, inadequate income distribution and poor childhood nourishment led to a decline in overall well-being. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further notes that technological progress often depends on institutional frameworks that support invention and innovation. However, Komlos (1998, p.8) qualifies this by showing that the benefits of technological progress are not uniformly distributed; relative price effects can lead to shifts in consumption patterns that do not necessarily improve nutritional outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.12). This underscores the importance of considering both technological advancements and their distributional implications when examining long-run economic growth. Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating technological progress, but their effectiveness is contingent on addressing broader socio-economic factors.

Economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements and institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets and Abramovitz both argue that the dynamism of economic growth is intrinsically linked to technological innovation. Kuznets highlights that technological innovations are often tailored to specific national needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention. This perspective underscores the idea that technological progress is a process deeply rooted in local contexts (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Abramovitz extends this by noting that rapid growth of the capital stock and productivity gains are closely tied to technological opportunities. These advancements reduce the price of capital goods relative to labor and stimulate broader economic modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

This linkage between technology and economic growth is further complicated by the role of social change, which Abramovitz identifies as a critical factor in the catch-up process. He argues that backwardness itself can present opportunities for modernization, both in embodied and disembodied technologies. Kuznets qualifies this by emphasizing the need for institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations. This highlights a crucial condition: technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional changes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they facilitate the efficient use of technological advancements and ensure their integration into the broader economic framework. Kuznets emphasizes that while technological progress is necessary for economic growth, it must be accompanied by institutional adjustments to fully leverage its potential (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This perspective aligns with Abramovitz's view that technological opportunities drive rapid modernization, which in turn accelerates capital stock growth and reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor costs (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.10). By reducing technological age towards chronological age, institutions enable a more efficient economic structure.

Institutions also play a crucial role in mitigating coordination failures that can hinder economic progress. Mokyr highlights that without institutional advancements to complement technological improvements, the sustainability of economic growth would be limited by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This aligns with North's argument that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The historical evidence from various contexts underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth. For instance, Hornbeck shows how barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This finding resonates with Kuznets's observation that technological advancements, when efficiently utilized through institutional support, can drive significant economic improvements (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

Institutions are active shapers of economic growth. They provide the framework within which technological innovations can be effectively implemented and sustained over the long term. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial European institutions illustrates how these structures were designed to address specific economic challenges, thereby enhancing efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This shared finding (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5) underscores that institutions are integral to the process of economic growth. There remains disagreement on whether distributional conflicts or efficiency concerns primarily drive institutional changes. Ogilvie argues for a focus on distributional conflicts while North emphasizes efficiency as the core driver (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing settles this debate by showing that efficient property rights enforcement, rather than distributional conflicts, was the key factor in enhancing agricultural development.
