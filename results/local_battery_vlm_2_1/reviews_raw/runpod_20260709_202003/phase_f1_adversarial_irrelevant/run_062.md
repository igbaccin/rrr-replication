The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is our understanding of how societies evolve economically over centuries and what factors drive sustained prosperity. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement mechanisms, determine economic outcomes. One prominent position emphasizes that secure property rights and effective governance, underpinned by robust political institutions, are crucial for fostering growth. This view contends that credible commitment from political regimes is essential for maintaining stable economic environments conducive to investment and innovation. Meanwhile, another perspective highlights the importance of institutional changes introduced through colonialism in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies, suggesting that extractive institutions can hinder long-term development. These differing views underscore the complexity of institutional dynamics and their multifaceted impacts on economic growth over extended periods.

The evolution of political institutions governing public choice is pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) by ensuring that political regimes adhere to rules, thereby mitigating the risks associated with expropriation or arbitrary rule changes. Broadberry and Gupta extend this argument by demonstrating how institutional factors contribute to productivity gains in both tradable and non-tradable sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). They emphasize that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties in increasingly complex economic environments, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering growth.

Institutions matter significantly in shaping long-run economic growth. North contends that the evolution of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms can reduce transaction costs per exchange (North 1989, p.6). This reduction is crucial as specialization and division of labor increase, multiplying aggregate volume of exchanges.

The role of political institutions extends beyond economic exchange to encompass broader governance structures that shape long-term growth trajectories. North and Weingast argue that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints precisely where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective underscores the endogenous role of political institutions in fostering stable economic environments conducive to investment and innovation.

Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-run growth in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights how institutional legacies can persist and influence economic outcomes over extended periods.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-term economic outcomes in former colonies by influencing the design of political and economic systems that maximized rent extraction for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive institutions were established to maximize rents, promoting long-run growth. This is evident in the correlation between high mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies and the subsequent development of extractive institutions, which persistently affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The resulting institutional frameworks often prioritized the interests of colonizers over those of the indigenous populations, leading to economic structures that hindered growth. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies, underscoring the enduring influence of colonial legacies on contemporary economic performance.

Sokoloff and Engerman show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by creating barriers to entry for new participants. The elite groups, who controlled these institutions, were able to maintain their status by exploiting the labor force and resources at their disposal. This dynamic was further exacerbated by the lack of property rights security for non-elites, which prevented them from accumulating wealth or investing in productive activities.

The impact of inequality on institutional development is evident in the way that factor endowments influenced societal structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to high living standards but also created conditions for extreme inequality. This inequality was perpetuated by institutions that favored the elite, who controlled access to these resources and maintained their power through political and economic means.

Hornbeck shows how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This technological innovation enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. It also illustrates how institutions that secure property rights can lead to increased productivity and economic growth, as seen in the substantial increase in crop yields during this period.

The implications of these findings are profound for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The persistence of inequality in societies with extractive institutions highlights the need for policies that promote inclusive economic development. By ensuring that all members of society have access to opportunities and resources, institutions can foster a more equitable distribution of wealth and power, leading to sustained economic growth over the long term.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Whatley (2018, p.2) examine specific factors such as coal and gunpowder imports, highlighting their roles in economic development. Clark and Jacks estimate that the total factor productivity gains in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution were modest at best, suggesting that coal played a negligible role in driving this period of significant growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This mechanism underscores how specific technological advancements can drive economic changes, even if they are not institutional in nature. Clark and Jacks's analysis also shows that the assumption of large unexploited reserves of coal indicates deeper mining was required to reach the same quality reserves over time (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This pattern suggests that while specific factors like coal imports may have limited direct impact on economic growth, they can influence long-run economic development through their effects on resource extraction and technological adaptation. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding how external factors can shape economic activities in ways that institutions alone cannot fully capture.

The divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by factors such as arbitrage and wage equalization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Pamuk 2007, p.8). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the integration of international markets led to a single price for tradable goods measured in silver, which in turn influenced wages and productivity across regions. This arbitrage mechanism suggests that economic forces beyond institutional differences played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.8) provides further evidence by highlighting how the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to wage gaps between different regions of Europe by 1450. The linkages between urban and rural sectors, influenced by productivity disparities, contributed to these wage differences (Pamuk 2007, p.15). Pamuk also notes that the skill premium began to decline under conditions of severe labor shortages following the Black Death, indicating a complex interplay between demographic shifts and economic outcomes. This pattern suggests that while arbitrage and wage equalization were significant drivers of early divergence, other factors such as demographic changes and productivity disparities also played pivotal roles in shaping long-run economic growth. The implications of these findings are profound for understanding how external shocks and market integration can influence economic development independently of institutional frameworks.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both challenge conventional views of economic development by demonstrating that periods of growth did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living in Europe. Van Zanden critiqued optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, noting the complex interplay between productivity gains and stagnating real wages. The optimists' claims of sustained economic progress from 1580 to 1670 were questioned by those who highlighted long-term declines in real wages despite advances in total factor productivity (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This discrepancy suggests that while economic growth was occurring, it did not necessarily improve the living standards of the broader population. Komlos further elaborates on this disconnect by examining the Industrial Revolution, showing that biological well-being actually declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). The decline in total factor productivity during the 1840s underscores how economic growth can decouple from improvements in living standards.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is crucial to understanding why some economies develop while others stagnate. North and Weingast show that the evolution of political institutions governing property rights was instrumental for England's economic growth during the seventeenth century (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This argument complements North's broader claim that institutions matter by specifying how efficient property rights can reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The institutional framework in early modern Europe allowed for secure property rights and effective enforcement, which fostered economic growth through increased market exchanges. The development of credible commitments underpins this process.

The relationship between economic development and living standards is intricate. Van Zanden demonstrates that there was no clear correlation between economic development and improvements in living standards during 1500-1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This finding aligns with Clark and Jacks' analysis, which shows modest productivity gains in coal mining despite the Industrial Revolution. Van Zanden's work on real wages and total factor productivity reveals that economic growth does not guarantee better living standards; other factors such as income distribution and access to nutrition are pivotal (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This is consistent with Clark and Jacks' findings: the modest gains in total factor productivity during this period indicate that economic development alone does not ensure improvements in living conditions (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31).

Comparative studies further illuminate how institutions shape long-run growth. Broadberry and Gupta show that Europe's divergence from Asia began before 1800, driven by institutional differences (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). They argue that lower productivity in the tradable sector of Asian economies was a key factor. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-run growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies underscores how extractive institutions can impede economic development.

The precise role of institutions in shaping economic growth is thus settled by the empirical evidence from England and its colonies.
