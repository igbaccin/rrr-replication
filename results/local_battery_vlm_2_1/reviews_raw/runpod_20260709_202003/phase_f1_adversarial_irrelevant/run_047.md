The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages economists and historians alike, as it speaks to the fundamental drivers of societal prosperity over extended periods. At stake intellectually is an understanding of whether and how stable frameworks for social interaction, such as property rights, legal systems, and political structures, actively foster or impede economic development. This debate pits those who view institutions as critical determinants against those who see other factors, like technology or geography, as primary drivers. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitate growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable environment for investment and innovation; others contend that technological progress and the efficient use of resources are the key ingredients, with institutional adjustments playing a secondary role. Others propose that distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions. The tension between these views underscores the need to explore how institutions interact with other factors to influence long-run economic growth. This question is especially pertinent for understanding historical patterns of development and for informing contemporary policy debates about fostering sustained economic progress.

The central claim of Kuznets (1973, p.10) is that technological advancements are the primary drivers of modern economic growth, while institutional adjustments facilitate efficient use of these innovations. Institutions matter because they adapt to and support the mass application of technological innovations, which in turn stimulate further scientific and technological advances (Kuznets 1973, p.4). North (1989, p.6) agrees with this position: institutions influence long-run economic growth by shaping transaction costs through property rights and enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges. Together, these perspectives highlight how institutions support technological progress and economic growth by creating an environment conducive to innovation and efficient resource use.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological progress is driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and requires institutional support for sustainability. This view aligns with Kuznets's perspective on the importance of technological innovation but adds a layer of complexity by highlighting the role of institutions in preventing the dissipation of economic gains through rent seeking. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further develop this idea by showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can stifle long-run growth, as seen in former European colonies where colonial institutions were extractive rather than growth-promoting (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the nature of institutions, whether they support broad-based economic development or serve narrow interests, is crucial in determining their impact on long-run economic growth.

A rival explanation for the role of institutions in shaping economic growth comes from Ogilvie (2007, p.7), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions. She contends that many pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were shaped by power struggles rather than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective challenges the view that institutions are primarily designed to solve coordination or transaction problems efficiently; instead, it posits that these institutions often reflect the interests of dominant groups. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) offers a specific instance where technological change, a novel fencing technology, enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This case study supports the idea that institutional changes, particularly those related to property rights, can have significant economic impacts. It also underscores the importance of considering how technological innovations interact with institutional frameworks to drive growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They highlight the importance of political institutions in enforcing rules governing economic exchange. This enforcement mechanism involves setting rules and ensuring their effective implementation and modification over time. The development of efficient property rights and their enforcement through political institutions is pivotal in this regard.

North and Weingast emphasize that the credibility of these institutions lies in their ability to commit to rules ex ante, thereby preventing reneging on bargains with constituents. This commitment mechanism ensures that the state can maintain its part of agreements, such as not expropriating wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The Glorious Revolution serves as a historical example where institutional changes bolstered economic growth by providing credible commitments and enforcing property rights.

North (1989, p.6) also notes that neoclassical theories, which focus on inputs like land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, offer an incomplete picture of economic output (North 1989, p.5). Institutions play a complementary role in enhancing the efficacy of reputation mechanisms by acting as constraints where reputation alone is insufficient. This interplay between institutions and economic growth underscores the multifaceted nature of development, where institutional frameworks are not just supportive but essential for long-term economic prosperity.

Building on this, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. Extractive institutions, in turn, stunted economic development and industrialization. These institutions were actively shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers. Acemoglu et al (2002) note that European settlers in areas with low mortality rates, such as North America and Australia, established more inclusive institutions. These institutions fostered economic growth by encouraging investment, innovation, and productivity gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This pattern is evident in the stark differences in economic development between former colonies with extractive institutions, like those in Latin America and Africa, and those with more inclusive ones. The variation in institutional quality across former colonies underscores the profound impact of colonial legacy on long-run economic growth.

Institutional structures in pre-industrial Europe are better explained by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that key economic institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped more by power struggles between social groups than by any desire to enhance productivity or efficiency. She shows that the merchant guilds of early medieval Europe provided security and contract enforcement as club goods, which benefited their members at the expense of outsiders. This perspective is supported by Domar (1970, p.7), who explains slavery as a response to high land-to-labor ratios and scarce labor, rather than cultural or political factors. The economic efficiency of these institutions was not the primary driver of their emergence; instead, they were tools used by dominant groups to maintain control over resources and labor. This approach suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires looking beyond institutional efficiency. This indicates that even when conditions change, institutions may persist if they continue to serve the interests of powerful groups. The persistence of institutions rooted in distributional conflicts highlights their role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, but it also solidified elite control over resources. Sokoloff and Engerman further elaborate on this dynamic by noting how institutions in societies with greater inequality were shaped to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional framework restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The mechanisms through which these institutions operated are complex, involving not just legal frameworks but also social norms and economic structures that reinforced elite dominance. This suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is mediated by broader structural factors and technological advancements.

Kuznets and Mokyr both underscore that technological progress alone is insufficient; it must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure sustainable economic improvement. Technological advancements often rely on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel solutions (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process highlights the interconnectedness of knowledge and innovation, where the existence of certain technologies can alert inventors to new opportunities. This positive feedback loop underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that support both scientific and technological development.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and scientific advancements. Mokyr highlights the importance of institutional frameworks that support both scientific and technological development (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets elaborates on this idea by emphasizing the close link between mass application of technological innovations and further progress in science, which fuels additional technological advances (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Institutional frameworks also reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. North emphasizes that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs per unit (North 1989, p.6). This reduction is crucial because it enables increasing specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges.

The efficiency of institutions can be understood through their role in addressing distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions provided security and contract enforcement, which were essential for economic growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrating how political power and institutional design can influence long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the positive feedback loop between technological progress and scientific advancements. Mokyr notes that without concurrent institutional progress, technological gains may be limited or even reversed due to rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further elaborates on this idea by emphasizing that mass application of technological innovations is closely linked with scientific progress, which in turn fuels additional technological advances (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement is found in the introduction of barbed wire fencing. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This example illustrates how institutional changes can facilitate technological progress and economic growth.
