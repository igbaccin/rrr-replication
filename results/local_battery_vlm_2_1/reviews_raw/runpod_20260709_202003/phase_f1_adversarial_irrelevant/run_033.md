The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in development economics and economic history. Institutions are defined as the rules that govern human interaction, including formal rules like laws and informal norms; they structure incentives and shape economic behaviour over time. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. Scholars debate whether institutions are a primary driver of long-term growth or merely one factor among many. One live disagreement is between those who argue that institutions directly cause growth, and others who propose that institutions are themselves shaped by deeper structural factors like geography or colonial history. Another key debate focuses on the nature of institutional change: some scholars emphasize endogenous processes driven by political dynamics, while others highlight exogenous shocks as catalysts for institutional evolution.

Institutions matter in economic growth because they shape incentives and provide a framework for market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). Credible commitment underpins growth, and institutions that enforce property rights facilitate impersonal exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). These claims are substantiated by historical analyses of political changes that led to secure property rights in England; the Glorious Revolution of 1688 is often cited as a pivotal moment where institutional changes fostered economic growth by improving the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This political stability allowed for the development of capital markets and the enforcement of contracts, which are crucial for long-term economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta argue that institutional changes in Europe were already well underway before 1800, suggesting that these factors were instrumental in shaping the great divergence between Europe and Asia (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This divergence is evident in the productivity gains realized through efficient property rights and market exchanges.

This institutional foundation facilitates market exchanges by reducing transaction costs and enhancing confidence in remote interactions. North elaborates on this mechanism, noting that as economies grow more complex, the reliability of these institutions becomes essential to manage uncertainties and ensure successful transactions even among strangers. The evolution of such political institutions, which specify efficient property rights, is therefore critical for fostering economic growth. These institutional frameworks are necessary but insufficient on their own. North argues that reputational mechanisms must complement these institutions to fully mitigate incentive problems and prevent reneging on agreements (North 1989, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent upon the specific institutional changes introduced during colonialism, which significantly impacted current income per capita. These scholars identify a direct link between European mortality rates and the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, establishing a mechanism where the settler mortality rate influenced the type of institutions implemented by colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This mechanism stems from the political economy of colonial rule, where colonizers designed institutions to maximize their rents and foster long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) demonstrate that these extractive institutions persist over time, continuing to affect economic outcomes even after the colonial period has ended. This persistence suggests that the initial institutional framework laid down by colonizers creates a path-dependent trajectory for economic development.

Van Zanden shows that the relationship between economic development and changes in living standards during Europe's early modern period is complex and often counterintuitive (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). The period from 1500 to 1800 saw significant economic growth, yet this did not uniformly translate into improved living standards. For instance, despite advancements in agricultural productivity and urbanization, real wages and biological well-being often declined or stagnated. Komlos underscores this by noting that during the Industrial Revolution, while aggregate purchasing power increased, biological indicators such as height and life expectancy worsened due to poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). This suggests that economic growth alone does not guarantee improvements in living standards; other factors, such as income distribution and nutritional intake, play crucial roles. The failure of early modern Europe's economic development to consistently improve living standards highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how different aspects of economic activity interact with broader social and biological indicators.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) (Austin 2008, p.11) both examine historical economic trajectories without explicitly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth. They focus on the 'great divergence' between Europe and Asia before 1800, attributing it to differences in labour productivity and wage rates rather than institutional factors. Broadberry and Gupta establish that the lower silver wages in Asia reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Austin further argues that economic rent and growth often occur together, but the institutions fostering growth in one context may not be optimal in another (Austin 2008, p.1). This suggests that the institutional environment plays a crucial role in shaping economic paths. The divergence between Europe and Asia can thus be understood through the lens of varying productivity and wage structures, which were influenced by the specific institutional contexts of each region. This highlights how institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing labour productivity and wage rates, even if they are not explicitly addressed as the primary drivers.

In contrast to this, the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that this is due to extreme inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power. These societies began with an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, leading to high living standards on average; however, other aspects of their factor endowments varied significantly. This variation meant that most New World economies were characterized by extreme inequality from the outset, which influenced the evolution of institutions in ways that perpetuated these inequalities.

Hornbeck (2010, p.34) illustrates how such institutional frameworks can affect economic development through specific mechanisms. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural productivity. This technological innovation enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, demonstrating how secure property rights can drive economic growth. This mechanism's applicability depends on the specific institutional context and the ability of institutions to adapt to new technologies and changing economic conditions.

This suggests that while secure property rights can foster economic development, the effectiveness of such institutions is contingent upon the broader social and political environment in which they operate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with secure property rights being a critical factor. North and Weingast highlight that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework reduced uncertainties by providing reliable enforcement mechanisms, which were essential for facilitating market exchanges and encouraging long-term investments (North 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7).

The relationship between property rights and economic development is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes, showing that institutions matter not just for their immediate effects but also for their long-term implications on growth.

While secure property rights are crucial, they alone do not guarantee economic development. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, indicating that institutional factors were at play even in pre-industrial economies (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions depends on broader social, political, and economic contexts.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin's critique highlights that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are best understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment (Austin 2008, p.11). This indicates that economic growth is influenced by a complex interplay of institutional factors, technological innovations, and environmental conditions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is grounded in their ability to reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. North shows that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, which in turn drives economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. North's work shows that the development of efficient products and factor markets, along with a reliable medium of exchange, are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence settling this disagreement is the institutional changes introduced by colonialism in former European colonies, which provide empirical support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization.
