Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing technological progress and resource allocation. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect growth. One stream posits that institutions matter because they facilitate the efficient use of innovations (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). A rival stream argues that distributional conflicts shape institutions and drive growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). Another stream asserts that technological progress and social change are the key drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Understanding these dynamics is crucial for appreciating how institutions can either foster or hinder sustained economic development.

North (1989, p.6) and North & Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement. This perspective highlights the role of political institutions in creating an environment conducive to market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs per exchange. The emergence of such political institutions leads to a growing proportion of transactions relative to GNP, as specialization and division of labor increase the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that these institutions complement reputation-building strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by constraining behavior in situations where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework underpins economic growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and enforced. However, North (1989, p.6) also notes that neoclassical theories focusing on population and savings as principal determinants can be misleading, as they do not account for the critical role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.5).

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the advancement of technology and useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr elaborates on this point by explaining how technological progress often depends on existing techniques and "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process of technological advancement involves more than individual inventions; it also involves the cumulative growth of knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.10) reinforces this idea by highlighting that modern economic growth is closely linked to the mass application of technological innovations, which in turn are based on recent scientific discoveries (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be irreversible and monotonic, institutional progress is far less certain. He suggests that without complementary institutional advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements could be limited by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that although technology drives growth, institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that this growth is sustained over the long term. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further clarifies that technological progress requires ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4), suggesting that while institutions may not be the primary drivers, they are necessary for harnessing the full potential of technological advancements. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intertwined with the advancement of technology and knowledge, even if it is not the sole determinant.

This is further supported by Komlos and Abramovitz who both underscore the complexity of economic growth, highlighting factors such as technological progress and social transformation (Komlos 1998, p.8; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This complexity is evident in the catch-up process of economic growth, where modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock due to returns from modernization itself and the reduction of capital goods' relative price. Furthermore, the dynamics of technological progress and social change create a broader horizon for scale-dependent technological advancement. However, Komlos shows that despite these advances, biological well-being can decline during periods of economic growth due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in outcomes underscores the intricate relationship between economic growth and social welfare, where technological advancements do not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. While Abramovitz points out that the acceleration in capital stock growth can reduce its chronological age (Abramovitz 1986, p.4), Komlos's findings suggest that this acceleration does not always lead to improved biological well-being, especially when relative prices of nutrients increase and consumers substitute industrial commodities for food products (Komlos 1998, p.8). This qualitative divergence highlights the need to consider multiple dimensions of economic growth and social change beyond aggregate measures such as GDP.

Historical events significantly influenced societal structures by shaping economic and social institutions in ways that affected long-run economic growth (Domar 1970, p.7; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Domar (1970, p.7) argues that the emergence of slavery or serfdom was driven by a high land-to-labor ratio, making labor scarce. This scarcity increased the profitability of slave labor, as seen in medieval England and American plantations, where agricultural techniques and capital investment were more intensive (Domar 1970, p.7). Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, leading to a wage gap between northwest Europe and other regions by 1450. This event triggered substitution of land and capital for labor, driven by rising wages relative to rents and the price of capital (Pamuk 2007, p.22). Substitution effects were not uniform across Europe; technological innovations, particularly in urban guilds, played a crucial role in this process (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Pamuk 2007, p.22). However, Domar (1970, p.7) notes that without technological progress and capital accumulation, the end of slavery becomes more certain as population growth drives down the marginal product of labor to subsistence levels (Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that while historical events like the Black Death and the emergence of slavery shaped societal structures, their impact on long-run economic growth was mediated by subsequent institutional developments and technological changes.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that technological innovations like barbed wire fencing played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by enhancing agricultural development on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). By enabling farmers to protect their property rights more effectively, barbed wire reduced externalities from livestock damage. This protection allowed farmers to invest more confidently in their land, leading to increased productivity and economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire thus exemplifies how institutional changes, driven by technological advancements, can facilitate long-term economic development. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks, suggesting that the benefits of innovations like barbed wire fencing are contingent on broader socio-economic dynamics. These dynamics can either amplify or mitigate the positive effects of technological changes on economic growth.

This is further explored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) who show that colonial institutions established by European settlers had a lasting impact on economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for colonists. This dynamic is evident in the high correlation between European mortality rates and the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with unfavorable conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that the design of colonial institutions was influenced by powerful groups seeking to maintain their political power and economic rents. This institutional legacy, shaped by historical factors such as European settlement patterns and mortality rates, continues to affect current income levels in former colonies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with technological progress. Institutions provide the framework that facilitates the application of new technologies, which are themselves a key driver of economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective is echoed by North, who contends that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms which lower transaction costs. As a result, the size of the transactions sector grows as increasing specialization multiplies the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5).

This emphasis on institutions is further supported by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts are fundamental to understanding core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie shows that pre-modern institutions were efficient solutions and responses to power dynamics and social structures. For instance, merchant guilds provided security and contract enforcement as club goods (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of colonial institutions, where powerful groups shaped institutional design to maintain political power and economic rents.

Abramovitz extends this discussion by highlighting that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and technological advance. He argues that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This perspective aligns with North's view on institutions, as it underscores how institutional frameworks can facilitate or hinder the adoption of new technologies. Mokyr reinforces this idea, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements would be unsustainable and might eventually be eliminated by rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The historical evidence from colonial institutions in former colonies illustrates how initial institutional design profoundly affects long-term economic trajectories. The extractive nature of these institutions has hindered development, showing that the role of institutions is not just about facilitating growth but also about determining its trajectory. This point is supported by Hornbeck's study on barbed wire fencing, which shows how technological change can enhance agricultural development when property rights are better protected (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The historical evidence from colonial institutions and technological changes in agriculture both show that the initial design of institutions and the protection of property rights are crucial for long-term economic growth.
