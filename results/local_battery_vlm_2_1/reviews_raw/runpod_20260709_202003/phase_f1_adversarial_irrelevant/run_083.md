The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate efficient use of resources by reducing transaction costs, or if they are shaped more by distributional conflicts and power dynamics. Some scholars emphasize the role of technological progress and institutional adjustments in driving economic growth, while others focus on the importance of property rights and enforcement mechanisms. Still others argue that institutions are better understood as responses to underlying social and political conflicts rather than as efficient solutions to economic problems.

The debate over how institutions influence long-run economic growth is further complicated by the question of whether technological progress alone can sustain economic improvement without concomitant institutional development. Some scholars suggest that technological advancements, while necessary, are insufficient on their own to ensure sustained growth. They argue that institutional progress must complement technological progress to prevent rent-seeking behaviors and ensure the sustainability of economic improvements.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that without institutional progress complementing technological advances, economic improvements may be limited by coordination failures and rent-seeking activities (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly posits that while technological innovations drive growth, they require institutional adjustments for efficient use. These findings suggest that institutions play a crucial role in harnessing the benefits of technological progress.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as North (1989, p.6) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shared finding (North 1989, p.6; Hornbeck 2010, p.30). Institutions matter beyond the neoclassical emphasis on population and savings. For instance, North (1989, p.6) posits that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges and encourages specialization and division of labor, thereby increasing aggregate volume of exchanges. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) complements this view by demonstrating how technological improvements in property protection, such as barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). However, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) qualify that while institutions are crucial, they must be appropriately chosen to complement reputation-building mechanisms and constrain ex post behavior of the state, thereby improving its ability to maintain bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The role of political institutions in fostering secure property rights is thus essential for sustained economic growth.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that these institutions were not merely efficient solutions to transactional problems, but also reflected the power dynamics and bargaining outcomes between different social groups. For example, merchant guilds in medieval Europe emerged as a means for merchants to secure their interests against competition from other traders and to protect their investments (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These guilds provided security and contract enforcement, acting as club goods that benefited their members at the expense of outsiders. This distributional perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always efficient solutions to economic problems; instead, it highlights how institutions can be shaped by the interests and conflicts of powerful groups. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates on this point by noting that technological innovations often arise from specific needs within a country (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The efficiency of these innovations is thus tied to the institutional frameworks that support them, which in turn are influenced by distributional conflicts. This suggests that although distributional conflicts shape economic institutions, these institutions must also adapt to changing conditions to remain effective.

Thus, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Domar 1970, p.7) focus on how inequality and institutional policies shaped development paths. They argue that in New World societies, institutions often favored elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in societies where large plantations dominated economies, leading to extreme wealth and human capital inequalities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Domar further elaborates on this by explaining how slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce. In such scenarios, technological progress and greater use of capital do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave as compared with free labor (Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that in societies where large plantations were prevalent, the efficiency gains from technological advancements might have been offset by the institutional structures that maintained inequality. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between technological change, labor dynamics, and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

However, Mokyr (2005, p.54); (Abramovitz 1986, p.4) highlight the growth of useful knowledge as the primary driver of economic growth, contrasting with institutional explanations. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological progress is driven by the accumulation and recombination of existing knowledge. This process often relies on analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) supports this view by noting that technological opportunities encourage rapid growth in capital stock. The dynamics of technological advance are not merely about new inventions but also about integrating existing techniques to create more efficient processes, a mechanism that can drive economic growth independently of institutional frameworks.

Mokyr questions the long-term viability of economic gains driven exclusively by technological advancements. He argues that without complementary institutional progress, technological gains might be limited or even reversed due to rent-seeking behavior and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This qualification underscores the importance of institutions in sustaining long-run economic growth, even if they are not the primary drivers. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) adds complexity by suggesting that broader horizons of scale-dependent technological progress can also influence productivity growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The interplay between technological advancement and institutional frameworks is nuanced; while knowledge growth may initiate economic improvement, institutions play a crucial role in maintaining it over the long term.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) identify colonial institutions as pivotal in shaping the long-run economic trajectories of former European colonies. They argue that extractive institutions established during colonial periods were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, a pattern reinforced by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo. These institutions, shaped by European settlers' mortality rates and settlement patterns, persisted over time and significantly influenced current income levels in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that differences in European mortality rates led to divergent institutional outcomes; high mortality areas saw the establishment of extractive institutions which hindered economic development. This finding underscores how initial institutional frameworks, rooted in colonial policies, laid the groundwork for enduring economic disparities. The legacy of colonial institutions thus remains complex and multifaceted, influencing both the stagnation and eventual economic resurgence of former colonies.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and managing transaction costs. Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is a key driver of economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Technological progress often depends on existing knowledge and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets agrees with this perspective, emphasizing that technological advancements are closely linked to scientific progress. He argues that the mass application of technological innovations provides positive feedback for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The dynamics of technological change and social adjustments are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Abramovitz highlights that modernization encourages rapid growth in both capital stocks and productivity, which in turn accelerates technological progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This process is not merely about the adoption of new technologies but also involves broader institutional and ideological adjustments. Kuznets adds that these adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations, as they help manage transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Institutional frameworks significantly influence economic outcomes by shaping property rights and enforcing contracts. North contends that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework reduces transaction costs, allowing for greater specialization and division of labor, which are essential for economic development. Ogilvie's work underscores this by showing how various pre-modustrial European institutions, such as merchant guilds and craft guilds, were efficient solutions to specific economic challenges (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions provided security and contract enforcement, which were crucial for economic activity.

The legacy of colonial institutions further illustrates the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic growth. Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights how initial institutional frameworks laid the groundwork for enduring economic disparities. These institutions also significantly contributed to industrialization in previously impoverished colonies (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This paradox shows that while extractive institutions may initially impede growth, they can paradoxically stimulate later development through structural changes. The historical evidence from colonial policies and their long-term effects on economic development provides clear support for this perspective.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex but undeniable. Both North and Kuznets agree that institutions are fundamental to economic progress (North 1989, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.10). The primary disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. North emphasizes the importance of property rights and enforcement, while Kuznets focuses on technological advancements and scientific progress. Historical evidence from colonial policies and their long-term effects on economic development settles this disagreement by showing that both mechanisms are at play. Institutions facilitate technological progress by providing the necessary framework for innovation and efficient use of resources.
