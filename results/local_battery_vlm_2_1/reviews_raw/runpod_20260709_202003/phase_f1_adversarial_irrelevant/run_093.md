The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that lies at the heart of debates over the drivers of prosperity and poverty worldwide. At stake intellectually are the competing explanations for why some countries thrive economically while others stagnate. On one side stands the view that institutions, particularly those governing property rights and public choice, are pivotal in fostering secure economic environments that encourage growth. This perspective is contested by arguments that prioritize other factors such as population dynamics or geographic endowments as primary determinants of economic development. The disagreement hinges on whether institutional frameworks are the linchpin of long-run economic success or merely one piece of a complex puzzle where other variables hold greater sway. Austin (2008, p.11) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) agree on the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, but diverge in their interpretations of how these institutions influence growth over time.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice are essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.2). They assert that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that rules are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon procedures. This institutional reliability is crucial because it allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence, even when they lack personal knowledge of their trading partners. North (1989, p.6) similarly emphasizes the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). He contends that as economies grow more specialized, the volume of transactions increases, making efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms vital for economic development. Broadberry and Gupta complement this view by highlighting how institutional frameworks can influence productivity and wages in both tradable and non-tradable sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23; van Zanden 1999, p.2). They argue that lower silver wages in Asia reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector, suggesting that institutional differences may have contributed to the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800.

Their work on seventeenth-century England highlights how political institutions can enhance the efficacy of reputation mechanisms by constraining state behavior, thereby improving the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. This institutional evolution was pivotal for economic development as it facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. These institutions enable individuals to engage in impersonal exchange with confidence, which is crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with increasing specialization and division of labor. Thus, the institutional framework that evolved during the Glorious Revolution was instrumental in promoting economic growth by providing a reliable medium of exchange and efficient markets.

Extending this idea globally, Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The authors find that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and had a lasting impact on economic development. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth. This is because politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, often resist institutional changes that could reduce their political power or fail to directly benefit them economically (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). In the context of former colonies, this meant that institutions were not geared towards fostering economic development but instead aimed at extracting resources for the colonizers. This extractive nature of colonial institutions had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as these institutions did not encourage productivity gains or technological advancements.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.3). This institutional framework fostered the persistence of inequality by limiting opportunities for social mobility and economic advancement for the broader population. The specific focus on how the different environments in which Europeans established their colonies led to societies with very different degrees of inequality is crucial. These differences persisted over time and affected the course of development through their impact on institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Nunn demonstrates that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, complementing the findings of the New World context where slavery also resulted in the evolution of institutions not conducive to economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.16; Nunn 2008, p.3). The mechanisms by which these institutional frameworks operated are varied. For example, they include the restriction of competition and the concentration of wealth and political power in the hands of a few. These factors collectively shaped long-run economic trajectories, as seen in the divergent paths of development across different regions. This points to the broader implications of institutional inequality on economic development, highlighting how historical legacies can have enduring effects on contemporary economic outcomes.

Hornbeck (2010, p.34) and Whatley (2018, p.2) examine how technological advancements impacted economic development in distinct ways that only indirectly relate to the role of institutions. Hornbeck demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural productivity on the American Plains, enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and mitigate livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed for more efficient land use and increased crop yields, highlighting how new technologies can drive economic growth by addressing specific challenges in resource management. Whatley's study (Whatley 2018, p.15) suggests that gunpowder imports played a significant role in increasing slave exports through intertemporal production links and social processes, supporting the gun-slave cycle hypothesis. This technological advancement facilitated more efficient capture and transportation of slaves, illustrating how external factors can influence economic activities and development trajectories. These studies do not directly address the institutional frameworks that underpin long-run economic growth; they focus on the direct impacts of technology on specific economic sectors.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) share that economic growth during the Industrial Revolution was accompanied by a decline in biological well-being due to inadequate income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This finding is consistent with the broader observation that despite significant economic progress, improvements in living standards did not keep pace with economic development. One specific mechanism through which this occurred is via relative price effects: as technological change and capital accumulation lagged in agriculture compared to industry, the relative price of nutrients increased (Komlos 1998, p.8; van Zanden 2009, p.27). This shift led consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns, substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) jointly argue that economic divergence between regions is driven by complex historical paths shaped by colonial policies and economic dynamics. They show how these forces influenced economic outcomes in Africa and Asia through distinct mechanisms. Austin (2008, p.11) posits that the Atlantic slave trade was motivated by commercial interests, which led to a lower exchange-value product of Africans' labor in Africa compared to their destinations (Austin 2008, p.11). This suggests that labour scarcity in Africa involved population density and specific aspects of the natural environment and choice of production techniques. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) further elucidate this by demonstrating how arbitrage led to a single international price for tradable goods, measured in silver (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This price equilibrium was influenced by labour productivity in both tradable and non-tradable sectors, with lower wages in Asia reflecting lower productivity in the tradable sector. This mechanism highlights how economic dynamics, driven by colonial policies, shaped regional economies differently. Austin (2008, p.11) qualifies this pattern by noting that economic rent and growth were often joint products, indicating that institutions favourable for growth in one context might not be optimal in another (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuance underscores the complexity of historical paths leading to economic divergence, suggesting that colonial policies and economic dynamics played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and reduce transaction costs. North and Weingast show that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth by providing secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange, which is essential for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North emphasizes that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are necessary to reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby enabling individuals to engage in impersonal exchanges with confidence (North 1989, p.2).

The importance of institutions in economic development is further highlighted by the work of Broadberry and Gupta. They argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in labour productivity and institutional frameworks (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This divergence suggests that institutions play a critical role in determining long-run economic outcomes by influencing productivity levels and economic growth trajectories.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the historical contexts in which these institutions operate. Austin argues that economic rent and growth were often joint products, indicating that institutions favourable for growth in one context might not be optimal in another (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuance underscores the complexity of historical paths leading to economic divergence. For example, colonial policies and economic dynamics shaped regional economies differently by influencing productivity levels and institutional frameworks.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and encourage long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights how institutions can have significant and lasting impacts on economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast show that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise impact of institutions on economic outcomes remains a subject of debate. Austin argues that the kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.1), suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and context-dependent. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of productivity levels across different regions and time periods, as shown by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of labour productivity in Europe and Asia before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).
