The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, as it addresses how societies transition from stagnation to sustained progress. At stake are the mechanisms that drive modern economic growth, such as technological advancement and institutional adaptation. The debate revolves around whether institutions facilitate or hinder growth by influencing resource allocation, innovation, and technological diffusion. One position argues that efficient institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce contracts, are essential for fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. Another perspective posits that institutions often reflect the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to extractive practices that stifle long-run growth. This tension highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, where the efficiency versus distributional conflict frameworks compete to explain institutional formation and change.

The neoclassical model describes the output of an economy as a function of inputs such as land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, given some production function derived from the state of technological knowledge (North 1989, p.5). This model misleads because it ignores institutions' role in economic growth. Institutions matter because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up as a development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This indicates that institutions are crucial for reducing transaction costs and increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges, which in turn drives economic growth. As Kuznets notes, technological progress is closely connected with the further progress of science, providing a positive feedback loop that enhances modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Technological opportunity permits rapid progress by modernization, encouraging rapid growth of the capital stock. This is because technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor. Additionally, backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied, as well as in embodied, technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This suggests that institutions play a critical role in facilitating the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, which are essential for economic growth.

Institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress for sustainable economic improvement. Without institutional progress, economic gains might be frittered away by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the importance of institutions in sustaining long-run economic growth by preventing the erosion of economic improvements. Technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use and analogical thinking, where inventors transform existing ideas into something novel (Mokyr 2005, p.12).

Institutions are not merely efficient solutions to transaction costs; they also reflect distributional conflicts within societies. The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, can be better explained by these conflicts and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective underscores that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and may not always promote efficient resource allocation. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and encourage long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay between efficiency and distributional conflicts. Institutions facilitate technological progress by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment, but they also reflect power dynamics that can lead to extractive practices. Understanding this dual nature is crucial for designing policies that promote sustainable economic growth.

Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They note that the establishment of extractive institutions was often a result of high European mortality rates, which made it difficult for Europeans to settle and establish productive institutions. This resulted in the creation of institutions designed to maximize rents for colonial powers and promote long-run economic development. These extractive institutions persisted over time, significantly impacting the current income per capita of former colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by their analysis that institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This historical institutional framework underscores the enduring effects of colonial policies on contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting how initial conditions set during colonization can have lasting implications. The persistence of these extractive institutions suggests that understanding the historical context is essential for grasping current economic disparities.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7); (Domar 1970, p.7) jointly establish that specific economic institutions like serfdom and craft guilds are shaped by distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations. Ogilvie posits that merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries provided security and contract enforcement as club goods, highlighting how these institutions were efficient in addressing specific problems within their historical context. Domar's analysis of slavery or serfdom emerging when the land-to-labor ratio is high complements this view, illustrating how institutional structures can be driven by economic conditions such as labor scarcity (Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective underscores that the persistence and evolution of these institutions are intrinsically linked to the broader economic landscape. The efficiency approach, which views institutions as solutions to obstacles in transactions, has been applied broadly across various pre-modern institutions. Domar's analysis qualifies this by showing how the profitability of slave labor can be influenced by technological progress and capital use, complicating a straightforward efficiency narrative (Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that while these institutions may have emerged to solve specific problems, their long-term impact on economic growth is nuanced and context-dependent.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Temin 2002, p.4) establish that economic growth is driven by technological advance and social change and institutional factors. Abramovitz's work highlights the complex interplay between technological opportunities and capital stock growth, suggesting that rapid modernization accelerates both productivity gains and capital accumulation. This dynamic reduces the relative price of capital goods and enhances the overall efficiency of resource allocation (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Temin extends this perspective by emphasizing the role of labor allocation in driving economic growth. He argues that the post-WWII Golden Age was characterized by a disequilibrium in labor distribution between agriculture and industry, which significantly contributed to Europe's unusually rapid economic expansion (Temin 2002, p.3). This view underscores how shifts in labor allocation can catalyze technological progress and economic development.

Temin also acknowledges that new growth theories provide extensions to old growth theory, complicating the picture by introducing multiple factors such as education and other inputs into the growth equation. The implication is that although institutions may not be the primary drivers, they can still shape the conditions under which technological progress and labor allocation occur.

The relationship between technological progress and institutional frameworks remains nuanced. On one hand, technological advancements can lead to structural changes that necessitate institutional adaptations. On the other hand, existing institutions can either facilitate or hinder the adoption of new technologies. This bidirectional influence highlights the importance of considering both technological and institutional factors in understanding long-run economic growth.

Economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Kuznets (1973, p.10) posits that economic growth is fundamentally tied to technological progress, arguing that the mass application of technological innovations constitutes much of modern economic growth. This perspective aligns with Mokyr's (2005, p.54) assertion that economic improvement requires institutional progress to complement technological advancements. The efficiency of these technological innovations is contingent on their effective integration into existing institutions. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, the institutional environment must evolve in tandem to ensure long-term sustainability of these gains.

This ability is crucial because it ensures that markets function efficiently and transactions occur with minimal friction. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.5) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show that the evolution of political institutions is crucial for specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn fosters economic growth. This institutional framework supports market exchanges and ensures that the costs per transaction decrease as the economy grows. The mechanism here is clear: as specialization and division of labor increase, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies, making efficient institutions vital for managing these transactions effectively. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) elaborate on this by highlighting how political institutions complement reputation-building strategies to prevent reneging on agreements, thereby enhancing the efficacy of economic exchanges.

This institutional stability is particularly important in contexts where reputation alone may not suffice to enforce agreements. The specificity of these institutions matters: they must be tailored to address the unique challenges posed by each historical context. The broader implication is that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, the institutional framework must evolve in tandem to ensure long-term sustainability of these gains.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth through their role in facilitating technological progress and ensuring credible commitments (North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Mokyr argues that the expansion of useful knowledge is essential for economic growth, but this process relies on institutions to address coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets supports this view by noting that technological advancements necessitate institutional adjustments for efficient use and sustainability (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.10). The Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies how institutional changes can foster an environment conducive to economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The specificity of these institutions matters: they must be tailored to address the unique challenges posed by each historical context. For example, Kuznets highlights that technological progress often depends on prior knowledge and analogical thinking, which in turn relies on effective institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further underscore this dynamic, showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Institutions also play a critical role in managing distributional conflicts and ensuring efficient use of resources. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms, which are essential for market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). The broader implication is that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, the institutional framework must evolve in tandem to ensure long-term sustainability of these gains. This point is reinforced by Abramovitz, who argues that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions are critical for shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring credible commitments (North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). There remains a disagreement over whether the primary driver of institutional change is efficiency or distributional conflicts. Kuznets shows that technological progress often depends on prior knowledge and analogical thinking, which in turn relies on effective institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North 1989, p.6), whereas Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the case of merchant guilds, which Ogilvie shows were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' in the tenth and eleventh centuries, but later became inefficient cartels by the twelfth to fifteenth centuries (Ogilvie 2007, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.7).
