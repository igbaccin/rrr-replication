The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is an issue of central importance in understanding global disparities in prosperity and development. At stake is whether the institutional framework of a society, its rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms, can act as a catalyst for sustained economic expansion, or if other factors such as technological innovation or resource endowments are more decisive. The debate centers on two primary positions: one emphasizes institutions as the principal driver of growth while the other highlights rival causes that operate independently of institutional frameworks. This disagreement is crucial because it determines how policymakers should prioritize their efforts in fostering economic development.

The first position, which underscores the role of institutions, is evident in the work of North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (Stern 2004, p.15). North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth. This view aligns with Stern's analysis of technological improvements and their impact on productivity. Stern argues that increases in total factor productivity (TFP) can lead to lower emissions per unit of output, indirectly supporting economic growth by reducing the environmental costs associated with production (Stern 2004, p.15). This perspective is further supported by van Zanden’s critique of optimistic interpretations of early modern European economic growth, which highlights the complex relationship between economic development and living standards. Van Zanden notes that while some studies suggest long-term productivity gains in agriculture, these do not necessarily translate into improved living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This complexity underscores the need for robust institutional frameworks to ensure that economic gains are broadly shared and sustainable.

The second position, which highlights rival causes of growth such as technological innovation or resource endowments, is exemplified by Clark and Jacks' analysis of coal mining during the Industrial Revolution. They argue that while technological improvements in coal extraction were modest, these gains were not the primary driver of economic growth during this period (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This perspective is complemented by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which suggests that the Atlantic slave trade and colonial exploitation played significant roles in shaping global economic disparities. Austin argues that the low exchange-value product of African labor was a key factor in the reversal of fortune, highlighting the importance of commercial motivations and resource endowments (Austin 2008, p.11). This view is further supported by North's argument that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby enabling economic growth. North emphasizes that efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms are crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2).

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled. While some scholars emphasize the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering sustained economic expansion, others highlight rival causes such as technological innovation or resource endowments. This disagreement underscores the need for further research to fully understand the complex interplay between institutions and other factors that drive economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.6) argue that political institutions governing public choice are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work on seventeenth-century England highlights how institutional changes can enhance the credibility of commitments to property rights, thereby facilitating market development. The Glorious Revolution, for instance, significantly altered the political landscape by curbing the monarchy's power to expropriate wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This shift enhanced the state's capacity to uphold its commitments to constituents and established a basis for economic growth grounded in secure property rights. The evolution of these institutions is pivotal because they complement reputation-building mechanisms, which alone are insufficient to prevent reneging on agreements. This institutional framework creates an environment where individuals can confidently engage in impersonal exchange, driving economic expansion (North 1989, p.2). The effectiveness of these institutions hinges on their capability to mitigate uncertainties tied to complex social frameworks, a prerequisite that North (1989, p.6) underscores as vital for achieving productivity gains from specialization and division of labor.

This historical context is crucial because Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal in explaining the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. These authors highlight that institutions established during colonial periods were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) finding that institutional differences significantly influenced the surge in industrialization and economic growth among formerly poor colonies, contributing substantially to current income disparities. The mechanism through which these institutions operated involved creating extractive frameworks that hindered productivity gains from specialization and division of labor. These extractive institutions persisted because they were designed to benefit politically powerful groups who had little incentive to promote long-run economic development in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al.'s findings are further corroborated by their own research which shows that mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This implies that historical factors such as settler mortality played a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks. The consequence is that these extractive institutions hindered economic growth during colonial times and left a lasting impression on the economic development paths of former colonies, illustrating how institutional legacies can exert persistent effects over centuries.

This critique underscores the complexity of economic development because it requires considering factors beyond institutions, which is evident in critiques that challenge simplistic views on institutional theories (Austin 2008, p.11; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For instance, Austin (2008, p.11) notes that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa indicates prevalent labour scarcity despite higher population density. This suggests that the institutional frameworks and economic conditions were not uniform across different regions and periods, implying that historical contexts significantly influenced economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) supports this perspective by arguing that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations. These institutions often emerged as solutions to specific local problems rather than universal efficiency goals (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The implication is that economic development cannot be solely attributed to institutional frameworks designed for efficiency; instead, it involves a complex interplay of historical, social, and political factors. This perspective challenges the notion that efficient institutions are universally beneficial, suggesting that different contexts require different solutions.

Moreover, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) emphasizes the interdependency of individual institutions within broader frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For example, the European family system was interconnected with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions. This interlinked institutional system contributed to early industrialization and capitalism in Europe. The complexity of these interdependencies underscores that economic development is a multifaceted process influenced by various factors beyond just institutions. Thus, understanding long-run economic growth requires considering the dynamic interplay of historical contexts, social structures, and political influences.

This finding aligns with the argument that Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) together argue that economic development in Europe did not always translate into improved living standards between 1500 and 1800. One mechanism for this decoupling is the uneven distribution of income gains during periods of growth, particularly among lower-income groups. This disparity led to insufficient improvements in biological well-being, as Komlos (1998, p.13) shows that the relative price effect movements in real income did not determine food consumption trends. Instead, rising prices for nutrients and a shift in consumption patterns towards cheaper carbohydrates exacerbated nutritional deficiencies.

The divergence in living standards is further complicated by regional variations in economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.8) demonstrates that long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to significant wage gaps between different regions of Europe by 1450, highlighting the role of demographic shifts in shaping economic outcomes. This historical example illustrates how exogenous shocks can alter the trajectory of economic growth and its impact on living standards, with regional disparities persisting due to variations in urban-rural linkages (Pamuk 2007, p.8). These patterns underscore that economic development is a complex process influenced by multiple factors beyond institutions alone; it involves intricate dynamics between demographic changes, income distribution, and consumption patterns.

Environmental and technological factors play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth by altering production processes and efficiency. Stern (2004, p.3) and Kuznets (1973, p.12; 1973, p.1) argue that technology and environmental changes can drive economic growth through improvements in productivity and emissions reductions, even when these changes are not the intended outcome. Technological advancements often lead to increased total factor productivity, resulting in lower emissions per unit of output as well as process-specific emission reductions. This mechanism is evident in how countries adopt best-practice technologies, which Stern (2004, p.15) describes as moving closer to an efficiency frontier that represents cutting-edge technological practices. The adoption of these technologies differs greatly among countries, with some falling behind due to inefficiencies or limited access to advanced production methods.

The implications of this variation are significant for economic growth trajectories. This points to a broader theme: while environmental and technological factors can spur economic development, their impact is contingent on the institutional framework that supports or hinders their adoption and implementation. The role of institutions in facilitating or impeding these processes underscores the intricate relationship between technology, environment, and long-run economic outcomes.

This dynamic is illustrated by Clark and Jacks' argument that coal extraction costs played a minor role in driving industrial productivity gains during the Industrial Revolution (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). They estimate only modest increases in total factor productivity (TFP) in coal mining between 1710 and 1869, suggesting that technological barriers and extraction costs did not significantly hinder economic growth. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of pre-1800 economic conditions; they argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before the Industrial Revolution. The implication is that long-term economic growth may have been more contingent on pre-existing economic structures than on technological innovations or resource availability.

Broadberry and Gupta also emphasize that labor productivity in both tradable and non-tradable sectors was instrumental in shaping these early economic divergences (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This highlights the importance of institutional frameworks that facilitate labour productivity. For instance, institutions that support efficient labour allocation and productivity improvements can amplify the effects of pre-existing economic conditions, further driving long-run growth. The nuances in this relationship suggest that while technological factors like coal extraction costs may not have been decisive, institutional settings that enhance productivity could have compounded the effects of earlier economic developments, thereby shaping long-term economic outcomes.

This points to a broader theme: institutions' role in facilitating or impeding labour productivity and resource utilization is central to understanding long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast show that institutions governing public choice are essential for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Stern's work on productivity improvements in terms of using less polluting inputs per unit of output highlights how technological advancements can complement institutional settings to drive long-run growth (Stern 2004, p.3; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This alignment between institutions and technology underscores the importance of a holistic approach to understanding economic development. While North and Weingast focus on political factors underpinning economic growth, Stern's emphasis on productivity improvements shows how institutional reliability can facilitate technological advancements that enhance resource utilization.

Both Clark and Jacks and van Zanden provide insights into the nuances of long-term economic development, albeit through different lenses. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that coal extraction costs did not significantly impact total factor productivity in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This finding suggests that technological factors alone may not be sufficient to drive long-run growth without supportive institutions. Van Zanden highlights the complexity of economic development by showing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This complexity indicates that institutional settings play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes, as they can either facilitate or impede productivity improvements.

The debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth shows underlying agreement that institutions matter. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Clark and Jacks' examination of coal mining productivity during the Industrial Revolution indicates an ongoing debate over the precise mechanisms through which institutions affect economic outcomes. The historical evidence on coal extraction costs shows that while technological factors may not have been decisive in driving long-term growth, institutional settings that enhance productivity could compound the effects of earlier economic developments (Stern 2004, p.3). Therefore, the precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by measurement of total factor productivity gains.
