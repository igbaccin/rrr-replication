The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses whether human-created rules and norms fundamentally drive prosperity over the long term. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions can be leveraged for sustained economic performance versus being merely incidental or even detrimental to growth. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes: some scholars argue that institutions facilitate economic growth through efficient resource allocation and reduced transaction costs, while others contend that distributional conflicts and power dynamics within institutions are more critical factors in shaping long-run economic trajectories. Moreover, the interplay between technological progress and institutional development remains a contentious area, with debates over whether institutions adapt to technological changes or vice versa. This tension underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other drivers of growth, such as technological innovation and social change. The causal relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring careful consideration of both theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence.

Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This perspective aligns with Kuznets' view that institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations. The latter emphasizes that technological innovations, driven by scientific discoveries, require complementary institutional and ideological changes to be fully realized in economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Institutions facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies by providing a stable framework for economic activities. Kuznets' argument highlights how institutions can enhance the effectiveness of technological innovations through positive feedback loops, where mass application of these innovations fosters further scientific progress.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by North's seminal work on the importance of property rights and enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). North posits that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are crucial for reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development. This perspective underscores how institutions can create a conducive environment for market exchanges by lowering costs per transaction, which in turn supports increased specialization and division of labor. Hornbeck's study on the introduction of barbed wire fencing provides empirical support for North's theory (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The adoption of barbed wire enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, illustrating how institutional changes can drive technological progress and economic growth. This example shows how institutions both foster and demand technological advancements for sustainable long-term growth.

The relationship between institutions and technological progress is further complicated by Mokyr's argument that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Mokyr 2005, p.12). Mokyr highlights how technological progress depends on the recombination of existing techniques, a process that is inherently linked to institutional frameworks. He argues that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains from technological advancements. This perspective aligns with North's view that institutions are essential for sustaining long-term growth by providing a stable framework for economic activities and reducing transaction costs. Together, these arguments underscore how institutions facilitate both the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, thereby driving long-run economic growth.

North's work on institutions highlights that political and economic institutions shape long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs through secure property rights (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies this dynamic. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of public choice institutions during this period was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and economic growth. This institutional framework enhanced credibility by constraining the state's ex post behavior, thereby improving its ability to maintain commitments to constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). A key mechanism here is the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange, which complements reputation-building strategies and mitigates incentive problems that could otherwise hinder growth. Acemoglu et al. extend this idea by stressing that institutions can be extractive when they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, emphasizing long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies a nuanced understanding of institutional impact: while secure property rights are conducive to economic development, the specific design and enforcement of these institutions critically determine their effectiveness. Thus, the institutional framework governing public choice both facilitates and influences the trajectory of long-term economic growth by affecting the distribution of rents and the credibility of commitments within a society.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.23) shared that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions (Austin 2008, p.23; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). When Europeans encountered high mortality rates in colonies, they created extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains and foster long-run economic growth. These institutions were characterized by coercion and the extraction of rents, which had profound implications for the economic development of these regions. The establishment of extractive institutions was closely tied to the profitability of colonial ventures, particularly those involving slavery and Atlantic trade (Nunn 2008, p.3). This mechanism highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, as extractive institutions both hindered growth and redirected resources away from productive investments.

The adverse effects of extractive institutions are further evident in the long-term economic stagnation experienced by regions governed by such systems. Nunn (2008, p.3) found that Africa's slave trades had detrimental impacts on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.3), illustrating how the production and trade of slaves created institutional frameworks that were not conducive to growth. This finding complements Acemoglu et al.'s argument, showing that the legacy of extractive institutions persists over centuries, significantly affecting current income per capita. The historical context of colonialism thus provides a lens through which to understand the enduring impact of institutional design on economic outcomes.

The role of extractive institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between mortality rates and institutional development, as these factors are antecedents and mediators of economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2).

This perspective positions institutions as secondary factors in this process. The accumulation of useful knowledge fuels technological advancement, which in turn drives economic growth. Together, these insights indicate that while institutions may facilitate or hinder the adoption of new technologies, the primary engine of long-run economic growth is technological progress itself.

Mokyr recognizes that institutional progress complements technological advancement and is necessary for sustaining economic improvements. This interplay highlights that although technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, institutions remain essential for harnessing its full potential. Thus, the relationship between technology and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is synergistic.

This section's claim about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can be further developed by examining how institutional frameworks influence technological adoption and diffusion across different economies.

The social and technological dynamics of economic growth are complex, shaped by factors such as technological advance, social change, and distributional conflicts (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argue that the catch-up process in economic growth involves more than just convergence; it is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This dynamic creates a broader horizon for scale-dependent technological progress, where backwardness presents an opportunity for modernization in both embodied and disembodied technology. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) further elaborates on this by showing that distributional conflicts can drive the formation of economic institutions, such as craft guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). These institutions, often seen as inefficient cartels, were actually efficient providers of security and contract enforcement in pre-modern societies. This interplay between technology and distributional conflicts underscores how institutional frameworks evolve to facilitate economic growth by addressing specific challenges posed by technological advancements.

These shifts have altered economic structures and reshaped societal norms and expectations. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22) focus on these historical dynamics but do not directly address the role of institutions in this process. Their work highlights how technological advancements can reshape economic landscapes. This technological change illustrates how new tools can alter the economic landscape by addressing specific challenges, such as securing land and mitigating externalities. Pamuk (2007, p.22), meanwhile, shows how the Black Death led to long-term structural changes in Europe, including a wage gap between regions (Pamuk 2007, p.22), suggesting that demographic shifts can drive economic divergence through altered labor markets and technological adoption. However, Komlos (1998, p.8) qualifies this by noting that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). This underscores that while technological changes and demographic shifts can drive economic growth, their benefits may not be universally distributed. The role of institutions in mediating these historical factors remains an open question.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Temin 2002, p.4) share the view that various factors influence economic growth and social inequality, but they do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. They note that differentials in paths of development have been a central concern to scholars of Latin America, with explanations often focusing on institutional variations such as property rights security, corruption prevalence, financial sector structures, public infrastructure investment, social capital, and entrepreneurial inclination. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight the importance of these institutions in shaping economic growth trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). For instance, they describe how societies dominated by large slave plantations and extreme wealth inequality faced unique challenges that persisted over time. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) extend this perspective by emphasizing the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that understanding these contributions is crucial for formulating policies aimed at strengthening women's economic roles, as the historical processes that led to the marginalization of women's labor are complex and multifaceted.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with technological progress and social change. Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations by providing frameworks for property rights and contract enforcement (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). This interaction between technology and institutions is evident in historical contexts where institutional progress complements technological advancements, ensuring sustained economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). For instance, Kuznets highlights that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of these innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The dynamics of technological advance and social change further illustrate how institutions influence long-run economic growth. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the interplay between technological progress and social changes, which are crucial for understanding the complexities of modern economic growth beyond simple convergence hypotheses (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, as they define property rights and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency (North 1989, p.6).

Institutions also play a crucial role in addressing coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors, which can erode economic gains if left unchecked. Mokyr emphasizes the importance of institutional progress in sustaining economic improvements by mitigating coordination failures and preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking activities (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This is further supported by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions, which can be seen as efficient solutions to specific obstacles in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains provides a concrete example of how institutional changes can enhance agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities. Hornbeck shows that barbed wire enabled farmers to better protect their land, illustrating how technological advancements combined with institutional frameworks can drive economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This historical case study underscores the broader point that institutions are active agents in shaping long-run economic trajectories.
