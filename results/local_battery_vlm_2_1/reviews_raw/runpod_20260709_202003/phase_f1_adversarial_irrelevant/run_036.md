The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages scholars across multiple disciplines. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of economic development and the mechanisms through which societies evolve from stagnation to prosperity. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, and those who propose alternative explanations such as geographic factors or distributional conflicts. Institutions can be defined in many ways, but they are generally understood to be the rules of the game in a society, including formal constraints (laws, constitutions) and informal norms (conventions, codes of conduct). The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors play a more significant role. This question is crucial for policy implications, as understanding the mechanisms behind long-run economic growth can inform strategies to promote development in underdeveloped regions. Institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing how resources are allocated and how transactions are conducted, thereby affecting productivity and innovation. The evolution of political institutions that specify property rights and provide effective enforcement has been linked to economic growth through secure investment environments and efficient market exchanges (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.6) argue that institutions governing public choice are pivotal for shaping long-run economic growth, particularly as they underpin secure property rights. The institutional changes in England during the seventeenth century illustrate this dynamic. North and Weingast demonstrate that institutions governing public choice are critical because they complement reputation mechanisms by constraining state behavior ex post, improving the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework reduces uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing for confidence in remote outcomes. The evolution of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is essential for realizing productivity gains from impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2). The role of institutions encompasses political stability and governance structures that support long-term growth, extending beyond economic efficiency. This institutional framework is essential for reducing transaction costs per exchange, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth. The presence of reliable property rights allows individuals to engage in transactions with those they do not personally know, underpinning the confidence necessary for complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes by shaping income per capita in former colonies. The mechanism of this influence lies in the extractive nature of these institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The persistence of these extractive institutions can be linked to mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies, which influenced settlement patterns and subsequent institutional development. High mortality rates led to less European settlement and the establishment of extractive institutions, which have enduring effects on current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) show that these institutional differences significantly affect income per capita in former colonies by influencing industrialization and economic growth processes. The results highlight the critical role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, suggesting that the specific nature of these institutions is more influential than general institutional frameworks.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Ogilvie 2007, p.7) both argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. They assert that these institutions do not necessarily emerge from efficiency considerations but rather from power struggles between different social groups, which in turn shape long-term economic outcomes. In Europe's case, Ogilvie argues that merchant guilds initially served as efficient providers of security and contract enforcement before evolving into less efficient cartels due to changing conditions and distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this perspective by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were shaped by elites through government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic indicates that a society's institutional framework is a product of power dynamics as well as a reflection of economic efficiency. However, Ogilvie notes that this interdependency within an institution's wider framework means studying one institution in isolation can be misleading as they are interconnected with other institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks and their impact on long-run economic growth.

The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe from 1500 to 1800 is complex and does not follow a straightforward trajectory (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Van Zanden (1999, p.2) argues that there is no clear correlation between economic growth and improvements in living standards during this period, challenging optimistic interpretations of early modern economic development. This perspective is supported by Komlos's (1998, p.13) findings that despite significant economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being actually declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence suggests that economic growth alone does not translate into improved living standards; other factors, such as income distribution and nutritional access, play crucial roles.

One specific mechanism through which this disconnect occurs is the relative price effect on nutrient consumption. This price increase led consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods, thereby altering their dietary patterns in response to economic changes. This mechanism is qualified by Komlos' observation that total factor productivity even declined during the 1840s (Komlos 1998, p.13). The implication is that while economic growth can drive changes in consumption patterns, it does not guarantee improvements in overall well-being or living standards; other factors such as technological progress and income distribution are necessary to ensure a positive impact on biological well-being.

The role of historical and institutional factors in shaping Africa's economic development has been extensively explored by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.3). Both scholars argue that specific historical events and institutions have significantly impacted Africa's long-term economic trajectory. A key mechanism through which these factors operate is the influence of historical slave trades on institutional development. For instance, Nunn shows how domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping in Africa for the production of slaves had negative impacts on subsequent development (Nunn 2008, p.3). This finding complements research showing that slavery resulted in institutions detrimental to economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.16), suggesting that the legacy of slave trades has left enduring institutional marks. However, Hopkins qualifies this perspective by noting that institutions can be long-standing without being immutable, and thus policy can adapt inappropriate institutions to present development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.10). The implication is that while historical factors have shaped Africa's economic trajectory, there remains potential for institutional reform to drive future growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving both historical legacies and contemporary adaptations. Institutions are pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This has been shown by North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.27), who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, while Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These scholars demonstrate that institutions are not static; they evolve and adapt to changing circumstances.

The institutional framework shapes economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs and property rights. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.2) shows how efficient property rights and effective enforcement reduce uncertainties in complex interdependent situations, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This is supported by van Zanden (1999, p.2), who argues that optimists have ignored the work of price historians showing a long-run decline of real wages or claimed that there was something wrong with the way these were calculated for the pre-industrial period. These insights highlight how institutions can either hinder or promote economic growth, depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms.

There is broad agreement on the fundamental importance of institutions in economic development: credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
