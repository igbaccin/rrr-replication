The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding whether the persistence of poverty and inequality can be attributed to institutional failures, or if other factors such as geography or culture are more decisive. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions, defined as formal rules and informal norms that shape behavior, are primary drivers of economic growth or secondary to technological progress. One position argues that efficient institutions facilitate economic transactions, reduce uncertainty, and encourage investment, thereby driving long-run growth. Conversely, another position posits that institutions are merely responsive to deeper forces such as technology and social change, and do not independently determine economic outcomes.

This debate is crucial for policy implications: if institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could be highly effective in promoting development. However, if technological progress and social change are more fundamental, then efforts to enhance economic performance through institutional design may prove futile. This review will explore how institutions shape long-run economic growth by examining the interplay between technological innovation, institutional efficiency, and broader socio-economic dynamics.

One key point of contention is whether institutional progress is a necessary complement to technological advancement or if technology can drive growth independently. Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that technological innovations require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This highlights a complementary relationship where institutions facilitate the application of new technologies, thereby enhancing economic productivity. Institutions matter not just as facilitators but also as enablers of sustained growth by ensuring that technological advancements are effectively integrated into the economy.

The interplay between technology and institutions is further illustrated by Abramovitz (1986, p.4), who argues that "backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied, as well as in embodied, technology" (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This suggests that institutions can leverage technological progress to accelerate economic growth. North (1989, p.6) extends this idea by asserting that efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms are crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Together, these perspectives underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in harnessing technological innovations for long-run economic growth.

Institutional efficiency is also a critical factor in shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) challenges the notion that pre-industrial European institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Instead, she argues that distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping these institutions, suggesting that institutional design can be influenced by power dynamics and political struggles rather than purely economic efficiencies. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who contend that "equilibrium institutions may be extractive" (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. These views highlight the complex interplay between institutional design, power dynamics, and economic outcomes.

Understanding how these factors interact is essential for grasping the broader implications of institutional efficiency on economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends beyond mere facilitation of technological progress; it encompasses the broader socio-economic context in which institutions operate. This context includes not only technological advancements but also the political and social dynamics that influence institutional design and effectiveness.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms. They emphasize that the emergence of efficient political institutions reduces transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and fosters economic development. The Glorious Revolution marked a significant turning point, as it established political institutions that constrained the state's ex post behavior, thereby improving its ability to maintain bargains with constituents. This institutional framework ensured that reputation mechanisms were complemented by effective constraints, preventing reneging on agreements and enhancing economic stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The efficacy of these institutions in mitigating incentive problems highlights their role in fostering a stable environment conducive to long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.6) cautions against overemphasizing the role of political institutions alone; he argues that the broader socio-economic context, including technological advancements and social dynamics, also significantly influences institutional design and effectiveness (North 1989, p.5).

In this context, distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped pre-industrial economic structures like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective challenges the conventional wisdom that these institutions emerged as efficient solutions to specific economic problems. Serfdom, for instance, can be seen as a mechanism through which elites secured their interests by controlling labor and land resources in response to demographic pressures or political struggles (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.6). By contrast, Domar's analysis of the profitability of slavery under varying land-to-labor ratios highlights that economic efficiency is not always the primary driver; rather, the socio-political context plays a crucial role. The implications of this distributional conflict hypothesis are significant: it suggests that pre-industrial economic structures were shaped by power dynamics rather than purely economic rationality. This understanding aligns with a broader view of institutional development, where the design and effectiveness of institutions are deeply intertwined with social and political forces.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Temin 2002, p.4) share a view of economic growth that highlights the multifaceted nature of development. Their perspectives emphasize technological advance, social change, and macroeconomic conditions as pivotal factors in shaping long-run economic growth. Abramovitz posits that the catch-up process involves not just technological adoption but also dynamic interactions between capital accumulation and labor market adjustments. This perspective underscores how rapid productivity growth can spur increased aggregate output by fostering a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Temin, meanwhile, attributes Europe's post-WWII economic boom to a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, highlighting the role of structural shifts in driving growth. This view contrasts with those that emphasize policy or other ordinary factors.

This focus on complex interactions between technological progress and social structures has implications for understanding institutional roles. Temin's work, by highlighting structural shifts in labor allocation, indicates that institutions managing sectoral transitions are also critical. For instance, policies that ease the movement of labor from agriculture to industry can accelerate economic growth by aligning labor supply with industrial demands.

However, Abramovitz acknowledges that the catch-up process is not uniform and can be influenced by exogenous factors (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This qualification suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effectiveness can vary based on external conditions. For example, during periods of rapid technological change or significant social upheaval, institutional frameworks may need to adapt quickly to maintain their positive impact on growth. Consequently, the resilience and flexibility of institutions become critical factors in sustaining long-term economic development.

In contrast to this view, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions are key to understanding the relative economic performance of former European colonies today. They show that differences in mortality rates faced by early Europeans led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality areas, which persisted and shaped current economic outcomes. This focus on rent extraction is crucial because it highlights how the incentives of colonial powers shaped institutional development in ways that hindered economic progress. Consequently, the persistence of these extractive institutions over time has significantly impacted the current income per capita of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrate that European settlement patterns, influenced by mortality rates, are strongly correlated with both early and contemporary institutional quality. This correlation underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic performance. However, this pattern is qualified by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) who note the importance of exogenous variation in institutions. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a pivotal role, other factors such as technological advancements or social changes could also influence long-term economic development. The resilience and flexibility of institutions become critical factors in sustaining long-term economic development.

However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) (Mokyr 2005, p.54) propose that technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological advancement is not a linear process but depends on existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel innovations. This recombination of techniques enables continuous technological improvement, which in turn drives economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10), meanwhile, notes that advancements in science and technology are closely interconnected, creating a positive feedback loop where scientific discoveries fuel further technical innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Institutions support this process by facilitating the efficient use of these innovations.

Nonetheless, Mokyr (2005, p.54) acknowledges that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advancement. Without complementary institutional changes, economic improvements might be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. The interplay between technological progress and institutional support highlights the complex nature of economic development, where both factors must work in tandem for sustained growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with various scholars highlighting different aspects of this relationship. North (1989, p.5) argues that neoclassical theories, which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants of growth, are misleading because they overlook the critical role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges. Kuznets (1973, p.4) supports this view by showing that technological progress is closely connected with further advancements in science, which in turn provide a positive feedback loop for economic growth. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in enabling and sustaining long-term economic development.

Technological progress alone cannot sustain long-run economic growth without complementary institutional changes. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological advancement must be supported by institutional progress to avoid coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors, which can erode economic improvements over time. Similarly, Abramovitz (1986, p.4) notes that backwardness presents an opportunity for modernization in both embodied and disembodied technology, highlighting the dynamic interplay between technological advance and social change. This shows that institutions are crucial in harnessing the full potential of technological progress for sustained growth.

The efficiency approach to understanding economic institutions is not universally applicable. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) challenges the notion that pre-industrial European institutions were primarily driven by efficiency, arguing instead that distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping these institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further support this view by demonstrating that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. This indicates that the design of institutions is influenced by power dynamics and distributional conflicts, which can hinder economic development if not properly addressed.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth remains a subject of scholarly debate. Both Mokyr (2005, p.54) and North (1989, p.6) agree that institutions are crucial for sustaining technological progress and facilitating market exchanges; however, they differ on the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Mokyr emphasizes coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors, while North focuses on the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. The historical evidence from the Industrial Revolution in England shows that institutions played a pivotal role in enabling technological innovations and sustaining long-term economic growth by providing the necessary framework for market exchanges and property rights enforcement.
