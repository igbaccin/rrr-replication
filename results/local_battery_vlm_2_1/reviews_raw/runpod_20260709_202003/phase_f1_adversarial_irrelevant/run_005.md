The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars who study economic history and development economics. The stakes are high because the answer determines how we understand the origins of wealth disparities between countries today, whether they are rooted in geography, culture, or the power dynamics that shape institutions over time. Two prominent positions exist: one emphasizes the importance of political institutions in securing property rights and fostering market growth; the other challenges this view by highlighting the complexities of historical causation and the multifaceted nature of economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) argue that productivity differences between Europe and Asia were already pronounced before 1800, suggesting a deep-seated role for institutions in long-term economic divergence. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) similarly contend that colonial-era institutional changes had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. These perspectives highlight how institutions may have shaped economic outcomes over centuries. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if they interact with other factors in complex ways.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn fueled economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework provided a reliable foundation for market exchanges, reducing transaction costs and enhancing overall economic efficiency. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a significant turning point, as it established institutions that constrained the state's expropriatory behavior, thereby securing property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional reliability was crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth, as it allowed individuals to engage in complex transactions with strangers without fear of expropriation. North emphasizes that the development of efficient property and factor markets, along with a reliable medium of exchange, were essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The institutional changes during this period created an environment where economic agents could confidently participate in markets. However, North also acknowledges that the evolution of these institutions was not automatic but required specific political conditions to emerge and function effectively.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, which is why they were not conducive to economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) find that these institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth.

This institutional framework was a product of immediate political interests and had lasting effects on economic performance. These extractive institutions persisted over time, influencing current income differences and accounting for significant fractions of current income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This persistence is crucial because it highlights how historical institutional legacies can have enduring impacts on economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) acknowledge that establishing the causal impact of institutions requires a source of exogenous variation, suggesting that while these institutions played a significant role, other factors may also contribute to long-term economic growth.

This suggests that the historical legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality facilitated elite advantage through government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by shaping institutional frameworks that favored the powerful. For instance, land abundance relative to labor led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This initial inequality set the stage for institutions that protected elite interests rather than fostering broad-based economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) highlights a specific mechanism through which property rights can significantly enhance agricultural productivity. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their land and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This example underscores how secure property rights, a key institutional factor, can drive economic growth by encouraging investment and efficient use of resources. This mechanism may not always translate uniformly across different contexts with varying levels of inequality.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) examine specific institutions to critique historical economic theories, arguing that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is more complex than often portrayed. Austin highlights that the Atlantic slave trade was driven by commercial motivations, where the exchange-value product of Africans' labour was lower in Africa compared to their destinations (Austin 2008, p.11). This perspective challenges simplistic views of causation and underscores the importance of considering disaggregated factor endowments and environmental contexts. This critique is important because it suggests that institutions may not always be optimal for growth under all conditions, thus qualifying the notion of their universal efficacy. Austin (2008, p.11) further notes that economic rent and growth can be joint products, implying that institutions supporting growth in one context might not do so in another (Austin 2008, p.1). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) elaborates on this by highlighting the interdependency of institutions within broader frameworks, suggesting that studying individual institutions in isolation may overlook crucial interactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency implies that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on their interplay with other factors.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and Pamuk (2007, p.8) share a focus on historical events that triggered long-term structural changes (Komlos 1998, p.13; Pamuk 2007, p.8). They examine the Industrial Revolution's impact on biological well-being and the Black Death's effects on wage gaps. Komlos (1998, p.13) shows that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that long-term structural changes do not necessarily align with improvements in health and welfare metrics. Pamuk (2007, p.8) argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450, highlighting how demographic shifts can alter economic structures over extended periods. These long-term structural changes can thus shape broader economic outcomes in ways that are not immediately apparent from short-term economic indicators such as GDP growth. This variability underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with long-term economic trends to shape growth outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with some scholars focusing on specific factors that drove the Industrial Revolution and economic divergence without directly addressing institutional influences. Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). They note that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output, suggesting that technological barriers did not significantly impede coal mining productivity gains during this period. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of pre-1800 economic conditions in Europe and Asia, which indicates that the great divergence between these regions was already well underway before the Industrial Revolution. These findings suggest that economic divergence was driven by factors beyond institutional frameworks alone, such as variations in labour productivity and technological constraints. This perspective does not fully account for the broader institutional contexts that may have facilitated or hindered these economic trends. For instance, while Clark and Jacks emphasize the modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining, they do not explore how institutions might have influenced these productivity changes over time. This oversight highlights the need to consider the interplay between technological factors and institutional environments when examining long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing and enforcing property rights, which are essential for fostering secure investment environments (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast show that the political factors underpinning economic growth are critical. This includes institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed. Institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints where reputation alone is insufficient, thereby improving the efficacy of market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.7; North 1989, p.2). North argues that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, enabling economic growth through increased specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework allows individuals to engage in impersonal exchange with confidence, even in complex interdependent situations.

The role of institutions is further emphasized by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth, highlighting how institutional design can significantly impact economic outcomes. Austin argues that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products in certain historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with low productivity in the tradable goods sector contributing to lower silver wages in Asia. This divergence underscores the importance of institutions in determining economic trajectories over the long run.

These institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, improving market exchanges by providing constraints where reputation alone was insufficient. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the differing institutional designs and their impact on economic outcomes in seventeenth-century England versus colonial settings.
