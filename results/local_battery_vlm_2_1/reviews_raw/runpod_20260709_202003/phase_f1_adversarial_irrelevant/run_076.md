The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question for understanding historical and contemporary economic trajectories. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder sustained economic development over extended periods. Institutions are dynamic systems that evolve with societal changes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The disagreements centre on whether institutions primarily drive economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, and technological advancements play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those governing property rights and political stability, is crucial for fostering an environment conducive to long-term economic prosperity. Others posit that institutional changes are often responses to deeper underlying forces, such as demographic shifts or technological innovations, which render institutions secondary factors in economic growth.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the role of credible commitments and secure property rights in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and promoting market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is complemented by Austin, who highlights the complex interplay between economic rent and growth, noting that institutions supporting enterprise can vary across historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.1). Together, these views underscore how institutional reliability can enhance confidence in economic exchanges, thereby facilitating growth. This dynamic is crucial because it shows that even as societies become more interconnected through specialization and division of labor, reliable institutions help maintain stability and predictability in economic interactions.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast demonstrate that the Glorious Revolution marked a critical juncture where political institutions were restructured to enforce property rights more effectively. This institutional shift significantly reduced transaction costs, as North elaborates, by creating a stable framework for market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). The emergence of these institutions facilitated economic growth by enabling individuals to engage in complex transactions with confidence, even when dealing with strangers.

The efficacy of these political institutions is intertwined with broader social dynamics. For instance, this institutional reliability is crucial because it ensures that as societies become more interconnected through specialization and division of labor, economic interactions remain stable and predictable (North 1989, p.6). This stability underpins the growth of markets by fostering trust in economic exchanges. North and Weingast highlight that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, particularly in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging on agreements (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). For example, political restrictions on ex post behavior can improve the state's credibility in maintaining bargains with constituents.

This institutional framework is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. By developing efficient markets and a reliable medium of exchange, these institutions allow individuals to engage in complex transactions without personal knowledge of their counterparts (North 1989, p.2). This reliability fosters economic growth by enabling the division of labor and specialization, which are fundamental drivers of long-run economic development.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income per capita and are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not promote long-term economic development. This pattern is evident in former colonies where high mortality rates among Europeans led to the establishment of such institutions, which persisted and significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The correlation between early measures of these extractive institutions and contemporary institutional frameworks underscores their enduring influence on economic performance. This relationship is bidirectional: the role of European settlers in shaping these institutions highlights a specific pathway through which colonial legacy continues to affect economic outcomes today.

Distributional conflicts among elites and peasants shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts led to the formation of institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communities. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that these institutions were outcomes of power struggles between different social groups. This perspective challenges the prevailing view that institutions are designed for efficiency in economic transactions. The specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions involved the use of political power by elites to secure their interests, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that similar dynamics played out in New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to high inequality, which in turn influenced the development of institutions that perpetuated elite advantages. This pattern suggests that distributional conflicts shaped institutional frameworks and determined their longevity and impact on economic growth.

The downstream implication of this institutional formation is evident in the persistence of these structures over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that once established, these institutions are resistant to change (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This resistance can hinder economic growth by preventing the adoption of more efficient practices and technologies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) qualify this by highlighting that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was due to continued elite dominance as well as institutional rigidity (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that while institutions shaped by distributional conflicts can be enduring, their impact on economic growth is mediated by ongoing power dynamics.

The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during 1500-1800 is complex. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both argue that there was no clear relationship between economic growth and improvements in living standards during this period, a point van Zanden (1999, p.2) makes by critiquing the 'optimistic' interpretations of early modern European economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). This is surprising given that during this timeframe, there was significant urbanization and industrial development in Europe. For example, Komlos (1998, p.13) notes that by 1850, the combined population of Paris and London had reached 3.6 million people, indicating substantial economic growth (Komlos 1998, p.13). Despite this economic expansion, there was a decline in biological well-being due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. Komlos (1998, p.13) argues that technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture were slower than in industry, leading to an increase in the relative price of nutrients (Komlos 1998, p.8). This suggests that while economic growth occurred, it did not translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. The discrepancy between economic development and living standards highlights the importance of considering distributional effects when examining long-run economic growth.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Austin 2008, p.11). Broadberry and Gupta and Austin argue that this divergence is best understood by considering different historical periods and paths. In particular, the economic trajectories of both regions were shaped by distinct institutional frameworks. The commercial motivations behind the Atlantic slave trade illustrate how European institutions facilitated the extraction of resources from Africa, leading to a lower exchange-value product of Africans' labor in their home continent (Austin 2008, p.11). This suggests that the divergence was also the result of institutional differences, not solely technological advancements or capital accumulation. This points to a nuanced understanding of how institutions can shape long-run economic growth through their impact on labor productivity and the exchange-value product of labor. The role of arbitrage in equalizing international prices for tradable goods further highlights how institutional frameworks can influence economic outcomes by affecting wages and labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-established by studies that show how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms foster economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.6) argue that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England, as evidenced by capital markets' success. This is further supported by van Zanden's analysis, which highlights that long-term growth of total factor productivity occurred between 1450 and 1800 due to the development of efficient institutional frameworks (van Zanden 1999, p.2). These findings underscore that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms. This is shown by North's argument, which posits that political institutions are necessary for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on labor productivity and wages is another critical aspect of their role in shaping long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that arbitrage equalizes international prices for tradable goods, influencing wages and labor productivity through the institutional frameworks governing these exchanges (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This aligns with Austin's argument that the exchange-value product of Africans' labour was lower in Africa than in other regions due to specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11), indicating that institutions favoring economic growth can vary across different historical contexts. These studies show that institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes by affecting wages and labor productivity, thereby shaping long-run economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in fostering or hindering growth remains a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast's analysis, which shows that political factors such as the degree to which a regime is committed to property rights are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement involves measuring how institutions designed to maximize rents affect long-term economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) show that arbitrage equalizes international prices for tradable goods, influencing wages and labor productivity through the institutional frameworks governing these exchanges. This highlights the importance of understanding how different institutional frameworks can influence economic outcomes by affecting wages and labor productivity.
