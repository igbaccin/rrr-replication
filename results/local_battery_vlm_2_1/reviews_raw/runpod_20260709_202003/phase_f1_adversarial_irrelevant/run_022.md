The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has engaged economists for decades, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms driving prosperity over extended periods. At stake is understanding whether and how political, legal, and social frameworks influence sustained economic development. The live disagreement centers on the primacy of institutional factors versus other determinants such as technological progress or exogenous shocks. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial in facilitating efficient market exchanges and property rights enforcement, thereby enabling long-term growth. Some scholars assert that technological progress and knowledge accumulation are primary drivers, with institutions playing a supporting role in these processes (Author Year p.N). The interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes is complex, involving both direct effects on productivity and indirect influences through technological adoption and social change.

The centrality of technology in economic growth is evident in the work of Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10). Mokyr posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the primary engine of economic progress. He argues that technological advancements often rely on the recombination of existing techniques, highlighting the iterative nature of innovation. This perspective underscores the importance of knowledge diffusion in driving economic growth. Kuznets similarly emphasizes the role of technology, noting that modern economic growth is characterized by the mass application of technological innovations, which are closely linked to scientific progress. Both scholars' arguments reveal a feedback loop between technological advancements and economic growth, suggesting that new technologies drive economic expansion and create opportunities for further innovation. This dynamic interplay between technology and economic growth sets the stage for understanding how institutions might complement or hinder these processes.

The role of institutions in supporting long-run growth is significant. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter significantly in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms. He contends that the development of political institutions that reduce transaction costs is crucial for facilitating market exchanges and enhancing economic efficiency. This perspective aligns with Abramovitz's (1986) view on technological progress, as he argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Institutions play a critical role in this process by providing the necessary framework for efficient resource allocation and technological adoption. The interplay between institutions and technological progress is evident in these scholars' arguments, as they suggest that effective institutional frameworks are essential for sustaining long-term economic growth.

The relationship between institutions and technological progress is further complicated by the presence of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. This perspective highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and social dynamics, suggesting that institutions may not always be optimized for economic efficiency but rather reflect the power struggles within society. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provides a complementary view by demonstrating how technological changes, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, enhanced agricultural development by enabling better protection of property rights. This example illustrates how institutional frameworks can interact with technological advancements to drive economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, involving both direct effects on productivity and indirect influences through technological adoption and social change.

While technological progress and knowledge accumulation are crucial drivers, institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in facilitating efficient market exchanges and supporting technological advancements. The interplay between these factors is dynamic, with institutions complementing or hindering technological progress depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms. Understanding this complex relationship is essential for formulating policies that promote sustainable economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. This is because the design of these institutions was heavily influenced by the conditions faced by colonizers, particularly their mortality rates. These extractive institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) show that there is a high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the type of institutions established, which in turn affected early measures of institutions and their persistence to this day. The institutional framework set during colonial times thus played a critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also highlight that these institutions were not static; they could evolve over time depending on political and economic pressures, which means that the long-term effects of colonial institutions are contingent upon subsequent institutional changes and adaptations.

Distributional conflicts and institutional efficiency shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie's work challenges the prevailing view that institutions are structured around efficiency by arguing that merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries provided security and contract enforcement as club goods (Author Year p.N). Domar similarly argues that slavery or serfdom emerged in response to a high land-to-labor ratio, highlighting how economic structures adapted to scarcity rather than cultural or political influences (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective underscores the role of distributional struggles in shaping institutions, suggesting that long-term economic growth is influenced by these conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies shows that greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These findings suggest that the institutional frameworks established during periods of distributional conflict can have lasting effects on economic growth by shaping the distribution of resources and opportunities. These institutions evolve over time in response to changing economic and political pressures. This evolution means that the long-term effects of colonial institutions are contingent upon subsequent institutional changes and adaptations.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) extend this analysis by examining how colonial institutional changes can alter the trajectory of economic development. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth. These rules complement reputation-building by acting as a constraint where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. Institutions improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by mitigating incentive problems that cannot be adequately policed by reputation strategies alone, such as those illustrated in the literature on the theory of the firm (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, as restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state enhance its ability to maintain bargains with constituents.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Temin 2002, p.4) build on this by showing how these dynamics are further influenced by technological advancements and labor market adjustments. They show that technological advancements reduce the relative price of capital goods, thereby accelerating the growth of the capital stock. This process is further amplified by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress, which Abramovitz highlights as a key driver of productivity increases and overall economic output (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Temin extends this view, arguing that labor reallocation between sectors can lead to rapid economic growth; for example, post-WWII Europe experienced significant growth due to the disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This mechanism underscores how structural changes in labor markets can catalyze economic development by redirecting human resources towards more productive activities (Temin 2002, p.3). Temin also notes that these dynamics are subject to broader cyclical patterns, suggesting that while technological progress and labor reallocation are crucial, they operate within the constraints of larger economic cycles. This implies that institutional frameworks, which can influence both technological adoption and labor market flexibility, play an indirect but significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) emphasize that the interplay between technology and resources is crucial for understanding economic development in this context. Hornbeck shows how barbed wire fencing revolutionized agricultural development on the American Plains by enhancing property rights protection and reducing livestock damage, thereby increasing productivity (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This underscores a direct mechanism through which technology can augment agricultural output, a critical sector in many economies. This nuance suggests that while technological advancements are pivotal, their impact is often mediated by broader institutional and economic factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological progress depends on existing knowledge, analogical thinking, and the recombination of techniques. This process often reveals gaps in current technologies, guiding inventors to develop new solutions. Kuznets (1973, p.10), likewise, highlights how technological innovations are deeply intertwined with scientific advancements; this interdependence creates a positive feedback loop where mass application of innovations fuels further scientific progress (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Together these authors suggest that the accumulation and efficient use of knowledge is fundamental to economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) also notes that while technological progress can be substantial, its sustainability relies on institutional factors that prevent rent-seeking behaviors and ensure coordination failures are rectified (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests a nuanced role for institutions in supporting the long-term benefits of technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates on how economic growth requires adjustments not just technologically but also institutionally and ideologically to efficiently use innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements and managing coordination failures. Mokyr (2005, p.54) shows that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is complemented by Kuznets (1973, p.10), who argues that economic growth requires adjustments not just technologically but also institutionally and ideologically to efficiently use innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.10). The importance of institutions in enabling long-term benefits of technological advancements is underscored by the shared finding that useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, but institutions enable and sustain this process (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond merely facilitating technological progress. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) shows that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This complexity highlights the need for institutions to manage not just technological but also social changes, ensuring that economic growth is sustained over the long term. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this point by arguing that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement (North 1989, p.5). Institutions thereby reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for economic growth.

Institutional arrangements also address distributional conflicts that arise during economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) shows that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by these distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions operate. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) add that technological constraints also played a significant role in shaping institutional arrangements during the Industrial Revolution, highlighting how coal output expanded mainly due to increased demand rather than technological innovations (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.5). The precise remaining disagreement between Abramovitz's (1986) emphasis on exogenous factors and North's (1989) focus on efficient property rights is settled by the historical evidence of institutional arrangements during the Industrial Revolution. This period shows that institutions managed technological progress and addressed distributional conflicts, thereby enabling sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.5).
