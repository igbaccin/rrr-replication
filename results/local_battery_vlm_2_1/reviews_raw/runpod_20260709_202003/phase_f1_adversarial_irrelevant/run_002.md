The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development theory. Institutions, defined as rules and norms that shape human behavior, are pivotal because they influence how societies organize themselves to produce goods and services over extended periods (Acemoglu et al 2001, p. 2). Two key positions exist in the literature. One position emphasizes technological progress as the primary driver of economic growth, arguing that institutions play a secondary role by facilitating the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. The other position asserts that institutions are fundamental to economic growth because they shape incentives, reduce transaction costs, and allocate resources efficiently. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms underlying sustained economic development and for designing effective policies aimed at fostering long-term growth. Institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

North 1989 and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Political institutions play a pivotal role in this process. They establish the rules governing economic exchange, ensuring that these rules are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon procedures. This commitment to rules is crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn encourages investment and economic growth. North & Weingast 1989 illustrate how political institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints where reputation alone was insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These institutional features mitigate incentive problems that reputation strategies cannot fully address. For instance, specific institutional arrangements within firms can prevent reneging on agreements and ensure long-term commitments. This perspective highlights the endogenous role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

The emergence of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement is a key mechanism through which political institutions influence economic growth. North 1989 describes how transaction costs fall as political institutions develop, facilitating market exchanges and increasing the volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6). This development is essential for sustaining long-term economic growth, as it reduces barriers to trade and investment. This period underscores the importance of credible commitments by political institutions, which ensure that rules are enforced consistently over time. North 1989 argues that neoclassical theories, focusing on population and savings, offer an incomplete picture of economic growth (North 1989, p.5). The role of institutions in specifying property rights and providing enforcement is a crucial factor that these theories often overlook. This institutional framework supports efficient market exchanges while fostering an environment conducive to technological innovation and long-term investment.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). The technological improvement in fencing allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated long-term investment in agriculture. This security of property rights was crucial for fostering an environment where farmers could plan for the future with greater certainty. Domar (1970, p.7) highlights that the profitability of agricultural labor is intricately linked to technological progress and capital accumulation (Domar 1970, p.7). When land-to-labor ratios are high, as in the American Plains during this period, the introduction of barbed wire protected property and enhanced the efficiency of agricultural practices (Acemoglu et al 2001, p. 3). Domar (1970, p.7) notes that the relative profitability of free versus slave labor can be complex and depends on various factors including technological progress (Domar 1970, p.6). This complexity underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions of institutional change when examining long-run economic growth. The role of property rights in agricultural development involves securing land while creating an environment where technological advancements can flourish, driving long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al 2001, p. 48).

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Temin 2002, p.4) highlight the multifaceted nature of economic growth dynamics. Both authors emphasize that technological progress plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Abramovitz argues that technological opportunities can accelerate the growth of capital stock, as they reduce the relative price of capital goods to labor and increase returns on modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This mechanism underscores how technological advancements can drive economic growth by enhancing productivity and fostering a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. Temin (2002, p.4) adds that rapid economic growth is driven by policy or ordinary factors and disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.3). This perspective suggests that the reallocation of labor from less productive to more productive sectors can significantly boost economic output, complementing Abramovitz's emphasis on technological progress. Temin qualifies this view by noting that new growth theories provide extensions to old growth theory while often complicating Solow's simplicity and elegance (Temin 2002, p.3). This qualification implies that while technological progress and labor reallocation are crucial for economic growth, the complexity of these dynamics requires a nuanced understanding.

The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of knowledge, is a primary force behind long-run economic growth. Technological advancements often build upon existing techniques, with inventors combining known methods to create novel solutions (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process of recombination and analogical thinking underscores the incremental nature of technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further emphasizes that mass application of technological innovations, many based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop, driving additional advances in technology and science. Mokyr qualifies this view by noting that technological progress is relatively irreversible whereas institutional progress is less monotonic (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, the sustainability of these gains depends on complementary institutional developments that prevent rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic improvements.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) establish that these institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization, accounting for significant income differences today. The mechanism through which colonialism shaped institutions is rooted in the mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies. High European mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists and not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). These institutions persisted, significantly affecting current income per capita despite changes in geography or climate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development if they are shaped by politically powerful groups who fear losing their power through institutional change. This suggests a complex interplay between the initial conditions set by colonialism and the enduring effects of extractive institutions on long-run economic growth, highlighting the need for further examination into how institutional legacies can be overcome to foster sustainable economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological progress and reduce transaction costs. Institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic interactions (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). This framework enables efficient use of resources and encourages innovation, which drives economic growth. Kuznets highlights that technological advancements are closely linked to scientific progress, and this interplay is crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr adds that institutional progress complements technological advancement, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Mokyr 2005, p.12).

Moreover, the dynamics of technological advance and social change play a significant role in the catch-up process of economic growth. Abramovitz shows that backwardness presents an opportunity for modernization through both embodied and disembodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This modernization involves catching up and leveraging new technologies to accelerate growth. Hornbeck's analysis of the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains illustrates how technological changes that enhance property rights can significantly boost agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the political dynamics that shape these institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development if they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This highlights the need for institutions that can adapt and evolve to support sustainable economic development, even in the face of resistance from those who might lose power through institutional change. The historical evidence of colonial legacies shows that extractive institutions designed by European colonists often hindered long-run economic growth in former colonies.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. This role is evident in how institutions facilitate technological progress and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutions can be shaped by politically powerful groups, potentially hindering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence of colonial legacies shows that extractive institutions designed for short-term gains often impeded long-run economic growth.
