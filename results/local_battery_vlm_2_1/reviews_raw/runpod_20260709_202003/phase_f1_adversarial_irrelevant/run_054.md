The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that cuts to the heart of how societies develop and prosper over time. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. At the center of this debate lies a live disagreement about whether institutional frameworks primarily facilitate technological progress, or if they operate through other mechanisms such as coordinating efforts in society. The former camp emphasizes that institutions enable the efficient use of innovations, while the latter argues that institutions directly influence economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights. This tension underscores the need to delineate how different institutional structures interact with economic processes over extended periods.

One stream of scholarship addresses the role of technological progress in conjunction with institutions in shaping long-term economic growth. The interplay between technology and institutions is evident in the works of both Kuznets and Mokyr. Kuznets highlights that the mass application of technological innovations, often based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop for further advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This view aligns with Mokyr's argument that technological progress depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking (Mokyr 2005, p.12), suggesting that institutions play a crucial role in sustaining economic improvement by preventing rent-seeking behaviors. By recognizing coordination failures, institutions can support sustained economic growth through policies that rectify these issues (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This stream underscores the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements, ensuring that economic improvements are not eroded over time.

Institutions also play a critical role in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for lowering costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is reinforced by Hornbeck's analysis of the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Together, these studies illustrate how institutions can foster economic growth by creating an environment that supports efficient market transactions.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) maintain that political institutions determine the efficiency of property rights. This perspective is supported by the English financial revolution's success in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The emergence of these institutions led to a reduction in transaction costs, which facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization, ultimately driving economic development (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast also highlight that economic growth relies on both rules governing exchange and institutions enforcing these rules. Acemoglu et al. modify this idea by pointing out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) (Domar 1970, p.7) posit that this dynamic was particularly pronounced in pre-industrial Europe, where powerful groups often shaped economic structures to their advantage. They assert that these institutions were primarily designed to maintain political power and extract rents from society. Ogilvie's analysis underscores how merchant guilds, often seen as inefficient cartels, actually provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Domar’s work on serfdom and slavery supports this view, showing that these systems emerged in response to high land-to-labor ratios. Domar further elaborates that the profitability of slave labor versus free labor depends heavily on technological progress and capital use (Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie’s critique of the efficiency approach adds nuance to this view, suggesting that many premodern institutions were only later interpreted as efficient solutions to transactional obstacles. This highlights a key implication: distributional conflicts often drive the creation of economic institutions, even if these institutions are later justified through efficiency narratives (Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

Specific technological changes and historical events significantly influenced economic development by altering the incentives and constraints faced by economic actors, often independently of institutional frameworks (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, directly enhancing agricultural development. The implications of this technological change are multifaceted: it improved resource allocation by allowing for more efficient land use and farming practices. This effect is not solely due to direct technological improvements but also stems from increased protection from others' cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Pamuk similarly demonstrates how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes in Europe, leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of the continent by 1450. The substitution of land and capital for labor across the continent was driven by a significant increase in wages relative to rents and the price of capital (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This shift had profound implications for technological innovation, with urban guilds playing a crucial role in developing new technologies. This perspective is nuanced by the observation that while technological changes can drive economic development, they do not necessarily alter underlying institutional structures. For instance, the increased effectiveness of soldiers with firearms during the late medieval centuries highlights how technological advancements can emerge independently of institutional frameworks (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The shared finding is that long-term structural changes in wages and technological innovation are driven by historical events and technological progress, but these do not necessarily translate into immediate or direct institutional reforms.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) share the finding that technological advance and social change play crucial roles in shaping long-term economic growth. This dynamic is particularly relevant for economies undergoing modernization, where backwardness creates opportunities for rapid technological adoption and scale-dependent progress.

Acemoglu et al. support this view by showing that European settlements in colonies with high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which have persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical perspective underscores how social dynamics, such as colonial settlement patterns, can influence institutional frameworks and economic outcomes over extended periods. The implication is that while technological advancements drive growth, the social context in which they occur significantly shapes their impact.

Acemoglu et al. also note that exogenous factors, such as mortality rates faced by European settlers, played a critical role in determining institutional structures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This qualification suggests that while technological and social dynamics are essential, they operate within broader historical and geographical constraints. These constraints can either facilitate or hinder the beneficial effects of technological progress on economic growth. Thus, the interplay between technology, society, and institutions is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of how these factors interact over time.

Technological advancement and knowledge growth are primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is pivotal in driving economic growth. The interplay between technology and knowledge creates a dynamic system where each advance builds upon previous ones, fostering sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10) qualifies this by noting that the efficient use of innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. Thus, while technological progress is a key driver, it operates within a framework that includes but is not limited to institutions.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for technological progress to translate into sustained development. Kuznets (1973, p.10) shows that the efficient use of innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Institutions facilitate market exchanges, reduce transaction costs, and enforce property rights, all of which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Without these adjustments, technological progress alone may not be sufficient to sustain long-term economic improvements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that institutional progress complements technological advancements by addressing coordination failures and preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The interaction between technology and institutions is dynamic and interdependent. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) demonstrates how technological opportunities drive both modernization and capital stock growth, creating a feedback loop that accelerates economic development (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This process involves complex interactions where each advance builds upon previous ones, fostering sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms, which are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This framework underscores the importance of institutional development in leveraging technological progress for long-term economic benefits.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by historical evidence. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This example highlights how institutional changes, such as improved property rights enforcement, can drive economic growth by facilitating technological advancements. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) provides a broader perspective by arguing that pre-industrial institutions, often criticized for inefficiency, actually played crucial roles in providing security and contract enforcement, which were essential for economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

Institutional progress complements technological advancements by addressing coordination failures and preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors. The precise historical evidence of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth by facilitating technological advancements. This shows that institutional adjustments are crucial for leveraging technological progress, as they provide the necessary framework for sustained economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; North 1989, p.6).
