The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is an understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained prosperity. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutional factors, such as property rights and political stability, drive economic growth versus other determinants like geography or resource endowments. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions in fostering economic development, while others highlight the importance of antecedent factors that shape these institutions. This debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote sustainable economic growth in developing regions.

One stream of scholarship addresses how specific institutional changes have historically facilitated long-run economic growth by enhancing property rights and market stability. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are a critical factor underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering these secure property rights, which in turn supported the development of capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is complemented by North’s broader argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth (North 1989, p.2), as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. The establishment of efficient property rights allows individuals to engage confidently in complex interdependent situations, thereby realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.6). Institutions thus play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and stable markets.

Another stream identifies antecedent factors that influence the development of institutions conducive to economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23), suggesting that underlying economic conditions played a significant role in shaping institutional frameworks. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They emphasize that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are shaped by politically powerful groups and designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth. The significance of these antecedent factors highlights how pre-existing conditions can influence the development of institutions that ultimately shape economic outcomes.

This focus on institutional changes and their antecedents underscores the complex interplay between political stability, property rights, and economic growth. Understanding this dynamic is essential for designing policies that foster sustainable economic development in diverse contexts.

Their work highlights how political institutions governing public choice underpinned the development of markets by ensuring credible commitments to rules, complementing reputation-building mechanisms rather than substituting them. This is because institutional constraints can mitigate incentive problems where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North further elaborates on this dynamic, emphasizing that as economies grow and become more specialized, efficient property rights and their enforcement reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The Glorious Revolution of 1688 serves as a historical example of how institutional changes can enhance economic growth. The revolution led to restrictions on the state's ex post behavior, improving its ability to maintain bargains with constituents and prevent wealth expropriation. North acknowledges that this institutional reliability is a result of evolving social frameworks designed to reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependencies (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These frameworks are essential for realizing productivity gains from specialization and division of labor. The establishment of efficient property rights allows individuals to have confidence in dealings with strangers, which is crucial for economic growth in highly interconnected societies.

This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as they provide the necessary conditions for secure property rights and market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions established by European powers have had enduring effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a dynamic evident in the divergence of income levels among formerly colonized nations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mortality rates faced by Europeans in these colonies played a crucial role in shaping institutional outcomes: high mortality led to extractive institutions that persisted over time, significantly impacting current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2).

The mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced economic growth is rooted in the initial settlement patterns and institutional frameworks imposed by European powers. Colonies with favorable conditions for European settlement tended to develop more inclusive institutions, fostering economic growth over time. Colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates were subject to extractive institutions that hindered long-term development. This pattern highlights how early institutional choices, driven by colonial policies, have had lasting impacts on the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). However, it is important to note that this perspective does not negate the role of other factors, such as geographic endowments and cultural legacies, in shaping economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are more influential than efficiency in shaping pre-industrial European economic institutions. They show that the traditional view of pre-modern economic institutions as efficient solutions to transactional problems overlooks the role of power struggles and distributional conflicts. This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were primarily designed for economic efficiency, suggesting instead that they often served to entrench existing power structures. For example, the merchant guilds of the 12th to 15th centuries are argued to be inefficient cartels, while elder guilds were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as club goods. This analysis suggests that institutions designed for efficiency can also serve to maintain elite control over resources. The persistence of these institutions highlights how distributional conflicts can shape economic frameworks in ways that may not always align with long-term growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate this point by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic suggests that even if institutional arrangements are efficient, they can perpetuate economic disparities that hinder long-term development.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Bryant (2006, p.29) argue that historical paths and periods are more complex than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). These scholars contend that the causal mechanisms underlying economic growth and development are not uniform across time or space. Austin notes that the motivation for the Atlantic slave trade was commercial, driven by the higher exchange-value product of Africans' labour in the destinations than in Africa itself (Austin 2008, p.11). This dynamic highlights how specific historical contexts shape economic outcomes in ways that are not captured by simplistic causal narratives. The complexity of these historical paths is further illustrated by Bryant's analysis of agricultural productivity and labour dynamics in Europe and China. He argues that the European pattern of concentrating handicrafts within peasant households fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country, leading to significant gains in per capita productivity (Bryant 2006, p.29). In contrast, China's economic arrangement tied its economy to subsistence farming and domestic handicrafts, limiting both the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production and the capital accumulation necessary for expansion. This comparison underscores how different institutional frameworks can lead to divergent economic trajectories over time. Austin further qualifies this by noting that colonial regimes often supported African economic enterprise (Austin 2008, p.1). The implication is that institutions are not static but adapt to historical contexts, influencing economic outcomes in nuanced ways. This highlights the need for a more granular understanding of institutional dynamics and their impact on long-run economic growth.

The relationship between economic development and living standards is complex and multifaceted. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) both examine economic growth over historical periods without explicitly framing institutions as a cause of change, but they do point to mechanisms that can be traced back to institutional frameworks. The optimists' view is that the new approach shows long-term productivity gains in agriculture and other sectors, for example, Hoffmann's work on France between 1450 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This divergence is likely underpinned by institutional differences, which influence productivity and wages through complex economic mechanisms.

The role of institutions becomes evident when considering labour productivity and wage differentials between regions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) show how silver wages reflect productivity in tradable sectors, low silver wages in Asia indicate lower productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This suggests that institutional factors, such as property rights or market regulations, could be influencing these productivity levels through their impact on investment and innovation. The optimists' focus on long-term growth in total factor productivity may overlook broader economic conditions that affect living standards. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) notes that while there is evidence of productivity gains, these do not necessarily translate into improved living standards for the general population (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This highlights a key implication: institutional frameworks that support broad-based economic growth are more likely to lead to improvements in living standards than those that focus solely on productivity gains.
