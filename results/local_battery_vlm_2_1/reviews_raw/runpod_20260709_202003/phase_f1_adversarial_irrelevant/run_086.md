The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. Institutions are understood as the rules of the game that structure social interactions (North 1989, p.6), and their influence on economic performance has been hotly debated. At one end stands the perspective that institutions are foundational to economic growth, facilitating technological progress, market exchanges, and efficient use of resources; at the other end is the view that distributional conflicts and specific institutional arrangements can hinder or even subvert economic efficiency. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions primarily enable economic growth through their role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing productivity (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.7), or if they are more often shaped by power dynamics and distributional struggles that prioritize the interests of certain groups over overall economic efficiency.

Abramovitz argues that technological progress is a key driver of long-run growth, but this process is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that while technological innovations are crucial, their efficient use requires institutional adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This implies that institutions play a complementary role in supporting the economic benefits of technological progress. Institutions can facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies by providing the necessary framework for market exchanges and property rights enforcement.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. This is because the extractive institutions established during colonial rule persisted over time and had a lasting impact on economic development. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism through which mortality rates influenced institutional outcomes was via settlement patterns: higher European mortality led to less settlement, which in turn resulted in the establishment of extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over local development. This pattern was exacerbated by the fact that colonizers who faced high mortality were more likely to implement institutions that facilitated exploitation and investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions is crucial because they shaped economic performance during the colonial period and in the post-colonial era, leading to significant differences in income per capita among former colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization, accounting for a substantial fraction of current income differences. Other factors, such as subsequent policy choices and global economic conditions, can also influence long-run economic outcomes.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that this institutional change allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated investment in agriculture. The protection of property rights was crucial because it reduced the risk of livestock damage, encouraging farmers to invest more in their land. This is evident from the fact that barbed wire's effects are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Pamuk (2007, p.22) further illustrates this point by highlighting how structural changes, such as those initiated by the Black Death, led to long-term economic impacts. This underscores the broader significance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes over extended periods.

The ability to protect property rights through institutional means, such as barbed wire fencing, is about technological advancement and creating a stable environment for investment. Pamuk (2007, p.22) notes that the substitution of land and capital for labour began to occur across the continent as wages increased relative to rents and the price of capital (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This dynamic illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic development by altering the incentives for different types of investments. These effects are not uniform across all regions or periods. This suggests that while institutional changes can be powerful drivers of economic development, their impact is contingent on broader economic and social conditions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted. They create an environment where property rights are secure, encouraging investment and reducing the risk of externalities. The effectiveness of these institutions depends on various factors, including technological advancements and broader economic conditions. This interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes highlights the complex nature of long-term economic development.

Ogilvie and Domar argue that economic institutions are better understood as outcomes of distributional conflicts and efficiency drivers (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). They contend that serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by power struggles over resource allocation, not by their potential to enhance productivity. Ogilvie highlights how merchant guilds, traditionally viewed as inefficient cartels, actually provided security and contract enforcement as club goods (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective aligns with Domar's analysis of slavery or serfdom emerging when the land-to-labor ratio is high, making labor scarce (Domar 1970, p.7). Economic institutions are deeply rooted in the socio-economic power dynamics of their time. This view does not negate the role of technological advancements and broader economic conditions in shaping these institutions. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they interact with broader economic factors to determine institutional outcomes.

Abramovitz and Temin both emphasize that technological progress and labor reallocation are pivotal drivers of economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Temin 2002, p.4). Abramovitz extends this by noting that the same technological opportunities facilitate rapid capital stock growth through returns to modernization, as well as a reduction in the price of capital goods relative to labor. This dynamic accelerates the rise in the capital-labor ratio, further enhancing productivity and aggregate output (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

Temin argues that Europe's post-WWII Golden Age of economic growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, suggesting that shifts in labor distribution can significantly impact economic performance. This perspective is supported by Abramovitz’s observation that backwardness provides opportunities for modernization, both in embodied and disembodied technology (Temin 2002, p.3). The interplay between technological progress and labor reallocation thus creates a virtuous cycle where each factor reinforces the other, leading to sustained economic growth.

Temin qualifies this by noting that while new growth theories extend traditional models, they also introduce complexity at the expense of simplicity. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about setting the stage for technological advancements and facilitating the efficient reallocation of labor and capital.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They emphasize the role of political institutions in specifying efficient property rights and ensuring their effective enforcement. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, reducing transaction costs and thereby promoting economic development. The evolution of these institutions was critical because they provided a stable environment for investment and innovation, which are essential for long-term economic growth. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate can be complex. North and Weingast highlight that these political institutions complement reputation-building strategies by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). North's broader argument is that traditional neoclassical models, which focus on population, savings, and technological knowledge, miss the crucial role of institutions. He contends that these economic inputs are necessary but not sufficient for growth; the institutional framework that governs their use is equally important. Economic development is a function of resource availability and the institutional context in which those resources are managed.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Technological progress, not institutional frameworks, is the primary engine of economic development. Technological advancements often rely on existing knowledge and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.54). For instance, Mokyr notes that much invention during the Industrial Revolution involved recombining existing techniques (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets similarly highlights how technological innovations are closely tied to scientific progress and the specific needs of the country in which they originate (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interconnectedness suggests that economic growth is a function of institutional support and the continuous generation and application of new knowledge. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be irreversible, institutional progress is less so (Mokyr 2005, p.54). In essence, the sustainability of economic improvement depends on both technological advancements and the complementary role of institutions in managing these innovations effectively.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with technological progress and institutional development both playing crucial roles. Institutions provide the framework necessary for managing technological advancements effectively, ensuring that innovations are integrated into the economy sustainably (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in the historical context of modern economic growth, where technological innovation has driven significant economic changes (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Institutions facilitate the efficient use of these innovations by providing property rights and enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs and enhance market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The continuous generation and application of new knowledge are essential for long-run economic growth. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be irreversible, institutional progress is less so (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that the sustainability of economic improvement depends on both technological advancements and the complementary role of institutions in managing these innovations effectively. Institutions matter in economic growth because they provide a foundation for efficient market exchanges and property rights enforcement, which are critical for long-run economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between institutional progress being less irreversible than technological progress is shown by Hornbeck's analysis of barbed wire fencing. This technology significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). The introduction of barbed wire shows how institutions can adapt to new technologies, demonstrating that institutional progress is possible and crucial for leveraging technological advancements effectively.
