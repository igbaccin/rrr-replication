The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that addresses how societal structures and rules influence sustained prosperity over extended periods. This question matters because it challenges purely neoclassical views that focus on population growth and savings as the primary determinants of economic development. Instead, it posits institutions, formal and informal rules, norms, and organizations, as critical drivers of economic outcomes. One key disagreement revolves around whether institutions facilitate economic growth by enhancing efficiency or by mitigating distributional conflicts. Efficiency theorists argue that institutions reduce transaction costs and foster market exchanges, thereby promoting economic activity (North 1989, p.6). Those who emphasize distributional conflicts suggest that core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, are shaped more by power dynamics than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

Institutions facilitate long-run growth through their impact on technological progress. Institutions can support technological advancements by ensuring the efficient use of innovations and providing a stable environment for scientific discovery. Kuznets highlights that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which requires institutional adjustments to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Similarly, Mokyr emphasizes the role of institutions in complementing technological progress, noting that without institutional advancements, even significant technological improvements might not lead to sustainable economic improvement. This suggests that institutions play a crucial role in sustaining long-term economic growth by ensuring that technological innovations are effectively integrated into the economy (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional structures imposed during colonialism were designed to extract rents for European colonists, which shaped long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This extractive nature of institutions was influenced by factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns. The persistence of these institutions over time has had a lasting impact on the economic performance of former colonies, highlighting the critical role of institutional quality in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This institutional change facilitated more efficient land use and increased productivity. Barbed wire allowed farmers to enclose and protect their lands, which was crucial for agricultural development in the region. The technology directly improved farming practices by enabling better control over livestock and reducing damage from stray animals. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) notes that the effects of barbed wire were not solely technological; its primary impact came from enhancing property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This institutional innovation allowed farmers to invest more confidently in their land, knowing that their efforts would be protected. Pamuk (2007, p.22) highlights how similar institutional changes can drive long-term economic growth by enabling more efficient use of resources and increased productivity gains (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The enhanced protection of property rights through barbed wire fencing exemplifies how specific institutional changes can have profound effects on economic development, illustrating the broader role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts shaped institutions more than efficiency considerations in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) argue that institutions such as serfdom and craft guilds were driven by power dynamics rather than efficiency. These institutions emerged due to conflicts over resource distribution, with powerful groups imposing rules that favored their interests. For instance, serfdom arose in contexts where labor was scarce relative to land (Domar 1970, p.7). This scarcity created a situation where those controlling the land could extract more value from serfs, who had limited alternatives for employment. Similarly, craft guilds often acted as cartels, restricting competition and maintaining high prices for their members' goods at the expense of consumers.

The efficiency approach to institutions has been widely applied, with many premodern institutions reinterpreted as efficient solutions to specific problems (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) shows that this approach can overlook the distributional aspects. The merchant guilds, for example, were often seen as inefficient cartels, but they provided security and contract enforcement as "club goods" in certain periods. This perspective highlights how institutional forms can be efficient under specific conditions but may not always align with overall economic efficiency. Domar (1970, p.7) further illustrates this point by noting that technological progress and capital accumulation do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor, depending on various factors such as population growth and agricultural techniques (Domar 1970, p.7; Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that the persistence of inefficient institutions can be explained by the interests of powerful groups rather than a lack of technological progress or capital.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) examine how institutions shape long-run economic growth by focusing on the role of political factors in fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework is crucial for sustaining economic development over the long term. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that these institutions do not operate in isolation; they complement reputation-building strategies by providing constraints that prevent reneging on agreements (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The Glorious Revolution of 1688 serves as a pivotal example, where institutional changes ensured that the state could maintain its commitments to constituents, thereby enhancing economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further argues against neoclassical theories by emphasizing that economic output is determined by inputs and technology but also by institutional frameworks (North 1989, p.5). These findings underscore how political institutions can foster secure property rights and support sustained economic growth through credible commitments, which are essential for long-term economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) posits that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, propels economic growth through a process that involves recombining existing techniques and analogical thinking. This mechanism fosters innovation by alerting inventors to gaps in current technologies, thereby preventing wasted resources. Kuznets's framework complements this view by emphasizing that mass application of technological innovations is closely linked to scientific progress, creating a positive feedback loop (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets (1973, p.10) also acknowledges that while the Industrial Revolution in England produced major inventions tailored to specific needs, institutions are necessary for sustainable economic improvement. This suggests that while technology may drive initial growth spurts, institutions are crucial for sustaining these gains over the long run.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Kuznets' observation that technological progress alone does not guarantee sustained economic improvement (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr's warning about the erosion of economic gains due to rent-seeking behavior without institutional progress echoes this sentiment (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions are essential for translating technological advancements into long-term economic benefits. North's work emphasizes that institutions define property rights and enforce them, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The efficiency of institutions in fostering economic growth is a recurring theme across different historical contexts. Ogilvie challenges the notion that pre-industrial European institutions were merely inefficient cartels, arguing instead that they provided essential security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective aligns with Hornbeck's findings on the American Plains, where barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development by securing property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Both cases illustrate how institutions can adapt to specific contexts to drive economic growth.

The impact of institutional changes introduced by colonialism further highlights the critical role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that former European colonies experienced a reversal in relative incomes due to these institutional changes, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This finding underscores how institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic development, depending on their design and enforcement. The historical evidence of colonialism and its institutional legacy provides a clear example of how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions are crucial for sustaining technological progress and driving long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while Kuznets shows that technological progress is driven by advancements in science and technology tailored to specific needs (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The historical evidence of colonialism and its institutional legacy settles this disagreement. It shows that the design of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly impacts long-run economic growth.
