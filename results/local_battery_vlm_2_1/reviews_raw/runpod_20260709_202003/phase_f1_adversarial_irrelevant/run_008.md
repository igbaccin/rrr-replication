The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is an issue at the heart of debates over the drivers of development and stagnation. At stake intellectually are competing explanations for why some economies thrive while others languish, with implications for both historical understanding and contemporary policy-making. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutional frameworks primarily facilitate or constrain economic growth, and if so, through which mechanisms. One key position is that institutions provide the stable rules and enforcement necessary for markets to function effectively, thereby fostering growth. This view emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and reliable contracting mechanisms in reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. A competing perspective posits that geographic factors or resource endowments hold greater sway over economic outcomes. Moreover, some scholars identify antecedent conditions such as colonial legacies that shape institutional development, thus influencing long-run growth trajectories.

One stream of scholarship highlights the critical role of political institutions in underpinning economic growth by ensuring credible commitments and secure property rights. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.2). Their analysis underscores how institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations. This perspective is complemented by North’s broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). By specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, political institutions create an environment where individuals can confidently engage in transactions with strangers, thereby enabling the productivity gains associated with specialization and division of labor. The institutional reliability that underpins this process is essential for realizing the potential benefits of impersonal exchange in a growing economy.

Another stream identifies antecedent conditions that shape institutional development and thus influence long-run economic growth trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with significant implications for subsequent economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This perspective complements Acemoglu et al.'s argument that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Their analysis highlights how institutions molded by politically influential groups can be exploitative, intended to maximize rents and not encourage long-term growth. This stream highlights the importance of understanding historical contexts and power dynamics in shaping institutions that either facilitate or constrain economic development. The role of colonial legacies in determining institutional frameworks provides a critical antecedent condition for understanding contemporary economic disparities.

The question of how institutions shape long-term economic outcomes remains open, but it is clear that their influence is profound and multifaceted.

North and Weingast highlight that the Glorious Revolution marked a critical shift where political institutions began to effectively enforce rules, thereby securing property rights. This institutional change enabled market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitated long-run economic development.

The role of credible commitment underpins growth; it ensures that rulers adhere to agreements with their constituents (North 1989, p.6). The establishment of reliable political frameworks reduced uncertainties associated with complex economic interactions. This reliability was essential for fostering confidence in remote transactions and enabling specialization and division of labor, which are key drivers of economic growth. The relationship between institutional evolution and economic outcomes is bidirectional; it is influenced by broader social dynamics.

Efficient markets require the establishment of property rights as well as a reliable medium of exchange (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework allows individuals to engage in transactions with strangers, expanding the scope of economic activity. North's analysis emphasizes that the evolution of complex social frameworks is contingent on institutions that can reduce uncertainties and provide reliable enforcement mechanisms. The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and enforce them effectively is a necessary condition for long-run economic growth.

This perspective is supported by the findings of Acemoglu et al., who demonstrated a high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies and the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, were not conducive to private property rights or market development, thereby hindering economic growth. The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to affect current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on long-run economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2).

This mechanism underscores how geographic factors, such as disease environments, indirectly shaped institutional outcomes through their impact on settlement patterns and colonial policies. The resulting institutional frameworks were crucial in determining long-term economic trajectories, with extractive institutions impeding growth and inclusive ones fostering development. Acemoglu et al. also note that the relationship between mortality rates and institutional quality is probabilistic, influenced by a complex interplay of historical, political, and economic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This nuanced view underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its impact on economic growth, suggesting that while colonial institutions were critical, their effects were mediated by various contextual variables.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This institutional bias restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by enabling elites to protect their interests at the expense of broader economic development. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the control over land and resources, where elites implemented policies that concentrated wealth and power in their hands. The differentials in paths of development have long been a central concern for scholars studying Latin America (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions shaped both the distribution of economic opportunities and the broader socio-political landscape, resulting in entrenched inequalities that hindered inclusive growth. For instance, Hornbeck (2010, p.34) demonstrates how agricultural development on the American Plains was significantly enhanced by the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This example illustrates how institutional changes can directly impact economic productivity and development, even if they do not always lead to more inclusive growth patterns. The persistence of inequality in these societies highlights the enduring effects of initial institutional arrangements on long-term economic trajectories.

Van Zanden argues that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not translate into improved living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2), a finding shared by Komlos, who shows that despite growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.13). Both authors challenge conventional views of early modern economic growth, suggesting that aggregate measures such as GDP per capita do not fully capture the impact on living standards. Van Zanden criticizes optimists who overlook the long-run decline in real wages and miscalculate pre-industrial income levels (van Zanden 1999, p.2), while Komlos emphasizes the divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower-income groups, highlighting how relative price effects influenced consumption patterns of nutrients and industrial commodities during this period (Komlos 1998, p.8). The nuanced relationship between economic growth and living standards suggests that institutional factors, such as income distribution and childhood nourishment, play a critical role in translating economic development into improved well-being. The institutional mechanisms driving these outcomes remain largely unexplored in this literature.

Pamuk (2007, p.8) and Broadberry and Gupta (Pamuk 2007, p.15; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23) share the finding that historical events like the Black Death significantly altered economic conditions in Europe. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) similarly argue for pre-1800 divergences in economic conditions, highlighting how differences in labor productivity between tradable and non-tradable sectors influenced wages and prices. The Black Death precipitated a severe labor shortage, which in turn drove up wages and encouraged human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.8). The wage gap that emerged from these structural changes had profound implications for the long-term economic development of Europe. The varying impact of these historical events underscores the complex interplay between demographic shocks and economic development, shaping long-run growth trajectories in ways that are still being explored.

Austin and Bryant both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). The thesis does not account for the complex interplay between economic rent and growth, which have often been joint products in various historical contexts. Austin notes that colonial regimes sometimes supported African economic enterprise, indicating that institutions favouring growth in one context may not be optimal in others (Austin 2008, p.1).

Bryant highlights a structural differentiation between European agriculture and Chinese peasant households, where the latter lacked the articulations between town and country that fostered industrialized commodity production. This pattern limited economies of scale and capital accumulation necessary for expansion in China. The difference in labour substitution is stark: while Europe saw a shift from human to animal labour, Chinese peasants were driven into crops-only patterns, reversing this substitution (Bryant 2006, p.28). European institutions facilitated a more synergistic mix of grain production and livestock rearing, significantly improving diets and productivity.

The demographic realities of land scarcity and cheap labour in China decisively governed economic considerations, even resisting the spread of labour-saving technologies. This institutional context limited the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production (Bryant 2006, p.29). The historical paths of Europe and China thus reveal that institutions shaped by specific economic conditions can either foster or hinder long-run economic growth.

This divergence highlights how institutions can influence economic outcomes over the long run.
