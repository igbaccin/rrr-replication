The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of inquiry that addresses fundamental questions about the drivers of prosperity and development. At stake intellectually is understanding whether institutional frameworks are essential catalysts for sustained economic advancement or merely incidental factors. The live disagreement revolves around two primary positions. First, some scholars emphasise that institutions facilitate economic growth by providing stable rules, property rights, and incentives that foster innovation and investment. Second, others argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role in adapting to or exploiting these advancements. The interplay between technology and institutions therefore becomes crucial for understanding long-term economic performance.

One stream of research highlights how institutions complement technological progress by ensuring sustainability through coordinated efforts. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that without institutional progress, technological improvements can be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviour (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) expands on this idea by noting that the mass application of technical innovations is closely linked with scientific advancements. Institutions are thus essential for harnessing these innovations effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress is further underscored by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who posits that well-functioning labour markets and capital markets, along with effective human capital formation, contribute to long-term economic growth. A low skill premium, indicative of efficient markets and robust institutions, enhances investment in human capital (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This stream illustrates how institutions can create an environment conducive to technological progress, thereby driving long-run economic growth.

Another stream focuses on the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. North (1989, p.6) argues that political institutions specifying property rights and providing enforcement are crucial for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges and supports a growing economy by increasing specialization and division of labor. Bryant (2006, p.11) provides historical context, noting that the lack of structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors in China limited economic dynamism (Bryant 2006, p.29). The absence of effective institutions hindered technological adoption and capital accumulation, demonstrating how institutional frameworks can either enhance or impede long-term growth.

Institutions are also pivotal in shaping the catch-up process in economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) points out that the same technological opportunities driving modernization also influence the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic highlights how institutions can accelerate the adoption of new technologies and foster economic convergence. Kuznets' observation on the necessity for institutional adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations further supports this view, suggesting that institutions play a vital role in adapting to technological changes and driving sustained growth.

However, the role of political institutions extends beyond mere economic exchange, as highlighted by North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.6) elaborates on how the emergence of such institutions should facilitate market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs and fostering economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. This is because these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of commitments made to constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The implication is that political stability and credible commitment underpin economic growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and enforceable. This is because these institutions ensure that rulers adhere to the rules of economic exchange, thereby fostering a stable environment for investment and growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) emphasize that the growth of useful knowledge is a critical factor in driving long-run economic growth. This process relies on a complex interplay between existing techniques and innovative thinking, where new inventions often build upon established technologies. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights how technological progress frequently involves recombining existing techniques and leveraging analogical thinking to transform familiar ideas into novel solutions. Kuznets (1973, p.10) adds that this process requires institutional and ideological adjustments as well as the accumulation of knowledge. These adjustments are necessary for efficiently utilizing innovations, ensuring that technological advancements translate into sustained economic growth. This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, it must be supported by robust institutions to ensure long-term economic benefits.

The papers in this cluster focus on specific historical factors such as technological change and agricultural development rather than institutions' role in long-run economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Komlos 1998, p.8). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological advancement facilitated more efficient use of land, allowing for increased crop yields and economic productivity. The paper argues that despite overall economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that technological advancements alone may not always translate into widespread improvements in living standards without complementary institutional support. Temin (2002, p.4) adds nuance by noting that rapid economic growth can also be driven by disequilibria in labor allocation between sectors, such as agriculture and industry. These findings collectively illustrate how specific historical factors, particularly technological changes, have shaped economic development trajectories. They also underscore the importance of considering broader institutional contexts to fully understand long-term economic outcomes.

Economic inequality and structural changes influence long-term economic performance by shaping labor markets and human capital investment (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions facilitating the efficient transfer of skills are crucial for sustaining low skill premiums, which in turn drive long-term growth. These institutions, such as guilds, played a significant role in ensuring that apprenticeships were effectively managed and human capital was adequately developed. Pamuk (2007, p.22) highlights how structural changes initiated by events like the Black Death led to shifts in labor markets, with wages increasing relative to rents and the price of capital. These changes fostered technological advancements, particularly within urban guilds, which were key producers of new technology (Pamuk 2007, p.22). However, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) qualify this by noting that in societies where inequality was high, elites often used government policies to restrict competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. This dynamic underscores how structural changes and economic inequality can interact with institutional frameworks to either promote or hinder long-term economic growth.

They share the view that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and cannot be reduced to simple convergence hypotheses. Additionally, growth in productivity increases aggregate output, broadening the horizon of scale-dependent technological progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

The dynamics of technological advance and social change are not uniform across societies. This dynamic contrasts with China's pattern of concentrating handicrafts within peasant households, which limited economies of scale and capital accumulation necessary for industrial expansion. The demographic realities of land scarcity and abundant cheap labor in China governed economic considerations, even resisting the spread of labor-saving technologies and the concentration of employment in town workshops and factories (Bryant 2006, p.29). This historical context underscores how institutional frameworks and social structures can either facilitate or impede technological advancements and economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring efficient resource allocation. Mokyr shows that the sustainability of economic improvement hinges on institutional progress complementing technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without effective institutions, rent-seeking behavior can erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The role of institutions in fostering technological progress is further underscored by the differences between European and Chinese economic trajectories. Europe's guilds, which guaranteed fair execution of apprentice contracts and facilitated skill transfer, played a crucial role in economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.19). In contrast, China's institutional framework concentrated handicrafts within peasant households, limiting economies of scale and capital accumulation necessary for industrial expansion (Bryant 2006, p.29).

Technological progress is not merely about the accumulation of knowledge; it also requires institutions that support its application. Abramovitz highlights that technological opportunity permits rapid growth by modernization, which encourages rapid growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). North contends that efficient property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6), whereas van Zanden points out that a low skill premium reflects access to efficient labor markets and institutions for human capital formation, which in turn drive long-term economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The historical contrast between Europe and China underscores the importance of institutions in shaping technological progress. While Europe's institutional framework fostered industrial expansion through economies of scale and capital accumulation, China's demographic realities and institutional constraints limited similar advancements.

The precise role of institutions in long-run economic growth is a matter of agreement that institutions matter. All scholars agree that institutions facilitate technological progress (North 1989, p.5; van Zanden 2009, p.27). The remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which these institutions must be formalized or can emerge organically. While North emphasizes the need for political institutions to specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, van Zanden highlights the importance of informal institutions that support human capital formation and efficient labor markets. The historical contrast between Europe's guilds and China's peasant households provides a compelling example: the skill premium in Europe was lower because of formalized training mechanisms (van Zanden 2009, p.19).
