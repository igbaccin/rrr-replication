The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked significant debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural attitudes, play more significant roles. Institutions can shape long-term economic trajectories by influencing property rights, governance structures, and market regulations. However, some scholars argue that institutional frameworks alone do not account for sustained economic development, suggesting that other variables must be considered in tandem.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the political and economic institutions that underpin secure property rights and foster economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the importance of credible commitments by regimes to enforce property rights, which are crucial for economic development. This perspective is complemented by North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.2). He posits that the evolution of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges. This reduction in transaction costs is essential for fostering a growing economy, as it allows for increased specialization and division of labor. The interplay between credible commitments and institutional reliability highlights how robust frameworks can support economic growth by ensuring that transactions are secure and predictable. North's argument (North 1989, p.2) aligns with Broadberry and Gupta's findings (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23), which suggest that the divergence in economic performance between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, underscoring the long-term impact of institutional differences on economic outcomes.

The debate extends to how institutions influence economic growth through the lens of colonialism and its legacy. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective highlights how institutions can be extractive and shaped by politically powerful groups, thereby hindering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.11) provides a complementary view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across time and space, and that other factors, such as production techniques and environmental conditions, also play crucial roles.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore the significance of political factors that underpin economic growth, emphasizing that effective enforcement of rules and constraints on state behavior are crucial for maintaining commitments to constituents. This institutional framework is essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.6) extends this argument by highlighting the importance of reliable institutions in managing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, which are inevitable as economies grow more specialized and interconnected. This institutional reliability is critical for enabling individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even those with whom they have no personal knowledge or ongoing exchange relationships (North 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.6) also notes that developing efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms requires a medium of exchange with reliable features. This suggests that while institutional frameworks are necessary for economic growth, their effectiveness depends on the broader economic context in which they operate. The political factors underpinning economic growth must be considered alongside the evolving social frameworks and market conditions that shape long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. along with Acemoglu and Robinson (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. This perspective highlights how extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, rather than fostering long-term development, significantly impacted current income per capita. This extractive institutional framework persisted because it was designed to serve the interests of colonial powers rather than promote economic growth in the colonies themselves. For instance, European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies; high mortality led to extractive institutions that persist today and have a significant impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further emphasize how differences in European settlements due to mortality rates created exogenous variation, leading to differing institutional outcomes across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these extractive institutions over time suggests that while initial conditions set by colonial powers were crucial, subsequent political and economic developments also played a role in maintaining or altering these institutions.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman share a finding that distributional conflicts played a key role in shaping economic institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Ogilvie argues that efficiency explanations for pre-industrial European institutions fail to account for the persistence of seemingly inefficient practices, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions, far from being efficient solutions to transactional challenges, were often maintained due to power struggles among elites who benefited from their control over resources and labor. For instance, merchant guilds in early Europe are often portrayed as inefficient cartels, but Ogilvie suggests they emerged as a means for elites to maintain their dominance by controlling trade routes and markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Sokoloff and Engerman expand this perspective to New World societies, showing that institutions in these regions were shaped by the need of elites to maintain control over abundant land and natural resources. This was done through policies that restricted competition and ensured elite advantage (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of inequality in these societies can be directly linked to institutional frameworks that favored the powerful, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts shaped institutions not just in pre-industrial Europe but also in colonial contexts, where elite interests drove institutional development.

The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding long-term economic growth. If institutions are primarily shaped by power dynamics rather than efficiency concerns, then efforts to promote growth through institutional reform must account for the underlying distributional conflicts. Ogilvie notes that studying individual institutions in isolation is insufficient; instead, entire institutional frameworks must be considered (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This approach underscores the need for a holistic view of economic development, one that recognizes the interconnected nature of institutions and the role of power dynamics in their evolution.

These challenges were often compounded by the unequal distribution of economic gains within societies. Van Zanden notes that optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, such as those proposed by De Vries and Van der Woude, may overlook critical factors contributing to stagnation in living standards. Komlos provides a complementary perspective, arguing that the biological well-being of individuals declined during the Industrial Revolution due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.8). This divergence between economic growth and improvements in living conditions suggests that economic development alone is not sufficient to enhance overall welfare. Additionally, Komlos' analysis underscores the importance of relative price effects on food consumption patterns, indicating that technological changes and capital accumulation in agriculture lagged behind those in industry. These findings imply that the distribution of resources and the specific economic conditions of different sectors play crucial roles in determining the impact of growth on living standards. While van Zanden focuses on the period between 1500 and 1800, Komlos extends this analysis to the Industrial Revolution, revealing a consistent pattern of economic growth failing to translate into improved biological well-being for large segments of the population. This pattern persists even as cities grow rapidly in size, highlighting the complexity of factors influencing living standards during periods of economic expansion.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that economic divergence is a complex process shaped by historical contexts and specific economic conditions rather than institutions. For example, labour productivity in the tradable goods sector was significantly lower in Asia compared to Europe before 1800. This disparity in productivity is a key factor in understanding the great divergence between these regions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Bryant (2006, p.29) adds that structural differentiation and economies of scale were more pronounced in European agriculture, which released labour for industrial employments while Chinese peasants remained tied to subsistence farming, a pattern that limited both demand stimulus and capital accumulation necessary for industrial growth (Bryant 2006, p.29; Bryant 2006, p.28). The role of institutions in shaping these historical trajectories remains under-explored.

The literature on African economic development and institutions focuses on specific historical factors that influenced economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment essential for development. He notes that by the 1990s, development policy had expanded to include governance, with a focus on institutions and organizations (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shift enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists. The role of institutions is pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth because they create an environment conducive to investment, which drives development. His findings indicate that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding negatively impacted subsequent development. These historical factors underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, as they influence the allocation of resources and labor. Nunn (2008, p.3) further elaborates on geographic controls such as rainfall and temperature that affect agricultural productivity (Nunn 2008, p.16). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, other factors like geography also play a significant role in long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing resource allocation and labor productivity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is exemplified by the political factors underpinning economic growth in seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions was critical for maintaining market exchanges and ensuring economic stability. Similarly, North (1989, p.2) emphasizes the necessity of institutional reliability to reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations. The development of efficient property rights allows individuals to engage confidently in impersonal exchange relationships.

The role of institutions extends beyond securing property rights; they also facilitate market exchanges and economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This is evident in the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms. The development of such institutions leads to falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges.

Institutions are also influenced by historical factors and economic development processes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth. This is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.23) analysis of labor productivity in the tradable goods sector, which highlights how low silver wages in Asia reflect lower productivity due to institutional factors. The historical context of economic development in different regions underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further complicated by geographic and environmental factors. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that low labor productivity in Africa can be understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment. This perspective aligns with van Zanden's (1999, p.2) analysis of long-term economic growth in Europe between 1500 and 1800, which shows that traditional agriculture was able to generate growth in total factor productivity despite environmental challenges.
