The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists. At stake is the understanding of how societies achieve sustained prosperity and whether certain institutional frameworks are more conducive to economic development than others. The live disagreement revolves around two main positions: one emphasizes the primacy of technological progress, while the other highlights the crucial role of institutions in facilitating economic growth. Technological optimists argue that advancements in technology drive economic growth by increasing productivity and enabling innovation. This perspective suggests that institutional changes follow technological developments, often viewed as necessary adjustments (North 1989, p.6). Institutionalists argue that strong institutions are key to leveraging technological progress and ensuring its sustainable economic impact. They assert that well-designed institutions can reduce transaction costs, protect property rights, and foster an environment conducive to innovation and investment (Hornbeck 2010, p.4; North 1989, p.5). The interplay between these positions underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where both technology and institutions play interconnected roles.

Institutions matter in economic growth because they shape the environment in which technological progress can thrive. This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges and encourages investment, thereby fostering economic development. This historical case illustrates how institutional changes can directly impact economic growth by addressing specific challenges faced by different sectors. Furthermore, institutions that promote technological progress are essential for sustained economic improvement. The growth of useful knowledge is a key driver of economic growth, and without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of this growth would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54; North 1989, p.6). Institutions play a critical role in coordinating efforts to address coordination failures and ensure that technological advancements are effectively integrated into the economy. This coordination is essential for maintaining long-term economic growth and preventing the erosion of gains achieved through technological progress.

Institutional changes can also influence the dynamics of technological advance and social change, contributing to the catch-up process in economic growth. The catch-up process includes both technological modernization and broader social and institutional adjustments that enable rapid growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Kuznets 1973, p.4). For instance, the adoption of new technologies often requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to ensure their efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). These adjustments can include changes in property rights, legal frameworks, and educational systems, all of which are necessary for harnessing the full potential of technological innovations. Moreover, backwardness itself carries an opportunity for modernization in both disembodied and embodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This means that countries with less developed institutional frameworks can catch up by adopting and adapting technologies from more advanced economies, provided they have the right institutions in place to support this process. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, encompassing both direct and indirect effects on technological progress and social change.

The relationship between institutions and technological progress is further complicated by the fact that institutions can be shaped by politically powerful groups with varying interests (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These groups may design institutions to maximize their own rents rather than promote long-run economic growth. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the political economy of institutional change and how it affects economic development (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). For example, in the context of former European colonies, equilibrium institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This institutional design had significant implications for economic development and contributed to the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies. The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth involves creating efficient frameworks and understanding political dynamics that influence their design and implementation.

This political influence on institutional design can be traced back to the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, as North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue. They identify political factors as underpinning economic growth and the development of markets. The evolution of political institutions is key to understanding how these rules are enforced and changed, which can be seen in the success of capital markets during this period. This commitment to rules by the regime or sovereign is critical for maintaining long-term economic growth. This perspective suggests that political stability and secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth.

These findings resonate with North's broader argument (North 1989, p.5) on the importance of institutions in economic growth, which contrasts with neoclassical theories emphasizing population and savings as principal determinants. Institutions, by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. This reduction in costs per transaction, coupled with increasing specialization and division of labor, leads to a growing proportion of GNP dedicated to the transactions sector (North 1989, p.5). This view does not imply that neoclassical factors are irrelevant; it highlights that institutions play a complementary role in shaping economic outcomes.

This complementary role is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27), who document how colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes. The nature of these institutions was not merely a function of geography but was fundamentally driven by the political and economic interests of the colonizers. The persistence of these institutions into the modern era has had lasting effects on economic performance. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that the institutional legacy of colonialism explains a significant portion of current income differences among former colonies. This underscores how the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to shape contemporary economic trajectories. It is important to note that while institutions play a crucial role, other factors such as technological advancements and global economic shifts also interact with these institutional frameworks to determine long-run economic growth.

Temin (2002, p.4) posits that Europe's post-WWII economic growth was driven by an unusual disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, while Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that technological advance and social change are key drivers of the catch-up process in economic growth. This cluster examines the dynamics of economic growth through various lenses, including technological advance, social change, and labor allocation, without explicitly linking these factors to institutional frameworks.

Abramovitz extends this by emphasizing the importance of technological progress in reducing the price of capital goods relative to labor, which in turn accelerates the growth of the capital stock. This interplay between technological advancement and labor allocation creates a complex environment where economic growth is influenced by both institutional frameworks and broader socio-economic dynamics (Temin 2002, p.3; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The implications are far-reaching: while institutions provide the foundation, it is the interaction with technological progress and labor shifts that truly drives long-run economic growth.

Secure property rights are vital for agricultural development, as shown by Hornbeck’s examination of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains and Domar's exploration of land-to-labor ratios. These scholars share the view that enhancing farmers' ability to protect their land leads to significant economic gains (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing protected farmers' property and reduced externalities from livestock damage, thereby fostering agricultural development. This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their investments and mitigate losses, creating a more stable environment for economic growth. Domar’s analysis complements this by illustrating how high land-to-labor ratios and scarce labor conditions can drive the emergence of systems like slavery or serfdom, highlighting the critical role of property rights in shaping economic structures. The implications are clear: secure property rights enable farmers to invest confidently in their land, leading to increased productivity and economic development. Domar observes that technological progress and capital accumulation do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor (Domar 1970, p.6). This qualifies the straightforward benefits of secure property rights, suggesting that the interaction between property rights and other socio-economic factors is complex and multifaceted.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective posits that institutions are less critical to economic development. Mokyr discusses how technological advancement depends on existing techniques, often involving "analogical" thinking where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel solutions (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets supports this view by emphasizing the interrelation between technological innovations and scientific progress, noting that mass application of these innovations provides a positive feedback loop for further advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). In other words, while technological progress is crucial, it must be supported by effective institutions to ensure long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding sustained economic development. Institutions provide the framework within which technological innovations are applied and scaled. For example, Kuznets argues that technological progress is closely connected with scientific advancements, creating a positive feedback loop (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This dynamic is supported by Mokyr's observation on the role of complementary institutional progress in sustaining economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54), highlighting that without effective institutions, rent-seeking behavior and coordination failures can undermine growth.

Institutions also play a critical role in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North emphasizes that political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is aligned with Hornbeck's findings on the impact of barbed wire fencing, which enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4), illustrating how institutional changes can drive agricultural development. This shared finding shows that effective institutions are necessary for translating technological advancements into sustained economic growth.

While technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the effectiveness of this progress depends on institutional support. Abramovitz notes that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This view is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly impacted the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, emphasizing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Thus, technological progress and institutional development are interdependent. While the growth of useful knowledge drives economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54), it must be complemented by effective institutions to ensure sustainability (North 1989, p.6). The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is best understood through historical evidence such as the impact of barbed wire fencing on agricultural development, which shows that institutional changes can drive technological progress and economic growth.
