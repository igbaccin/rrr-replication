The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has spurred significant debate among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game in society, can systematically influence economic performance over extended periods. The disagreement centers around whether institutional factors are primary drivers of economic growth or merely secondary to other determinants such as technological progress and resource availability. Proponents of the former view argue that institutions establish the framework within which economic activities occur, thereby shaping incentives and constraints that guide individual and collective behavior. This perspective is pitted against those who emphasize the primacy of technological advancements and material resources in driving growth. Institutions matter because they create conditions for sustained economic development by providing stability and predictability. The question concerns how institutions influence long-term economic outcomes by interacting with other factors.

One stream of scholarship highlights the interplay between institutional progress and technological change, arguing that both are necessary for sustainable economic growth. Kuznets argues that modern economic growth is characterized by the mass application of technological innovations, which in turn relies on scientific advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This process involves technological improvements and institutional adjustments to efficiently utilize these innovations. Mokyr elaborates on this idea by noting that technological progress often depends on existing knowledge and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel applications (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Economic improvement sustainability depends on institutional progress that complements technological advancements, preventing rent-seeking behaviors from eroding gains. Institutions facilitate the coordination necessary for widespread adoption of new technologies, thus driving long-run economic growth.

Another stream identifies antecedent factors that trigger institutional development, thereby influencing economic outcomes. Kuznets posits that the accumulation of material and social technology necessitates institutional adjustments to efficiently harness these innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This view is complemented by Hornbeck's analysis of how barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage. The introduction of new technologies, such as barbed wire, can spur institutional changes that support economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). These findings underscore the importance of technological innovation in prompting institutional reforms that facilitate long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by studies that examine specific instances or more nuanced aspects of this relationship. North argues that political and economic institutions are crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which in turn drives economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Clark and Jacks' analysis of coal output during the Industrial Revolution, where increased demand for coal led to technological innovations in mining but required institutional frameworks to support this expansion. Institutional development is not a linear process; it involves complex interactions between technology, resources, and societal structures that collectively shape economic outcomes over time.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for technological progress and resource utilization. This interplay underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve in response to changing economic conditions and technological advancements.

The evolution of public choice institutions, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2), is pivotal in securing property rights and promoting economic growth. The evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England exemplifies how political frameworks can enhance market efficiency by specifying and enforcing "efficient" property rights, which in turn reduces transaction costs and facilitates economic exchanges. North (1989, p.5) further elaborates that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs like land, labor, and capital, overlook the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. In England during this period, secure property rights developed through political commitments that ensured rules were enforced and could be reliably changed when necessary. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) provide evidence from capital markets, demonstrating how these institutional changes led to remarkable success in economic growth by complementing reputation-building mechanisms with enforceable constraints on state behavior.

The Glorious Revolution serves as a pivotal example of how political institutions can endogenously support economic growth. By restricting the ex post behavior of the state, these institutions improved the state's credibility in maintaining its commitments, thereby fostering an environment where private wealth could be accumulated and invested without fear of expropriation. This institutional framework mitigated incentive problems and enhanced the efficacy of reputation mechanisms, ensuring economic exchanges occurred under predictable and enforceable rules. The interplay between political institutions and economic growth highlights how secure property rights, enforced through credible commitments, can drive long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the origins of pre-industrial economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). They argue these institutions were shaped by power dynamics between social groups, as well as efficiency considerations. For instance, merchant guilds in medieval Europe provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' for their members, which was efficient under specific conditions; however, this efficiency was a secondary outcome of the primary function of redistributing wealth and power among elites (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Similarly, Domar (1970, p.7) posits that slavery or serfdom emerged in response to high land-to-labor ratios and scarce labor, which created specific distributional conflicts. The implications of this view are significant: if institutions are primarily shaped by distributional conflicts, then changes in power dynamics can lead to institutional shifts that may not necessarily enhance economic growth. For example, the dissolution of feudalism in Europe was driven by changing power relations between lords and peasants, which altered the institutional landscape (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This suggests that understanding the long-run effects of institutions on economic growth requires a nuanced appreciation of the underlying distributional conflicts that shape them.

Acemoglu et al.'s work demonstrates that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their research shows that high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions. This pattern emerged because Europeans were reluctant to settle in regions with high disease environments, leading to the implementation of institutions designed to extract resources rather than promote local economic development. These institutions persisted over time, shaping long-run economic outcomes. This perspective highlights how colonial institutions were influenced by the political and economic interests of colonizing powers, in addition to geographic factors. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, illustrating the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Hornbeck finds that technological changes in agriculture, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). Agricultural improvements involved technological innovation and changing resource utilization methods. The effectiveness of barbed wire fencing in protecting property rights highlights that the impact of technological changes extends beyond direct productivity gains to include broader institutional and economic effects. These factors underscore the importance of resource utilization and technological adoption in shaping long-run economic growth, even if these developments are not directly linked to formal institutions. However, Clark and Jacks show that the expansion of coal output during the Industrial Revolution was primarily driven by increased demand rather than technological innovations in mining (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.5). This suggests that while technological changes can influence economic development, their impact is contingent on broader economic conditions and resource availability. The role of specific factors such as coal and barbed wire fencing illustrates how technological advancements and resource utilization can drive economic growth independently of institutional frameworks but are subject to the constraints imposed by demand and resource availability.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share the view that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr's work highlights how technological advancements are contingent on existing knowledge, often involving the recombination of known techniques. This process can be facilitated by analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. For example, during the Industrial Revolution, Cort's invention of the puddling and rolling process for iron production exemplifies this phenomenon. This perspective indicates that useful knowledge accumulation is pivotal and self-reinforcing. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be sustained and irreversible, institutional progress is necessary to complement it. Without such institutional support, the benefits of technological advancements might be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors, limiting long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores a nuanced view where technology drives growth but needs institutional frameworks to fully realize its potential.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and mitigating rent-seeking behaviors. Mokyr shows that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement is limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets also argues that technological advancements require institutional adjustments for efficient use, highlighting that mass application of innovations provides positive feedback to further advance technology (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North 1989, p.6).

Institutional frameworks are essential for realizing the full potential of technological progress. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.5). This institutional support is crucial for sustaining long-term economic improvement, as it prevents rent-seeking behaviors from undermining technological advancements (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by their ability to address distributional conflicts. Ogilvie shows that core economic institutions like serfdom and merchant guilds were efficient solutions to specific problems, such as providing security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions ensured that economic activities could proceed efficiently, even in the face of potential disruptions. This efficiency involved optimizing resource use and addressing broader social and political challenges. Institutions thus create an environment where technological progress can thrive by managing conflicts and ensuring stability.

The precise role of institutions is evidenced by their ability to enhance economic development through property rights protection, as shown by Hornbeck's study on barbed wire fencing (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This example illustrates how institutional changes can directly influence agricultural productivity and overall economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire not only improved technological efficiency but also protected farmers' investments, thereby encouraging further innovation.

The historical evidence shows that institutions are essential for sustaining long-term economic improvement by providing the necessary framework to support technological progress (North 1989, p.6). The precise nature of this institutional support remains a subject of debate. North argues that institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.5), while Ogilvie highlights their role in addressing distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains demonstrates how institutions can enhance economic development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This empirical comparison settles the disagreement, showing that institutions shape long-run economic growth through both efficiency gains and conflict resolution.
