The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that bridges economics and political science, addressing whether and how the rules governing societies influence their prosperity over extended periods. The focus here is on comprehending the mechanisms that enable economies to develop sustainably and avoid stagnation or decline. The live disagreement revolves around two primary positions: one emphasizes technological progress as the primary driver of economic growth, while the other highlights the crucial role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining this growth. Technological progress can spur economic development, but its effects may be limited without complementary institutional advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions, by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms, can reduce transaction costs and foster market exchanges (North 1989, p.6), thereby enabling the efficient use of technological innovations. The interplay between technology and institutions is complex, with each influencing the other in a dynamic process that shapes economic outcomes over time.

The role of institutions in fostering long-run growth becomes apparent when examining their impact on the adoption and implementation of new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.10) notes that technological progress often responds to specific needs within a country, suggesting that local institutional contexts can shape the direction and effectiveness of innovations. This reduction allows for increased market exchanges and specialization, which in turn drive economic growth. The dynamic between technological progress and institutional development is not unidirectional; rather, it involves a feedback loop where technological advancements create new opportunities for institutional change, which then facilitates further technological progress (North 1989, p.5). In this way, institutions play an active role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to the sustained adoption of new technologies.

Institutions also influence the process of catch-up and convergence in economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) highlights that backwardness can provide opportunities for modernization through both embodied and disembodied technology, suggesting that institutions play a role in facilitating this process. The interplay between technological progress and institutional development underscores the complex nature of catch-up processes, where institutions not only facilitate the adoption of new technologies but also shape the broader economic landscape in which this adoption occurs.

The role of institutions is further illustrated by their impact on property rights and the reduction of transaction costs. This perspective aligns with Hornbeck's findings that technological improvements, such as barbed wire fencing, enhance agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The interplay between technological progress and institutional development highlights the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to sustained economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affect current income per capita in former colonies. The authors establish this link through the mechanism of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. These institutions, which were shaped in part by politically powerful groups, often did not encourage economic development and instead focused on securing benefits for the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This extractive nature was particularly pronounced in colonies where high mortality rates among Europeans discouraged settlement and led to the establishment of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over local development. The persistence of these extractive institutions over time highlights how historical factors can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes.

The implications of this institutional legacy are profound: countries with a history of extractive colonial institutions often face challenges in achieving sustained economic growth. These institutions were designed to exploit resources for colonizers, creating a structural barrier to long-term economic prosperity. One downstream implication is that reforms aimed at promoting growth must address these deeply rooted institutional legacies; however, Acemoglu et al. also point out a complicating factor:  the reluctance of politically powerful groups to initiate institutional change because they would not be direct beneficiaries (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

This perspective posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels technological advancements, which in turn propel economic development. The dynamic interplay between technology and institutions is crucial: technological progress often depends on existing knowledge and techniques, facilitating a process of recombining and analogical thinking (Mokyr 2005, p.12). The interplay between technology and institutions indicates that economic growth depends on both technological advancements and complementary institutional changes to sustain long-term prosperity (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This implies that while technology drives growth, institutions play a critical role in managing and directing this process for enduring economic benefits.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8) both highlight that economic growth is multifaceted, involving various extensions beyond institutional changes (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Komlos 1998, p.8). Abramovitz observes that rapid progress through modernization encourages capital stock growth due to returns on investments in modernization and the relative price reduction of capital goods. This dynamic accelerates the rise of the capital-labor ratio, contributing to overall productivity increases. Komlos (1998, p.8) notes that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined because of insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). The relative price effect, where technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture lag behind industry, further complicates this scenario. This pattern suggests that while rapid modernization can drive economic growth, it does not guarantee improvements in all dimensions of human well-being. Komlos (1998, p.8) adds nuance by noting that population growth alone does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices; the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply also play crucial roles (Komlos 1998, p.12). This implies that while economic growth can be rapid, its benefits may not be evenly distributed across different aspects of societal well-being.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) both show that institutional efficiency can significantly enhance economic development by improving property rights protection (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates this through the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which enabled farmers on the American Plains to better secure their land and reduce damage from livestock. This institutional change led to enhanced agricultural development by creating a more stable environment for investment and growth. The downstream implication is that when institutional changes align with technological advancements, they can drive economic growth more effectively. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) qualifies this by noting that not all pre-modern institutions were efficient; some were driven by distributional conflicts rather than pure efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This suggests that while institutional efficiency is crucial for economic development, the specific context and motivations behind institutional changes must also be considered.

Colonial institutions fundamentally altered economic structures by reshaping property rights and governance frameworks (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the introduction of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and their effective enforcement in colonial settings facilitated market exchanges. The Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies how credible commitments by sovereigns to enforce rules can stabilize markets and drive economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate that the political institutions established during this period complemented reputation-building mechanisms, ensuring that property rights were secure and transactions were reliable (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional change was pivotal in maintaining long-term economic growth by preventing expropriation and ensuring stable bargaining between constituents. The Glorious Revolution's impact on economic development underscores how colonial institutional changes can have lasting effects on economic structures.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring stable property rights and transactional reliability (North 1989, p.6; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Abramovitz points out that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets elaborates on this by arguing that institutions are pivotal to harnessing technological innovations, which drive modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The Glorious Revolution serves as a historical example where political changes led to better enforcement of property rights and reliable transactions. This institutional framework was fundamental in maintaining long-term economic growth by preventing expropriation and ensuring stable bargaining between constituents.

The role of institutions extends beyond merely facilitating technological progress; they also mitigate coordination failures that can hinder sustainable economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr highlights the importance of institutional progress complementing technological advancements to ensure sustained economic growth. This is evident in how the development of institutions, such as those established during the Glorious Revolution, supported long-term growth by preventing rent-seeking behavior and ensuring that property rights were secure.

The efficiency approach to understanding pre-industrial institutions provides additional insights into their role in economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Ogilvie notes that various pre-modern institutions, initially seen as inefficient cartels, were actually efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. This perspective underscores how institutions can adapt to changing conditions to maintain efficiency, thereby supporting long-run economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains by Hornbeck provides a historical example of how technological changes can enhance property rights protection and reduce externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions drive growth. Kuznets shows that mass-uses of technical innovations based on recent scientific discoveries provide a positive feedback loop (Kuznets 1973, p.4), while Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are more significant than efficiency in shaping core economic institutions such as serfdom and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The historical evidence of the Glorious Revolution, which led to secure property rights and reliable transactions, settles this disagreement by showing how institutional changes can drive long-term growth through both technological progress and stable bargaining.
