The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and development studies. At stake is understanding how societies can sustainably enhance their productivity and prosperity over extended periods. One live disagreement concerns the extent to which institutional frameworks drive economic performance. On one hand, some scholars emphasize that institutions are crucial for facilitating technological progress and efficient resource allocation, thereby fostering long-term growth. On the other hand, there are those who argue that while institutions do play a role, they are not the primary drivers of economic development; rather, technological advancements and other exogenous factors are more decisive. Another point of contention is whether institutions primarily serve to maximize efficiency or to distribute power and resources among different groups within society. This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional structures interact with economic processes to shape long-term growth trajectories. To elucidate this, it helps to examine how institutions facilitate technological progress as well as the broader implications of these interactions on economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions play an instrumental role in fostering economic growth by shaping the political landscape to secure property rights. They assert that the evolution of political structures governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for creating a stable environment conducive to market exchanges and economic development, with this institutional framework facilitating lower transaction costs. This was achieved through the establishment of rules that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms, thereby reducing the costs per transaction and promoting increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The result was a growing proportion of GNP dedicated to market exchanges, which in turn fostered economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further explain how these institutions complemented reputation-building strategies by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional framework improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth; a critical factor demonstrated by the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.6) underscores that neoclassical models focusing on inputs like land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship fail to capture the full picture of economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping these factors through political and social structures is essential for understanding why some societies are rich while others lag behind (North 1989, p.5).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) contend that colonial institutions engineered by European settlers have been pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. This rent-seeking behavior was driven by politically powerful groups who saw little benefit in initiating institutional changes that would not directly enrich them. Thus, the institutions that emerged were extractive and aimed at securing wealth for the colonizers rather than promoting economic development for the colonies themselves. In regions where mortality was high, European powers implemented extractive institutions to maximize their returns with minimal investment. The persistence of these extractive institutions has significantly hindered long-term economic growth in many former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff & Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts rather than efficiency explain key economic institutions. These scholars demonstrate that institutions are not merely responses to economic challenges but are shaped by the distribution of power within societies. Ogilvie illustrates this through her analysis of pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. She contends that these institutions were often inefficient yet persisted because they served the interests of dominant groups who could enforce their preferences despite the economic costs. For instance, merchant guilds in early medieval Europe are shown to have been more about securing power for elites than optimizing market efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how institutions in New World societies with high inequality were structured to maintain elite advantages through policies that restricted competition and limited opportunities for social mobility. In regions like the Caribbean and South America, large plantations dominated by slave labor led to extreme wealth disparities, which were reinforced by institutional frameworks that protected these unequal distributions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). However, Ogilvie qualifies this perspective by noting that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they do not operate in isolation; other factors such as technological changes and international market dynamics also play significant roles. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic growth, where power dynamics are just one piece of a multifaceted puzzle.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates that barbed wire fencing was instrumental in agricultural development on the American Plains by enhancing farmers' ability to protect their property rights and mitigate externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated better resource management and increased productivity. The introduction of barbed wire reduced the incidence of livestock trespassing, thereby minimizing conflicts over property use and enabling a more efficient allocation of agricultural resources. Domar (1970, p.7) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress and capital accumulation can significantly alter the profitability of different labor systems, including slavery (Domar 1970, p.7). The increased efficiency in land use due to barbed wire fencing likely had downstream effects on labor productivity, as farmers could focus more on cultivation rather than defending their fields. However, Domar (1970, p.7) qualifies this by pointing out that the relative profitability of free versus slave labor depends on various factors, including technological progress and capital use (Domar 1970, p.6). This nuanced view suggests that while barbed wire fencing enhanced property rights and agricultural development, its impact on long-run economic growth was also influenced by broader economic conditions and technological advancements.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are necessary for economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Kuznets (1973, p.10) highlights the importance of scientific advances in driving technological innovation. He argues that many innovations are tailored to specific national needs, as seen during the Industrial Revolution in England. This suggests that technological progress is often driven by local demand and institutional context. Mokyr (2005, p.54) builds on this idea by emphasizing that technological advancements depend on existing knowledge and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel solutions. However, Mokyr also points out that economic growth requires complementary institutional progress to ensure the sustainability of improvements. Without such adjustments, rent-seeking behaviors could erode these gains. This interplay between technological innovation and institutional frameworks underscores the complexity of economic development, where both factors must evolve in tandem for sustained growth. Kuznets's observation about the tailoring of innovations to specific national needs suggests that local institutions play a crucial role in shaping the direction and impact of technological progress. Mokyr’s emphasis on institutional adjustments further illustrates that while technology drives growth, institutions are essential for harnessing its full potential. This dynamic highlights the importance of adaptive institutions in facilitating long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring efficient resource allocation. Kuznets's work highlights that the tailoring of innovations to specific national needs underscores the importance of local institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This is reinforced by Mokyr, who argues that without institutional adjustments to complement technological progress, sustainability would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr terms it, is far from monotonic and irreversible; it requires continuous adaptation. Institutions also facilitate the diffusion of knowledge, which is crucial for technological progress. Technology often depends on existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel ones (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process is enhanced by institutions that provide security and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs. North argues that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is a key factor in reducing costs per transaction.

Institutions also address distributional conflicts, which can hinder economic progress. Ogilvie shows that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds were not merely inefficient cartels but provided security and contract enforcement as club goods (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were solely driven by efficiency considerations. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups can maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For example, colonial institutions designed to benefit European colonists often did not foster economic development in the colonies themselves. This shows that while institutions are crucial for harnessing technological progress, their design and implementation can significantly influence whether they support or hinder economic growth.

The role of institutions in enhancing agricultural development further illustrates their importance in long-run economic growth. Hornbeck demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This technological innovation, combined with institutional changes, allowed for more efficient use of land and resources. Kuznets also emphasizes the importance of mass application of technological innovations, which is closely connected with further progress in science (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The positive feedback loop between technology and institutions underscores their interdependent relationship in driving economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate. All scholars agree that institutions matter. North shows that neoclassical models, which focus on population and savings, are misleading because they do not account for the crucial role of institutions (North 1989, p.5). However, Acemoglu et al. show that institutions designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth can hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from colonial institutions settles this disagreement. Colonial institutions often maximized rents for European colonists at the expense of long-run growth in the colonies themselves, demonstrating that institutional design is crucial for harnessing technological progress and achieving sustained economic growth.
