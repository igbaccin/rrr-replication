The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks either facilitate or hinder sustained economic advancement. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily endogenous responses to economic conditions or exogenous factors that drive economic outcomes. Some scholars contend that credible commitment mechanisms within institutions underpin growth by fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others argue that the role of institutions is overstated, and that geopolitical factors such as colonial legacies are more critical in shaping long-run economic trajectories (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The debate hinges on whether institutions are best understood as adaptive responses to economic pressures or as powerful determinants of economic performance.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.6) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in long-run economic development by fostering secure property rights. The institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for this purpose. North and Weingast point out that the commitment of the regime to enforce rules was essential for economic growth; they observed that political institutions can complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving their efficacy when reputation alone is insufficient. This endogeneity of political institutions means that restrictions on the state's ex post behavior enhance its ability to maintain agreements with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North further elaborates that effective enforcement and specification of property rights reduce uncertainties in a growing economy, enabling confidence in transactions even among strangers. This institutional reliability is crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The establishment of reliable institutions allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations without personal knowledge of their trading partners, thereby facilitating economic growth through specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Political institutions serve multiple purposes; they enforce property rights and provide a framework for market exchanges. North argues that as economies grow more complex, efficient product and factor markets, along with a reliable medium of exchange, become essential. This reduction in transaction costs is pivotal for sustaining long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts among elites, rather than efficiency-based institutional frameworks, significantly shaped economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.7); Sokoloff and Engerman 2000 (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)). These distributional conflicts involved wealth as well as political power and social status. Ogilvie argues that merchant guilds served multiple purposes; they provided security and contract enforcement and also maintained elite dominance through 'club goods' (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations. Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate how these distributional conflicts led to institutions that advantaged elites, particularly in New World societies, where extreme inequality persisted due to government policies restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000 (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4)). The persistence of inequality, in turn, influenced the evolution of institutions. This relationship is nuanced by Ogilvie's observation that these distributional conflicts often led to interdependent institutional frameworks which could either foster or hinder economic growth depending on their configuration (Ogilvie 2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.27)). These findings suggest that while institutions play a role in shaping long-run economic growth, they are themselves shaped by underlying social and political dynamics.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth by determining the extent to which countries were able to foster development and industrialization. The mortality rates influenced settlement patterns; high mortality in certain colonies meant fewer Europeans settled there, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions that persisted over time. These institutions were designed to extract resources efficiently. Acemoglu et al. show a correlation between these early institutional structures and current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This relationship is nuanced by the fact that politically powerful groups may resist changes that threaten their power or do not directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of extractive colonial institutions thus significantly affected long-run growth trajectories.

Hornbeck (2010, p.34) and Whatley (2018, p.2) examine how specific technologies influenced historical developments (Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Whatley 2018, p.2). Hornbeck (2010, p.34) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed for more efficient use of land and resources, directly impacting agricultural productivity. Whatley (2018, p.2), meanwhile, argues that gunpowder imports played a significant role in increasing slave exports through intertemporal production links and social processes. This supports the gun-slave cycle hypothesis, where European gunpowder technology increased "productivity" in capturing slaves (Whatley 2018, p.2). The technological advancements in both cases highlight how specific innovations can drive economic activities, even if they are not directly aimed at long-run growth. These studies do not fully address the broader question of institutional relevance to long-run economic growth. Whatley (2018, p.2) also notes that the dynamics of slave trading networks involved various intermediate imports like iron and steel (Whatley 2018, p.15), suggesting a complex interplay between technology, resources, and economic development that goes beyond simple productivity gains. This complexity underscores the need for further investigation into how technological factors interact with institutional structures to shape long-term economic outcomes.

This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, their impact on long-term economic outcomes is mediated by broader social and economic factors such as aggregate purchasing power (Komlos 1998, p.13; van Zanden 2009, p.19). This disconnect between economic growth and living standards is evident during the Industrial Revolution, where despite significant economic expansion, there was a decline in biological well-being due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). The skill premium plays a pivotal role in this dynamic: efficient labor markets, capital markets, and institutions for human capital formation are crucial for translating economic growth into improved living standards. When these institutions function well, they contribute to long-term economic performance by ensuring that households have access to the necessary resources (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The benefits of these institutions can be counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels, which may restrict entry and keep relative pay above market-clearing levels.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) argue that economic divergence between regions is influenced by complex historical paths shaped by factors such as labour productivity and colonial policies. Austin (2008, p.11) notes that the Atlantic slave trade was driven primarily by commercial interests, with Africans' labour having a higher exchange value in destination regions than in Africa (Austin 2008, p.11). This suggests that historical economic dynamics, including labour market conditions and productivity levels, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.11) highlights that colonial regimes often supported African economic enterprise, which could have implications for economic growth in those regions (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced view underscores how the interplay of historical factors and institutional frameworks can lead to varied economic trajectories over time.

The evolution of institutions plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. North and Weingast show that the development of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for establishing credible commitments to property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint on reneging, which is insufficiently policed by reputation alone. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions that improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize this point by arguing that equilibrium institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, who may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The relationship between economic rent and growth is context-dependent, meaning that institutions favourable to growth in one historical setting might not be optimal in another. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting how different institutional frameworks can lead to varied economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Austin supports this view by noting that colonial regimes often supported African economic enterprise, which could have implications for economic growth in those regions (Austin 2008, p.1).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by the fact that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of developing economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. North argues that as a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that relatively low silver wages in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector, which is indicative of institutional constraints on economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26).

The historical evidence shows that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, Broadberry and Gupta caution that the relationship between economic rent and growth is context-dependent (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23), meaning that the specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of silver wages and productivity in the tradable goods sector.
