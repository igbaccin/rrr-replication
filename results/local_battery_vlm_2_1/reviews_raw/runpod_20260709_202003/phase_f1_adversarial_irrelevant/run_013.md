The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. At stake intellectually is the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. The disagreement centers around two key positions: those who argue that strong property rights and effective governance foster sustained growth, and those who contend that institutions primarily reflect underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and for designing policies that promote long-term prosperity.

The first stream of scholarship focuses on the causal role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize the importance of political institutions in securing property rights and enforcing contracts, arguing that these factors underpin sustainable economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They note that institutions can complement reputation mechanisms by providing constraints that prevent reneging on agreements, thereby enhancing the efficacy of market transactions (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Acemoglu et al. build on this idea, highlighting how extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

North's work further elaborates on the role of transaction costs and institutional reliability in economic growth. He argues that as economies grow more complex, efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms become essential for reducing uncertainties and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North also posits that reliable institutions allow individuals to engage in impersonal transactions with confidence, which is crucial for the division of labor and specialization that drive economic growth (North 1989, p.2). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income levels among former colonies. They suggest that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persisted and impacted long-term economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The interplay between credible commitment and institutional reliability sets a foundation for understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes over the long run.

North and Weingast argue that the Glorious Revolution led to significant institutional changes that enhanced the security of property rights by limiting the monarch's power, which in turn promoted economic growth. This institutional framework provided a credible commitment mechanism that reduced uncertainty and encouraged investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.2). North further elaborates on this point by highlighting how the development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms lowered transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and overall economic efficiency. However, North also acknowledges that while institutions are essential for reducing uncertainties and fostering growth, the actual process of institutional change can be slow and incremental. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run economic development. The Glorious Revolution's political changes set a foundation for sustained economic growth by establishing reliable institutions that supported secure property rights.

This perspective highlights the significance of extractive institutions established during colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic development. These institutions persisted over time and played a crucial role in shaping current income disparities among former colonies. The mechanism through which this occurred involves the interplay between political power and institutional design: politically powerful groups often resist changes that could undermine their control, even if those changes might foster economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where institutions were tailored to benefit European colonizers at the expense of long-term economic prosperity for the colonies. This historical legacy underscores how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic outcomes.

Hornbeck and Clark and Jacks show that specific technologies and resources have shaped agricultural development and industrial productivity by influencing how economic activities were organized (Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). Hornbeck demonstrates the profound impact of barbed wire fencing on American Plains agriculture, revealing a 23.4% increase in average crop productivity from 1880 to 1890 in counties with minimal woodland, highlighting how technological advancements can enhance agricultural output by securing property rights and mitigating livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This finding underscores the role of technology in facilitating economic growth through improved resource management. This qualification indicates that while technology is instrumental, its impact on economic growth can be constrained by underlying resource limitations. The influence of these technologies extends beyond immediate productivity gains; they also shape long-term economic structures by altering how resources are allocated and utilized.

Distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that the merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries are often misinterpreted as inefficient cartels, but the elder merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. This perspective highlights how institutions evolved in response to distributional conflicts rather than solely for efficiency gains. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further emphasize that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). However, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) points out that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that we cannot study one institution in isolation; entire institutional frameworks must be considered. The implication is that understanding long-run economic growth requires examining not just the direct effects of distributional conflicts on institutions but also how these conflicts interact with other institutional elements to influence economic outcomes. Institutions in pre-industrial societies were thus shaped by a complex interplay between efficiency needs and the power dynamics of distributional conflicts, which in turn influenced long-term economic structures.

The evolution of institutions governing economic growth is underpinned by credible commitment to rules. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are crucial for fostering markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is echoed by North's broader argument that the development of efficient property rights and their enforcement reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The establishment of reliable institutions allows individuals to engage in complex economic interactions with confidence, even when dealing with strangers. This institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is also significant. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, not necessarily to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further illustrate that extractive institutions can persist when they benefit powerful elites, even if they hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of understanding how power dynamics influence institutional design and economic outcomes.

The impact of colonialism on institutional development provides another lens through which to view the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This perspective is supported by Clark and Jacks' findings on the negligible role of coal in driving the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that institutional factors were more critical than resource availability (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism had profound effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, accounting for significant income differences today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The evidence from capital markets supports the view that secure property rights and credible commitment to rules are essential for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is reinforced by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.2).

The specific historical evidence of colonial mortality rates shows that extractive institutions were established when European settlers faced high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement about whether institutional changes introduced by colonialism had profound effects on economic growth or not, demonstrating how power dynamics influence institutional design and economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.7).
