The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, as it addresses how societies can sustainably develop over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies the disagreement between those who prioritize technological progress and those who emphasize institutional frameworks. Technological progressivists argue that advancements in technology drive economic growth by increasing productivity and creating new opportunities for wealth generation. Institutionalists assert that creating effective institutions is vital for leveraging technological innovations and promoting their sustainable use. This debate underscores a fundamental intellectual challenge: understanding whether the primary driver of long-run economic growth is technological innovation or the institutional environment that supports it. Institutions can facilitate or hinder economic development by influencing the efficiency of resource allocation, the enforcement of property rights, and the stability of political structures. The resolution of this question has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of historical economic trajectories.

North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, positing a causal link between institutional frameworks and long-run economic performance (North 1989, p.6). Kuznets (1973, p.10) echoes this perspective by highlighting the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize technological innovations. For Kuznets, institutions serve as intermediaries for translating scientific advancements into practical applications that drive growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This shared finding underscores the importance of institutions in channeling technological progress toward sustainable economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and North (1989, p.6) both emphasize the role of coordination failures and institutional progress as essential for maintaining long-term economic growth. Mokyr notes that without institutional advancements, technological progress can be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Similarly, North argues that institutions specifying efficient property rights are crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This linkage between institutional efficiency and long-run growth suggests that the sustainability of economic development hinges on the effective functioning of institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) build on this framework by examining how colonial institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, asserting that extractive institutions can impede long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further elaborate on this idea, highlighting that secure property rights and credible commitments underpin economic growth. They argue that political factors, such as a regime's commitment to rules, are critical for fostering an environment conducive to economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in creating stable conditions necessary for sustained economic progress.

Extending this idea to historical contexts, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The nature of these institutions was deeply rooted in the settler mortality rates, which determined whether Europeans established long-term settlements or merely extractive institutions. This pattern is evident in regions where European settlers faced high disease environments, leading to the implementation of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that these institutional frameworks persisted over time and significantly impacted current income levels. The persistence of these institutions highlights how historical legacies can shape long-term economic trajectories, as former colonies with extractive institutions continue to face economic challenges due to their colonial past.

Kuznets highlights that technological progress responds to specific needs, often originating from the country in which they emerge. This implies that economic institutions must adapt to harness these technological advancements effectively. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that technological progress often depends on existing knowledge and techniques, emphasizing the importance of institutional frameworks that facilitate the recombination and application of innovative ideas. This interdependence between technology and institutions indicates that economic growth relies on both technological advancement and supportive institutional environments.

The role of institutions in fostering technological progress becomes evident when considering the feedback loop between scientific discoveries and technological innovations. This underscores the necessity of institutional support to translate scientific knowledge into practical technologies. Institutions thus play a critical role in ensuring that technological advancements are efficiently utilized and sustained over the long term, thereby driving economic growth.

Kuznets highlights the interconnected nature of modern economic growth with technological progress, emphasizing that mass application of innovations, often based on recent scientific discoveries, provides positive feedback for further advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr complements this view by arguing that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited due to rent-seeking behaviors that could erode gains over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the necessity of institutions in translating scientific knowledge into practical technologies and sustaining them.

The role of institutions extends beyond technological advancement; they also facilitate market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement. North argues that as economies grow, political institutions emerge to develop economic institutions that lower transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic growth. North and Weingast further illustrate this point by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for securing property rights and promoting market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Institutions influence long-run economic outcomes by addressing transaction problems and shaping the distribution of power. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and not promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions, though often seen as inefficient cartels, provided security and contract enforcement as club goods, highlighting the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the evolution of property rights in England during the Glorious Revolution. North and Weingast show that the Glorious Revolution led to institutions that constrained the ex post behavior of the state, improving its ability to maintain bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This empirical comparison shows that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), as it ensures that technological advancements are efficiently utilized and sustained over the long term.
