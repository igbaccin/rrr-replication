The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of economic progress over extended periods. Institutions are active forces that can facilitate or hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). One live disagreement centers on whether secure property rights and effective governance are essential for sustained growth, or if other factors such as technological innovation, resource endowments, or cultural attitudes play a more significant role. Another debate revolves around the timing and nature of institutional changes: do they precede economic growth, or do they emerge as a response to it? The extent to which institutions can be designed or engineered to promote growth is also contested.

North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They highlight that the Glorious Revolution in seventeenth-century England established political institutions that ensured secure property rights and constrained the state's ability to expropriate wealth. This political stability was crucial for the development of capital markets and the enforcement of contracts, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North elaborates on this by emphasizing that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby enabling individuals to engage in impersonal exchange with confidence (North 1989, p.2). These findings underscore the importance of political institutions in creating an environment conducive to long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions extends beyond political stability to include the development of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms. North argues that as economies grow, the size of the transactions sector increases, necessitating more complex institutional frameworks to manage these exchanges efficiently (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's demonstration of how labour productivity in both tradable and non-tradable sectors influences wage rates and prices, highlighting the economic implications of institutional efficiency (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Together, these insights suggest that institutions play a multifaceted role in shaping long-run economic growth, encompassing political stability, property rights, and market efficiency.

By establishing property rights and enforcing them effectively, institutions ensure the predictability of outcomes in complex interdependent situations. This mechanism fosters confidence among individuals engaging in exchanges with strangers, a critical component of impersonal market transactions. North and Weingast further illustrate how political institutions complement reputation-building strategies, enhancing the efficacy of market exchanges. These institutions constrain ex post behavior of states, thereby preventing wealth expropriation and maintaining bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional reliability involves enforcing rules and ensuring their adaptability to changing economic conditions, a dynamic process that underpins sustained growth (North, 1990).

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They show that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a pattern that persisted through extractive institutions established during the colonial period. This institutional legacy was a direct result of political and economic decisions made by colonizing powers, not merely a consequence of geography or climate (Engerman & Sokoloff, 2005). The impact of these institutions is evident in the differences in income per capita among former colonies today. The mortality rates faced by European settlers, for example, directly influenced the type of institutions implemented (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). High mortality rates often led to extractive institutions that stifled economic growth, whereas lower mortality rates facilitated the establishment of more inclusive institutions conducive to development. This persistence highlights how initial institutional choices can have lasting implications for economic performance, shaping long-run growth trajectories in profound ways.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Hornbeck 2010, p.34), along with Hornbeck (2010, p.34), show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. Institutions are shown to have advantaged elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The mechanisms by which these institutions operated were multifaceted: they included policies that favored elite interests at the expense of broader economic development. The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural productivity on the American Plains, enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This development underscored the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic growth. However, Hornbeck's analysis of crop productivity reveals that while agricultural productivity increased significantly during this period, it also highlights the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

Economic development and changes in living standards did not follow a straightforward trajectory in Europe between 1500 and 1800. van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) share a finding that the relationship between economic growth and improvements in living standards was complex and indirect. Both authors highlight significant economic activity during this period, but they also point out that this did not uniformly translate into better living conditions. For instance, van Zanden notes that while there were periods of economic growth, such as the long-term increase in total factor productivity in French agriculture between 1450 and 1800, these gains did not always result in widespread improvements in living standards. Komlos supports this claim by noting that despite increased urbanization and industrial productivity during the Industrial Revolution, there was a decline in biological well-being due to poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.13). This suggests that while economic growth can lead to changes in living conditions, it does not necessarily result in widespread improvements without additional factors at play. The impact of economic development on standards of living is mediated by other factors, such as income distribution and nutritional quality. Komlos's analysis of relative price effects further illustrates this point: the rising cost of nutrients relative to industrial goods led consumers to alter their consumption patterns (Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift, in turn, had implications for overall health and well-being, highlighting how changes in economic conditions can have nuanced and varied impacts on living standards.

The Industrial Revolution and economic divergence are discussed without reference to institutional frameworks. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that technological barriers played a crucial role in shaping the trajectory of industrialization and economic growth. Their analysis focuses on the modest gains in total factor productivity (TFP) in coal mining, highlighting that extraction costs rose only slightly with cumulative output. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's emphasis on international price arbitrage, which they argue equalized wages across sectors within each economy. The implication of these findings is that economic divergence was already well underway before the Industrial Revolution, driven primarily by productivity differences in tradable goods sectors. This narrative overlooks the role of institutions in facilitating or hindering technological adoption and productivity growth (Aghion et al., 2005). For instance, the institutional environment could influence the ease with which new technologies are adopted, affecting TFP gains.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views debate the historical determinants of economic development (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). These debates have highlighted how the institutional environment can shape long-run economic growth. This position is supported by Bryant, who contends that the revisionist stance dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. The Eurocentric view is that European agricultural productivity revolution released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments. This structural differentiation between sectors fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country (Bryant 2006, p.29). In contrast, China's economic pattern concentrated handicrafts within the peasant household without fostering any comparable growth-enhancing articulations (Bryant 2006, p.28). The agricultural system in England allowed for a more synergistic mix of grain production and livestock rearing. This improved diets, increased productivity through substituting horse and oxen for human labour, and enhanced soil fertility through increased manuring and the rotation of staple cereals with nitrogen-fixing forage crops (Bryant 2006, p.28). Chinese peasants, however, were increasingly driven into a crops-only pattern due to contracting plots. These plots ruled out the planting of fodder cereals or grasses, leaving room only for scavenger pigs and poultry, and led to the reverse substitution of human for animal labour power. This divergence in agricultural practices highlights how institutional factors, such as land tenure systems and agricultural policies, can significantly influence long-run economic growth by affecting productivity and resource allocation.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing productivity and resource allocation. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic growth. This perspective aligns with North's broader argument that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and provide a reliable framework for economic exchanges (North 1989, p.2). These institutional frameworks are essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, allowing individuals to engage confidently in complex interdependent situations.

The role of institutions is further highlighted by van Zanden. He shows how long-run changes in total factor productivity can be inferred from detailed reconstructions of economic rents (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of institutional reliability and efficiency in facilitating market exchanges and driving economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) demonstrate that differences in labour productivity between tradable and non-tradable sectors are crucial for understanding the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. These findings support the idea that institutions influence long-run economic growth by affecting the allocation of resources and the efficiency of production.

Institutional factors also play a significant role in agricultural practices, as seen in the divergent paths taken by Chinese peasants and other agricultural systems (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The contracting plots in China led to a crops-only pattern, which ruled out the planting of fodder cereals or grasses. This institutional constraint highlights how land tenure systems and agricultural policies can significantly influence long-run economic growth by affecting productivity and resource allocation.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the evidence on total factor productivity gains in coal mining (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). Clark and Jacks show that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output, establishing upper- and lower-bound estimates of the gains in total factor productivity. This empirical evidence settles disagreements about the role of institutions by providing a clear measurement of productivity changes over time. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the detailed reconstruction of economic rents (van Zanden 1999, p.2), which allows for an accurate assessment of long-run changes in productivity and the efficiency of production.
