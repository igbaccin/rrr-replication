The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate growth by enhancing efficiency or if they are shaped by distributional conflicts that reflect power dynamics within societies. Another key point of contention is whether technological progress alone can drive long-run economic growth, even in the absence of institutional support.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and North (1989, p.5) both argue for the significance of institutions in economic development. Institutions play a crucial role by shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. When institutions are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, they can stifle long-run growth. When institutions support property rights and enforce contracts effectively, they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). Kuznets (1973, p.4) also highlights the importance of technological progress in economic growth but emphasizes that this progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. This perspective highlights that institutions are essential enablers of long-run economic growth as they create an environment where technological innovations can thrive.

Institutions matter because they shape the incentives for innovation and investment. This dynamic is crucial for long-run economic growth, as it allows societies to leverage technological progress more effectively. Kuznets (1973, p.4) further elaborates on this by noting that the mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with scientific advancements, creating a positive feedback loop. Institutions thus play a pivotal role in harnessing the benefits of both technological and scientific progress for economic growth.

The interplay between institutions and technological progress is further illustrated by the example of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains. Hornbeck (2010, p.4) demonstrates how this technological innovation significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This case study highlights how institutional support can amplify the effects of technological progress, leading to substantial economic benefits. Similarly, Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. Institutions therefore act as a complementary force, ensuring that technological progress is sustained and translated into long-run economic growth.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth involves both efficiency and power dynamics within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) challenges the notion that pre-industrial European institutions were solely designed for efficiency, arguing instead that they were shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective suggests that institutions are often the result of struggles between different groups vying for resources and influence. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) support this view by noting that institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth.

Institutions thus play a multifaceted role in shaping long-run economic growth, acting as both enablers of technological progress and reflectors of societal power dynamics.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They emphasize the role of political institutions in specifying property rights and providing enforcement. This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth.

This success underscores how effective enforcement of property rights can drive market development and economic expansion. The role of institutions encompasses more than mere rules; it includes the mechanisms governing how these rules are enforced and changed. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by noting that as economies grow, the transactions sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic illustrates how institutional frameworks adapt to economic growth, ensuring that transaction costs remain manageable.

They illustrate this with the example of the Glorious Revolution, showing how restrictions on state behavior improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective highlights that political institutions play a critical role in maintaining credible commitments, which are essential for long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.6) cautions against overly simplistic views of institutional effectiveness, noting that neoclassical models focusing solely on inputs and technology fail to capture the complexities of economic development (North 1989, p.5). This nuanced view underscores how institutions shape economic growth by addressing transaction costs and ensuring credible commitments.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth through their impact on property rights and technological change (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights. This innovation allowed for more effective land enclosure, which reduced livestock damage and improved agricultural productivity. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) emphasizes that "barbed wire's effects are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle" (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Domar (1970, p.7) further illustrates how the profitability of slavery is influenced by economic factors, such as land-to-labor ratios. He argues that in societies where labor is scarce and land abundant, the marginal product of labor can descend to subsistence levels, making slave labor less profitable (Domar 1970, p.7). This dynamic highlights that technological progress and capital accumulation do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor; instead, it depends on the specific economic conditions. The downstream implication is that technological advancements like barbed wire fencing, which enhance property rights protection, can significantly alter economic outcomes by changing the incentives for land use and labor deployment. Domar (1970, p.7) qualifies this by pointing out that 'we cannot be sure that technological progress and greater use of capital necessarily reduce the profitability of slave as compared with free labor' (Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that while institutions like property rights are crucial, their impact on economic growth is contingent upon broader economic conditions.

Long-term structural changes in economic conditions have profound effects on economic outcomes such as wage gaps and biological well-being (Pamuk 2007, p.22; Komlos 1998, p.8). Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death led to significant increases in wages relative to rents, driving a substitution of land and capital for labor. This dynamic was particularly pronounced in urban areas where guilds played a crucial role in technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.22; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This decline was exacerbated by rising relative prices of nutrients, which led consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products (Komlos 1998, p.8; Komlos 1998, p.12). While these structural changes had significant impacts on wages and well-being, they also underscore the broader economic conditions that shape long-term outcomes. The impact of technological change on labor markets can be complex. This suggests that structural changes alone do not determine economic outcomes; they interact with other factors to shape long-term growth.

Ogilvie and others (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) extend this by arguing that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These conflicts arose from the unequal distribution of power among different social groups, leading to institutional arrangements that favored specific interests over broader efficiency gains. For instance, Ogilvie highlights how merchant guilds and craft guilds, often portrayed as inefficient cartels, actually served as mechanisms for securing property rights and contract enforcement. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.5) further illustrate this dynamic in New World societies where institutional frameworks supported elite interests through government policies. These policies restricted competition and maintained inequality, thereby reinforcing the distributional conflicts that shaped economic institutions. However, the persistence of these institutions is not merely a reflection of power dynamics but also influenced by broader social structures. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.6) notes that the craft guilds' influence on technological change was significant, suggesting that while distributional conflicts were crucial, other factors also played a role in shaping economic outcomes. This interplay between institutional frameworks and distributional conflicts underscores the complex nature of long-term economic growth, where power dynamics are just one piece of the puzzle.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) shared that technological advancements are central to long-term economic development. This claim is supported by the observation that technology often builds on existing knowledge, combining techniques to create novel solutions; thus, the growth of useful knowledge is crucial for sustained economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.12). The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is evident in Kuznets' argument: mass application of technological innovations requires institutional adjustments. This suggests that while institutions are not the primary drivers, they facilitate the efficient use of technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets also notes that technological progress often addresses specific needs, highlighting that inventions respond to local requirements and thus may require tailored institutional support (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to technological innovation. The institutional framework must evolve in tandem with technological progress to sustain economic improvements.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is driven by institutional changes introduced through colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutions established during this period were not designed to maximize long-run growth but rather to extract rents for European colonists. The institutional framework established during colonialism thus shaped long-term economic outcomes in a way that was largely independent of geographic factors. For example, Acemoglu et al. show that high mortality rates faced by European settlers led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies where settlement was difficult; these institutions persisted and significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic highlights how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic development, underscoring the importance of considering historical institutional legacies when analyzing long-run growth patterns. Mokyr argues that even with robust institutions, the relentless erosion of rent-seeking can undermine economic gains if not managed properly (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency of resource allocation and the sustainability of technological progress. This is evident in Mokyr's argument that institutional progress complements technological advancements to ensure sustained economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further supports this view, emphasizing that technological innovations require institutional adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4), highlighting a shared finding on the interdependence of institutions and technology. North adds that effective property rights and enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6), which is crucial for economic growth.

The role of institutions in mitigating rent-seeking behaviors and coordinating economic activities cannot be overstated. Mokyr points out that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic gains can be undermined by relentless erosion due to rent-seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms are necessary for reducing transaction costs in a growing economy (North 1989, p.6), underscoring how institutions help manage and mitigate these issues. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is found in the institutional legacies of former colonies. Acemoglu et al show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), highlighting how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic development and growth patterns.
