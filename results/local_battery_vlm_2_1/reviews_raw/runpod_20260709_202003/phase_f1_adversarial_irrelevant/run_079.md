The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether and how the rules that govern societies influence their economic trajectories over extended periods. The debate centres on two main positions: one emphasizes technological progress as the primary driver of economic growth, while another highlights the critical role of institutions. Technological progress can spur economic development by enhancing productivity and enabling new industries to emerge. The sustainability of this growth may depend on complementary institutional developments that mitigate rent-seeking behaviour. Institutions can reduce transaction costs and enforce property rights, thereby fostering a more efficient allocation of resources and encouraging investment in innovation. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where both factors are intertwined and operate together. The question remains: to what extent do institutions themselves drive long-run economic growth? Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the development of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in establishing secure property rights, which then fostered long-run economic growth. The evolution of these institutions facilitated market exchanges by defining efficient property rights and ensuring their enforcement. This institutional framework reduced transaction costs per exchange while increasing the overall volume of transactions, thereby driving economic growth through greater specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that these institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the state's credibility in maintaining its commitments to constituents. This institutional evolution was particularly evident during the Glorious Revolution, which solidified the state's restrictions on ex post behavior, thereby improving its ability to uphold bargains and prevent wealth expropriation (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.6) notes that neoclassical models alone cannot fully explain economic growth. These models often overlook the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes by focusing primarily on inputs such as land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. While technological knowledge is crucial, it must be complemented by a robust institutional framework to achieve sustained economic development (North 1989, p.5). The interplay between political institutions governing public choice and secure property rights highlights the foundational role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, not institutional efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) both challenge the idea that institutions are pivotal for long-run economic growth. They argue that distributional struggles among social groups determined the nature of these institutions, often resulting in arrangements that served to maintain power balances. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) notes that merchant guilds were not simply inefficient cartels; rather, they provided security and contract enforcement as club goods. Domar (1970, p.7) similarly posits that slavery or serfdom emerged in contexts where the land-to-labor ratio was high and labor scarce. This perspective is crucial because it highlights how institutional arrangements can be driven by power dynamics and distributional conflicts as well as purely economic efficiency considerations. Domar (1970, p.7) acknowledges that technological progress and increased capital use could complicate this dynamic. Thus, while distributional conflicts are a significant driver, the interplay with technological changes adds another layer of complexity to understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) illustrate how technological innovations can enhance property rights protection, which in turn facilitates long-run economic growth. Hornbeck's study on the American Plains shows that barbed wire fencing enabled farmers to better safeguard their land from livestock damage, thereby reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.30); this technological change allowed for more efficient agricultural practices and increased productivity. Pamuk (2007, p.22) similarly highlights how the Black Death led to structural changes that increased wages relative to rents, driving a substitution of land and capital for labor across Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This shift indicates that technological progress can alter the economic landscape by changing the relative costs of production factors, influencing long-term growth patterns. The impact of such technological changes is not uniform; Hornbeck (2010, p.30) notes that the direct technological effects of barbed wire were less significant than its role in enhancing property rights protection (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This suggests that while technological progress can drive economic development, its effectiveness is contingent on the institutional context and how it interacts with existing property rights frameworks. Therefore, understanding the interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for comprehending long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) (Mokyr 2005, p.54) both argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. Mokyr's analysis highlights how technological progress relies heavily on the recombination of existing techniques, a process facilitated by analogical thinking where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This perspective underscores the importance of cumulative knowledge and its role in driving economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.12). However, Kuznets (1973, p.10) qualifies this view by noting that technological progress is often tailored to specific needs, meaning that innovations are not universally applicable across different contexts (Kuznets 1973, p.10).

The interplay between technology and institutions is crucial here. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be relentless and irreversible, its sustainability depends on complementary institutional progress. This insight aligns with Kuznets' observation that the efficient use of innovations requires not just technological advances but also institutional and ideological adjustments. As such, the accumulation of useful knowledge, while pivotal, must be supported by a robust institutional framework to ensure long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former European colonies. The institutional framework established during colonial rule, which was often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, has had enduring effects on these economies. The mortality rates faced by European settlers significantly influenced the type of institutions that were implemented (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). High mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which impeded economic development and persisted over time. These extractive institutions, characterized by poor property rights and high levels of rent-seeking, created significant barriers to economic growth. Consequently, the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes introduced during colonial rule. The enduring impact of these institutions is evident in the current income disparities between countries with different colonial histories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This raises a critical question about how contemporary policies can address and mitigate the persistent effects of historical institutional legacies on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological progress and mitigate coordination failures. North argues that institutional progress complements technological advancements (North 1989, p.5). Without this complementarity, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited due to rent-seeking behavior (North 1989, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets similarly highlights that economic growth requires not only technological innovations but also ideological and institutional adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial because technological progress often depends on the existing knowledge base and analogical thinking, which in turn relies on effective institutional frameworks (North 1989, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.12).

The enduring impact of historical institutional legacies is evident in contemporary economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth have had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is reinforced by Ogilvie, who argues that pre-industrial economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts as well as efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The introduction of new technologies, such as barbed wire fencing in agricultural development on the American Plains, illustrates how institutional changes can enhance property rights and reduce externalities. Hornbeck shows that barbed wire significantly improved agricultural productivity by enabling better protection of property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This example underscores how technological advancements are not sufficient on their own; they must be supported by effective institutions to realize their full economic potential.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the comparative analysis of former European colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced during colonial rule, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). This analysis provides empirical support for the view that institutions play a crucial role in determining long-term economic development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North 1989, p.6).
