The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as natural resources or geographical advantages, play more significant roles. One perspective emphasizes the crucial role of credible commitments by political institutions to enforce property rights and support market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). Another view argues that economic growth is driven by technological advancements and increased productivity in key sectors such as coal mining (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). The stakes are high, as understanding the causal mechanisms behind long-run economic growth can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development. This intellectual tension underscores the need for a nuanced examination of how institutions interact with other factors to drive economic growth over extended periods.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the commitment of the regime to these rules was a key factor in driving economic growth. This institutional framework not only governed economic exchanges but also ensured their enforcement, complementing reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints where mere reputation was insufficient. The institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution exemplify this dynamic, illustrating how restrictions on state behavior improved its ability to maintain bargains with constituents and prevent expropriation of wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights led to the emergence of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. This institutional reliability is crucial for reducing uncertainties in complex interdependent situations, allowing individuals to engage confidently in transactions with strangers. The establishment of a reliable medium of exchange and efficient markets enabled productivity gains from specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.2). This institutional framework ensures that as the economy grows more specialized, the costs per transaction decrease while the aggregate volume of exchanges increases.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The design of these institutions was driven by the interests of the colonizing powers, which often prioritized extracting resources and maximizing rents rather than fostering long-run growth. This extractive institutional framework led to persistent economic underdevelopment in many colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Even where European settlers established institutions that facilitated market exchanges and protected property rights, these benefits were not universally distributed; instead, they often reinforced the power of the colonial elite at the expense of the indigenous population.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) further illustrate this dynamic by showing how differences in European mortality rates influenced institutional development. High mortality rates in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as Europeans were less likely to settle permanently and develop inclusive institutions. These extractive institutions persisted over time, significantly affecting current income per capita levels. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes, as they created barriers to economic development that continue to affect former colonies today. This underscores how the initial conditions set by colonial powers can have long-lasting effects on institutional quality and economic performance.

Sokoloff and Engerman show how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This dynamic is evident in policies that limited economic opportunities for the broader population. The impact of natural environments on agricultural development was a significant factor in this process, as these institutions evolved to favor elites by restricting access to resources and economic opportunities. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) demonstrates how technological innovations such as barbed wire fencing could enhance agricultural productivity by securing property rights and reducing livestock damage; the introduction of barbed wire allowed farmers to protect their investments more effectively and foster a competitive agricultural sector. However, this same protectionism also reinforced existing inequalities by enabling larger landowners to consolidate their holdings and maintain control over resources. Thus, while institutions shaped by greater inequality initially restricted competition, they also created conditions for specific technological advancements that could enhance productivity under certain circumstances; the impact of these technologies on economic growth and inequality was complex, reflecting the broader institutional context in which they were deployed.

Van Zanden argues that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not consistently improve living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). This period saw significant population growth and urbanization, but these factors alone did not translate into higher biological well-being for the majority of Europeans. Economic growth during this time was often accompanied by declining real wages and poor childhood nourishment, which had detrimental effects on health outcomes. Komlos highlights that even as cities grew rapidly, total factor productivity could decline (Komlos 1998, p.8). The divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups further illustrates this point, as rising relative prices of nutrients led to changes in consumption patterns that did not necessarily improve overall well-being. Pamuk adds nuance by showing how long-term structural changes initiated by events like the Black Death could alter wage dynamics and productivity across regions, influencing standards of living in ways that were not immediately apparent from economic growth figures alone (Pamuk 2007, p.8). This dynamic suggests that while economic development can drive urbanization and productivity gains, its impact on living standards is mediated by a host of other factors.

Specific factors such as coal and gunpowder imports played distinct roles in historical economic changes (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Whatley 2018, p.2). Clark and Jacks demonstrate that coal's role in driving the Industrial Revolution was minimal. They show that extraction costs for coal rose only modestly with cumulative output, leading to modest gains in total factor productivity from 1710 to 1869 (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). The limited impact of coal on productivity suggests that other factors, such as institutional changes or technological advancements outside the coal industry, were more critical drivers of economic growth during this period. Whatley's analysis of gunpowder imports further illustrates how specific factors can influence economic dynamics. According to his guns-for-slaves-in-production hypothesis, imported gunpowder technology increased productivity in capturing slaves and marching them to the coast (Whatley 2018, p.2). This intertemporal production link highlights how external technological factors can shape economic outcomes, particularly in contexts where social processes are deeply intertwined with economic activities. However, Whatley's focus on specific imports like gunpowder underscores that these factors operate within broader institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder their impact (Whatley 2018, p.15). The nuanced roles of coal and gunpowder in historical economic changes reveal that while specific factors can drive certain outcomes, their effects are mediated by underlying institutions that govern resource allocation and technological adoption.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical economic divergences by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the Atlantic slave trade was driven by commercial motivations, where the exchange-value product of Africans' labour in Africa was lower than it was in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken. The scarcity of labor in relation to cultivable land and capital in Sub-Saharan Africa until well into the 20th century is a paradoxical factor that influenced economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.11). This perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gupta's (2006) argument that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. However, their analysis indicates that labor productivity in the tradable goods sector was relatively low in Asia, which must reflect low productivity in this sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This implies that economic rent and growth were joint products in certain historical contexts, including pre-colonial and colonial Africa (Austin 2008, p.11). The kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in one context were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. This suggests that the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex and contingent on specific historical circumstances (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).

The institutions governing economic exchange have been shown to play a pivotal role in shaping long-run growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of political institutions is crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions complement reputation-building by providing constraints where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective underscores how credible commitment underpins growth, as institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism and mitigate incentive problems that are insufficiently policed by reputation alone. North shows that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). These institutional frameworks reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing for confidence in remote outcomes and enabling productivity gains from impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2).

The specific historical contexts in which institutions influence growth are varied. Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The relatively low silver wage in Asia reflects low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that economic rent and growth were joint products in certain historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This is consistent with Austin's argument that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in one context were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.1).

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex and contingent on specific historical circumstances. Broadberry and Gupta show that the price of non-tradable commodities depends negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector, highlighting the intricate interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This complexity is further illustrated by Austin, who argues that prevalent labor scarcity south of the Sahara was not inconsistent with higher average population density and a relatively larger urban population, attributes that may be taken as evidence of higher real per capita income (Austin 2008, p.11).

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by providing the necessary frameworks for secure property rights and effective enforcement. The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver or merely one factor among many in economic development. North shows that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). In contrast, Austin argues that economic rent and growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa; the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of total factor productivity (TFP) gains over time (van Zanden 1999, p.2).
