The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutional frameworks drive economic growth versus other factors such as technological innovation, resource endowments, or geographical advantages. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions in creating stable environments that foster investment and productivity gains, while others point to the interplay between institutions and broader socio-economic conditions. Institutions are seen as both catalysts for long-term development and constraints that can hinder economic progress if not properly aligned with societal needs. This debate underscores the need to understand how institutional structures evolve and interact with other factors to influence economic outcomes over time. This is especially true in contexts where long-run growth has been historically variable, such as in Europe before 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The political stability and credible commitment to these rules underpinned the development of markets and capital flows, as evidenced by the robust performance of English capital markets during this period (North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.6) extends this argument by highlighting that the evolution of efficient property rights and their enforcement reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This institutional reliability is crucial because it allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence (North 1989, p.2). The efficacy of these institutions relies on the broader social context; as North (1989, p.6) notes, developing more complex social frameworks requires that institutional structures reduce uncertainties associated with increasing specialization and division of labor.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional frameworks established during the colonial period were often designed to extract resources rather than promote long-run growth. These early institutional frameworks had lasting effects on the economic performance of the former colonies. This indicates that while extractive institutions can hinder long-run growth, they are not immutable barriers to economic development.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) together show that economic growth during the early modern period did not uniformly translate into improved living standards. This lack of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living can be attributed to several factors. One notable mechanism is the divergence in income gains across different social groups, which led to unequal distributions of wealth. Komlos (1998, p.8) details that during the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that while aggregate measures like GDP per capita may indicate growth, they do not fully capture the nuances of how different segments of society experience these changes. Pamuk (2007, p.15) adds further complexity by highlighting how structural changes, such as those initiated by the Black Death, led to long-term wage gaps between regions. This indicates that historical events can have enduring effects on economic outcomes, shaping regional disparities in living standards. However, Pamuk (2007, p.8) also notes that the linkages between urban and rural sectors are not always equally strong, suggesting that the relationship between wages and standards of living is complex and context-dependent. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how economic growth translates into improved living standards over the long run.

Historical factors such as slavery and colonialism have shaped economic development in different regions by influencing institutional evolution and social structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) illustrate that the institutions established in societies with greater inequality, often a result of these historical factors, tended to advantage elites through various government policies. This institutional bias restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. For instance, the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized many New World economies from their inception led to institutions that were not conducive to economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions often prioritized the interests of elites, limiting opportunities for broader economic development.

This institutional impact extends beyond historical contexts. Nunn (2008, p.3) shows that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, highlighting how the production and use of slaves created institutional frameworks detrimental to growth. This finding underscores that the legacy of slavery extends beyond direct economic impacts, shaping institutions in ways that hinder long-term development (Nunn 2008, p.3). Moreover, these historical factors have enduring effects even when adapted for present needs. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that while institutions can be long-standing, they are not immutable and can be adapted to meet current development requirements, suggesting that policy interventions can mitigate some of the adverse institutional legacies (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This adaptation is crucial for eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

This is particularly relevant given the findings of Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Whatley (2018, p.2), which highlight the limited impact of specific factors on long-run economic growth. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that gains in total factor productivity in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution were only modest. This finding underscores that technological improvements in resource extraction did not drive industrialization as much as institutional changes might have done. Whatley (2018, p.15) further illustrates this by showing how gunpowder imports influenced slave exports through intertemporal production links and social processes, highlighting the importance of external factors in economic development within specific contexts. These factors do not directly address institutional frameworks that support long-term growth. Instead, they illustrate how certain inputs can shape economic activities without fundamentally altering institutional structures. This suggests that while specific factors like coal mining costs or gunpowder imports can influence economic outcomes, their impact is often limited to particular sectors or short-term gains, rather than fostering the broad-based institutional changes necessary for sustained economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are discussed in terms of commercial motivations, colonial regimes, and economic rent. This discussion illustrates how the economic gains from Africa's slave trade were driven by labour productivity differences rather than institutional changes. Austin shows that the low exchange-value product of Africans' labour in Africa was due to specific factors such as production techniques and natural environmental conditions (Austin 2008, p.11). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) further demonstrate that wage equalization between sectors within each economy leads to a dependence of non-tradable good prices on productivity differences. The implication is that economic growth in certain historical contexts can be driven by factors other than optimal institutions, suggesting that institutional frameworks are not always the primary drivers of long-term economic development. However, Austin (2008, p.11) also highlights that colonial regimes often supported African economic enterprise (Austin 2008, p.1), indicating that while commercial motivations and economic rent were significant, the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes cannot be entirely dismissed.

This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and context-dependent.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. A key point of agreement across sources is that institutions do matter for long-term economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The centrality of institutions in economic growth is underscored by North and Weingast, who highlight that political factors underpinning secure property rights are crucial for fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), while Broadberry and Gupta show how the divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in productivity and labor conditions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). A precise remaining disagreement is that different institutional frameworks can be optimal for different historical contexts. Austin shows that economic rent and growth are often joint products, but the institutions favoring growth in certain contexts were not necessarily optimal in others (Austin 2008, p.11), while Clark and Jacks find that coal mining productivity gains during the Industrial Revolution were modest despite technological advances (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the long-term economic performance of different regions under varied institutional settings, which shows how contextual factors influence the effectiveness of institutions in driving growth.
