The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that goes to the heart of how societies develop and prosper. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic advancement over centuries and why some nations thrive while others stagnate. The debate centers around two main positions: one emphasizes technological progress as the primary driver, arguing that institutions play a complementary role in sustaining growth; the other posits that institutional frameworks are the key determinants, suggesting that technology alone is insufficient without supportive institutions. This disagreement highlights the need to understand how institutions interact with technological advancements and economic dynamics. The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is crucial for sustained economic growth, as both factors reinforce each other in complex ways. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6) argue that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. This perspective aligns with Kuznets's view that technological innovations require institutional adjustments for optimal use (Kuznets 1973, p.10). The precise nature of these interactions continues to be an area of active scholarly investigation.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political institutions are crucial for economic growth through their role in specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms. They emphasise that the development of political institutions that facilitate market exchanges reduces transaction costs and fosters economic growth. This is because they provide a framework for enforcing rules governing economic exchange and mitigate the risks associated with reputation-building strategies alone (North 1989, p.5). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate that institutions can complement reputation mechanisms, particularly in situations where reputation alone may be insufficient to prevent reneging on agreements (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This view is supported by the observation that specific institutional features of firms are necessary to mitigate incentive problems that cannot be fully addressed through reputation. Therefore, political institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges and enforcing property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs. This perspective highlights the importance of credible commitments by the state to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, which is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Extending this analysis, Ogilvie and Domar (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts provide the key to understanding core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective shifts focus away from efficiency considerations towards power dynamics and resource allocation. The distributional conflict framework emphasizes the role of institutions in managing disputes over resources, such as land and labor. Ogilvie emphasizes that merchant guilds provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods', which was efficient (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This interpretation suggests that these institutions evolved to address specific problems arising from distributional conflicts, rather than simply maximizing economic efficiency.

Domar complements this view by arguing that slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce. These institutions can be seen as mechanisms for managing resource allocation in response to demographic pressures (Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the broader social and economic context in which institutions operate, focusing on more than just their role in facilitating transactions. Domar further elaborates that in a traditional society without technological progress or capital accumulation, the end of slavery becomes more certain as population increases and labor becomes abundant (Domar 1970, p.7). This insight qualifies the role of demographic factors in shaping institutional change, highlighting how shifts in resource availability can drive transitions from one form of economic organization to another. Institutions like serfdom and guilds are deeply rooted in the power dynamics and distributional conflicts of their time, not merely about efficiency.

In a related vein, Komlos and Abramovitz share a finding that economic growth is influenced by numerous factors beyond simple convergence (Komlos 1998, p.8; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This multifaceted nature of economic growth is evident in the complex interplay between technological advancement and social change. Komlos demonstrates that, during the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). The decline in biological well-being was exacerbated by rising relative prices of nutrients, as technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture lagged behind industrial sectors. This dynamic underscores how economic growth does not uniformly benefit all segments of society; rather, it can create disparities that affect health and well-being. Abramovitz elaborates on this complexity by highlighting how the same technological opportunities that drive modernization also influence the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The reduction in the price of capital goods relative to labor further accelerates the rate at which the capital-labor ratio increases, illustrating how technological progress can both drive and complicate economic growth. Abramovitz clarifies that productivity growth itself does not solely determine trends in food consumption; relative prices also play a crucial role (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This insight qualifies the direct impact of productivity gains on economic well-being, suggesting that the benefits of technological progress are mediated by broader economic dynamics.

The multifaceted nature of economic growth highlights how various factors, including technological advance and social change, interact to shape long-run outcomes. These interactions can lead to uneven distributions of benefits, as seen in the decline in biological well-being despite overall economic growth during the Industrial Revolution.

Expanding on this idea, Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22) both illustrate how exogenous factors can drive long-term economic changes through technological shifts and demographic upheavals. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This technological change improved agricultural efficiency, but not through direct productivity gains; rather, it facilitated more effective land use and protection.

The shared finding is that exogenous factors such as technological innovations and demographic shocks can reshape economic landscapes by altering underlying conditions for productivity and resource allocation. These factors do not directly boost growth; instead, they create new institutional environments that enable or constrain long-run economic outcomes. This dynamic is evident in how barbed wire fencing altered property rights and land use on the American Plains, and how the Black Death reshaped labor markets and technological adoption in Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These changes do not operate in isolation; they interact with existing institutions and economic conditions to produce complex outcomes. For instance, the effectiveness of barbed wire fencing depended on pre-existing property rights frameworks and the ability of farmers to enforce them.

These interactions underscore that technological changes and demographic shocks can drive long-term economic developments by creating new institutional contexts that shape how resources are allocated and productivity is enhanced. This perspective highlights the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where exogenous factors interact with institutions to produce lasting impacts on economic development.

Furthering this line of thought, technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge are key drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This is because technological advances generate new opportunities for innovation, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where technological progress fuels further scientific advancements. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.10) highlights that mass application of technological innovations, many based on recent scientific discoveries, provide a positive feedback loop (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that the accumulation of useful knowledge often involves recombining existing techniques and analogical thinking. These mechanisms suggest that economic growth is propelled by a continuous cycle of innovation and technological advancement.

The sustainability of this economic improvement relies on institutional progress. Mokyr (2005, p.54) acknowledges that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements might be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that these gains are sustained over the long run.

Therefore, although technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth, it must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure sustained and widespread benefits.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are critical for understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial powers established institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, which inhibited economic development in these regions. This extractive institutional framework was shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, who implemented systems that favored their own exploitation of resources over local economic advancement (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For example, Acemoglu et al. show how high European mortality rates influenced the establishment of colonies with extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita by limiting long-term growth and development opportunities (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had lasting effects on economic performance, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological progress and manage transaction costs. Institutions provide the framework necessary for the sustainable growth of knowledge and technology, which Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues are the mainsprings of economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement can significantly reduce transaction costs in a growing economy. This institutional environment fosters market exchanges and increases specialization and division of labor, both critical for long-term economic development.

Institutions also play a crucial role in managing the complexities of technological advancement and social change during economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) highlights that technological opportunities encourage rapid progress by modernization, which in turn accelerates the growth of the capital stock. Kuznets (1973, p.4), similarly, points out that mass application of technological innovations provides positive feedback, driving further advancements in technology and science. This interplay between institutions, technology, and social change illustrates how institutional frameworks are essential for harnessing the full potential of technological progress.

The historical evidence from various studies shows that institutions have a profound impact on long-term economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective highlights the enduring influence of institutional frameworks established during colonial times, which persistently affect contemporary economic performance. Hornbeck (2010, p.4), for example, demonstrates how the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their impact on technological progress and transaction costs. North (1989, p.5) shows that neoclassical theories, which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants of economic growth, are misleading because they overlook the critical role of institutions. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is shown by Kuznets (1973, p.10). He argues that modern economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating technological progress and managing transaction costs, which are essential for long-term economic development.
