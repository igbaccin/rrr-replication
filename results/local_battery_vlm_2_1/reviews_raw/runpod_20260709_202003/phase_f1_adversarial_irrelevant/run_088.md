The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists, with scholars debating both the mechanisms and magnitude of institutional influence on economic development. At stake are fundamental questions about how societies evolve economically over time. One key disagreement centres around whether institutions are primarily a facilitator or an independent driver of economic growth. On one side, some argue that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting economic efficiency (North 1989, p.6). Some scholars argue that institutions can be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, thereby hindering long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of wealthy and poor nations alike. This disagreement is not merely academic; it informs policy debates about how best to foster economic development in both developed and developing economies.

To understand the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth, a comprehensive view must account for the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks. Kuznets notes that technological advancements drive economic growth but require complementary institutional adjustments to be fully realized (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This perspective is echoed by Abramovitz, who highlights how technological opportunities can accelerate capital stock growth and productivity increases, thereby fostering modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4) . The interdependence of technology and institutions highlights the need for both innovation and institutional capacity to implement and sustain it in effective economic development. Moreover, Mokyr points out that without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvements may be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors that erode gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for understanding how societies can achieve sustained economic growth over the long term.

The role of institutions in shaping human capital development further highlights their importance in economic growth. Van Zanden argues that institutions guaranteeing fair execution of apprentice contracts, such as guilds, play a critical role in transferring skills across generations (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions can also restrict entry and keep wages artificially high, potentially negating their beneficial effects on skill development (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The complex interplay between institutions and human capital formation suggests that while institutions can enhance economic performance through skill development, their impact is contingent on how they are structured and enforced. This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional arrangements that promote long-term economic growth.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth extends beyond technological progress and human capital development to include broader social and political dynamics. This perspective aligns with North's critique of neoclassical models, which overlook the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes by focusing solely on inputs like land, labor, and capital (North 1989, p.5). The extractive nature of some institutions highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of how power dynamics influence institutional design and, consequently, economic growth. This perspective indicates that institutions actively shape economic trajectories, reflecting the interests and power structures within societies.

The interplay between technological progress, human capital development, and broader social and political dynamics underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth. This complex interplay suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutional arrangements is essential for fostering sustained economic development.

Technological advancement is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both underscore that technological progress drives long-run economic development. The growth of useful knowledge enables innovation, which in turn propels economic expansion. This process is not merely about the accumulation of new ideas but also involves the recombination of existing techniques (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Technological advancements often build on prior knowledge, leveraging analogical thinking to transform familiar concepts into novel inventions. Kuznets (1973, p.10) notes that many major innovations arise from specific needs within a country, highlighting the contextual nature of technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

van Zanden (2009, p.19); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) propose that institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by influencing the skill premiums and inequality. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) identifies specific institutions such as guilds that facilitated efficient labor markets, capital markets, and human capital formation. These institutions ensured fair execution of contracts, thereby promoting the intergenerational transfer of skills. This aspect is crucial because it directly links to economic performance by enhancing the efficiency of labor and capital markets. However, van Zanden (2009, p.19) acknowledges that guilds could also act as local cartels, restricting entry and maintaining higher wages than market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, thereby affecting long-term economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Institutions that ensure fair market operations and human capital investment are pivotal for sustaining economic growth over extended periods.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) assert that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering secure property rights during the economic growth of seventeenth-century England. They emphasize that effective enforcement of these institutions reduces transaction costs by facilitating market exchanges and promoting specialization, which in turn increases the volume of transactions. The evolution of these institutions was not merely about establishing rules but also about ensuring their consistent enforcement. This credibility is essential for sustaining long-term economic growth as it ensures that investors can trust that their property rights will be protected. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) observe that institutions complement reputation-building strategies, indicating that while institutions are crucial, they must work in conjunction with other mechanisms to fully support economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This interplay between institutional frameworks and reputational mechanisms underscores the complexity of fostering secure property rights and highlights the need for a multifaceted approach to institutional development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) document that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. Colonial powers established different types of institutions based on the expected mortality rates of Europeans, which in turn affected long-term economic development. These institutions persist today and have a profound impact on current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). A key mechanism is the rent-seeking behavior of colonial powers, which prioritized immediate gains over long-term development. This institutional legacy continues to affect economic outcomes by perpetuating systems that do not incentivize growth or innovation. This institutional path dependency is not deterministic; changes in political power dynamics or external shocks can alter institutional trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The Industrial Revolution brought significant economic growth, yet it did not uniformly improve biological well-being (Komlos 1998, p.8; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) posits that the process of technological modernization during this period led to an acceleration in capital stock growth. This growth was driven by both the returns from modernization itself and the reduction in capital goods prices relative to labor costs, which encouraged investment. One key factor driving this divergence is the relative price effect: as technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture lagged behind industry, the price of food relative to industrial goods increased. This shift led consumers to substitute cheaper carbohydrates for more nutritious protein-rich foods, exacerbating nutritional deficiencies. While population growth does not necessarily lead to rising food prices, the specific nature of technological change and the elasticity of the global food supply during this period played a crucial role in determining outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.12). These dynamics highlight that economic growth alone is insufficient for improving biological well-being; the distribution of income and the relative prices of essential goods are equally important.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring efficient resource allocation. Mokyr's work emphasizes the importance of institutional progress in sustaining economic improvement, highlighting that without it, technological advancements might be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). In line with this perspective, North argues that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This alignment demonstrates that institutions actively drive economic growth. They create an environment where technological innovations can be effectively utilized, ensuring that the benefits of new knowledge are widely distributed.

Moreover, institutions influence long-run economic performance through their impact on human capital formation and skill premiums. van Zanden demonstrates that efficient labor markets and effective institutions for human capital development contribute to sustained economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This aligns with Kuznets' assertion that technological progress is closely linked to institutional adjustments, which are necessary for the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Together, these findings underscore that institutions shape not only the pace but also the sustainability of economic growth by influencing the distribution of skills and knowledge.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth remain a subject of debate. The disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate technological progress or directly enhance productivity. Mokyr's work suggests that institutional progress complements technological advancements, preventing rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth by creating extractive institutions that do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a crucial role in determining economic outcomes. This is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s findings that institutions accounted for a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).
