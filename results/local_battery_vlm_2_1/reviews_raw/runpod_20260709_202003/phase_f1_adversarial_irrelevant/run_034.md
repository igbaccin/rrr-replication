The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics that addresses how societal structures and rules influence sustained prosperity. At stake is the understanding of whether and how institutional frameworks can foster technological advancement, capital accumulation, and efficient resource allocation over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on the primacy of technological progress versus institutional development as drivers of economic growth: some scholars emphasize the role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and protecting property rights, while others highlight the direct impact of technological innovations on productivity and output.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) shared finding that technological advancements are central to modern economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Both authors recognize that the application of new technologies requires institutional adjustments, but they diverge in their emphasis on the specific mechanisms through which this occurs. Abramovitz focuses on how technological opportunities reduce the cost of capital goods and encourage investment in modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4), while Kuznets highlights the necessity of institutional and ideological changes to efficiently utilize innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This tension underscores the complex interplay between technology and institutions, where each can influence long-run economic growth both directly and indirectly.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice are pivotal for economic growth by fostering secure property rights. This process is essential because it allows the economy to handle a growing number of transactions efficiently as specialization and division of labor increase (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast also emphasize that these institutional arrangements complement reputation-building strategies by providing constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This complementary role of institutions is crucial because it enhances the efficacy of the reputation mechanism, ensuring that economic agents can rely on the stability and predictability of property rights. This stability is vital for fostering long-term economic growth, as it encourages investment and innovation. However, North also cautions against overemphasizing neoclassical models that focus solely on inputs like land, labor, and capital, arguing that these models miss the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (North 1989, p.5). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing transaction costs and the enforcement of property rights. The evolution of political institutions governing public choice is therefore a fundamental aspect of long-run economic development, as it creates an environment conducive to market exchanges and sustained growth.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that this technological change allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated investment in agriculture. This was particularly evident during the period from 1880 to 1900, where the adoption of barbed wire fencing led to increased agricultural productivity by protecting crops from stray livestock and encouraging long-term investments in farming infrastructure.

The direct technological improvements brought about by barbed wire were substantial, but its primary impact lay in enhancing property rights enforcement. By creating clear boundaries, barbed wire reduced disputes over land use and minimized the damage caused by wandering cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This reduction in externalities created a more stable environment for agricultural development, as farmers could invest with greater confidence that their efforts would not be undermined by others' livestock. The technological effects of barbed wire were thus intertwined with its role in strengthening property rights, illustrating how institutional changes can amplify the benefits of technological innovations.

Technological advancements alone do not guarantee sustained growth; they must be accompanied by institutional frameworks that support long-term investment and innovation. The experience on the American Plains highlights how institutional changes can create an environment conducive to economic development, but it also underscores the need for a comprehensive approach that integrates technological, institutional, and social factors.

Temin (2002, p.4) shows that rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by labor reallocation from agriculture to industry (Temin 2002, p.4; Komlos 1998, p.8). This perspective is supported by Komlos, who notes that the Industrial Revolution led to significant shifts in food prices, driving a substitution of industrial commodities for agricultural products. The relative price of nutrients increased as technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture lagged behind those in industry. In Britain, this increase was 66 percent between 1770 and 1795. This shift in labor allocation had profound implications for economic growth, as it facilitated the movement of workers into more productive sectors. Komlos also highlights that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices (Komlos 1998, p.12). Rather, this outcome depends on factors such as the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply, which can mitigate or exacerbate price increases. These findings suggest that while labor reallocation was a key driver of growth during the post-WWII period, it must be understood within the broader context of technological advancements and global food supply dynamics.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) share the finding that economic growth is complex and influenced by various factors beyond just institutions. This complexity includes both exogenous factors and technological advancement, which can drive productivity gains through a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize rent extraction over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic highlights how institutional design can be influenced by factors such as the fear of losing political power or reluctance to initiate change, even if it means foregoing direct benefits from resulting economic gains. Acemoglu et al. also show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). In essence, this interplay between exogenous factors and technological advancement underscores how economic growth is a multifaceted process influenced by both institutional dynamics and broader socio-economic conditions.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be a primary driver shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) contends that the core economic institutions of this era, such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were not merely efficient responses to economic challenges but rather the outcomes of power struggles among different social groups. These conflicts often led to the establishment of institutions that protected the interests of the dominant classes at the expense of overall efficiency. Domar (1970, p.7) supports this view by illustrating how slavery or serfdom emerged when the land-to-labor ratio was high and labor was scarce, thereby highlighting how economic conditions influenced institutional development. These distributional conflicts have significant implications; they suggest that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe reflected the balance of power among different social groups. This perspective challenges the notion that efficiency is the sole driver of institutional development, instead emphasizing the role of power and conflict in shaping economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. Technological advancement accumulates useful knowledge which becomes a crucial resource for economic development. Mokyr notes that technological progress often depends on existing techniques, analogical thinking, and the recombination of ideas; this process helps to generate new inventions that drive economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress complements technological advancement and ensures the sustainability of economic improvements by mitigating rent-seeking behaviors. Without such institutional support, economic gains could be eroded over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay implies that while technological progress is crucial, it does not operate in isolation from institutional frameworks. Instead, institutions play a pivotal role in harnessing the benefits of technological advancements for sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and involves both technological progress and institutional adjustments. Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations by providing the necessary frameworks for technological adoption and implementation. The interplay between technology and institutions is evident in Kuznets' argument that institutions must adjust to efficiently utilize innovations, which suggests a complex relationship where technological advancements are harnessed through effective institutional support (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North 1989, p.5). This relationship is further supported by Mokyr's assertion that without institutional progress complementing technological progress, economic improvements could be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.12).

The dynamic interplay between institutions and technology is also evident in the catch-up process of economic growth. Abramovitz shows that the same technological opportunity permits rapid progress through modernization, encouraging a higher rate of capital stock growth and reducing the price of capital goods relative to labor (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic highlights how institutions enable countries to leverage technological opportunities for sustained growth. North underscores this by arguing that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which in turn supports economic growth (North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.6).

The historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains illustrates how institutional changes can significantly enhance agricultural development. Hornbeck demonstrates that barbed wire provided direct technological improvements and increased protection for property rights, reducing externalities from livestock damage and enabling farmers to better manage their resources (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The case of Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe’s core economic institutions further shows how distributional conflicts can shape the efficiency of these institutions. This highlights that while institutions aim to enhance efficiency, they are also influenced by broader social dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a pivotal role in harnessing technological advancements for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence this process. North argues that efficient property rights and enforcement are crucial, while Ogilvie highlights how distributional conflicts shape institutional efficiency (North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The historical evidence from barbed wire's impact on agricultural development settles this disagreement by showing that both effective property rights enforcement and social dynamics are essential for institutions to enhance technological adoption and economic growth.
