The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. At stake is understanding whether and how the structure of societies influences their economic trajectories over extended periods. Institutions are broadly defined as the rules that govern behavior, encompassing both formal rules like laws and informal norms such as social conventions (North 1989, p.6). The debate centers around two main positions: one argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.5) while the other posits that technological progress is the primary driver of long-run economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role. Some scholars emphasize institutional development as complementary to technological progress, suggesting that without supportive institutions, even significant technological advances may not translate into sustained economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The interplay between technology and institutions thus forms a critical nexus in understanding the dynamics of long-run economic growth.

Institutions facilitate market exchanges by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms. As economies grow, increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the volume of exchanges, making efficient institutions even more critical. This highlights how institutions shape long-run economic growth by enabling more efficient market operations, reducing the friction associated with economic exchanges. The role of institutions extends beyond just facilitating transactions; they also play a crucial part in mitigating coordination failures and fostering technological progress. This interdependence between technology and institutions underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic growth.

The catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. The same technological opportunities that permit rapid progress through modernization also encourage a higher rate of capital stock growth, partly because of the returns to modernization itself (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor, leading to an increase in aggregate output (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress indicates that backwardness carries opportunities for modernization through both embodied and disembodied technology. The accumulation of material and social technologies is a critical driver of economic growth, but the suitability of these innovations to the needs of less developed countries is also important. Even if basic scientific progress is not closely tied to specific technological needs, applied advances often respond directly to those needs (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This highlights how institutions that support technological innovation and adaptation are essential for leveraging these opportunities in economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in supporting the mass application of technological innovations. However, institutions also face distributional conflicts that can shape their development and efficiency. These conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency alone for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). The efficiency approach to understanding these institutions has been broadly applied, but it overlooks the broader social and political contexts in which they operate. This perspective emphasizes that while institutions may strive for efficiency, their actual functioning is often influenced by underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) support this view by highlighting how the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). This perspective emphasizes that political institutions are pivotal in maintaining commitments to rules governing economic exchanges. The Glorious Revolution is a key example: it led to institutional changes that constrained the state's ex post behavior, enhancing its ability to uphold agreements with constituents and thus fostering growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This approach underscores how institutions can complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie and Domar both argue that distributional conflicts are more significant than institutional efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Pre-modern institutions such as serfdom were shaped by the need to manage these conflicts rather than by a focus on economic efficiency. For example, when land was abundant but labor scarce, slavery or serfdom became prevalent, as Domar's model shows that this arrangement maximizes profits under those conditions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie elaborates on the guilds of pre-industrial Europe, noting that these institutions were often seen as inefficient cartels but were actually mechanisms for managing power dynamics and securing contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). The merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries exemplify this dynamic; they provided security and contract enforcement as club goods, highlighting the importance of distributional conflicts in their formation. However, Domar's analysis suggests a more nuanced view: while technological progress and capital accumulation can influence the profitability of slave versus free labor, it is not guaranteed that these factors will always reduce the economic viability of slavery (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.6). This insight underscores the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional structures in shaping long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Temin 2002, p.4) focus on technological advance and labor allocation as key drivers of economic growth. They suggest that technological progress can increase the capital-labor ratio by reducing the price of capital goods relative to labor, thereby accelerating economic growth. This dynamic is further amplified when backwardness presents opportunities for modernization in both embodied and disembodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This perspective is supported by Abramovitz's observation that technological progress can lead to an increase in aggregate output (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). However, Temin (2002, p.4) notes that while new growth theories have expanded on these ideas, they often do so at the expense of simplicity and elegance, introducing multiple inputs to growth such as education (Temin 2002, p.4). This suggests a nuanced view of economic growth where technological dynamics play a crucial role. This underscores the importance of considering both endogenous and exogenous factors when examining long-run economic growth.

Hornbeck demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This technological innovation allowed farmers to delineate and secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated more intensive and productive farming practices. This had a cascading effect on agricultural productivity, as farmers could invest more confidently in their land knowing that their property rights were better enforced. However, this technological advancement was not uniformly beneficial across all regions. The specific effects of barbed wire varied depending on local conditions, such as the availability of timber and the existing agricultural practices (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This variation underscores the importance of considering regional context when evaluating the impact of technological innovations on economic growth.

Mokyr notes that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel ones (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets elaborates that economic growth is closely tied to advancing technology, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This suggests a positive feedback loop between technological progress and scientific discovery.

This perspective underscores the importance of knowledge accumulation in driving economic growth through technological innovation. Furthermore, Kuznets highlights that mass application of technical innovations is closely connected with the further progress of science (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This implies that while institutions may not be the primary drivers, they still play a crucial role in facilitating and sustaining technological advancements. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is complex, suggesting that both factors are necessary for long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The mechanism hinges on the extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth; this pattern persists because politically powerful groups resist institutional change. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) argue that the mortality rates faced by European settlers influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which in turn shaped current income per capita. This finding is bolstered by a high correlation between these mortality rates and institutional measures over time. The persistence of these extractive institutions highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional arrangements on contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring efficient use of innovations. Mokyr shows that technological progress is heavily dependent on the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets argues that institutions must adapt to integrate new technologies into the economy, ensuring that scientific advancements translate into practical applications (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technology and institutions highlights how economic growth is not merely a function of technological innovation but also relies on institutional frameworks. These frameworks support the development and deployment of useful knowledge, thereby driving long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Institutions are essential for managing transaction costs and ensuring efficient property rights enforcement, which in turn supports economic growth. North argues that institutions that specify property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and enhancing overall economic efficiency (North 1989, p.6). Abramovitz shows that technological progress is influenced by the dynamics of social change and institutional adjustments, which support the adoption and diffusion of new technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). These insights underscore how institutions create an environment conducive to economic growth by reducing transaction costs and promoting the efficient use of resources.

Institutions also play a role in mitigating distributional conflicts that can hinder economic progress. Ogilvie shows that pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were often shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This suggests that institutional arrangements are not always driven by purely economic efficiency but also by the need to address social and political tensions.
