The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The stakes are high because the underlying debate concerns whether institutional frameworks can be designed to foster sustained economic development, or if growth is primarily driven by other factors such as technological progress or geographical advantages. One key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions themselves are shaped by broader socio-economic forces versus being independent drivers of growth. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and ensuring property rights, while others contend that distributional conflicts and power dynamics within societies play a more significant role in determining institutional structures. Technological progress is also seen as a critical factor, with some researchers highlighting its dependence on existing knowledge and the need for complementary institutional support to ensure sustainability. The debate ultimately hinges on whether institutions are best understood as outcomes of deeper historical processes or as independent variables that can be manipulated to achieve desired economic outcomes.

The relationship between technological progress and institutions is particularly intriguing. Technological advancements often require institutional support to realize their full potential. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which in turn demands institutional adjustments for efficient use. He notes that the mass application of technological innovations is closely linked to scientific progress, creating a positive feedback loop (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr echoes this sentiment, emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may be unsustainable. He highlights coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors as potential obstacles that institutions must address to ensure long-term growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the importance of a supportive institutional framework in harnessing the benefits of technological progress for sustained economic development.

The critical role of property rights in shaping long-term economic outcomes is underscored by North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.6) shows how the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling transaction costs per exchange despite a growing aggregate volume (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) add that these efficient institutions can be traced back to colonial experiences, where lower mortality rates among European settlers led to the establishment of inclusive institutions that fostered economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is evident in former colonies where European settlement was feasible, resulting in long-term economic benefits. Acemoglu et al (2002) point out that equilibrium institutions may encourage development but they can also be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the long-run impact of institutions on growth is contingent upon their inclusivity and ability to mitigate rent-seeking behaviors. These studies collectively highlight how institutional frameworks, shaped by historical contexts, significantly influence economic trajectories over centuries.

Building on this foundation, Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and North and Weingast (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) emphasize the pivotal role of secure property rights in agricultural development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows this through the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which enabled farmers to better protect their property rights. This protection allowed farmers to invest more in their land and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This highlights how well-defined property rights can mitigate rent-seeking behaviors and encourage investment. The effectiveness of these institutions depends on their ability to enforce rules and maintain commitments over time. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate this by showing that restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve its ability to maintain bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This suggests that while secure property rights are essential for long-run economic growth, the institutions governing these rights must also be robust and enforceable.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share a view that technological advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting how useful knowledge accumulation fuels invention, which in turn drives economic development. This process involves complex interactions and dependencies among existing technologies and new ideas. For instance, much invention during the Industrial Revolution took the form of recombining existing techniques (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets complements this view by noting that technological progress often responds to specific needs, illustrating how necessity can drive innovation. This interplay between technology and economic growth is further complicated by institutional factors. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure sustainability. Kuznets supports this idea with evidence from historical periods where mass application of innovations required significant institutional adjustments for efficient use. The accumulation of useful knowledge and technological progress are essential drivers of economic growth, but their effectiveness is contingent upon supportive institutions that can manage the complexities arising from these advancements.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7); (Domar 1970, p.7) extend this analysis by examining the historical context of pre-industrial Europe's institutional arrangements. The authors focus on the efficiency of institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, arguing that distributional conflicts are a better explanation for their existence than efficiency considerations alone. Ogilvie highlights that various pre-modern institutions have been reinterpreted through an efficiency lens, suggesting that they were solutions to specific obstacles in early trade and economic activities (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). For instance, merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries provided security and contract enforcement as club goods, while craft guilds played a role in technological change and apprenticeship. Domar's analysis supports this by showing how slavery or serfdom emerged when the land-to-labor ratio was high and labor was scarce (Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective suggests that institutions like serfdom were responses to specific economic conditions and social structures. Domar notes that technological progress and capital accumulation can alter the profitability of slave versus free labor, indicating that institutional arrangements are dynamic and responsive to changing economic factors (Domar 1970, p.6). This dynamism implies that while institutions may initially serve efficiency purposes, their long-term effects on economic growth depend on how they adapt to evolving conditions.

Institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the framework within which economic activities operate and evolve over time (North 1989, p.6). Kuznets shows that technological progress alone is insufficient to sustain modern economic growth; it must be complemented by institutional adjustments that facilitate efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North 1989, p.5). Institutions, therefore, act as the bridge between technological advancements and sustained economic development. Mokyr elaborates on this dynamic by emphasizing how institutions can mitigate coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors, thereby ensuring long-term economic sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.12).

The role of institutions extends beyond merely facilitating transactions; they fundamentally shape the incentives and constraints that drive economic behavior. Ogilvie argues that many pre-industrial European institutions were efficient solutions to transaction costs but also responses to distributional conflicts among different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective highlights how institutions can reflect power dynamics within society, influencing economic outcomes accordingly. Hornbeck's analysis of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains illustrates how technological changes can interact with institutional frameworks to enhance property rights and reduce externalities, thus fostering agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.4).

Institutions also play a critical role in determining long-run economic growth by shaping the distribution of resources and opportunities. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often impeded long-term economic development in former colonies, underscoring how extractive institutions can hinder growth (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that the nature of institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, significantly impacts economic trajectories over extended periods. Kuznets adds that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advancements for sustained growth, as it ensures that innovations are effectively utilized and integrated into the economy (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North 1989, p.5).

The disagreement lies in whether institutions primarily serve efficiency purposes or reflect distributional conflicts. Ogilvie's work suggests that many institutions were responses to power dynamics rather than purely efficient solutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of colonial institutions shows how extractive frameworks can stifle long-term growth by concentrating resources and opportunities in the hands of a few (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from former European colonies settles this disagreement. The variation in economic performance among these colonies, despite similar geographic endowments, highlights that institutional arrangements, whether inclusive or extractive, are decisive factors in shaping long-term economic growth trajectories.
