The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding how different institutional arrangements can either facilitate or hinder sustained economic progress over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors such as technology and population play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that technological advancements are the main catalysts for economic growth, while others contend that efficient institutions are crucial for leveraging these advancements effectively. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.5) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, but Kuznets (1973, p.10; 1973, p.4) suggests that technological progress and institutional adjustments are both necessary for sustained economic improvement.

The relationship between institutions and technological advancements is complex. Mokyr (2005, p.12) notes that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of existing techniques, highlighting the importance of institutions in facilitating the dissemination and application of new technologies.

This connection between technology and institutions underscores that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be accompanied by institutional changes to drive long-run economic growth.

North argues that institutions governing public choice are critical for economic growth, as they facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework reduces transaction costs by ensuring that property rights are well-defined and effectively enforced. When political institutions specify "efficient" property rights, it translates to lower costs per transaction while increasing the volume of economic exchanges due to greater specialization and division of labor. In this context, North and Weingast argue that credible commitments by political institutions are crucial for maintaining agreements with constituents, such as preventing expropriation of wealth. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional reliability in sustaining long-term economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions can be extractive if they maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). This implies that while secure property rights are essential for economic development, the nature of these institutions and their enforcement mechanisms must align with broader societal goals.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.3) extend this idea by showing that the legacy of colonial institutions can perpetuate or disrupt long-term economic trajectories. The historical context of European colonization and the associated mortality rates played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions implemented. This institutional framework was a product of colonial policies and also a response to the environmental challenges faced by European settlers. Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates on this by highlighting how centralized governance and well-developed markets in regions like the Kongo kingdom facilitated trade with Europeans, underscoring the importance of pre-existing institutional structures. However, Austin (2008, p.22) qualifies that economic rents played a crucial role before and during industrialization, often creating surpluses from restricted entry to or exit from markets. This suggests that while extractive institutions can hinder growth, they can also drive initial accumulation processes necessary for later development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54); Kuznets (1973, p.10) build on this by illustrating how technological innovations are not just drivers of growth but also catalysts for broader societal changes. The growth of useful knowledge propels economies forward by enabling new production methods and enhancing productivity. This process of recombination and analogical thinking is crucial for sustaining economic improvement over the long term. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further illustrates this by noting that inventions are often responses to specific needs, highlighting how technology evolves in tandem with societal demands (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that while technological progress is relatively irreversible and monotonic, institutional progress is less so. Without complementary institutional advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements can be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors, which might dissipate gains from technological advances (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets (1973, p.10) supports this point by noting that mass application of innovations requires not just technical but also institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This suggests that while technology is a powerful engine of growth, its benefits are maximized when institutions facilitate its effective deployment.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30); Domar (1970, p.7) further illustrate that the impact of technology on economic development is deeply intertwined with historical contexts and institutional frameworks. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological advancement allowed for more efficient land use and increased productivity, directly linking institutional changes in property rights with economic growth. However, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) qualify this by noting that institutional frameworks in societies with greater inequality often advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This indicates that while technological advancements can spur economic growth, the institutional context in which they occur is pivotal. The efficacy of property rights in fostering economic development is contingent on the broader institutional landscape, which can either amplify or mitigate their benefits.

Pamuk's work shows that historical events like the Black Death had profound effects on wage structures and technological innovations across Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22; Komlos 1998, p.8). The demographic shock of the Black Death led to increased wages relative to rents and capital prices, driving a substitution of land and capital for labor. This process was not uniform; urban guilds played a crucial role in producing new technologies, highlighting the regional disparities in technological advancement. Komlos's analysis complements this by showing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These findings suggest that while historical events can spur economic changes, the distribution of these changes is influenced by pre-existing structural factors such as technological capabilities and income inequality. Temin's examination of post-WWII Europe further illustrates this point, arguing that rapid economic growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.3). This disequilibrium highlights how shifts in economic sectors can lead to significant growth spurts, but these shifts are contingent on broader structural conditions. However, Temin also notes that new growth theories, which emphasize factors like education, offer additional inputs to growth beyond traditional labor allocation, suggesting a more complex interplay of factors (Temin 2002, p.4). This complexity underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions when analyzing economic growth, including historical events, technological advancements, and structural changes.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4); Ogilvie (2007, p.7) underscore that the multifaceted nature of economic growth necessitates a holistic approach, integrating technological progress and social transformations. This dynamic is driven by the reduction in technological age and the increasing returns to modernization, which lower the relative price of capital goods compared to labor (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) extends this perspective by examining how distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions, suggesting that these conflicts are crucial in understanding the efficiency and longevity of pre-industrial European institutions. For instance, merchant guilds initially provided security and contract enforcement but evolved into cartels over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.7), indicating that institutional forms can adapt to changing economic conditions. This adaptation is not merely a response to technological progress but also reflects broader social changes and the evolving nature of distributional conflicts within societies. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) cautions that while these institutions may initially enhance efficiency, they can become obsolete as societal needs shift (Ogilvie 2007, p.6), highlighting the need for continuous institutional adaptation in response to new economic demands. Long-run economic growth is about technological progress and how societies manage and adapt their institutions in the face of changing social dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted. The growth of useful knowledge underpins technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54), but Abramovitz shows that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change(Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Long-run economic growth is about technological progress and how societies manage and adapt their institutions in the face of changing social dynamics. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). The role of institutions is therefore paramount in facilitating long-run economic growth by ensuring efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6).
