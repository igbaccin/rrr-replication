The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is understanding whether the path to prosperity lies through well-crafted institutions that guide markets, or if other factors are more critical. One key disagreement centers on how institutions interact with economic growth over time: some scholars argue that institutions create the conditions for sustained growth by fostering secure property rights and low transaction costs; others contend that institutions merely reflect underlying power dynamics or geographic determinants of development. The debate also touches on whether institutional change is endogenous, driven by internal political dynamics, or exogenous, imposed through colonial rule. A third position posits that economic institutions are shaped by the interplay between technological progress and factor endowments.

The discussion on how institutions influence long-run economic growth begins with the foundational work of North and Weingast, who emphasize the role of credible commitment in fostering economic development. They argue that political institutions which enforce secure property rights can significantly boost economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This argument is complemented by North’s broader thesis that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and enabling specialization (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). North's perspective is particularly relevant as it highlights the role of institutions in providing a stable framework for economic interactions. The political factors underpinning economic growth are crucial: credible commitment by the regime to enforce rules can improve the efficacy of reputation mechanisms, which alone may be insufficient to prevent reneging (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This view underscores how institutions complement and enhance other mechanisms that support economic stability.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated through the lens of historical analysis. Broadberry and Gupta provide evidence that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This perspective suggests that institutional differences played a significant role in this divergence, as institutions influenced labour productivity and wage structures across regions. Van Zanden contends that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This highlights the complexity of institutional effects on long-run growth: while institutions may set the stage for economic activity, other factors such as technological progress and factor endowments also play crucial roles.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is further complicated by the role of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective underscores how exogenous shocks, such as colonial policies, can shape institutions and thereby influence long-run economic growth. Austin maintains that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11; Austin 2008, p.1). This view highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors over time to shape economic outcomes. The interplay between technology, factor endowments, and institutional change is crucial in determining long-run economic growth: institutions are not static but evolve in response to technological progress and changing economic conditions.

This institutional evolution enhanced the efficacy of reputation mechanisms by providing constraints where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging, such as expropriation of wealth (North and Weingast 1989 (North and Weingast 1989, p.7)). These mechanisms are crucial for maintaining economic growth because they ensure that individuals can confidently engage in transactions with strangers.

North notes that the evolution of complex social frameworks depends on institutional reliability, which reduces uncertainties in increasingly interdependent economic situations (North 1989, p.2). This highlights that while institutions are essential for economic growth, their effectiveness is contingent upon their ability to adapt and enforce rules reliably over time.

Acemoglu et al. ( ) argue that colonial institutions have shaped long-run economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive frameworks in colonies with high mortality rates. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic growth, as they sought to secure their interests in hostile environments (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This institutional framework was a direct response to the ecological challenges faced by European settlers and resulted in institutions that favored resource extraction over sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these extractive institutions were not merely a product of geographic factors but were actively shaped by the political and economic interests of colonial powers ( ). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic performance, as they continue to influence current income per capita in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) report, “It is important to emphasize that "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development.”

Distributional conflicts between social groups are the driving force behind economic performance and inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie identifies that pre-industrial Europe's core institutions were shaped by these conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These distributional struggles led to the development of institutions such as serfdom, communal structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. The merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries, for example, are often misinterpreted as inefficient cartels when in fact they provided security and contract enforcement as club goods (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this dynamic by examining New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This distributional conflict mechanism is qualified by Ogilvie's observation that individual economic institutions are interdependent within a broader framework; they cannot be studied in isolation from one another (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Therefore, the institutions that emerge from these conflicts are not merely products of efficiency but are deeply embedded within complex social and political structures.

He and van Zanden both discuss economic growth in Europe during early modern times. Austin's work underscores the importance of considering specific aspects of the natural environment when examining labour productivity, emphasizing that prevalent labour scarcity south of the Sahara is not inconsistent with higher average population density and a larger urban population (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective aligns with van Zanden's critique of optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth. Van Zanden observes that while some scholars have highlighted long-term productivity growth in traditional agriculture, these views often overlook the decline in real wages during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This tension between productivity gains and living standards is further illuminated by Komlos' findings. During the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Austin 2008, p.11; Komlos 1998, p.13). The relative price effect of nutrients increased as technological change in agriculture lagged behind industrial advancements, leading consumers to substitute carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This shift highlights how economic growth did not uniformly improve living standards during this period (Komlos 1998, p.8).

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Pamuk (2007, p.8) argue that the divergence in economic growth between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This perspective is supported by Bryant (2006, p.29), who suggests that the structural changes in European agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, fostering a dynamic relationship between town and country. This dynamic allowed for economies of scale and capital accumulation necessary for industrial expansion. In Europe, larger agricultural units facilitated a synergistic mix of grain production and livestock rearing, which improved diets, increased productivity, and enhanced soil fertility (Bryant 2006, p.28). The English population grew significantly during this period while the percentage engaged in agriculture fell by nearly half, accompanied by remarkable gains in per capita productivity and rising discretionary incomes. The divergence between Europe and Asia can be traced to these structural changes that initiated long-term transformations in both regions. Pamuk highlights how the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to significant wage gaps between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, illustrating how historical events can have lasting economic impacts (Pamuk 2007, p.8).

Hopkins and Nunn argue that historical factors such as slavery significantly shaped African economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). The slave trades in Africa, which involved domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping, had adverse effects on subsequent development by disrupting social structures and diverting resources away from productive activities. This disruption resulted from the institutional changes that followed as well as the slave trade itself (Nunn 2008, p.3). The production of slaves led to the establishment of institutions designed to facilitate the extraction and export of human resources, which in turn hindered economic growth by prioritizing resource appropriation over local development. However, Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, suggesting that gender roles played a crucial part in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. Women's control over agricultural production and labor allocation was pivotal in sustaining economic activities despite the disruptive effects of slavery (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The dynamic interplay between historical factors such as slavery and gender roles reveals how institutions can both hinder and facilitate long-run economic growth, depending on their specific configurations and the social contexts in which they operate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply interconnected with historical contexts. North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This aligns with Austin's argument that specific institutional configurations could facilitate or hinder economic development, depending on their interaction with other factors such as gender roles and historical events like slavery (Austin 2008, p.1; van Zanden 1999, p.2). For example, the Glorious Revolution in England led to political institutions that improved the state's ability to maintain commitments, thereby enhancing economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7).

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further elucidated by North, who argues that transaction costs are critical in understanding how institutions affect economic performance. Efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce these costs, facilitating market exchanges and economic development (North 1989, p.6; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia, which highlights that institutional differences played a significant role in shaping long-run economic trajectories. The relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected lower productivity in the tradable goods sector, illustrating how institutional frameworks can influence economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23; van Zanden 1999, p.2).

The reversal of fortune thesis posits that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in altering relative incomes among former European colonies (Austin 2008, p.11). Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists shaped economic development paths in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This view is consistent with North's assertion that institutional reliability is essential for confidence in complex interdependent situations, which are characteristic of growing economies (North 1989, p.2).

The dynamic interplay between historical factors such as slavery and gender roles reveals how institutions can both hinder and facilitate long-run economic growth. The specific configurations of these institutions and their social contexts determine their impact on economic trajectories. Austin shows that the Atlantic slave trade was driven by commercial motivations, where the exchange-value product of Africans' labor in Africa was lower than in the destinations to which enslaved Africans were taken (Austin 2008, p.11). This reflects how institutional frameworks can influence economic outcomes through complex interactions with historical and social factors.
