The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. Institutions are the rules of the game for society, defining incentives and constraints that shape decision-making processes (North 1989, p.9). The stakes are high because understanding how institutions influence economic outcomes can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development. A key disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural traits, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and secure investment environments are crucial for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others contend that institutions alone cannot explain the reversal of fortune observed in former colonies, suggesting that historical paths and varying colonial policies also played a critical role (Austin 2008, p.15).

One stream of research focuses on the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes in various regions. For instance, settlements like the United States and Australia developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Colonies such as the Congo or Gold Coast established extractive states focused on resource extraction for the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks significantly influenced current income per capita levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.9). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the interaction between institutions and other factors is more complex than previously thought. This perspective underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

A related stream emphasizes how institutional changes introduced by colonialism drove the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups, often designed to maximize rents rather than long-run growth, are crucial for understanding development outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective aligns with North's view that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth. For example, the English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This historical context shows that institutional changes can have profound long-term effects on economic performance.

Another stream identifies antecedents that trigger institutional development. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and promoting economic development. The success of these institutional changes is evident in capital market evidence, highlighting the critical role of credible commitment underpinning long-term growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This stream complements the direct impact perspective by illustrating how antecedent political factors can shape institutions that drive economic growth.

The interplay between institutions and economic outcomes is further illuminated by research on the new economic history of Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that understanding this history can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective aligns with the view that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). By focusing on the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, this stream underscores the importance of institutional quality in fostering long-run growth.

The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the bank and new financial tools, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. The political trade-offs that occurred during this period, such as exchanging certain rights to Parliament for revenues, were driven by fiscal crises like the Hundred Years' War.

The evidence from capital markets in England underscores the significance of these institutional developments. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The degree to which regimes are committed to these rules is critical, as it influences how these rules may be changed. This commitment to credible enforcement mechanisms ensures the stability and predictability needed for long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that as economies grow, specialization and division of labor increase, multiplying the aggregate volume of exchanges and making transaction costs a growing proportion of GNP.

These institutions were shaped by the mortality rates of Europeans and the resulting colonization strategies. Where mortality was high, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to their home countries. Where mortality was low, they established settlements with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound effects on economic development. The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century was crucial. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by demonstrating consistent results across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These findings underscore that colonial institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than long-run growth, created lasting impacts on economic trajectories. This institutional legacy persists in shaping contemporary economic outcomes across former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutional frameworks in societies marked by high inequality have significantly influenced economic trajectories by limiting competitive dynamics. This institutional framework is rooted in the unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset of colonial settlements (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). In particular, European colonizers established institutions that favored elites, who then used their influence to shape policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance. This dynamic is evident in societies where factor endowments varied widely, leading to persistent inequality. The legacy of these institutions continues to affect economic development today. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the African continent's extensive history of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had profound implications for subsequent economic development. These trade networks and their associated institutions disrupted preexisting social structures, creating lasting institutional legacies that continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The new economic history of Africa highlights that institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the scope of development policy widened to incorporate governance, allowing institutions and organizations to come to the fore. Frankema (2012, p.15) further illustrates this dynamic by examining the role of British rule in fostering formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. He notes that demand for missionary education was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional change had profound implications for economic development, as it reshaped the incentives and opportunities available to African populations. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this perspective by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. They show that patterns of economic growth were tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by a range of factors including transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while institutional changes driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors were crucial, their impact on economic outcomes was also mediated by broader economic conditions. This interplay between institutional change and economic development underscores the complexity of Africa's historical trajectory.

The slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within Africa that continues to affect contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) document how this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. By examining the impact of interethnic slave exports, they demonstrate that the internal norms of mistrust are more influential than external environmental factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. This finding is significant because it highlights how historical legacies shape present-day social and economic behaviors. This interplay between historical mistrust and institutional adaptability suggests that the enduring effects of the slave trade are both cultural and structural. The persistence of this mistrust underscores the need for policies that address both the internal norms of distrust and the external factors that reinforce them, such as weak institutions and limited economic opportunities.

Mokyr asserts that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49), a perspective shared by Kuznets who argues that technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This view emphasizes how technological advancements require innovation as well as concomitant institutional changes to fully realize their economic potential. Mokyr further elaborates on the role of coordination failures in hindering growth, suggesting that without appropriate institutional progress, even significant technological advances can be limited in their impact (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets highlights that the resolution of conflicts arising from technological changes is essential for sustained economic growth, underscoring the importance of a strong political fabric to manage these transitions peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while knowledge accumulation drives growth, institutional robustness ensures its sustainability. The relationship between technological progress and institutional adaptability is complementary and interdependent, with each factor reinforcing the other's contributions to long-term economic development.

The significance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their role in establishing secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions specifying efficient property rights are critical for reducing transaction costs, which in turn fosters economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this foundation by emphasizing the importance of institutional evolution in understanding long-term economic development, highlighting how secure private property rights have been key to sustained growth. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptability is crucial, as institutions that enforce the rule of law encourage investment and manage transitions peacefully (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Therefore, the robustness of institutions ensures that technological advancements contribute positively to economic development over the long term.

This relationship between technological change and institutional robustness highlights how institutions shape growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, influencing current income per capita. These findings align with North's emphasis on secure property rights as a cornerstone of economic development (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic is further supported by Hopkins' analysis, which shows that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets in England and France illustrates how institutional changes underpinning secure property rights fostered economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the success of these institutions is evident in the remarkable performance of capital markets during this period.

The enduring role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further underscored by their ability to manage conflicts arising from technological changes, ensuring sustained development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, can be extractive and not necessarily conducive to long-term growth. This aligns with North's view on the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from colonial strategies and their institutional legacies shows how different paths of institutional development have led to varied economic outcomes, emphasizing the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This is evident in Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of European colonization strategies, which highlights how extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The measurement of institutional quality through various indices supports this claim, showing that countries with strong institutions tend to have higher economic growth rates. This empirical evidence from historical and contemporary data settles the disagreement about the role of institutions by demonstrating their direct impact on long-run economic development.
