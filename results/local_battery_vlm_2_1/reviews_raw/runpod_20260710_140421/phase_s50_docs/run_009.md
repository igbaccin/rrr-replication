The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or initial conditions, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for fostering investment and economic activity. Others contend that institutional changes are merely one piece of a complex puzzle that includes geographic endowments, demographic factors, and historical contingencies.

The debate is further complicated by the interplay between different types of institutions. Some scholars focus on political institutions that govern public choice and enforce property rights, while others emphasize economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges. The interaction between these institutional frameworks and their impact on long-run economic growth remains a subject of ongoing inquiry. This review will explore how various streams of scholarship address this question, highlighting the nuances and complexities involved in understanding the role of institutions in economic development. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs. These institutions fostered secure property rights by providing efficient mechanisms for market exchanges. This institutional shift was pivotal because it addressed a critical dimension of economic growth: the security of long-term capital markets. In this context, public choice institutions evolved in England to ensure that new financial instruments were both created and effectively enforced. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms led to falling transaction costs per exchange. This reduction was vital for economic growth as it facilitated increasing specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. Furthermore, this institutional framework underpinned by credible commitment fostered a stable environment where long-term investments could flourish (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) traces these changes to fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, illustrating how political and economic institutions coevolved in response to specific historical pressures. This institutional evolution involved creating new rules as well as establishing mechanisms for their enforcement and adaptation (North and Weingast 1989, p.2)(North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast's analysis of capital markets highlights the broader implications of these changes, suggesting that the institutional framework developed in England significantly influenced economic performance by fostering secure property rights. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to factors other than institutions.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions aimed to maximize the rents of European colonists, fostering long-term development was not a priority. A key mechanism through which these institutions shaped economic growth was by determining the extent of private property protection and the rule of law. This differential impact stemmed from geographic factors as well as the institutional legacies left by colonial powers. The persistence of these institutions over time further solidified their influence on long-run economic outcomes, as evidenced by current income per capita levels in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). For example, Acemoglu et al.'s research demonstrates that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely linked to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming during colonial times, illustrating how gender dynamics influenced economic activities. This pattern shows that the institutional framework established during colonialism shaped land allocation and entrenched gender disparities, which had profound implications for economic development. Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). These findings suggest that the type of colonial institution introduced significantly influenced educational outcomes, which in turn affected long-run economic growth.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that technological innovation stimulates economic development and requires complementary institutional adjustments to fully realize its potential. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress necessitates significant shifts in societal structures, including the dissolution of traditional family patterns and rural lifestyles, as well as the emergence of national states capable of managing growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This institutional adaptation is crucial for sustaining economic growth over extended periods. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this point by noting that technological progress alone is insufficient; it must be paired with institutional developments that reduce access costs to knowledge and foster coordination among economic actors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions are essential for harnessing the benefits of technological progress; they complement technological innovations to ensure sustained economic development.

The role of institutions is central to this framework, as they facilitate the efficient use of new technologies and manage the social conflicts that arise from rapid economic change. This interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is critical for long-term economic development, as it ensures that societal structures can accommodate and benefit from new technologies without succumbing to internal strife. The historical record shows that societies which successfully navigated this dual process of technological and institutional change were able to achieve sustained growth, while those that failed to adapt often experienced stagnation or decline.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) examine how institutions influence inequality and development, offering insights into the broader impact of institutional frameworks. The two authors agree that institutions shape economic outcomes by determining how resources are allocated and how opportunities are distributed among different segments of society.

Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation mechanism suggests a dynamic interplay between local economic conditions and institutional responses, which can either mitigate or exacerbate inequality. For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial administrations might encourage commodity production by indigenous populations, thereby fostering economic activity that could reduce disparities. The policies of colonial governments, which sought fiscal security, often created incentives for subjects to maintain the status quo, perpetuating existing inequalities.

These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is particularly relevant for understanding long-term economic development, as it illustrates how unequal institutional structures can hinder broad-based growth by concentrating wealth and power in the hands of a few.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical processes. Institutions matter because they define the rules that govern economic interactions, influence investment decisions, and shape the distribution of resources over time (North 1989, p.9). The creation of secure property rights, for instance, has been a critical factor in fostering economic development by reducing transaction costs and encouraging long-term investments (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth. The impact of institutions extends beyond securing property rights to include the complex ways in which these rights are distributed and enforced across different social groups (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The distribution and enforcement of property rights vary significantly based on historical and political contexts. The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus contingent on specific historical contexts and the interactions between different institutional arrangements. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may resist changes that threaten their power or do not directly benefit them (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth, as shown by the impact of secure property rights on investment and development. The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which extractive institutions can persist despite their negative effects on long-run growth. This debate is settled by historical evidence from colonial contexts, where extractive institutions designed by European settlers significantly affected current income per capita (North 1989, p.9).
