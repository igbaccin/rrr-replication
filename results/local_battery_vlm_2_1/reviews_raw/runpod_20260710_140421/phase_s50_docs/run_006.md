The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and historical sociology. The stakes are high: a compelling account of institutional influence on economic development can explain global inequalities in wealth and standards of living. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who emphasize the primacy of institutions, specifically those that enforce property rights and encourage investment, as key drivers of growth versus those who argue that other factors such as cultural beliefs or specific historical contexts are more influential. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. A clear understanding of how institutions influence long-run economic growth can guide policymakers in designing effective interventions to foster sustainable development. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they operate alongside other factors such as cultural beliefs or specific historical contexts, remains unresolved. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33); (North 1989, p.9) establish that institutional determinants of economic growth are pivotal, with institutions shaping long-run economic outcomes through their influence on transaction costs and property rights. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this by examining the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The establishment of secure property rights was pivotal for the development of long-term capital markets. This institutional change facilitated market exchanges and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further support this perspective by showing that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive institutions established in some colonies significantly impacted their economic trajectories.

This insight qualifies the downstream implication of institutional change, highlighting that even when institutions are established to facilitate economic activity, they may still be extractive. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies underscores how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide empirical evidence supporting this perspective by demonstrating the persistence of institutional effects through various measures of institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This consistency across different indicators strengthens the argument that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, even when controlling for other factors.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They demonstrate that political factors driving economic growth encompassed both the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules, which secured credible commitments from the regime or sovereign to maintain them (Author Year p.N). This institutional framework allowed Britain to avoid the pitfalls of extractive institutions seen in other parts of Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) elaborates on this by emphasizing that a robust understanding of institutions should be an indispensable tool for economic historians, noting that institutions are essential instruments for long-term research programs. This perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and credible commitments in fostering economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) also acknowledges the complexity of institutional change by highlighting how shared beliefs within a society can influence its institutional structure. This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness is contingent on broader cultural and social factors. The role of these factors in shaping long-run economic growth remains an area for further exploration.

When a society establishes institutions that guarantee the stability and enforceability of property rights, it fosters a climate conducive to investment and economic expansion. This institutional framework protects individual rights and encourages innovation and entrepreneurship. Therefore, the long-term success of economic growth depends on both the institutional framework and the cultural environment in which it operates.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa has had enduring effects on contemporary trust levels, which are significantly lower due to a culture of mistrust. This claim is shared by Austin who shows that the slave trade's legacy persists through its impact on institutional development (Austin 2008, p.2). The slave trade created a persistent climate of mistrust within African societies, where individuals were more likely to perceive others as untrustworthy and less inclined to cooperate with one another. This mistrust, in turn, undermined the development of strong institutions by eroding social capital and reducing collective action. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) explain that this climate of mistrust is an institutional legacy that continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes. The slave trade's impact on trust levels has had profound implications for Africa's development trajectory, as it has hindered the formation of effective institutions necessary for sustained economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), alongside van Zanden (2009, p.19), examine historical roots of economic divergence between Europe and Asia by focusing on factors such as productivity in traded goods sectors. They show that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, which underscores a crucial role for service sectors in driving economic growth during the early modern period. The skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.3). High wages for skilled labourers in Europe yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than those for unskilled labourers, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed. This divergence was already well underway before 1800, challenging narratives that attribute it solely to post-1800 industrialization. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) points out that while institutions like guilds facilitated skill transfer, they also acted as local cartels, potentially hindering broader economic growth by restricting entry into trades. This tension highlights how institutional structures can both enable and constrain long-term economic performance.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that women played pivotal roles in African economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), even as institutions like customary law reinforced their marginalization. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack describe how women were often relegated to less fertile lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production under colonial rule. Hopkins elaborates on this institutional dimension by highlighting how the new economic history of Africa emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. The focus on 'good' institutions suggests that effective governance and legal frameworks can drive economic growth by fostering an environment conducive to investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This institutional complexity indicates that while strong institutions can promote growth, their impact is contingent on how they are implemented and interacted with by different societal groups.

Frankema and other scholars show that British colonial rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This influence was mediated by the introduction of new social and political institutions that altered Africans' perceptions of the benefits of formal education. Frankema's analysis reveals that African leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that were influenced by these institutional changes, which in turn shaped demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). The broader economic implications of this educational development are nuanced. While formal education was promoted under British rule, the post-independence period saw significant shifts in budget policies, including increased military spending and subsidies. The escalation of these expenditures displaced capital and recurrent spending, which could have dampened economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), Simson 2019). Nevertheless, despite these challenges, the legacy of British colonial education policies continued to influence long-term economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements necessitate institutional adjustments to facilitate efficient use. For example, Kuznets highlights that modern technology demands significant institutional changes, such as resolving conflicts arising from technological shifts without weakening societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Institutions may not always adapt swiftly enough to support technological progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that while economic reasonableness grows alongside technological advancements, this growth is often non-linear and can be reversible if institutional changes lag behind (Mokyr 2005, p.54). In other words, the sustainability of economic improvement relies on timely institutional adjustments that complement technological progress.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across several streams of literature. One stream focuses on how historical institutional changes have influenced current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to extractive states or inclusive ones (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This points to the enduring impact of historical institutional decisions on contemporary economic performance. North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions matter in economic growth by reducing transaction costs and increasing security of property rights (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation, indicating that different historical periods and paths must be considered to understand institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2).

Another stream examines how distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002) finding that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These insights highlight the importance of understanding power dynamics in institutional development. Hopkins emphasizes the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development, showing that African economies can benefit from better governance and institutional reforms (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This indicates that institutional quality is crucial for economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) shows that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to declining transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement lies in how different historical periods and paths influence institutional evolution. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the case of European settlers establishing different institutions based on mortality rates, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27).
