The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science. At stake are the mechanisms driving sustained prosperity versus stagnation across nations. The debate centers on whether institutions are primary drivers or secondary factors influenced by other variables such as geography, culture, or technological progress. Institutions matter because they shape incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity. This is not to claim that institutions operate in a vacuum: there are live disagreements about the extent of their influence relative to other factors. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving growth, while others argue for rival causes such as the role of culture or the impact of colonial policies in shaping economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. and North both underscore the importance of institutions in fostering long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments illustrate how transaction costs can be reduced, facilitating market exchanges and economic expansion (North 1989, p.9). These findings highlight that the institutional framework established during colonial times persists over time and continues to affect long-term economic outcomes.

North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, and this perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 was pivotal, as it led to a decline in transaction costs through financial innovation, which in turn facilitated long-term economic growth. This development highlights how institutions can reduce transaction costs and enhance market efficiency (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. provide further support for this perspective by detailing the impact of colonial institutions on current income per capita: European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and may not always promote economic development. This highlights the complex interplay between political power, institutional design, and economic performance.

Building on this, North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework, which included mechanisms for enforcing rules and ensuring commitment to them, underpinned the remarkable success of capital markets during this period. The establishment of these institutions involved a complex interplay between political factors and economic development, with the regime's commitment to binding rules playing a critical role (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) echoes this perspective by highlighting that institutional changes in England involved more than just economic exchange; they also concerned enforcement and modification of rules. The secure property rights and credible commitments fostered an environment conducive to long-term economic growth, as evidenced by the robust performance of capital markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This view is nuanced by the recognition that shared beliefs and cultural factors also shape institutional structures. This suggests that while secure property rights are essential, they are not sufficient on their own; the broader socio-cultural context in which these institutions operate significantly shapes economic outcomes.

This historical context is further illuminated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2), who demonstrate how inequality and the slave trade shaped institutional frameworks in ways that influenced long-term economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the New World's extreme wealth disparities were instrumental in shaping institutions that favored elites. This dynamic was driven by a factor endowment that included abundant land but scarce labor, leading to societies where a few powerful individuals controlled resources (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.2). The resulting institutional arrangements, such as those governing property rights and political power, favored elite dominance over broad-based economic growth. Nunn extends this analysis by demonstrating that Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, significantly impeded economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). These trades disrupted social and economic institutions, creating conditions where centralized governments and well-developed markets were rare. The persistent inequality and institutional weaknesses resulting from these historical processes highlight how initial conditions can lock societies into trajectories of underdevelopment.

Technological advancements and knowledge accumulation are pivotal in driving sustained economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr's work emphasizes that the expansion of useful knowledge is the primary engine behind long-run economic development. This perspective aligns with Kuznets' assertion that technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use within growing economies. These epochal innovations, as Kuznets terms them, are those that significantly contribute to sustained growth over prolonged periods and spread across substantial regions of the world. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. Kuznets cautions that these technological changes may cause societal conflicts, which must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to occur. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a shared sense of community, plays a crucial role in managing such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by effective institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic stability.

In this vein, Hopkins (2009, p.18) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argues that African economic history can inform institutional reforms by highlighting the interplay between internal dynamics and external influences. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolved under colonial rule. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, highlighting that real wage levels in British colonies offer insights into long-term economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Hopkins adds that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The role of colonial governance in shaping these institutions cannot be overstated: it set the stage for both the successes and failures of African economies in the post-colonial era. Hopkins also notes that while institutions matter, they are not the only factor influencing economic outcomes. This suggests that while institutional reforms are necessary, they must be accompanied by other factors to ensure sustainable economic growth.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact has shown that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence contemporary economic performance in Africa. The interplay between endogenous and exogenous factors in shaping these institutions is a critical area for further research.

This complex interaction is further nuanced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who argue that women played pivotal roles in African economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, noting that their contributions were often overlooked. This dynamic is evident when examining how colonial institutions shaped land access for women. While women were frequently relegated to marginal lands for food farming, colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through customary law, limiting women's access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Frankema shows that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15), altering the perceived benefits of education. This institutional framework limited economic opportunities for women and influenced broader economic outcomes by restricting the productive capacity of a significant portion of the population. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, indicating that educational institutions were responsive to local needs despite colonial constraints (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that while colonial institutions imposed certain limitations, they also created opportunities for economic growth through education.

Institutions are crucial for shaping long-run economic growth because they define property rights and enforce rules that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This is evident in the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England, which dramatically lowered transaction costs and supported economic growth by enhancing security over time (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different institutions based on mortality rates, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment while others were subjected to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of institutions in economic growth is complex when considering distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be better explained by these conflicts than by efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights that institutions are shaped by powerful groups seeking to maximize their own rents, which may not always align with long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence from former European colonies underscores the significance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in reversing relative incomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how these institutional differences, not geographic factors, were pivotal in determining the long-term development trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). North and Weingast further illustrate that secure property rights and credible commitment underpin economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England where political institutions ensured enforcement of rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is found in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. This reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
