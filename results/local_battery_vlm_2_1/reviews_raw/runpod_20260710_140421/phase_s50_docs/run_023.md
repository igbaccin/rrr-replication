The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development and history. At stake are the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty across nations. The debate centres on whether institutions are primary determinants of economic outcomes, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions hold greater explanatory power. Institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts foster growth; however, institutional frameworks are merely one among many factors influencing development trajectories. A central theme in this debate is the nature of institutions themselves: are they endogenous to economic outcomes, or do they exert an exogenous influence on growth? Another key question is whether institutions can be understood as a unified concept, or if different types of institutions (e.g. political versus economic) operate through distinct mechanisms.

Acemoglu et al. and Austin argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Colonial powers implemented varied institutional frameworks depending on factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns. In settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that long-term growth is deeply intertwined with institutional development over extended periods.

North posits that institutions matter in economic growth by emphasizing secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution illustrates how institutions can reduce transaction costs, fostering a long-term capital market critical for economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of political institutions in specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs over time.

Acemoglu et al. further develop the institutional perspective by arguing that colonial institutions shaped long-run development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They contend that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-term growth. This view aligns with North's emphasis on secure property rights but adds a historical dimension, showing how colonial policies influenced institutional frameworks and subsequent economic outcomes.

The role of trust in shaping economic development is another crucial aspect highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4). Their work shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts contemporary economic outcomes. This perspective complements institutional theories by emphasizing the importance of social norms and trust in fostering economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth by securing property rights. This financial revolution was underpinned by political institutions that ensured efficient property rights and their effective enforcement. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also caution that while these institutional changes were significant, they should not be overstated. This nuanced view suggests that while financial institutions and secure property rights are crucial for economic development, their impact is moderated by broader institutional and political frameworks.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was mediated through the types of institutions imposed, which were shaped by European policies and mortality rates among colonizers. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as parts of Africa and Latin America, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions did not encourage investment or long-run economic development; instead, they maximized short-term rents for European colonists. This pattern suggests that the nature of colonial institutions was a critical determinant of long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2001, p.19) further qualify this by noting that the impact of these institutions interacted with other factors such as industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in shaping the long-run development trajectories of former colonies, indicating that while colonial institutions were crucial, their effects were contingent on broader economic and historical contexts.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that institutional frameworks in New World societies were heavily influenced by initial levels of inequality, which in turn shaped economic outcomes. These institutions often favored elites through policies that restricted competition and maintained social hierarchies. For instance, the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to high living standards but also extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional bias towards elites created barriers to broader economic development by limiting opportunities for the wider population, effectively locking in inequality over time. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) further illustrate this dynamic by highlighting how institutional changes in Europe led to significant investment in agriculture, which drove high agricultural labor productivity. This pattern of elite-favoring institutions echoes through various historical contexts where initial inequality was a defining feature. The persistence of these institutions underscores their role in perpetuating economic divergence, as seen in the differing development trajectories of regions with varying levels of initial inequality.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This dynamic was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. The demand for missionary education became a function of cost-benefit calculations made at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework created incentives for Africans to pursue education, leading to broader access and higher levels of educational attainment than in regions under different colonial administrations.

The mechanism by which British institutions promoted education is rooted in the unique set of social and political changes they introduced. This pattern of institutional change highlights how specific colonial policies can shape long-term educational outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.2) reconstruction of real wage levels in British Africa reveals that while education was fostered, economic conditions were not uniformly improved across all colonies. This suggests that while British institutions promoted formal education, the economic benefits were not evenly distributed, indicating a complex interplay between institutional changes and broader economic outcomes.

Mokyr's proposition that technological progress is a primary driver of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This claim underscores the role of knowledge accumulation in fostering innovation and productivity gains. Institutions facilitate this process by reducing access costs to new ideas, thus promoting economic development. Kuznets elaborates on this dynamic, noting that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to efficiently integrate innovations into society (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54). These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts arising from the introduction of new technologies and ensuring sustained growth.

However, this interaction is complex: Mokyr highlights that while technological progress can be continuous, economic reasonableness, defined as institutions' ability to promote growth without succumbing to rent-seeking, is less predictable. Thus, institutional quality can fluctuate over time, potentially undermining the benefits of technological advancement (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

This interplay between technology and institutions suggests a nuanced relationship where both factors are essential for long-term economic growth. Institutions support technological progress, but their effectiveness can vary over time, influencing the overall impact on economic development. This dynamic underscores the importance of robust institutional frameworks in harnessing the potential of technological advancements for sustained economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that Africa's slave trade has left enduring legacies that hinder contemporary economic development. The slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust is a persistent factor shaping current social and economic interactions. Austin (2008, p.2) complements this perspective by highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic paths, even in regions where European settlers were absent (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This mistrust manifests in various dimensions of social and economic life, including reduced intergroup trust. This dynamic underscores the deep-seated nature of the institutional legacies of the slave trade, which continue to shape African economies today.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in historical contexts and institutional legacies. European colonization strategies significantly influenced the establishment of institutions in colonies. Settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand saw the development of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Regions with high mortality rates for Europeans, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, developed extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks has had lasting effects on economic outcomes, with settler colonies experiencing higher income per capita today compared to those without European settlers (Austin 2008, p.15). The institutional legacies of colonial rule thus continue to shape contemporary economic development.

The relationship between institutions and long-run growth is further underscored by the importance of property rights and transaction costs. North argues that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al.'s finding that extractive institutions, which do not encourage economic development, can persist because they maximize rents to politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are enforced and that rules governing economic exchange are stable (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The legacy of colonialism extends beyond economic institutions to social norms. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly impedes economic development by eroding social cohesion and hindering collective action (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal norm of mistrust is at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law, underscoring the deep-seated nature of these institutional legacies. The historical context of colonial rule thus shapes both economic institutions and the social fabric of societies, with enduring consequences for long-run growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in both the persistence of extractive institutions and the enduring impact of colonial legacies on social norms. The central point of agreement across these studies is that institutions matter for economic growth; secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and stable rules governing economic exchange are crucial for fostering growth (North 1989, p.9). There remains a disagreement over the relative importance of internal versus external factors in shaping trust and social cohesion. Austin argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting that local contexts played a significant role in shaping institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize the enduring impact of the slave trade on creating a culture of mistrust within Africa, highlighting the lasting effects of historical legacies on contemporary social norms. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of intergroup trust in regions with different colonial histories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).
