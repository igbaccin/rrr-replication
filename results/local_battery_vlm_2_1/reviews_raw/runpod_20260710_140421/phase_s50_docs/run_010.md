The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics that addresses how societal rules and structures influence prosperity over extended periods. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. Some scholars emphasize the critical role of institutions in establishing secure property rights and enforcing contracts, which foster investment and innovation (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others argue that institutional frameworks are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutional quality can significantly impact economic performance. This perspective underscores how credible commitment mechanisms within institutions are essential for sustained economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

A competing view emphasizes cultural beliefs shaping institutions. This perspective suggests that institutions are intertwined with societal values and norms.

The relationship between European colonialism and the economic trajectories of former colonies highlights how different institutional frameworks were established based on initial conditions. For instance, in areas where Europeans settled and created institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, long-term economic growth was more likely (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This contrasts with regions where extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, which had persistent negative effects on income per capita (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. Institutions are not static but evolve in response to political, social, and economic pressures; thus, understanding their dynamics requires a nuanced approach that considers both their structural features and the broader context in which they operate.

These institutions facilitated market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement. North (1989, p.9) describes how the Bank of England's establishment led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional change was crucial for economic growth as it allowed for the development of a long-term capital market. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War had driven the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for these developments (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate on how political institutions governing public choice were pivotal in this process. They highlight that the regime's commitment to enforcing rules and the development of markets underpinned economic growth. This commitment was evident in the success of capital markets, which indicates that institutional changes were indeed revolutionary (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). However, North (1989, p.9) also notes that as specialization and division of labor increased, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplied, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP despite falling costs per transaction. This dynamic underscores the evolving nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic growth over time (North 1989, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The nature of these institutions varied widely depending on the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in different colonial settings. These institutions maximized short-term gains for colonizers.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this dynamic by noting that these extractive institutions persisted because they served the interests of politically powerful groups, including European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as they hindered the development of property rights and rule of law, which are crucial for fostering investment and innovation. This institutional legacy helps explain the divergent economic trajectories observed among former colonies today.

For example, in settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions laid a foundation for robust economic growth, demonstrating that the type of colonial institution matters significantly in shaping long-run economic development.

The interaction between institutional frameworks and economic opportunities during the 19th century played a pivotal role in determining the long-term development paths of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) show that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. The mechanism through which this occurred is complex and multifaceted, involving both macro-level political decisions and micro-level family choices. Frankema (2012, p.15) explains that the demand for missionary education was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). These calculations were altered by new social and political institutions implemented during British rule, which changed the perceived benefits of formal education. This suggests that the institutional framework established by the British created conditions where education became more valuable to both political leaders and families, thereby fostering educational development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) provide a comparative lens through which to understand these dynamics (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They argue that in societies with greater inequality, institutions often advantaged elites by restricting competition and limiting opportunities for broader social mobility. This implies that the British colonial institutions in sub-Saharan Africa may have been relatively more egalitarian compared to other colonial powers, thereby promoting education at a wider societal level. However, it is important to note that while British rule may have fostered formal education, the long-term implications of these educational gains are still subject to debate. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how initial differences in factor endowments and institutional frameworks can persist over time, shaping economic development trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that while British rule may have laid a foundation for education, the long-term economic benefits of this educational investment depend on various other factors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) find that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of persistent poverty in Africa and suggests that the region's economic performance was more dynamic than often portrayed. The economies of many Anglophone African countries were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, with growth patterns influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors highlight the role of institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct real wage levels for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to understand these dynamics better (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2), finding that these wage levels were influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments and political institutions. This suggests that the long-term economic benefits depend on various other factors beyond just educational investment, as noted in prior sections.

This variation highlights the complexity of economic growth in Africa, where multiple factors interact to shape outcomes. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which historical conditions continue to influence contemporary economic performance.

This dynamic is evident in the persistence of serfdom, where landowners maintained control over labor despite its inefficiency, as well as in the formation of craft and merchant guilds, which restricted competition to benefit established members (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions can be seen in colonial contexts as well. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, opting for higher production equilibria contrary to expectations based on efficiency alone (Austin 2008, p.15). This pattern underscores how institutions are shaped by the power dynamics and interests of various groups. Understanding long-run economic growth requires examining underlying political and social conflicts that shape institutions.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap in Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.23), contributing to the Great Divergence and challenging institutional explanations for long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) reinforce this perspective by highlighting that merchant distribution, finance, and service sectors played crucial roles in economic growth during the early modern period. The wage gap between skilled and unskilled laborers suggests that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed. Economic growth was driven by institutional changes and broader structural shifts triggered by exogenous events like the Black Death. This perspective does not negate the role of institutions entirely; it underscores their interaction with other factors. Pamuk (2007, p.16) shows that urbanization rates in northwest Europe increased significantly after 1450, further illustrating how structural changes contributed to economic development. This suggests that institutional flexibility was a key factor enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. The implications of these findings are significant: they highlight the complex interplay between exogenous shocks, structural changes, and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are primary drivers of long-run economic growth; institutions play a secondary role in facilitating this process (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological innovations are pivotal to economic development, while Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes the importance of institutional adjustments. Institutions facilitate technological progress by creating an environment conducive to innovation and diffusion. They do this by reducing access costs to knowledge and fostering coordination among stakeholders (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Institutions also mitigate conflicts that arise from growth-induced changes, such as those between traditional rural life and modern industrialization (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for sustainable economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.49) cautions that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements may be unsustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of "economic reasonableness", a concept encompassing rational decision-making and efficient use of resources, is essential for leveraging technological progress effectively. This interplay highlights how institutions can either enhance or hinder economic growth depending on their ability to adapt to technological changes.

Nunn and Wantchekon's research demonstrates that Africa’s slave trade engendered a pervasive culture of mistrust (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This culture of mistrust persists and significantly impedes economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade reduced trust within African societies, with this reduction accounting for at least half of its negative impact on economic outcomes. The mechanism is twofold: one channel involves external factors such as rule of law and trustworthiness of others; the other involves internal norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates that this culture of mistrust, rooted in historical slave trades, has enduring effects on economic institutions. He argues that the lasting legacy of mistrust undermines the development of strong, credible institutions necessary for sustained economic growth. The internal norm of mistrust is particularly detrimental because it erodes social capital and hinders collective action (Nunn 2008, p.19). This erosion of trust and social capital has profound implications for contemporary African economies, as it limits the effectiveness of policies aimed at fostering development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and enforce property rights. North's seminal work highlights how institutions reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate that extractive institutions, which do not encourage economic development, can persist due to the influence of politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2).

The historical context of colonialism further illustrates the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with some promoting investment and rule of law while others were extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Austin challenges the simplistic view of colonial institutions by showing how indigenous economic actions influenced colonial policies, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions is further underscored by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of political institutions was crucial for securing property rights and fostering economic growth. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as evidenced by the success of capital markets during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie adds nuance to this discussion by emphasizing distributional conflicts as a key factor in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions reflected underlying social power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al.'s focus on extractive institutions and Austin's emphasis on indigenous actions lies in the colonial policies of regions like Nigeria. The case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn’s hypothesis, illustrates how local economic dynamics influenced institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This specific historical evidence shows that while extractive institutions were prevalent, they were not immutable and could be adapted in response to indigenous actions, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions.
