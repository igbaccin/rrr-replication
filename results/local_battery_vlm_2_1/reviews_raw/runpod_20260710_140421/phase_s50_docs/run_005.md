The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. Institutions are understood as rules and constraints that structure political, economic, and social interactions; they encompass both formal rules such as constitutions and informal norms like cultural practices. The debate centers on the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes over extended periods. One position contends that well-designed institutions foster growth by protecting property rights, ensuring legal enforcement, and promoting investment; these institutions are seen as pivotal for long-term economic success. A rival perspective posits that geographic factors or exogenous events primarily drive economic development, with institutional frameworks playing a secondary role. This debate is crucial because it shapes policy prescriptions: if institutions matter most, then reforming them should be a priority for fostering growth. If not, other levers such as infrastructure and natural resources might be emphasized instead. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth thus has profound implications for economic theory and policy.

At the heart of this debate lies the question of how institutional frameworks shape long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional setups across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment; in others, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This variation in colonial institutions had lasting effects on current income per capita, suggesting a direct link between institutional design and economic performance. Similarly, North posits that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution exemplifies how institutions can reduce transaction costs and foster economic development by enhancing the security of property rights over time. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic outcomes, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity.

The debate also extends to the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were prevalent in many colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights how institutional design can be shaped by political power dynamics and the interests of dominant groups, leading to outcomes that may not optimize economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex and nuanced than often portrayed, involving multiple interacting factors over time.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another critical aspect. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the persistence of certain institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds are examples of institutions shaped by conflicts over resource allocation; this perspective sheds light on how institutional arrangements can be influenced by underlying power dynamics and social conflicts. Similarly, Hopkins highlights the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa suggests that effective governance structures are crucial for fostering growth, as they create an environment conducive to long-term economic success.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between population density, prosperity, and institutional design. Acemoglu et al. note that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by European colonizers (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that institutional frameworks evolve in response to changing demographic and economic conditions. Understanding this dynamic interplay is essential for grasping how institutions shape long-term economic outcomes, as it highlights the adaptive nature of institutional design in response to external factors.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs. The creation of new financial instruments during this period, described as an English financial revolution, significantly enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework was critical for both capital market development and sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further elaborate on this by highlighting that political factors underpin economic growth. The regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement is a critical factor in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This commitment ensures that the rules are established and consistently enforced, providing a stable environment for market exchanges. This suggests that the specific nature of institutional changes can significantly influence economic outcomes.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies; Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6). The differential impact of these institutions can be traced to the varying colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development. For instance, colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, which received inclusive institutions, experienced sustained growth; colonies subjected to extractive institutions, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, faced persistent underdevelopment. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping economic trajectories, their impact was mediated by subsequent political and economic dynamics within the colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Nunn and Wantchekon (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), argue that institutions shaped by inequality in New World societies have favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional bias towards elites has been a critical factor in shaping economic trajectories in these regions. The mechanism through which this occurs involves the creation of policies that limit economic opportunities for the majority, thereby stifling overall economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, characterized by unequal distribution of power and resources, has had lasting effects on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon provide further insight into this dynamic by examining how mistrust, fostered by historical inequalities, continues to impact economic outcomes in Africa. They show that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that the legacy of inequality involves institutional structures and deeply ingrained social attitudes that hinder trust and cooperation, which are essential for economic development. Therefore, addressing historical inequalities requires institutional reforms and efforts to build social trust and cohesion.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly highlight the role of technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge in long-run economic development. This process requires institutional adjustments to efficiently use innovations, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that technological advancements necessitate changes in societal structures, such as urbanization and shifts from extended family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of economic improvements could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This means that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by institutions that can manage and distribute resources effectively.

This process is not straightforward. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that conflicts arising from growth-induced changes can hinder economic progress if not resolved peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that the institutional framework must be robust enough to navigate these conflicts without causing long-term damage to societal cohesion. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further qualifies this by noting that economic reasonableness, which underpins effective institutional adjustments, is not always monotonic or irreversible (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, while technological progress and knowledge accumulation are fundamental drivers of growth, their benefits depend on the ability of institutions to adapt and manage societal changes effectively.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) both emphasize that institutional change in African colonies was influenced by endogenous factors such as local demand for education and the cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic interplay between African initiatives and exogenous factors shaped the quality of public goods provided by African governments, a key determinant of long-run economic growth. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift had downstream implications for economic development, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), who argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This growth was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, suggesting that while institutional changes were pivotal, they also necessitated integration into global economic networks. This points to a nuanced relationship between institutional quality and economic outcomes, where both endogenous and exogenous factors play crucial roles in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional explanations for historical economic phenomena by arguing that these explanations oversimplify causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They assert that these approaches often neglect the complexity of distributional conflicts and other factors. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates this simplification through his analysis of colonial administrations, showing that these governments adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions rather than following a rigid path determined by exogenous factors (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior highlights how institutions can evolve in ways that are not always predictable from purely efficiency-driven models. These insights suggest that institutional explanations must account for a broader range of historical forces beyond mere efficiency. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in their ability to reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England led to a significant decline in transaction costs and was critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects increased security in property rights over time, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and overall economic development (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in European colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in determining economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in their influence on distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations. Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than mere efficiency considerations, emphasizing the need to account for broader historical forces beyond efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Hopkins argues that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments, suggesting that institutional effectiveness can vary independently of ethnic heterogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
