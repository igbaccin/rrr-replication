The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks can either propel or hinder economic progress over extended periods. Two key positions emerge from this debate: one emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, while the other highlights the influence of geographic factors and colonial policies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional frameworks, with some colonies adopting extractive states that hindered growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, implying that a more nuanced understanding is necessary (Austin 2008, p.2). The debate underscores the need to integrate various factors, geographic, institutional, and historical, to fully grasp the dynamics of long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both argue that institutions are pivotal in determining economic trajectories. The former paper posits that European settlers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, with extractive states emerging in high-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize the role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Institutions are dynamic entities shaped by political power struggles and economic incentives. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which does not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with North's (1989, p.9) assertion that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9).

He suggests that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation indicates that institutions evolve through interactions between colonizers and local populations. Austin's argument underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including geographic conditions and colonial policies, in understanding long-run economic growth. The interplay between these factors creates a complex web of influences that shape institutional development and, consequently, economic outcomes. This complexity is further highlighted by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.8). By emphasizing the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, Hopkins aligns with the broader perspective that effective institutions are crucial for development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and concurrent innovations in financial instruments marked a pivotal moment in English economic history. These developments were instrumental in reducing transaction costs, which fostered secure property rights. This was crucial for long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth, as they reflect an enhanced security over time dimension of property rights. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War drove political institutions to specify efficient property rights and enforce them effectively, leading to falling per-transaction costs despite a growing aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Furthermore, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) point out that the success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets. Recent research has challenged traditional interpretations by showing that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was almost on par with England's. This implies that institutions governing public choice, particularly those ensuring credible commitments to property rights, were pivotal in fostering long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution against overstating the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes. The bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not hinder French economic growth as much as traditionally thought; instead, it suggests that institutional frameworks themselves were not the sole determining factor. This nuance underscores that while secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth, other factors also play significant roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by power dynamics and social structures. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were purely driven by efficiency considerations. She highlights how shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights influenced institutional outcomes, particularly in Latin America. Akyeampong and Fofack build on this idea by demonstrating that African women's economic contributions were marginalized due to patriarchal structures and customary laws codified during colonial rule. This dynamic reveals how institutional frameworks can be skewed by power imbalances, with significant implications for long-run economic growth.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is evident in the marginalization of women's economic activities. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) detail how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances through customary law, which systematically excluded women from access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional framework constrained women's contributions and had broader economic repercussions. The codification of customary laws under colonial rule created a dual legal system that further entrenched gender disparities. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) adds depth to this argument by emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions in long-term research programs, suggesting that these power dynamics have lasting effects on economic outcomes.

These findings underscore the importance of considering institutional frameworks as products of complex social and political interactions. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions highlights that these frameworks were influenced by power dynamics and social structures, which had significant implications for long-run economic growth.

These institutions were designed to serve the interests of European colonists, often leading to long-term economic stagnation. The persistence of these institutions over centuries highlights their enduring impact on economic outcomes. This underscores the profound influence of colonial institutions on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. note that the specific policies pursued by European colonizers were also influenced by local population density and prosperity levels (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), suggesting a more nuanced interaction between institutional design and local conditions. These findings collectively illustrate how colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies through their extractive nature and enduring impact on local economies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share with Broadberry and Gardner (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) the finding that inequality significantly influenced economic performance in colonial settings. This influence was mediated through institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating unequal resource distribution. Sokoloff and Engerman emphasize that high initial inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutions that reinforced these disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, shaped by the extreme inequality from the outset, were characterized by policies that protected elite interests. This dynamic was particularly pronounced in Latin America, where colonial institutions often supported the concentration of land and resources among a privileged few, inhibiting broad-based economic development.

They argue that many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores that while inequality and elite-favoring institutions were detrimental in some contexts, other factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political conditions could also play crucial roles (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks interact with local conditions to shape long-term economic outcomes.

The literature on colonial education and African development highlights how colonial rule shaped institutional change in Africa through both negotiated and imposed governance aspects (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how colonial powers influenced the development of formal education systems, which in turn affected broader institutional frameworks. This finding is crucial because it shows how colonial policies could either facilitate or hinder the creation of effective educational systems, which are fundamental for long-term economic growth. For instance, the demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, illustrating how colonial policies could reshape local incentives and institutional development. This dynamic is significant because it demonstrates that the impact of colonial rule on African development varied based on specific policies and their interaction with local contexts. This shift highlights how the recognition of institutions' role in economic development enabled new economic history to gain traction among development specialists. The emphasis on 'good' institutions underscores their crucial role in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective suggests that understanding the historical context of institutional development in Africa can provide valuable insights into contemporary challenges and potential solutions for long-term economic growth.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) focus on specific institutional dynamics in Africa, namely budget policies and land systems, without directly addressing long-run economic growth (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). These authors show that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly portrayed. This was marked by high levels of subsidies and interest payments, which displaced capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.2). In Kenya, for instance, subsidies and interest peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s (Simson 2019, p.11). Meanwhile, Peters examines how negotiable land systems in Africa conceals processes of exclusion and class formation. This is contrary to views emphasizing flexibility and contingency. The colonial period saw a push for agricultural development, driven by prejudices against African landholding practices deemed 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment (Peters 2004, p.5). Such institutional dynamics had implications for long-run economic growth, as they shaped the allocation of resources and influenced patterns of investment and productivity. Simson qualifies this picture by noting that domestic military spending began to grow in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent, suggesting a more complex interplay between institutional factors and economic outcomes (Simson 2019, p.11). This interplay highlights how budget policies and land systems, while not directly targeting long-run growth, indirectly influence it through resource allocation and investment patterns.

The slave trade contributed to a lasting culture of mistrust in Africa that has had enduring economic implications (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon document how the slave trade significantly eroded intergroup trust. This mistrust is not merely a residual effect of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others; it persists as an internal norm that continues to shape African societies today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates that this mistrust has profound economic consequences, as it impedes cooperation and collective action. He illustrates how the absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets in early trade hubs hindered economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). Thus, the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond immediate historical impacts, embedding deep-seated distrust that continues to influence contemporary economic dynamics.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional narratives that view African poverty as an inevitable outcome of historical institutional failures. They argue that colonial labor policies played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. For instance, when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often aligned with the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic reveals how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, thereby influencing long-term welfare development. Frankema and van Waijenburg's work underscores that colonial policies varied significantly across different regions. This variation suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes was contingent on local conditions and adaptive governance practices. Austin (2008, p.2) points out that these adaptations were often limited by broader institutional frameworks. He argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique highlights how the focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth can obscure the complex interplay of labor dynamics, colonial policies, and local adaptations. The implication is that understanding African poverty requires a nuanced examination of how specific colonial policies interacted with local contexts to shape economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is highlighted by Acemoglu et al.'s examination of European colonization strategies, which established extractive states in high-mortality regions while fostering investment-friendly institutions where Europeans settled (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North's work builds on this foundation by emphasizing the critical role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England exemplifies how changes in political structures can lead to significant economic developments. These findings are complemented by Austin's critique, which underscores that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships and ignores the complex interplay between colonial policies and local adaptations (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced perspective is crucial for understanding how specific institutional frameworks interacted with historical contexts to shape economic outcomes.

The importance of institutions in long-run growth is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s argument that equilibrium institutions often prioritize the interests of politically powerful groups over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with Ogilvie's call for a deeper integration of institutional analysis into economic history, emphasizing that institutions are essential tools for understanding economic dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Hopkins extends this perspective by highlighting how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise role of institutions is thus multifaceted, encompassing both their direct impact on economic growth and their interaction with local contexts. The remaining disagreement centers on whether extractive institutions are inherently detrimental to long-run development or if they can sometimes be adapted for positive outcomes. This debate is settled by the historical evidence showing that extractive institutions often fail to maximize long-term growth because they prioritize short-term rents over sustainable economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The empirical comparison between regions with settler colonies and those without, as documented in Acemoglu et al. provides a clear measurement of this dynamic.
