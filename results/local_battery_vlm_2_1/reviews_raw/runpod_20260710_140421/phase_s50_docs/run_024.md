The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages scholars across disciplines. At stake is understanding whether and how the design and evolution of political and economic systems can drive sustained prosperity over generations. Two prominent positions clash on this topic. One side holds that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing their role in securing property rights, enforcing contracts, and fostering investment. The other side posits that institutional frameworks are shaped by historical factors, including population density, mortality rates, and colonial policies, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes. This disagreement underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions function within broader socio-economic contexts.

North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that institutions are critical to economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, which had lasting impacts on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings suggest that the type of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, shaped by colonial strategies significantly influenced economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this by documenting how institutional changes introduced during colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, highlighting the interaction between institutions and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is complemented by Austin (2008, p.2), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15), suggesting a more nuanced view of institutional evolution.

This nuanced view of institutional evolution is exemplified by the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, which North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction fostered secure property rights, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on how this financial revolution was a product of fiscal crises such as those during the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). The development of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement led to falling transaction costs per exchange, despite an increasing aggregate volume of exchanges in a growing economy (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show how the success of these developments can be observed in capital markets, indicating that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The historical context qualifies the role of transaction costs. This implies that while institutional changes reduce individual transaction costs, they also create a more complex economic environment.

Institutional adjustments are integral to harnessing technological innovations for economic growth (Kuznets 1973; Mokyr 2005). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress and economic growth require institutional and ideological adjustments. This is because the rural mode of life, large family patterns, and veneration of undisturbed nature are not compatible with modern technology.

The efficiency gains from technological innovations depend on the ability to manage conflicts arising from their integration into society (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.6) argues that these conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs or long-term political damage. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that institutional progress is crucial for sustaining economic improvement, as technological progress alone can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors.

Useful knowledge grows nonlinearly (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that economic reasonableness, the ability to make rational economic decisions, is essential for translating technological progress into sustainable economic improvement. This means that while institutional adjustments are necessary, they must also be accompanied by a growth in economic rationality to fully realize the benefits of technological innovations.

These structural changes, as Pamuk argues, led to a wage gap in Europe following demographic shocks such as the Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The resulting economic divergence between northwest Europe and other regions was driven by institutional flexibility. This process is further evidenced by Broadberry and Gupta's findings on the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity gains in northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.16). The Black Death triggered a series of structural changes that led to higher wages for skilled laborers compared to unskilled workers. This wage gap resulted from both technological progress and institutional adjustments that facilitated economic growth. The uneven distribution of these gains suggests that institutions played a crucial role in long-run economic growth, but were not the sole determinant. This underscores the importance of considering both institutional factors and broader economic conditions in understanding the Great Divergence.

The former facilitated economic development through investment and property rights protection, whereas the latter hindered growth by prioritizing short-term extraction over long-term institutional development. The mechanism by which institutions shaped long-run economic growth involved the interaction between colonial policies and local conditions. For instance, in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, extractive states were more likely to be established because colonizers sought to maximize short-term gains and did not invest in long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was also related to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while institutions played a crucial role, broader economic conditions and technological advancements were also pivotal in determining long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies African economic performance by compressing different historical periods into a single narrative. The shared posture of these studies is to highlight that African economies had significant levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the notion that colonial institutions were uniformly extractive and detrimental to long-term growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) emphasizes that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production as it aligned with their fiscal interests. This dynamic suggests that local economic actions could influence institutional changes in the colonies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further nuance this picture by showing that growth patterns were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of export production varied significantly due to factors like local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation indicates that while institutions played a role, broader economic conditions and market dynamics also shaped long-run economic outcomes in African economies.

This targeted approach is evident in Hopkins' highlighting that institutional reforms in Africa have been aimed at poverty reduction and not long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These interventions often focus on improving the quality of public goods and developing progressive institutions. For instance, Hopkins notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily hinder the provision of high-quality public goods or the development of effective institutions. Instead, societies can be ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and fostering institutional progress (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is echoed by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who emphasize that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Their reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies indicates that economic development patterns were dynamic and varied across different regions. These findings suggest that while institutional reforms are crucial for addressing immediate poverty concerns, they may not fully capture the complexities involved in achieving sustained long-run economic growth.

This includes examining the distribution of income and identifying specific conditions that have influenced African welfare development. Hopkins also underscores this point by noting how the prominence attached to institutions in the 1990s enabled new perspectives on Africa's economic history to gain traction among development specialists (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The emphasis on "good" institutions highlights their role in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for long-term growth. This suggests that while institutional reforms can mitigate poverty, they must also be tailored to promote sustained economic progress by fostering an environment conducive to investment and development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) highlight how historical factors shaped economic development in different regions by influencing institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the impact of slave trade on African institutions was profound. The institution of slavery, facilitated through extensive transatlantic trade networks, led to the concentration of wealth and power among elites, who could then manipulate governmental policies to maintain their privileged status. This dynamic is evident in societies where the initial endowment of land and natural resources relative to labor was high, leading to extreme inequality from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The same conditions shaped the evolution of institutions that were not conducive to broad-based economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by examining the adverse effects of slave trades on African economic development, arguing that sustained trade required robust political structures and well-established markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as they have shaped long-term trajectories of growth and development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they create the framework within which economies operate. Institutions determine the rules governing economic activities and enforce property rights, both critical for fostering investment and economic development (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence from Acemoglu et al. shows that European settlers established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, leading to divergent economic paths. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settler colonies adopted institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional frameworks were instrumental in promoting long-term growth by providing a stable environment for economic activities. This finding is supported by North's argument that secure property rights are essential for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes is profound. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive states set up by Europeans in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast hindered long-term development by prioritizing resource extraction over investment in local economies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy continues to influence current economic trajectories as these regions still grapple with the consequences of historical policies that maximized rents for European colonists and did not promote sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The persistence of these institutional legacies is evident in the economic stagnation observed in many former colonies. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting that colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior shows that institutional changes can occur in response to local conditions, but the initial framework set by colonial powers significantly shapes long-term outcomes. The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the development of former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6).

The quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by both ethnic fragmentation and the effectiveness of their institutions in upholding the rule of law and promoting progressive policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18; North 1989, p.6).
