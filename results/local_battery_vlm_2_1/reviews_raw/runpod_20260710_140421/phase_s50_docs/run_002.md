The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure interactions, can either facilitate or hinder economic development. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustained economic progress. Two main positions dominate the debate. One emphasizes institutions as the primary drivers of growth, arguing that certain institutional frameworks promote investment and innovation. Another position focuses on the distributional conflicts within societies, suggesting that these conflicts shape institutions in ways that may not always align with economic efficiency. These perspectives intersect and diverge around the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth trajectories.

Austin shares a finding with Acemoglu et al. that institutions are critical to understanding long-term economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin contends that the reversal of fortune thesis might oversimplify causation by condensing different historical periods and paths. This critique suggests that while institutions matter, their impact on growth is complex and contingent on historical context. Acemoglu et al. propose that European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15). The nature of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional choices made during colonial periods. This highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-term economic outcomes through their influence on property rights and investment incentives.

Institutions can shape growth by influencing transaction costs and economic efficiency. North argues that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.15). This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges and encourages investment, which are essential for economic growth. Ogilvie posits that distributional conflicts drive institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective implies that institutions may not always be designed to maximize long-run growth but rather to address the immediate concerns of powerful groups within society. The interaction between these two views, one emphasizing transaction costs and economic efficiency, the other focusing on distributional conflicts, illuminates how institutional frameworks can both enable and constrain economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between colonial legacies and contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15). These institutional changes, whether inclusive or extractive, have had lasting effects on economic development trajectories. Hopkins highlights how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in long-term economic growth. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on development trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. In a broader sense, this institutional change was a product of fiscal crises that led to a trade-off between certain rights and revenues (North 1989, p.9). This shift in political institutions facilitated the development of economic institutions that enhanced market exchanges and lowered transaction costs per unit of GNP. As specialization and division of labor increased, the aggregate volume of exchanges grew, highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic activity (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) advise that while these changes were significant, they should be viewed within a broader historical context. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was part of a broader pattern of institutional development that influenced economic outcomes over the long run.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) together argue that technological advancements are the primary engine of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr proposes that the accumulation of useful knowledge drives long-term economic development, highlighting how scientific and technical progress leads to innovation. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' observation that epochal innovations, such as those in agriculture or industry, have historically been pivotal in sustaining growth over extended periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). These technological breakthroughs transform the economic landscape and necessitate institutional adjustments to integrate new technologies efficiently. Kuznets' work indicates that these adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts arising from technological changes and ensuring sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr notes that while technological progress is more straightforward and irreversible, institutional advancements are less so. The sustainability of economic improvements hinges on complementary institutional progress that mitigates rent-seeking behaviors and ensures long-term stability. This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while technological advancements may be the spark, institutional frameworks play a critical role in sustaining economic growth over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied significantly across colonies and had profound effects on institutional development in those regions. In particular, the mortality rates experienced by Europeans upon arrival shaped their approach to governance. This divergence in institutional frameworks resulted from the settlers' differing motivations and expectations about their long-term presence in these colonies.

The extractive nature of institutions in high-mortality regions was characterized by a focus on maximizing rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run economic growth.

The implications of these institutional differences are far-reaching. The extractive institutions established in high-mortality regions not only hindered immediate economic growth but also created lasting barriers to development. These barriers persisted long after colonial rule ended, as subsequent governments often inherited and were constrained by these same institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). By contrast, inclusive institutions fostered more robust and sustainable economic growth, setting the stage for ongoing prosperity in regions where European settlers established such frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can offer insights into institutional reforms. He argues that African societies are capable of developing progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity, and the quality of public goods provision is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) view that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Akyeampong and Fofack introduce a nuanced pattern: colonial rule introduced customary laws that reinforced patriarchal alliances, marginalizing women's access to land for cash crops. This codification of subordinate status for women highlights how institutional changes during colonialism could have long-term economic implications by altering the distribution of resources and opportunities (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The downstream implication is that gender dynamics shaped by institutions can influence economic growth, although this specific mechanism remains underexplored in the literature.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and impacts trust levels and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The transatlantic slave trade created an environment where individuals could not rely on social norms to ensure cooperation. This institutionalized mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon argue, has had lasting effects on Africa's economic development by reducing trust levels within and between groups. This persistent mistrust is likely a significant factor in the slow pace of institutional reforms that support long-run economic growth; it reduces incentives for individuals to engage in collective action or to invest in public goods (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, and this is likely because they fostered a culture of mistrust. The legacy of the slave trade thus creates a negative feedback loop: low trust reduces investment in institutions, which in turn maintains or even exacerbates low trust levels, further hindering economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional explanations for long-run economic growth as oversimplifying causation by overlooking distributional conflicts. They note that the focus on secure private property rights fails to account for why similar institutions yield different outcomes across countries. Austin observes that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, challenging the notion of a uniform institutional path. This adaptation suggests that local contexts significantly influence the effectiveness of institutions. For instance, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency alone. She contends that beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the development of property rights and contract enforcement. Austin's work on colonial policies shows that even when European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production could be encouraged by colonial administrations seeking fiscal security. This implies that the success of institutional reforms is contingent on local incentives and responses, rather than a purely top-down imposition of efficient institutions. Furthermore, Ogilvie emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programmes, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with local contexts is essential for explaining economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to influence the efficiency and stability of property rights. North argues that the development of institutions such as the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a secure foundation for property rights, fostering economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and lowering per-transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight how European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes in colonies, with some establishing extractive states that hindered long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings illustrate how the type of institutions established, whether inclusive or extractive, directly impacts economic trajectories.

Institutional frameworks are shaped by local contexts and responses as well as being top-down impositions. Ogilvie emphasizes the importance of incorporating institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of institutional interactions with local contexts is crucial for explaining economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by Austin's work on colonial policies, which shows that even in the absence of European settlers, indigenous commodity production could be encouraged by colonial administrations seeking fiscal security. This implies that successful institutional reforms are contingent on local incentives and responses (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence from different colonial experiences provides a clear measure for evaluating how institutions shape economic growth. The reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies is directly tied to the institutional changes introduced during colonization, rather than purely geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical comparison shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonizers have lasting negative effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The specific historical evidence of colonial policies and their institutional outcomes settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The measurement of relative incomes among former colonies and the documentation of institutional changes during colonization provide a clear basis for understanding how institutions influence economic trajectories. This evidence underscores that institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
