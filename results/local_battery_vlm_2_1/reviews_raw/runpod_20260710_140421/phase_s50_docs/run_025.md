The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses how societal rules and structures influence prosperity over extended periods. This debate centers on the extent to which institutions, such as property rights and legal frameworks, drive economic outcomes versus other factors like geography or culture. One key position argues that institutions are paramount, shaping economic trajectories by determining the incentives for investment and innovation. Another view posits that institutions are necessary but insufficient without complementary factors, such as human capital or technological advancements. A third perspective suggests that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper historical and cultural forces, making them less autonomous in their impact on growth.

Acemoglu et al. in two separate papers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), present a compelling case for the primacy of institutions. They argue that colonial policies established extractive institutions in many colonies, which persistently hindered economic development. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, demonstrating how institutional frameworks can lock nations into underdevelopment paths. This perspective aligns with North and Weingast's argument that credible commitment underpins growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They highlight the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England as crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth.

In a similar vein, Austin (2008, p.15), while critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, acknowledges the significance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. He notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that institutions can adapt and influence economic outcomes. This dynamic view of institutions complements North's broader argument (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6), which posits that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is critical for reducing transaction costs and promoting growth.

Institutions evolve in response to political and economic pressures, shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. assert that European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Meanwhile, in areas where Europeans could settle safely, like the United States and Australia, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.

The differential impact of these colonial institutions is evident in the long-term development trajectories of former colonies. This persistence highlights how historical institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al.'s findings suggest that the influence of colonial institutions varies across former colonies. The interaction between these institutions and local conditions, such as population density and prosperity, played a crucial role in determining long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering both institutional legacies and local contextual factors when examining economic growth trajectories.

The persistence of colonial institutions and their enduring impact on economic outcomes suggest that historical policies can have lasting effects on a country's development path.

They argue that the creation of new financial institutions, such as the Bank of England (1694), and the development of innovative financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs. This reduction reflected an increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War sparked a trade-off where certain rights were relinquished to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). North further elaborates on this by noting that as political institutions evolve to specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement, transaction costs per unit fall while the aggregate volume of exchanges increases. This dynamic is driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies market transactions (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast advise against overstating the revolutionary nature of these changes. They note that while England's institutions fostered growth, France's bureaucratic structure did not hinder its economic performance as much as traditionally believed (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that while secure property rights are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and dependent on broader institutional frameworks.

Colonial rule in Africa significantly influenced institutional change through negotiation and imposition dynamics (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that colonial powers often imposed institutions tailored to extract resources and foster economic growth. This imposition led to the development of dysfunctional institutions, which persisted even after independence. Despite this difference, the broader pattern of institutional imposition remained consistent across different colonial regimes. This dynamic is crucial because it highlights how external forces can shape internal institutions in ways that may not always align with long-run economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further elaborate on this by demonstrating that the legacy of the slave trade has fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust can hinder economic development by undermining social cohesion and reducing cooperation. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also notes that some African societies have managed to develop progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity, suggesting that while colonial imposition is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of institutional quality (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The enduring impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic trajectory underscores the complex interplay between external imposition and internal adaptation in shaping long-run economic growth.

African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging conventional narratives about persistent poverty. This finding is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies from 1880 to 1965, which reveals periods of economic growth tied to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of this growth varied significantly across different regions, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings suggest that African economies experienced dynamic changes driven by both internal and external forces. The specific role of institutions in shaping these economic trajectories remains underexplored. The variation in growth patterns across different regions highlights the need for more nuanced analyses that consider the complex interplay between institutional factors and economic performance. This suggests that while institutions may have played a role in African economic development, their impact was not uniform or straightforward.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This dynamic was particularly pronounced in Latin America where extreme wealth disparities were entrenched from the outset of European colonization, leading to a persistent power imbalance that favored the elite class (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggest that this initial inequality shaped institutional development by restricting competition, which in turn influenced economic policies. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this perspective by noting that colonial governments often adapted their institutional frameworks to align with indigenous economic interests when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests a nuanced interplay between local conditions and colonial governance, where institutions evolved in response to both external pressures and internal dynamics.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements are fundamental to sustained economic development. This interplay between technological and institutional factors is crucial for long-term sustainability. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by noting that economic growth necessitates adjustments in both institutions and ideologies to accommodate technological changes effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) also recognizes the possibility of coordination failures, suggesting that policies to rectify these issues are essential for maintaining economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in this context is catalytic. They facilitate the diffusion and application of new technologies by reducing access costs and promoting knowledge sharing. Mokyr (2005, p.49) illustrates this through the development of scholarly societies, which played a significant role in advancing useful knowledge during the 17th and 18th centuries (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This institutional framework ensured that technological progress was sustained over long periods. The impact of these institutions is evident in the historical record, where regions with strong institutional support for knowledge dissemination experienced more robust economic growth. This highlights the importance of stable and effective institutions in leveraging technological advancements for long-term economic development.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap in Europe, offering an alternative explanation for the Great Divergence and long-run economic growth distinct from institutional factors (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Pamuk highlights how northwest Europe's response to the Black Death was characterized by institutional flexibility that fostered new towns and rural manufacturing. This flexibility enabled regions like Holland to rapidly adapt to emerging economic opportunities, contrasting with stagnating economies in areas like Tuscany where existing monopolies were strengthened (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta further elaborate on this divergence, noting that high productivity in northwest Europe's traded goods sector arose from production, merchant distribution, and finance. The implications of these structural changes are profound; they led to unevenly distributed benefits where skilled laborers saw larger surpluses over basic food needs compared to their unskilled counterparts. The structural changes initiated by the Black Death thus offer a compelling alternative narrative for understanding long-run economic growth in Europe, one that emphasizes the role of demographic shocks and institutional responses over purely institutional explanations.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that influence resource allocation and investment decisions. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework fosters market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2), which are essential for sustaining long-term growth. The specific impact of institutions on economic growth can vary significantly depending on historical context and political dynamics. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes in colonies, with some adopting extractive states that hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment underpinning economic growth by ensuring secure property rights through political institutions (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The historical evidence from colonial Africa further illustrates this point. Institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth often resulted in extractive systems that impeded economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This shows that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, directly influences economic outcomes over the long term. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. underscores the enduring impact of colonial-era institutional changes on contemporary economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation and compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative. Therefore, the specific historical context and path dependency are crucial for understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this debate is the measurement of institutional quality over time in former colonies. This measurement shows that inclusive institutions, which enforce property rights and encourage investment, correlate with higher levels of economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
