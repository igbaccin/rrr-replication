The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake is our understanding of the drivers of prosperity and poverty across nations over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinants of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or colonial policies play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and secure contracts, are crucial for economic development. Others contend that institutions are shaped by deeper historical, geographical, or cultural forces, making them secondary to these underlying determinants.

A key point of contention is the extent to which institutional changes can be traced back to specific historical events or policies, such as colonialism, and whether these changes have lasting effects on economic growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, while Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. These differing perspectives highlight the complexity of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between political and economic factors. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) both emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as it ensures that economic actors can rely on stable rules and enforcement mechanisms. This perspective underscores the importance of political stability and institutional credibility in promoting long-term economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that institutions should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, emphasizing their role as key drivers of economic growth. Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts provides a nuanced view of how institutions can be shaped by power dynamics and social structures. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth.

The interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes is further illustrated by North's (1989, p.9) discussion of the English financial revolution. He argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This highlights how specific institutional changes can have profound effects on economic growth by reducing barriers to trade and investment.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is not straightforward, as Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography has struggled with testing its approach quantitatively. The challenge of measuring the impact of institutions on economic outcomes underscores the need for more nuanced approaches to understanding their role in shaping long-term development.

The complexities of institutional change and its effects on economic growth are further illustrated by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) emphasis on population density and prosperity as factors influencing European colonial policies. This perspective highlights how demographic and economic conditions can shape the institutions that govern a society, ultimately affecting long-term development trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on their significance and mechanisms.

The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering secure property rights and thereby promoting long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The English financial revolution marked a pivotal moment where institutional changes directly influenced economic outcomes. This institutional development was crucial for creating a stable environment conducive to long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political commitment to enforce these rules played a critical role, as it ensured that the regime was bound by the established frameworks, thereby reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment. However, North (1989, p.9) notes that while falling transaction costs per exchange are beneficial, the overall volume of transactions in the economy will increase due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This implies that as economies grow more complex, the importance of efficient institutions becomes even more pronounced, as they facilitate the increasing number of market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) work shows that colonial institutions established by Europeans significantly shaped the long-run economic growth of former colonies. The distinct colonization strategies adopted in different regions led to varied institutional frameworks; these ranged from settler colonies with strong property rights and rule-of-law enforcement, such as the United States and Australia, to extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer, like those in the Congo or Gold Coast. These institutions, shaped by European policies, persisted over time and had profound effects on economic development. This institutional legacy continues to influence current income per capita levels across former colonies.

The impact of these colonial institutions varies. Acemoglu et al.'s findings indicate that while extractive institutions hindered economic development in many colonies, the specific mechanisms through which this occurred varied. For example, in some cases, politically powerful groups resisted institutional changes that could threaten their rents or political power (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), while in others, the reluctance to initiate change stemmed from a lack of direct benefit from potential economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This variation highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and local political dynamics in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Austin 2008, p.2) re-evaluate historical economic institutions by challenging simplistic views of causation in economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; this is an important critique because it suggests a more nuanced view of how institutions evolve over time. Colonial administrations, for instance, often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay indicates that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions, which shaped long-run economic outcomes. By focusing on distributional conflicts rather than efficiency, Ogilvie provides a different lens through which to view the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This approach underscores that institutional outcomes are deeply influenced by the broader social and political context, not just economic factors. This nuanced view qualifies the idea that institutions are simply efficiency-enhancing mechanisms; instead, they are shaped by complex interactions between various stakeholders.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), along with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), highlight the significant economic performance of Sub-Saharan Africa and Europe before 1800. While these studies do not directly link outcomes to institutional factors, they provide valuable insights into historical patterns of growth and income levels. These patterns underscore that economic divergence was already underway long before industrialisation, with export production being a key driver tied tightly to foreign trade (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This unevenness is also reflected in the varying wages of skilled versus unskilled labourers, indicating a complex interplay between economic sectors and social stratification. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) caution against overemphasising geographical factors alone, noting that high productivity in agriculture could also be driven by investment and institutional change (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuanced view suggests that while institutions may not be the sole determinant of economic outcomes, they play a crucial role in shaping growth patterns.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) assert that societies in the New World with greater inequality developed institutions that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional bias towards elites was a deliberate outcome of colonial policies. The initial abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor set the stage for extreme wealth disparities, which in turn shaped the evolving institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.2). These institutions, designed to maintain elite dominance, included restrictive property rights and limited access to education and political power for the majority. This institutional framework perpetuated inequality by stifling social mobility and economic opportunities for the lower classes, thereby reinforcing the status quo. The persistence of these unequal institutions had profound implications for long-term economic development, as they hindered innovation and productivity growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) qualifies this view by highlighting that while institutional factors are crucial, they interact with other historical legacies such as slave trades, which also significantly impacted Africa’s economic trajectory (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of slavery in Africa is a poignant example of how deeply entrenched institutions can shape long-term economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) posit that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Similarly, Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores that technological progress and institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations in the economy. While Mokyr focuses on the coordination failures and policies to rectify them (Mokyr 2005, p.54), Kuznets highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns, necessitating significant social and ideological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). Kuznets (1973, p.1) also notes the crucial role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts. This implies that while technological progress is essential, it must be complemented by institutional advancements to ensure sustainable economic improvement. The interplay between technological innovations and societal adjustments shapes long-term economic trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and enforcement of property rights and the creation of market-friendly environments. North shows that institutions matter significantly because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is evident in the historical context of England's financial revolution, where the establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and can be extractive rather than growth-enhancing. They show that European colonists often introduced institutions designed to maximize rents for themselves, not to promote long-run growth in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is supported by North & Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of political institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They demonstrate that credible commitment underpins growth: the regime or sovereign must be committed to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
