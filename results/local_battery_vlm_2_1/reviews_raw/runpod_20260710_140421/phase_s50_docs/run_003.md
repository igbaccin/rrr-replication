The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake is understanding how and why some societies achieve sustained prosperity while others stagnate. A central debate centres around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for fostering investment and innovation, which in turn drive economic growth. Others contend that institutional frameworks are merely one piece of the puzzle, with geographical endowments and cultural factors also playing critical roles. This debate is significant because it informs policy decisions aimed at promoting economic development in underdeveloped regions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in how different societies have developed over time. For instance, Acemoglu et al. highlight that European colonization strategies varied significantly across different regions, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions enforcing the rule of law, such as the United States and Australia, economic growth was robust. In areas like the Congo or Gold Coast, where extractive states were established primarily to transfer resources back to Europe, long-term economic development was hindered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This variation underscores how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or impede economic progress over extended periods.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is also evident in the historical evolution of financial systems. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was pivotal for fostering a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Similarly, North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for securing property rights and promoting economic development. Their work highlights how political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing exchange and the mechanisms enforcing these rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings suggest that institutional frameworks play a critical role in facilitating long-term economic growth by providing the necessary conditions for investment and innovation.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutional differences generate distinct economic outcomes. They show how institutions matter in long-term growth by influencing the security of property rights and the efficiency of market exchanges. This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by showing how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes in European colonies. They note that where Europeans settled and established institutions enforcing the rule of law, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, these institutions encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states were set up in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence highlights how political decisions shape economic trajectories by determining the nature of property rights and market exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this mechanism by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic development, their impact depends on the interests of those in power and the specific historical context.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a turning point, establishing credible commitment to property rights by limiting the monarch's power and creating a stable political environment. This institutional change facilitated long-term economic growth by ensuring that private investments were secure from arbitrary expropriation. The specific historical context matters; for instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production that aligned with local interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation highlights the conditional nature of institutional impact, where the effectiveness of property rights depends on the interplay between local economic activities and colonial policies. The evolution of institutions in England demonstrates how political stability and secure property rights can drive economic growth, but the broader applicability of this model hinges on the adaptability of institutions to diverse historical contexts.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight how colonial policies in Africa shaped gender dynamics and educational opportunities, which in turn influenced economic activities. The marginalization of women in land access for cash crops was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men's roles. This process systematically disadvantaged women, relegating them to less productive lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British colonial rule fostered a demand for missionary education among African communities; this was influenced by the altered social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule. The perceived benefits of formal education shifted, aligning with new economic opportunities and social structures. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) note that the codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal alliances, which further limited women's access to land and educational opportunities. This dual legal system, comprising European courts based on common and civil law alongside native courts, entrenched gender inequalities by legitimizing male dominance in both economic and social spheres. The implications for long-run growth are significant: the marginalization of women from productive activities and education under colonial rule had lasting effects on Africa's economic development.

Institutional reforms in Africa are seen as a means to eliminate poverty (Simson 2019, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18), aiming to enhance economic growth by improving governance and providing incentives for investment. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa has shifted attention towards institutional reforms, emphasizing their role in providing public goods and fostering development. This perspective is aligned with Simson's (2019) argument about the potential for post-independence budget policies to drive growth through effective resource allocation and service delivery. Both scholars suggest that by reforming institutions, African governments can create a more conducive environment for investment and economic activity. These reforms do not operate in isolation; they are influenced by historical legacies and regional dynamics. This complexity is evident in Simson, who shows that post-independence budget policies were shaped by factors such as colonial subsidies, debt burdens, and regional instability. These historical contexts can either facilitate or hinder the effectiveness of institutional reforms, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of Africa's economic development trajectory.

The legacy of the slave trades has had profound effects on contemporary African economies. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade instilled a pervasive culture of mistrust (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is not merely a function of environmental factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, but rather deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape social dynamics in profound ways, influencing contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn argues that the long duration of these slave trades, they spanned nearly 500 years, meant they had a more significant impact on African societies than colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context is crucial for understanding Africa's development trajectory, as it highlights how deep-rooted issues like mistrust can persist over centuries and hinder economic growth. The importance of trust in various aspects of economic development has been well-documented (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), underscoring the far-reaching implications of the slave trade's legacy on contemporary African economies.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share this view. They argue that the roots of institutional development lie in the struggles between different social groups over resource distribution. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows how pre-industrial Europe's core institutions, serfdom and guilds, were shaped by these conflicts. In societies where elites held disproportionate power, institutions evolved to maintain their advantage (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in the New World colonies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate how this inequality translated into institutional frameworks that perpetuated elite dominance through policies restricting competition. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by noting that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific forms institutions take also depend on broader cultural beliefs about legitimacy and rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This interplay between power dynamics and cultural norms suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of how institutions emerge from these complex social interactions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework, which ensures secure private property rights, is crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment significantly impacts current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The specific forms these institutions take are influenced by broader cultural beliefs about legitimacy and rights. Ogilvie notes that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interplay between power dynamics and cultural norms is evident in the historical context of colonization, where European settlers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates and established either extractive states or institutions enforcing the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The long-run effects of these institutional frameworks are evident in the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical evidence highlights how initial institutional choices can have lasting impacts on economic development.

The precise remaining disagreement centers on the role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions. While Ogilvie emphasizes that shared beliefs about legitimacy and rights are crucial, Acemoglu et al. focus more on the extractive nature of institutions designed by European colonists (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial policies and their long-term economic impacts settles this disagreement: the interaction between initial institutional choices and subsequent economic development shows that both cultural beliefs and extractive institutions play significant roles.
