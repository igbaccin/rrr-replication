The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that intersects economics and political science, addressing how societies organize themselves to foster prosperity. This question matters because it challenges the primacy of purely economic factors, such as capital accumulation or technological innovation, in driving growth. At its core lies a live disagreement over whether institutions matter primarily by incentivising investment through secure property rights (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), or if they instead reflect underlying distributional conflicts that shape economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This debate is crucial because it determines how policymakers should approach development, either by focusing on institutional reforms to reduce transaction costs or by addressing deeper societal power structures. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. posit that Europeans adopted distinct colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence was not merely a function of geography but rather a direct result of European policies aimed at maximizing resource extraction. These institutional differences had profound implications for economic development: Extractive institutions stifled growth by concentrating resources in the hands of a few, while inclusive ones fostered investment and innovation. However, Acemoglu et al. caution that the relationship between institutions and growth is complex, noting that even extractive institutions can be stable if they benefit powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This points to the enduring influence of colonial legacies on contemporary economic trajectories, where historical institutional frameworks continue to shape development paths.

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They highlight the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments as critical developments leading to secure property rights, which underpinned long-term capital markets. These institutions reduced transaction costs by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade-off where certain rights were relinquished to Parliament in exchange for revenues. North and Weingast's analysis reveals that the success of these institutions was evident in capital markets, showing a remarkable commitment to rules governing public choice. Thus, while secure property rights are crucial for economic development, other economic and political factors also play significant roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This advantage was achieved by restricting competition. The slave trade also played a critical role in shaping these unequal institutions, which in turn influenced long-run economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.19) demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for productive collaboration. Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this by noting that sustained trade did not occur until the Kongo kingdom had a centralized government and well-developed markets. This suggests that while inequality and mistrust are detrimental to development, the presence of strong institutions can mitigate some of these negative effects. The persistence of inequality and mistrust in societies with weak institutions remains a significant barrier to long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth. This view is supported by Mokyr's analysis of periodicals and scholarly societies, which highlights how formal institutions reduce access costs and foster economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Formal institutions play a crucial role in coordinating technological progress and mitigating coordination failures that could otherwise hinder sustained growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores this point by noting that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, is essential for resolving conflicts arising from economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr qualifies this by suggesting that while technological progress can be irreversible, institutional progress may not always follow a monotonic path. This highlights that although technology drives growth, the institutional environment must support and sustain it to achieve long-term economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Austin 2008, p.2) examine specific institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe to offer nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena. The shared focus of their work is the complexity of colonial institutions and their impact on local economies. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions, which influenced production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction suggests a degree of flexibility in institutional arrangements, even within rigid colonial structures.

This persistence can be attributed to entrenched interests and shared beliefs within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These entrenched interests could hinder the adoption of more efficient institutions, even when such changes might drive economic growth. The implication is that while institutions can adapt to some extent, deeply rooted social structures and beliefs can act as significant barriers to institutional change, thereby influencing long-run economic outcomes. This interplay between adaptive capacity and entrenched interests underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories over time.

The adaptability of colonial institutions to local conditions highlights their potential to drive growth under certain circumstances.

However, the legacy of colonial rule on African economic development was complex and varied based on local conditions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) jointly show that colonial policies played a pivotal role in shaping institutional frameworks across Africa. For instance, the demand for missionary education was shaped by new social and political institutions implemented during British rule, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional change had profound implications for human capital development, which is crucial for economic growth. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this effect, noting that the timing and scale of export production, an indicator of economic activity, varied widely due to factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while colonial policies were instrumental in shaping institutional frameworks, their impact on long-run economic growth was contingent on a multitude of contextual factors.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating frameworks that either facilitate or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North's work underscores this point, arguing that political and economic institutions are central to economic performance because they determine transaction costs, which in turn affect investment decisions and growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

The impact of colonial policies on institutional frameworks is evident in the divergent paths taken by different colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that where European settlers established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, economic growth was robust (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole stunted growth. This divergence is further highlighted by Austin's analysis, which shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local conditions, indicating that the impact of colonial policies was not uniform but contingent on various factors (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond colonial contexts. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence shows that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms was crucial for economic development. North illustrates this through the example of the Bank of England, which facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs and supported economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Hopkins further emphasizes the importance of institutions by noting that good governance and effective institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is vital for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions matter profoundly for long-run economic growth. North shows that secure property rights and low transaction costs underpin this process (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions shape economic outcomes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is found in the divergent experiences of colonies with different institutional frameworks. The empirical comparison of extractive versus inclusive institutions, as documented by Acemoglu et al. shows that the type of institutions established during colonization had lasting effects on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in promoting long-run economic development.
