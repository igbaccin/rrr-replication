The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake are fundamental explanations for why some countries thrive economically while others stagnate or decline. This debate pivots on whether institutions are the primary drivers of sustained economic development, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technology play more decisive roles. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth and whether these mechanisms are universal or context-dependent.

On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and ensure secure contracts are essential for fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). On the other hand, there is a camp that posits distributional conflicts as key determinants of institutional structures. The latter view suggests that institutions may be shaped by power dynamics and political struggles as well as efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The debate extends to whether colonial legacies have entrenched extractive institutions in some regions, thereby hindering economic development. This perspective underscores the lasting impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). One concrete mechanism underlying this process is the reduction in transaction costs facilitated by these institutions. This decline reflected increased security in property rights over time, a dimension pivotal for both capital markets and economic development. North's argument aligns with North and Weingast (1989, p.29), who emphasize that economic growth is underpinned by political factors ensuring the regime or sovereign is committed to enforcing rules governing property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also note that while France's economic performance before the French Revolution was upgraded in recent research, it did not significantly differ from England’s, suggesting that institutional changes alone may not fully explain economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies. The persistence of these institutions suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have long-lasting effects on economic development, shaping the institutional environment in which economies operate today. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), qualifying the direct impact of institutional changes on growth. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies, shaping their institutional environments and long-term economic trajectories.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by noting that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The thesis posits that European colonies with extractive institutions fell behind economically because they were unable to adopt growth-enhancing institutions. This view overlooks the complex interplay between institutional evolution and economic development. Austin argues that colonial governments often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction suggests that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and pressures.

Sokoloff and Engerman also highlight the role of factor endowments in shaping institutional paths. They argue that differences in factor endowments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, which in turn influenced the evolution of institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective complements Austin's critique by showing how initial conditions can shape long-term institutional trajectories. Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing efficiency as a driver of institutional change. She argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This qualification suggests that while factor endowments and local conditions are important, the power dynamics within societies also play a crucial role in shaping institutions.

Hopkins and Frankema argue that institutional reforms in Africa have primarily focused on reducing poverty rather than fostering long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). This focus is evident in the emphasis on "good" institutions that provide incentives for investment, a key driver of development. The historical context shows that British colonial rule facilitated formal education by altering the perceived benefits of such education through new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, this educational infrastructure did not always translate into broad-based economic growth; instead, it often prioritized consumption over public goods. This pattern is evident in post-independence budget policies, which were marked by clientelism and rent-seeking behaviors that diverted resources away from productive investments (Simson 2019, p.2; Simson 2019, p.11). These policies highlight how institutional reforms aimed at immediate poverty alleviation can lead to short-term gains but may not always support sustained economic growth.

Africa's mistrust culture may be rooted in its slave trade history; Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade created a pervasive distrust among African communities. This mistrust is deeply embedded within social norms, which are more influential than external factors such as legal structures. The internalization of this mistrust can be seen as a lasting legacy of the slave trades; Nunn (2008, p.19) shows how the lack of centralized governance and economic institutions in certain regions, like the Kongo kingdom, impeded sustained trade with Europeans. This suggests that the absence of strong institutional frameworks during the slave trade era may have exacerbated mistrust, further hindering economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that technological advancements and knowledge accumulation are pivotal drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores this perspective by noting that sustained growth over long periods is primarily attributed to epochal innovations in human knowledge, which spread across substantial regions of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These technological breakthroughs necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently integrated into economies. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations is crucial for economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that modern technology often conflicts with traditional societal structures, such as rural lifestyles and extended family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.6). For instance, early industrialization periods in developed countries illustrate how these conflicts can be resolved without excessive costs, ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. The role of the sovereign state is pivotal here, as it provides the necessary authority to manage growth-induced conflicts peacefully. Mokyr (2005, p.49) complements this by emphasizing that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements alone may not sustain long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Technological progress must be accompanied by a growth in "economic reasonableness" for sustained development. This implies that while knowledge and technology are fundamental drivers of growth, their effective utilization requires supportive institutions that can mitigate conflicts and ensure sustainable development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework necessary for sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, with some colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework was crucial in fostering economic growth in regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Similarly, North argues that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These secure property rights are critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. This shared emphasis on institutional frameworks underscores their importance in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which are essential for economic development.
