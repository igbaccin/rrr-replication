The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centering on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely supportive factors. One live disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some arguing that institutions facilitate secure property rights and market exchanges to drive growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Another key point of contention concerns the specific types of institutions that matter most for economic growth: are extractive or inclusive institutions more influential? These debates highlight the complexity of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The stakes intellectually involve refining our theories about economic development, with implications for policy and historical interpretation.

The role of property rights is central to understanding institutional influence on economic growth. The creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to falling transaction costs and increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9), which are essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This argument underscores that institutions matter in shaping economic outcomes by creating an environment conducive to investment and market exchanges.

The specific types of institutions that drive economic growth are still debated. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions, characterized by resource transfer to a metropole, have significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For example, European settlers in colonies like the United States established inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19), while in other colonies, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources. This highlights how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, which in turn influenced economic development paths.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is further complicated by the role of endogenous factors and the complexity of historical contexts. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over extended periods are crucial for understanding economic growth. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15), indicating that local dynamics also shape institutional outcomes.

The debate on institutions and economic growth extends to the role of distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions like serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the idea that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, it suggests that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), highlighting the complex interplay between cultural factors and institutional development.

The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how institutional reforms can inform policy to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with the idea that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18), suggesting that institutional quality can be improved through targeted reforms. The new economic history of Africa thus provides a framework for understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth and offers practical implications for policy-making, underscoring the importance of institutions in driving economic development.

North argues that institutions shape long-run economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs and property rights (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), which is pivotal for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to falling transaction costs because they increased security in the time dimension of property rights, an essential factor for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects how political institutions specifying efficient property rights can reduce transaction costs over time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. add that extractive institutions, which do not encourage economic development, may persist because they are shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were likely designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The persistence of such extractive institutions can hinder economic development by maintaining high transaction costs and insecure property rights, which in turn discourages investment and innovation. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policy-makers aiming to foster economic growth through institutional reform (Acemoglu et al 2001).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth are critical, as they involve not only the rules governing economic exchange but also the institutions enforcing these rules. This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange.

Ogilvie's view underscores the need to incorporate institutions deeply into long-term research programs, emphasizing their role in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective aligns with North and Weingast’s analysis, which suggests that the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for securing property rights and fostering economic growth.

These factors are deeply intertwined in shaping institutions that perpetuate economic disparities. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that unequal societies develop institutions favoring elites; these policies restrict competition and entrench inequality. In the New World, for instance, European colonizers established societies with extreme wealth, human capital, and political power inequalities from the outset. These disparities influenced institutional development, leading to policies that maintained elite advantages at the expense of broader economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of such institutions can be seen in Africa today, where a culture of mistrust, stemming from historical factors like the slave trade, continues to affect economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight that trust is crucial for various aspects of development including international trade and political institutions. The legacy of inequality and mistrust thus has far-reaching implications, not only in perpetuating economic disparities but also in shaping the broader social and institutional fabric of societies.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) posits that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. He suggests that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by arguing that modern technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is pivotal for long-term economic growth.

This process presents challenges. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that conflicts arising from technological progress must be resolved peacefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that coordination failures can hinder economic improvement if not addressed through institutional progress. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, they need to be complemented by robust institutions to ensure sustainable economic growth.

The role of institutions in facilitating the diffusion of useful knowledge is crucial. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that formal institutions aimed at reducing access costs play a significant role in this process. He notes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This underscores the importance of institutions in not only facilitating but also sustaining long-term economic growth through the efficient use of knowledge and technology.

van Zanden (1999, p.1); (Pamuk 2007, p.16) examine the economic dynamics of early modern Europe without explicitly focusing on institutions as fundamental drivers. They show that during this period, the shift in economic development towards North Sea countries like Holland and England after 1600 was marked by a rise in relative prices for basic foodstuffs. This geographic and temporal pattern highlights how economic development influenced regional price levels, with Amsterdam maintaining lower wheat prices due to abundant Baltic grain supplies and British export subsidies. Pamuk (2007, p.23; 2007, p.16) notes that urbanization rates served as key indicators of productivity and economic development, aligning with Kuznets' emphasis on structural change in early modern eras. This focus on urbanization and price levels sheds light on the economic transformations occurring during this period. Moreover, Pamuk's analysis of institutional flexibility in Holland (Pamuk 2007, p.23) suggests that regional differences in institutional structures played a role in how quickly economies could adapt to new opportunities. The varying responses to economic changes across different regions underscore the importance of local conditions and institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Van Zanden and Broadberry and Gupta both examine long-term economic performance and divergence without explicitly linking these outcomes to institutional frameworks (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). They reveal that the distribution of economic benefits was uneven in early modern Europe. Van Zanden posits that institutions facilitating skill transfers, such as guilds, played a role in shaping economic development by influencing the supply and demand for skilled labor. Broadberry and Gupta argue that merchant distribution and finance were crucial to productivity gains in north-western Europe’s traded goods sector. This suggests that institutional arrangements, even if not explicitly framed as such, had significant downstream implications for economic growth. They both imply that economic benefits from early modern development were spread unevenly across different sectors of the economy. An uneven distribution may have been influenced by factors beyond institutions, such as climate and geography. Broadberry and Gupta contend that while a warm climate might reduce the need for certain goods, high levels of investment in agriculture could also play a role in economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The interplay between institutional arrangements, geographical factors, and investment patterns underscores the complex nature of long-term economic performance.

The historical and social factors influencing African economic development highlight that women's roles in both pre-colonial and colonial periods were significant drivers of growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, while Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that women's contributions to agriculture and migration played pivotal roles in economic development. For instance, Krobos and Akuapems farmers migrated for farm land for cocoa, driving the Gold Coast’s economic revolution (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows how British rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, altering perceived benefits of education and creating new institutional dynamics (Frankema 2012, p.15). The interplay between these factors underscores the complex nature of long-term economic performance in Africa, where historical institutions, social norms, and colonial legacies continue to shape contemporary development trajectories.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (Simson 2019, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge conventional narratives about African economic policies post-independence. Simson argues that East Africa's budgetary practices were often rational responses to regional instability, rather than simply clientelist or unproductive. The expansion of military spending in countries like Kenya and Tanzania was driven by the need for domestic defense as colonial subsidies waned (Simson 2019, p.11). This pattern of increasing expenditure on security aligns with Broadberry and Gardner's finding that many African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence even before independence, suggesting a more complex picture of economic development than previously acknowledged. These budgetary shifts reflect broader institutional challenges in post-colonial Africa.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical economic paths by compressing different periods and trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the institutionalist approach to long-term economic growth requires a nuanced understanding of how secure private property rights were implemented in specific contexts. This dynamic institutional evolution challenges the notion of static, monolithic institutions driving growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies, demonstrating that economic development was not uniformly stagnant but varied significantly (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). However, this variation also indicates complex institutional adaptations, as different colonial policies led to diverse economic outcomes. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is particularly relevant in this context: it highlights how the institutionalist approach needs to account for both the adoption and adaptation of institutions over time.

The slave trades in Africa had profound effects on economic development and ethnic fragmentation (Nunn 2008, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the extensive duration of African slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had a lasting impact on subsequent economic development. This period is more than six times longer than the era of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to around 1960 (Nunn 2008, p.2). The Kongo kingdom's centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans, illustrating how institutional structures could support economic activity even under adverse conditions. Vansina (1990) noted that local coastal societies initially lacked the scale and differentiation needed for sustained foreign trade, highlighting the importance of institutional readiness in enabling economic exchange (Nunn 2008, p.19). Peters argues that the perception of African landholding practices as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment was a significant barrier to agricultural development during the colonial period. The push for agricultural modernization through 'modernized' farming schemes often led to dissatisfaction among agricultural officers, who faced resistance from communities with established landholding practices (Peters 2004, p.5). This dynamic underscores how pre-existing institutions can shape responses to external interventions, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally about the interplay between political and economic structures. Institutions determine how resources are allocated, how property rights are enforced, and how political power is distributed (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas leading to long-term economic stagnation (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution over time (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

The creation of secure property rights is critical to economic growth. North demonstrates that institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs and increased market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not promote long-run growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The persistence of extractive institutions is often rooted in distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by conflicts over distribution rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective complements Acemoglu et al.'s observation that politically powerful groups may resist institutional changes that threaten their rents or political power (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The historical evidence from former colonies shows the significant impact of institutions on economic development. Hopkins highlights that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s demonstration of a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. North argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic development (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that can settle the debate between institutionalist theories and other explanations, such as geographic factors. Austin's analysis shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, challenging the simplistic view of extractive institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional quality over time, which Acemoglu et al. show through their analysis of former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
