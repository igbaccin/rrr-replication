The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. Institutions are understood as the rules of the game that structure interactions among individuals and organizations. The stakes are high because, if institutions indeed matter for growth, then policy interventions can be designed to foster better institutions. However, there exists a live disagreement about how institutions shape long-run economic growth: some scholars emphasize the role of institutional quality in fostering economic development, while others point to the historical and contextual factors that determine the effectiveness of institutions. This debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic outcomes and whether their impact is uniform across different historical and geographical contexts. Institutions can be seen as both a cause and an effect of economic growth, and understanding this dynamic relationship is crucial for designing effective policy interventions. The complexity lies in determining the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run growth and identifying the conditions under which these mechanisms operate most effectively. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) share the view that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for long-run economic growth. This financial revolution was underpinned by political institutions that emerged from fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded for revenues. These political and financial developments reflect a broader pattern of institutional evolution that supports economic growth through reduced transaction costs and enhanced market efficiency (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast argue that the impact of institutions depends on enforcement and regime commitment to upholding them (Author Year, p.N). This commitment ensures a credible framework for property rights and economic exchanges, which in turn fuels long-term growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implications of these findings suggest that while institutional development is necessary for economic growth, it must be accompanied by robust enforcement mechanisms and political stability to yield sustained benefits.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (E0129) (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. They posit that these institutions were shaped by struggles over wealth and power, rather than efficiency considerations alone. The specific mechanism through which this occurred was via institutional arrangements that favored elites, who could use their political influence to shape policies that reinforced their dominance. For instance, in New World societies with greater inequality, elites implemented government policies that restricted competition and maintained their privileged status (Sokoloff&Engerman_2000). This process is evident in the persistence of institutions that advantaged elites at the expense of broader economic development. Ogilvie underscores this point by emphasizing that institutions should be seen as tools for understanding long-term historical processes, rather than merely fashion accessories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Distributional conflicts shaped the initial formation of institutions and their enduring impact on economic trajectories.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic development or extractive ones designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. For instance, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established. These institutions maximized short-term gains for European colonists but were not conducive to long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional framework persisted over time and shaped the economic trajectories of these regions, leading to significant differences in current income per capita between colonies that received different types of institutions from their colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The persistence of these institutions highlights a critical mechanism: the role of politically powerful groups in shaping institutional outcomes. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions in shaping institutional outcomes. This nuanced view suggests that the long-term impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. These scholars highlight how the influence of colonial institutions on African economies has often been overstated, with the focus instead shifting towards understanding local factors that shape institutional development and economic outcomes. This perspective is crucial because it emphasizes the importance of studying the interplay between colonial legacies and indigenous institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that ethnolinguistic fragmentation does not inherently impair the quality of public goods provided by African governments, suggesting that effective governance can emerge even in heterogeneous societies. These insights are particularly relevant for understanding how local adaptations to institutional frameworks can drive economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the need for targeted institutional reforms that consider both colonial legacies and local dynamics. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) also acknowledges that the prominence attached to institutions in development policy has enabled new economic history to claim attention from specialists, highlighting the role of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment essential for development. This focus on institutional quality suggests that understanding how to foster such institutions is key to addressing poverty and promoting growth in Africa.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight how technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) describes the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, suggesting that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress. This institutional support helps mitigate coordination failures and ensures that economic improvements are sustainable over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this by noting that modern technology requires significant ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. For example, the introduction of new technologies often conflicts with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1), necessitating institutional changes to resolve these conflicts peacefully. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for long-term economic growth. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) acknowledges that such adjustments can be costly and may weaken the political fabric of society if not managed properly (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and potentially long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the historical experience of the slave trade has left a lasting impact on societal norms. This mistrust is not solely an external response to a lack of rule of law or trustworthiness in others, but rather deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) corroborates this historical perspective by highlighting that while the slave trade may have disrupted traditional African societies, its legacy has had profound and enduring effects on contemporary social structures. The culture of mistrust is rooted in internalized norms shaped by the trauma of slavery (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), which complicates efforts to build trust essential for economic development (Knack and Keefer 1997; Fafchamps 2006). This historical legacy has significant implications for long-term growth, as a culture of mistrust can hinder cooperation, investment, and innovation. Therefore, addressing the deep-seated effects of slavery on societal norms is crucial for fostering an environment conducive to economic development in Africa.

The slave trade decimated populations and created a lasting culture of mistrust that continues to affect social capital and economic interactions.

Historical trauma from the slave trade has left deep scars in Africa, manifesting as internalized norms of mistrust that hinder cooperative behaviors essential for economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This legacy is part of a broader pattern where institutions shaped by colonial histories continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, illustrates how initial institutional frameworks can have long-lasting effects (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonial powers rather than promote long-run growth, underscore the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth extends beyond colonial legacies. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution highlights how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, showing that institutional frameworks significantly influence economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The new economic history of Africa informs institutional reforms and helps eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa by highlighting the importance of good institutions. These institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter significantly in shaping long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which historical institutional legacies from colonialism versus contemporary institutional reforms drive current economic outcomes. The historical evidence of African economic development, and particularly the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, settles this disagreement by showing that both historical legacies and contemporary reforms are critical.
