The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. The stakes are high because the answer to this question determines how societies can foster sustainable development over centuries. One live disagreement centres around whether institutions themselves drive economic growth directly or if they merely provide a conducive environment for other factors, such as technological innovation or human capital accumulation, to operate effectively.

The debate is further complicated by competing explanations for the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they shape incentives and constraints on economic actors, thereby determining the efficiency of resource allocation. Others contend that the impact of institutions is mediated through their effects on distributional conflicts and power dynamics within societies. These conflicting views highlight the need to unpack the causal pathways through which institutions exert their influence on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-term economic performance, as North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue. The creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment has historically driven significant economic growth. For example, the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs by increasing security for property rights. This pattern underscores how institutional design can either promote or hinder economic development based on the interests of those in power. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive even when they do not encourage economic development as politically powerful groups may resist changes that could threaten their power (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implication is that institutional change must consider the political dynamics at play to effectively promote long-term growth.

Institutional frameworks that fostered secure property rights in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29); Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate. North and Weingast explain how the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and the subsequent establishment of parliamentary supremacy were pivotal moments that reshaped English political institutions. These changes led to a significant commitment by the state to uphold property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could be changed only through consensus, providing a stable environment for investment and market development. These mechanisms were critical in establishing credible commitments from the state to private property holders.

Sokoloff and Engerman expand on this by highlighting how different initial conditions can lead to divergent institutional paths (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In New World societies characterized by extreme inequality, institutions often evolved to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how institutional evolution can be shaped by underlying socio-economic factors. This implies that while secure property rights are essential for growth, the path to achieving them can vary significantly based on historical and political contexts. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth involves both the rules themselves and the processes through which these rules are established and enforced.

This perspective emphasizes technological advancements as the primary engine of long-run economic growth. Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that innovations and the diffusion of technology are crucial for sustained economic improvement. Kuznets supports this view by noting that epochal innovations have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to ensure their efficient use. Kuznets further elaborates that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural modes of life, large family patterns, and the veneration of undisturbed nature (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that societies must adapt their institutions and ideologies to accommodate technological progress. Mokyr warns that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited due to coordination failures and rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, while technological advancements drive long-run economic growth, the institutional environment plays a critical role in facilitating or hindering this process. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where both factors must align to achieve sustained development.

Austin and Ogilvie both critique institutional theories for oversimplifying causal mechanisms (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This criticism extends to the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by Acemoglu et al., which Austin argues compresses different historical periods into a single narrative. The thesis posits that European settlers in Africa and America created institutions conducive to growth, while African societies with pre-colonial states did not. Austin demonstrates that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic actions, suggesting a dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view of historical causation is supported by Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial European institutions, which she argues are better explained by distributional conflicts than efficiency. She contends that core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by power struggles rather than a simple pursuit of productivity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the idea that efficient institutions naturally emerge from rational actors pursuing self-interest. Ogilvie also acknowledges that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they do not fully account for all institutional outcomes; other factors, such as beliefs and ideologies, must be considered. This complexity underscores the need for a more holistic approach to understanding institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18); Frankema (Simson 2019, p.2); and Frankema (2012, p.15) find that the development of public goods in Africa was influenced by various factors including colonial institutions. The new economic history of Africa has highlighted how different colonial powers shaped governance structures, with British rule fostering formal education more than other colonial regimes (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This educational development was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were altered by new social and political institutions implemented during the colonial period. The impact of these changes extended beyond education, influencing broader public goods provision. For example, East Africa's post-independence budget policies were characterized by significant shifts in spending priorities, with military expenditures increasing in response to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This pattern indicates that while colonial institutions set the stage for certain developments, post-colonial governance structures also played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Simson challenges the narrative of unproductive clientelism in East Africa's budget policies, showing that these policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades significantly impeded economic development over nearly five centuries (Nunn 2008, p.2). Shared finding (Nunn 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), Nunn and Broadberry and Gardner both argue that historical factors like the slave trades have shaped Africa’s economic trajectory. This historical trauma created a legacy of underdevelopment that persisted well into the colonial era. Broadberry and Gardner highlight how patterns of export production varied across Africa due to factors such as local endowments and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), suggesting that while slave trades laid the groundwork for economic stagnation, subsequent institutional developments also played a role in shaping economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions about Africa's long-term economic development unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This highlights the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the impact of these factors on Africa’s economic trajectory.

The slave trade's cultural legacy of mistrust has significantly influenced African economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon assert that the transatlantic slave trade fostered deep-seated mistrust within African societies by disrupting social cohesion. This mistrust persists today, affecting contemporary trust levels and economic interactions. Akyeampong and Fofack echo this theme, noting how colonial rule reinforced gender inequalities through customary law, further complicating social dynamics. The codification of these laws marginalized women's access to land for cash crops, which in turn affected agricultural productivity (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic extends beyond economic activities to broader societal interactions and governance structures as well. For instance, the mistrust engendered by the slave trade extends beyond immediate economic impacts; it influences political institutions and civic engagement. The legacy of mistrust undermines collective action and cooperation, which are crucial for effective governance and economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, with Europe's development driven by institutional changes that facilitated agricultural investment and productivity (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Broadberry and Gupta argue that the service sector played a crucial role in early modern economic growth, enhancing living standards through falling prices of manufactured goods. This dynamic is evident in Europe's rise as an economic powerhouse, where institutional changes led to agricultural investment and higher productivity (van Zanden 1999, p.9). However, van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies this by showing that the relationship between economic development and living standards was not straightforward; while England experienced rising food prices, Amsterdam maintained lower prices due to Baltic grain supplies. The nuanced interplay of institutions, agricultural productivity, and trade highlights the complex factors shaping long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is highlighted by Acemoglu et al.'s argument that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups. The establishment of extractive states in some colonies and the rule-of-law-based systems in others significantly influenced current income per capita, showing how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North emphasizes the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) finding that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not necessarily conducive to long-term growth. The interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes is further illustrated by North's observation that the creation of financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This development underscores how institutions can facilitate market exchanges and drive economic growth.

Austin challenges the simplification of causality in the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that different historical periods and paths are compressed into a single narrative. The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, showing how institutional prescriptions responded to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view is supported by Hopkins's argument that institutional reforms can inform poverty elimination strategies among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8), demonstrating that institutional changes can have significant impacts on long-term economic development. The new economic history of Africa shows that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, are better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. The creation of financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This development underscores how institutions can facilitate market exchanges and drive economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. There remains disagreement on the specific mechanisms by which these institutions operate. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), while Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of transaction costs and property rights security, as highlighted by North's analysis of the Bank of England (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
