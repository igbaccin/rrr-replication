The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers behind sustained economic development or merely one factor among many. The debate centers around two main positions. One group argues that certain types of institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and ensure secure contracts, are essential for fostering long-term growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another group posits that institutional frameworks are shaped by underlying economic and social factors, making them a secondary influence on development. This view argues that institutions may be necessary but not sufficient conditions for growth. The crux of the disagreement lies in whether institutions themselves can be manipulated to drive economic growth or if they are more likely to be the result of broader socio-economic dynamics. The debate has significant implications for policy, as it informs whether institutional reforms should be prioritized over other interventions. This tension is evident in the varying perspectives on how colonial institutions influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was essential for fostering secure property rights. This was achieved through the evolution of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms, which in turn reduced transaction costs. These reductions were crucial because they facilitated market exchanges by lowering the costs per transaction, thereby encouraging economic growth. This dynamic was not merely about economic rules but also about the political factors that ensured these rules were enforced and could be adapted over time. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of a regime committed to these rules, which underpinned the remarkable success of England's capital markets. However, North (1989, p.6) qualifies this by noting that while transaction costs per exchange may fall, the aggregate volume of exchanges increases with specialization and division of labor. This means that the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP, indicating that institutional changes not only reduce individual transaction costs but also expand the overall market activity.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) share that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap between northwest Europe and other regions. This divergence was driven by institutional flexibility in northwestern Europe, which allowed for rapid economic adaptation (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The service sector played a crucial role in this growth, facilitating merchant distribution and finance as much as production itself (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15).

Institutional changes were pivotal in fostering the growth of the service sector. This sector's development was not merely about reducing transaction costs but also about expanding market activity through increased exchanges. This institutional flexibility allowed northwestern Europe to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities, contributing significantly to its economic development.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff (2007) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were not merely about optimizing production but also about maintaining social hierarchies and controlling resources. The institutional environment of pre-industrial Europe was marked by a tension between elite control and the need for economic growth; this tension often led to the establishment of institutions that favored stability over innovation. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) notes the importance of integrating institutions into long-term research programs, highlighting how these structures can influence economic outcomes over extended periods. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.2) analysis of New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality. They argue that the institutional frameworks in these societies were shaped by the need to maintain elite control, which often involved restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for lower classes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic resulted in institutions that perpetuated inequality and hindered broader economic development. The persistence of such institutions underscores how distributional conflicts can lead to stagnation, as seen in pre-industrial Europe and the New World colonies.

Acemoglu et al., along with Acemoglu and Robinson, argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They propose that European colonization strategies varied based on mortality rates, leading to different types of institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the focus was on resource extraction rather than fostering local development. In contrast, colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights how colonial policies shaped economic trajectories by influencing the types of institutions implemented.

The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on economic development. This is because these institutions were shaped by European colonists who sought to maximize their own rents, often at the expense of long-term economic development in the colonies. The legacy of these institutions is evident in the current income per capita of former colonies, where extractive institutions have hindered economic progress. This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu and Robinson's work further qualifies this mechanism by showing that these extractive institutions were not just a product of initial endowments but also a result of political dynamics. This resistance highlights how distributional conflicts can perpetuate economic stagnation by maintaining institutions that benefit elites at the expense of broader economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.

The new economic history of Africa, championed by Hopkins and Frankema among others, can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This scholarship highlights the role of formal education in shaping long-run economic growth. The introduction of British rule in sub-Saharan Africa altered local cost-benefit calculations around formal education, thereby changing its perceived benefits (Frankema 2012, p.15). These new institutions created incentives for investment and development, which were essential for fostering economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner document that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that colonial institutional changes could have long-lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently using innovations and thus drive long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets argues that technological progress alone is insufficient; it must be paired with institutional changes to facilitate sustainable economic development. Modern technology demands shifts in societal structures, such as moving away from rural lifestyles and large family patterns, to accommodate new production sectors. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the role of economic reasonableness and institutional progress in sustaining economic improvements. He underscores that without complementary institutional advancements, technological progress can be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors, which erode long-term gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is essential for resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, as Kuznets notes. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a pivotal role in managing these conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This nuanced view suggests that while technological advancements are necessary for economic growth, they require robust institutional frameworks to ensure lasting benefits.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon's research shows that the legacy of the slave trade is deeply entrenched in contemporary African societies. This mistrust is not merely an external factor but also an internal norm deeply ingrained within individuals, making it at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The enduring impact of this mistrust is evident in various aspects of African society, including economic underperformance and political instability. This persistent mistrust not only affects economic outcomes but also undermines social cohesion, making it challenging to build the necessary trust for effective governance and development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical context and the specific institutional frameworks established during colonial periods. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely across different regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some colonies, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, with settler colonies generally faring better economically than those governed by extractive institutions. Similarly, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He highlights that European settlers were not always present to compete for African labor, allowing indigenous commodity production to flourish in some regions. This indicates that the economic outcomes in these regions were influenced by the specific institutional responses of colonial administrations to local conditions.

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is further underscored by North's analysis, which emphasizes the critical role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9). North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, facilitate market exchanges, and create an environment conducive to economic growth. This perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al., who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The creation of the Bank of England and other financial instruments in Europe illustrates how institutional changes can lead to significant economic transformations. Ogilvie, however, argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that the motivations behind institutional development are complex and often driven by power dynamics rather than purely economic considerations.

The persistence of mistrust in African societies highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. Hopkins shows that poor institutions in Africa are linked to the continent's dismal economic record (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This is consistent with Austin's analysis, which suggests that the lack of institutional evolution and investment in secure private property rights has hindered Africa's economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). The shared finding among these scholars is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), but they disagree on whether this impact is primarily due to efficiency gains from reducing transaction costs or the result of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. The historical evidence of settler colonies versus extractive states, as detailed by Acemoglu et al., shows that institutions designed for long-run growth have consistently outperformed those focused on short-term extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
