The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics, as it addresses how societies achieve sustained prosperity. At stake intellectually is the identification of causal mechanisms that explain why some nations experience persistent economic success while others stagnate or decline. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geopolitical influences, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures is crucial for fostering investment and economic development. Others contend that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies shape core economic institutions in ways that either facilitate or hinder growth. There are rival explanations suggesting that the reversal of fortune experienced by many economies can be attributed to different historical paths and periods as well as institutional frameworks alone.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of institutions on economic growth. This perspective highlights how secure property rights and effective governance structures create an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutions in different colonies, with some promoting rule of law and encouraging investment while others were extractive and focused on resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North emphasizes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This stream underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

North argues that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs and property rights (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional change reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita by creating an environment that did not encourage investment or economic development. This dynamic highlights how the nature of institutions can either facilitate or hinder long-run growth, depending on whether they promote secure property rights and low transaction costs.

This insight underscores the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes: institutions that prioritize the interests of a few at the expense of broader economic growth can stifle development. For sustained economic growth, institutions must be designed to support widespread investment and secure property rights, serving narrow interests is insufficient.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that societies with greater inequality tend to develop institutions favoring elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This phenomenon is also evident in the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who show how historical inequalities can shape societal trust levels, impacting economic development. The institutional mechanisms that advantage elites in unequal societies often involve policies designed to secure elite interests while limiting broader economic opportunities. For example, in Latin America, institutions were shaped by colonial legacies that concentrated wealth and power among a few, leading to persistent inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is not limited to historical contexts; contemporary economic development continues to be influenced by these deeply ingrained institutional structures. The persistence of inequality in such societies can be attributed to the entrenched nature of these institutions, which are resistant to change and perpetuate the status quo.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12)). This institutional transformation is exemplified by the Glorious Revolution, which established a credible commitment to property rights through constitutional constraints on executive power. North and Weingast's focus on political institutions highlights how credible commitments to property rights can drive economic growth. In their view, economic development is fundamentally influenced by the institutional framework that enforces rules and ensures stability. This perspective underscores the importance of political institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and innovation. Ogilvie's perspective on shared beliefs suggests that cultural factors can also play a role in economic outcomes, potentially complicating the link between property rights and growth. This complexity is evident when considering that exogenous cultural theories might influence institutional development, as seen in the decline of guilds due to changes in religious beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interplay between political institutions and cultural factors thus adds nuance to the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, highlighting the multifaceted nature of this relationship.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) together demonstrate that colonial governance structures can significantly influence long-run economic growth by shaping educational institutions. Frankema shows how British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more favorable to formal education than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, leading African families to invest more in it. This is consistent with Broadberry and Gardner's argument that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The impact of colonial governance on economic development can be seen through its influence on educational institutions, which in turn shaped long-run economic growth.

The relationship between institutional change and education is complex. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that demand for missionary education depended on various cost-benefit calculations by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that while colonial governance could facilitate educational development, local agency also plays a crucial role in determining its success. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further qualify this by noting that the growth patterns in export production varied due to factors such as transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This highlights how multiple institutional factors can interact to influence economic performance, beyond just education.

The interplay between colonial governance, educational institutions, and local agency underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional effects on long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share the finding that African women played crucial roles in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite facing marginalization under customary law (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Women were often relegated to less productive lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop cultivation. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary laws that further marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities.

This marginalization did not prevent women from making significant contributions to economic development, particularly during periods of rapid change. This highlights how African women adapted to and navigated colonial policies that sought to exclude them from productive activities.

The impact of ethno-linguistic fragmentation on economic development remains complex. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that while ethnic diversity can pose challenges to governance and institutional development, it does not necessarily hinder progress (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that despite facing marginalization, women's contributions to African economic growth were substantial, and their roles are often overlooked in historical analyses.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, particularly when considering the contributions of marginalized groups.

Austin (2008, p.2); Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty stems from the failure of institutions to adapt over time, not solely attributable to persistent historical phenomena. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis has been central in explaining Africa's economic struggles by focusing on structural impediments and colonial legacies. Austin (2008, p.15) ) demonstrates this through colonial governance, noting that colonial administrations sometimes altered their institutional frameworks in response to indigenous economic actions, which challenges the idea that institutional prescriptions were uniformly detrimental. This suggests that African poverty is more complex than a simple reversal of fortune; it involves the failure of institutions to evolve dynamically with changing economic conditions.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight the need for a deeper understanding of long-term economic development in Africa by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies. This research underscores that historical analysis must consider structural impediments as well as the nuanced evolution of institutions over time, and the distribution of income and material interests within societies. The implication is that future growth potential can be better assessed when these factors are taken into account, providing a more comprehensive view of Africa's economic trajectory.

Austin's argument about colonial administrations adapting to indigenous actions suggests that the relationship between institutional evolution and economic development is not straightforward. It requires nuanced analysis to understand how historical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development, suggesting that future research should focus on these dynamic interactions rather than static structural impediments.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonial strategies established different institutional frameworks in colonies with varying mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly impacted current income per capita. Similarly, North argues that the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England and the development of new political structures reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The historical evolution of institutions also highlights their dynamic nature. Austin demonstrates that colonial administrations often adapted to indigenous actions, suggesting a more nuanced relationship between institutional change and economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive process shows that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and pressures. Ogilvie further emphasizes the importance of understanding institutions as part of a broader social structure, noting that shared beliefs and distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in long-term economic growth is further underscored by their impact on investment and development. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who highlights that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions matter significantly in shaping long-run economic growth. North shows that the creation of political and financial institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms directly influences economic outcomes by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Austin's work highlights a remaining disagreement: the dynamic adaptation of colonial administrations to indigenous actions suggests that institutional evolution is influenced by local conditions and pressures (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence of colonial administrations adapting their policies in response to indigenous economic and political action settles this disagreement. This empirical comparison shows that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including local adaptations and the evolving needs of both colonizers and colonized populations, which ultimately influence long-term economic development trajectories.
