The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses how societal structures influence prosperity over extended periods. The intellectual stakes involve understanding whether and how institutions can foster sustained economic progress, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements are more determinant. One live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect growth: some scholars argue that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by providing stable frameworks for investment and exchange (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8), while others posit that institutions interact with broader historical and cultural contexts, making their impact contingent on these factors. Another key disagreement concerns the extent to which institutional changes are driven by efficiency considerations versus distributional conflicts: some scholars emphasize the role of efficient property rights and transaction costs in fostering growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3), whereas others highlight how institutions often emerge from struggles over resource distribution, reflecting power dynamics rather than purely economic rationales. The interplay between these perspectives is crucial for comprehending the multifaceted nature of institutional effects on long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions influence economic growth by shaping transaction costs and property rights (North 1989, p.9); Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show how different colonial institutions affected long-run economic outcomes in former colonies (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanisms through which these institutions operate are evident in the way they structured property rights and transaction costs.

This institutional change was driven by a political trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, reflecting a broader pattern of institutions emerging from power dynamics rather than purely economic rationales. These institutions were designed to maximize short-term rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions highlights how initial conditions can lock in certain economic trajectories, influencing long-run outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualifies this by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and do not necessarily encourage economic development; they are shaped by politically powerful groups aiming to maximize their rents rather than fostering growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional effects on long-run economic growth, suggesting that while institutions can facilitate growth through reduced transaction costs, the nature of these institutions is often determined by power dynamics and distributional conflicts.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29)(Austin 2008, p.2). This process was about establishing rules and ensuring their enforcement and adaptability. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a pivotal moment where the English political system evolved to limit the monarch's power, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. Austin notes that the behavioural patterns of older industrial economies today can still be understood through the lens of these historical institutional evolutions and policy experiences (Austin 2008, p.2). The secure property rights established during this period laid the groundwork for long-term economic growth, as they provided a stable framework for investment and economic activity. Austin qualifies that institutional changes were significant but did not occur in isolation. Colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between institutional evolution and local economic conditions highlights the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where institutions are both shaped by and shape the economic environment. The complexity of these interactions underscores that while secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth, the path to achieving them is influenced by a variety of political and economic factors.

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) argue that economic development and living standards in Europe from 1500 to 1800 did not correlate clearly. This period saw significant shifts in economic activity, with the focus moving from southern European regions to those bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England after 1700 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These changes underscore a complex relationship between economic development and living standards, where institutional factors played a crucial role. Institutional change, particularly in agriculture, drove investment and productivity gains in developing parts of northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15).

The benefits of early modern development were not uniformly distributed. The wages of skilled laborers yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than those of unskilled laborers, indicating that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly. This pattern suggests that while institutional changes fostered economic growth, they also exacerbated social inequalities during this period.

Hopkins (2009, p.18); (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) both emphasize that the historical and structural factors influencing poverty and growth in Africa are multifaceted. The role of institutions as fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth remains a focal point for understanding African development. The impact of colonial rule on African institutions is evident in the institutional legacies left behind by colonial powers (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This legacy is not monolithic; some African societies have managed to develop progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity and historical fragmentation (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the complex interplay between ethnic diversity and institutional quality. This finding underscores the importance of considering contextual factors when examining the relationship between ethnicity and economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa highlights the significance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. By the 1990s, the concept of governance had widened to include institutions and organizations, recognizing their crucial role in economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the experience of East Asia suggests that institutional reforms alone may not be sufficient for African growth; other factors, such as geographical conditions and historical legacies, also play a significant role.

The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates poses challenges in assessing the long-term effects of colonial rule on African economic development. Understanding these historical patterns is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms that can address poverty and promote sustainable economic growth in Africa, a task that requires a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across different regions.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting trust levels and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This culture of mistrust was a product of the external environment and deeply rooted in individuals' internal norms. For example, sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). These findings underscore how the legacy of the slave trade continues to influence contemporary African economies by shaping social norms that hinder economic development.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) argue that economic policies and class formation in Africa have been significantly influenced by budget policies and land systems. This process is evident in Kenya's post-independence era, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 percent of spending in the early 1990s. The displacement of both capital and recurrent spending highlights how economic policies can divert resources away from productive investments (Simson 2019, p.11).

The formation of classes in Africa has also been influenced by land systems that are often perceived as flexible but conceal processes of exclusion. These systems have led to a greater reliance on foreign firms and intermediaries, marking a departure from patrimonial dependence on decaying state institutions for political resources (Peters 2004, p.19). This shift is significant because it reflects how economic policies can drive class formation by altering the dynamics of resource distribution and political influence. However, Peters (2004, p.19) notes that the perception of 'custom' as static can have certain advantages for groups seeking to keep officials ignorant of their actions, which qualifies the extent to which land systems truly empower or exclude different classes (Peters 2004, p.5). This dynamic underscores how the interplay between economic policies and class formation in Africa is complex and multifaceted.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that these institutions were not primarily driven by efficiency but rather by the dynamics of power and wealth distribution. The persistence of serfdom, for example, can be understood as a mechanism to maintain existing power structures rather than an efficient use of labor. In New World societies, the initial abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to high average living standards but also to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites at the expense of broader economic growth. This dynamic is illustrated by how various government policies restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional development, where multiple forces interact to shape economic institutions over time.

Akyeampong and Fofack, alongside Frankema (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15), highlight that the role of women in African economic development was pivotal. They argue that gender dynamics significantly influenced access to resources and thus shaped economic outcomes. Women's contributions were particularly evident in pre-colonial and colonial periods, where they played crucial roles in agricultural production and economic growth. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule, which systematically disadvantaged women's access to land and resources (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Frankema (2012, p.15) suggests that the demand for missionary education was influenced by African political leaders' cost-benefit calculations. This indicates that the perceived benefits of formal education were altered by new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, suggesting that while gender dynamics and access to resources were crucial, broader economic factors also played a significant role. This interplay between gender roles, resource access, and economic institutions underscores the complexity of African development during colonial times.

This perspective highlights technological advancements as a primary driver of long-run economic growth. Mokyr underscores the role of increasing useful knowledge in driving economic development, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, sustainable economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets elaborates on this by pointing out that epochal innovations constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is crucial for efficient use of innovations, as Kuznets notes that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological changes to resolve conflicts arising from economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). However, Mokyr qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes institutional development, is far less so. This suggests that while technological advancements are a driving force behind economic growth, their impact can be limited without complementary institutional changes (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective underscores the complex relationship between technology and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, highlighting that while technological progress is essential, it requires supportive institutions to fully realize its potential.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply interconnected with historical contexts and colonial legacies. Acemoglu et al show that European colonization strategies varied significantly across different regions, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions, such as those set up in the United States and Australia, encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, fostering economic development. Extractive states in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, stifling local growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These differing institutional frameworks highlight how colonial policies have enduring impacts on contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that former European colonies with initially high income levels experienced a decline due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, oversimplifies causation. This perspective underscores the complexity of historical paths and the need for nuanced understanding (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's argument aligns with North’s assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). North further elaborates on how the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the belief that institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were efficient responses to economic conditions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie’s argument resonates with Acemoglu et al's thesis on the influence of population density and prosperity on colonial policies, which in turn shaped institutional outcomes. This interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Hopkins emphasizes that new economic history can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The prominence attached to the role of institutions has enabled this field to claim attention from development specialists, who recognize that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins's perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies and their impact on current income per capita. This shared finding underscores the enduring influence of colonial legacies on contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The precise remaining disagreement between Austin and Acemoglu et al is whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation or accurately captures the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from comparative studies across former European colonies, showing how differing colonization strategies led to either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison reveals that while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, their impact is limited without supportive institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
