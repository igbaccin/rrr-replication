The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding historical development and contemporary policy challenges. At stake intellectually are the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes over extended periods. The live disagreement hinges on whether institutions primarily shape growth by providing secure property rights and stable governance structures, or if other factors such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts are more critical. Institutions can be defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules (constitutions, laws) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions). The debate centers on whether institutions' efficiency in facilitating economic exchanges is the primary driver of growth or if other factors such as cultural values and distributional conflicts play a more significant role. This discussion has profound implications for policy, as it informs how to design institutions that foster sustained economic development.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing secure property rights. North (1989, p.6) argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs per exchange. This, in turn, facilitates market exchanges and encourages investment, which is essential for development. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth. They argue that a regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange is critical for the development of markets and sustained growth. Institutions reduce uncertainty by providing stable frameworks for economic activity, which encourages long-term investment and innovation. This perspective highlights the importance of institutional design in promoting economic growth.

While institutions' role in reducing transaction costs and providing secure property rights is well-established, some scholars argue that cultural beliefs also play a significant role in shaping core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) challenges the idea that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations. She argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions took shape through power struggles and the need to maintain social order, alongside concerns about efficiency. This perspective suggests that cultural beliefs and power dynamics are integral to understanding how institutions evolve and influence economic outcomes.

North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights leads to falling transaction costs per exchange, while North and Weingast emphasize the importance of a regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange for market development. This highlights the interconnectedness of political stability, secure property rights, and economic growth.

The role of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights is further supported by evidence from capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) provide striking indications that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, leading to an overhauling of traditional interpretations of British as well as French economic history. This suggests that institutional changes were not as revolutionary as previously thought and that the bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not a hindrance to economic growth. The evidence from capital markets underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing secure property rights and stable governance structures, which encourage investment and development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-term economic performance, with historical evidence showing that effective institutions can lead to significant economic growth. The political model outlined by North illustrates how trade-offs between certain rights and revenues shaped institutions that facilitated market exchanges, thereby reducing per-transaction costs while increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes can foster long-term economic development by providing secure property rights and stable governance structures.

Acemoglu et al (2001) further emphasize this point by showing that differences in European colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach aligns with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The enduring impact of these institutional differences is evident in current income per capita levels, illustrating how historical institutional frameworks can have lasting effects on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This pattern underscores that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.

This is particularly evident in the case of seventeenth-century England, where North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice were pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This process involved the establishment of credible commitments by the state to enforce rules and protect property rights, which in turn encouraged investment and market development. This focus on institutional enforcement aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.4) emphasis on how differences in factor endowments across New World societies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the evolution of institutions. They argue that societies with greater inequality tended to develop institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality; this institutional path dependence was critical for understanding long-run economic outcomes. The implications of these findings are significant: they suggest that initial conditions, such as factor endowments and the resulting social structures, can have enduring effects on institutional development and economic performance.

Extending this perspective to Africa, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Frankema 2012, p.15) highlight that the role of women and colonial education shaped economic activities. These scholars argue that African women played a pivotal role during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, contributing significantly to economic growth and development, contrary to views that marginalize their contributions. Colonial rule introduced dual legal systems; European courts based on common and civil law coexisted with native courts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This codification of customary law reinforced the subordinate status of women, as they were relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop cultivation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, where it became the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911, was driven by farmers like those from Krobo and Akuapem who migrated in search of farmland for cocoa. This dynamic underscores how gender dynamics influenced land use and agricultural productivity. British rule fostered formal education more than other colonial powers did (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education varied based on the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education related to new social and political institutions. This educational development had implications for economic activities, as it prepared a workforce with skills relevant to modern agriculture and other sectors.

This educational development is crucial because Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that understanding how institutions shape economic growth requires addressing Africa's poverty For instance, Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production despite the absence of European settlers to compete for labour. This dynamic illustrates how institutional prescriptions could shift based on local economic and political actions, suggesting that institutions are not static but respond to changing incentives and conditions. Hopkins qualifies this view by noting that while the new economic history of Africa has gained attention due to its focus on institutions, it is crucial to recognise that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This qualification underscores the importance of understanding how institutional reforms can be tailored to specific contexts to effectively eliminate poverty.

This qualification is supported by Ogilvie’s argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the prevailing notion that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's analysis focuses on core economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, highlighting how these structures emerged from power struggles rather than optimal resource allocation. These distributional conflicts were driven by elites seeking to maintain their status and control over resources, which led to institutional arrangements that favoured their interests. Frankema and van Waijenburg extend this framework by examining the historical trajectory of African economies, noting that poverty in Africa has not been a persistent phenomenon due to structural impediments (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores how distributional conflicts can shape economic institutions over time, influencing long-term development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2) show that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that this effect persists to this day in the form of a culture of mistrust within African societies, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development is not merely historical; it has enduring consequences for contemporary social and economic relations. This extended period of exploitation disrupted local economies and institutions, creating a legacy of underdevelopment that persists long after the end of the slave trades themselves.

Nunn (2008, p.19) argues that sustained trade did not occur until European powers encountered centralized African kingdoms like Kongo, which possessed well-developed markets and trading networks. The absence of such centralized governance in other regions led to fragmented economic structures that were ill-equipped to facilitate foreign trade. This historical dynamic highlights the importance of institutional development in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. The legacy of mistrust fostered by the slave trades poses a significant barrier to overcoming these structural impediments. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this view by showing that trust is crucial for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices, implying that the mistrust engendered by the slave trades has far-reaching implications for Africa's contemporary economic landscape.

This suggests that addressing the legacy of the slave trades requires institutional reforms as well as efforts to rebuild social trust.

Thus, addressing these legacies involves generating new technologies and making the institutional adjustments necessary to efficiently integrate these innovations into existing economic structures. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional changes is evident in the historical record, where epochal innovations have led to sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). For instance, Kuznets highlights that modern technology necessitates significant shifts in societal norms and institutions, such as the transition from rural life patterns to urban industrialization. This process often involves resolving conflicts between traditional ways of life and new technological demands, a challenge that requires robust institutional frameworks to manage effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. This suggests that while technology is the mainspring of economic growth, institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that these technological gains are sustained over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that efforts to foster long-term economic development must consider both the generation of new knowledge and the creation of supportive institutional environments.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in their capacity to facilitate technological progress and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Institutions play a pivotal role in ensuring that the benefits of new knowledge are sustained over time. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, which significantly influenced current income per capita(Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth, as North's work on the English financial revolution illustrates. This period saw a dramatic decline in transaction costs due to increased security of property rights, which facilitated long-term capital markets and overall economic growth(North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence shows that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic outcomes by determining how resources are allocated and how incentives are structured.

The persistence of institutions over time is a critical factor in understanding their impact on long-run economic growth. For instance, Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causality by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the specific institutional contexts and how they evolve over time. The interaction between institutions and economic development is dynamic, with institutions both influencing and being influenced by economic conditions. For example, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This dynamic interaction is evident in the historical experience of former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in shaping long-term development trajectories. The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies highlights the enduring impact of institutions on economic outcomes.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether institutions are shaped primarily by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. She contends that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than promote efficiency. The empirical evidence from Hopkins shows that good institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This historical evidence supports the view that distributional conflicts have played a significant role in shaping economic institutions and their impact on long-run growth.
