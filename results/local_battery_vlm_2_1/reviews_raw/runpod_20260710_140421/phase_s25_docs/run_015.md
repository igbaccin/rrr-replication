The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within economic history and development studies. The debate hinges on whether institutional frameworks, such as property rights, rule of law, and governance structures, are the primary drivers of sustained economic development or mere correlates with other underlying factors. One position argues that institutions are pivotal because they provide the necessary incentives for investment and growth; a competing view suggests that institutions are secondary to other determinants such as geography or culture. This debate is crucial for understanding historical trajectories of development and for crafting effective policies aimed at fostering economic growth in developing regions.

One stream of research focuses on the impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions significantly influenced current income per capita by shaping investment and growth dynamics. The persistence of these colonial institutions highlights their enduring impact on economic development trajectories. Austin shares this emphasis on the importance of institutions but cautions that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). He notes that the behaviour of industrial economies today can be understood through patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience. Austin's work underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. also argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). They document how population density and prosperity influenced European policies, leading to divergent institutional frameworks that affected industrialization and long-run development. This perspective aligns with Hopkins' assertion that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The emphasis on institutions as crucial for providing incentives for investment underscores their role in fostering economic growth.

The role of women in shaping long-run economic growth is another critical aspect. Acemoglu et al. highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with Akyeampong and Fofack's argument that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming under customary law codified by colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The de-privileging of women in access to land highlights how institutional frameworks can marginalize certain groups, affecting overall economic development. This marginalization was reinforced through patriarchy and the invention of custom under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The impact of British rule on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa provides further insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Frankema argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers, challenging the notion that all colonial powers had similarly negative impacts (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing demand for missionary education at both macro and micro levels. This institutional framework created opportunities for economic growth by fostering a more educated populace.

The initial conditions set by colonialism have shaped the path of development, influencing factors such as property rights, rule of law, and political stability (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional frameworks continue to affect economic outcomes today, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic growth.

This historical context sets the stage for understanding how property rights protection can drive agricultural development, as seen in Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Domar (1970, p.4) findings on the American Plains. By clearly delineating land boundaries, barbed wire allowed farmers to secure their investments in land and improvements. This security was crucial for fostering long-term agricultural development. Farmers could now invest in crops and infrastructure with greater confidence that their property would not be damaged by stray livestock or encroached upon by others. This mechanism is further supported by Domar's analysis, which highlights how secure land ownership can drive economic development by encouraging investment and productivity (Domar 1970, p.4). The impact of barbed wire varied across all regions. The effectiveness of these fences in protecting property rights varied depending on local conditions such as topography and existing legal frameworks. For instance, the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports indicate that the implementation and enforcement of herd laws were not consistent across different counties (Hornbeck 2010, p.8), suggesting that the benefits of barbed wire were contingent on effective institutional support. This variability underscores the importance of complementary institutions in maximizing the benefits of property rights protection.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) jointly argue that institutional changes in Africa were driven by shifts in land abundance and the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor. This transformation was influenced by local economic and political actions as well as exogenous factors. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to adjust their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic activities. Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium illustrates this point (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the importance of local agency in shaping institutional outcomes, as seen in Ghana where the transition from coerced to free labor had significant economic implications (Austin 2007, p.20). These institutional changes were not uniform across all regions. This nuanced understanding of institutional change in Africa challenges oversimplified narratives about colonial legacies, showing that local economic and political actions played a crucial role in shaping institutional outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) present the impact of British rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa as a critical factor influencing local development. Their work highlights that colonial policies under British rule were more conducive to fostering formal education than other colonial powers, contrary to broader claims about uniform negative impacts across African regions. This educational development was driven by demand for missionary education, which depended on cost-benefit analyses made by both African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, leading to increased investment in schooling.

The long-term effects of this educational policy are complex. This dynamic underscores how colonial policies shaped immediate educational outcomes and had lasting effects on socio-economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight that a lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates for Africa leaves significant gaps in understanding the full extent of these impacts, but their reconstruction of real wage levels provides crucial insights into economic trends during colonial rule.

These findings suggest that while British rule did promote educational development, it also created specific economic conditions that influenced long-term welfare. This nuanced view underscores how colonial policies laid the groundwork for both educational advancement and economic disparities, which continue to shape African development trajectories today.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms provides valuable insights into the historical context and development of institutions in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, but it does not directly address how these institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. The scope of development policy widened to incorporate governance, and policy recommendations allowed institutions to come to the fore (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shift enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim attention from development specialists, who recognized that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques if it is to be fully understood (Hopkins 2019, p.13). The literature on institutional reforms in Africa qualifies this claim by highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The new economic history of Africa has made strides in understanding institutional development, but much work remains to fully grasp how these institutions drive long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner's work (alongside BroadberryEtal) (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) establishes that long-term economic performance in Africa and India was influenced by patterns of export production tied to foreign trade. The timing and scale of growth in these economies varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) further elaborate on this mechanism: the agricultural sector in India remained dominant, with workers largely confined to the land, which had negative consequences for labour productivity and limited the development of industrial and service sectors (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This pattern was shared by much of the world at that time, not unique to India. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) also note that India lacked the state institutions needed to support investment and innovation, which were crucial for breaking out of the Malthusian trap (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This institutional deficiency helps explain why both population and per capita incomes increased in Britain and Holland but not in India. The broader implications of these findings is that economic performance is closely linked to the presence or absence of certain state institutions, which can influence productivity gains over time and affect long-term growth trajectories, even if export production patterns are similar.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Bryant 2006, p.12; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during this period. The merchant distribution and finance networks facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector, particularly in northwestern Europe. This productivity stemmed from institutional changes that supported development as well as agricultural investment (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Bryant (2006, p.12) supports this view by highlighting how European political institutions provided a supportive setting for developmental modernization. Bryant exposes ideological blinders of revisionist scholars who dismiss Eurocentric views, asserting that the alleged historical differences in political institutions have been overdrawn (Bryant 2006, p.1). The benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). High productivity in the traded goods sector led to increased living standards for skilled laborers but not necessarily for unskilled ones, indicating that economic gains were concentrated among certain segments of society (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The implications are that while institutional factors did contribute to the great divergence, their effects were nuanced and varied across different social strata.

Women played significant roles in economic growth and development both historically and contemporarily (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Goldin (2006, p.3) documents how shifts in employment opportunities, educational attainment, and household production technology facilitated women's entry into the workforce. This quiet revolution began with increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century, which opened up new job opportunities for women. Simultaneously, the expansion of secondary education from the 1910s to the 1940s greatly increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3). This period also saw changes in household production technology and institutional reforms that made part-time work more accessible and removed barriers to the employment of married women. This migration was driven by a shift from subsistence farming to cash crop production, which required more labor-intensive practices. Men dominated the codification of customary law under colonial rule, so the benefits were unevenly distributed. This legal framework reinforced gender inequalities and restricted women's access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The downstream implication is that while women contributed significantly to economic growth, their roles were often marginalized by institutional frameworks that favored male dominance.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) argue that technological advance and social change are pivotal in shaping economic growth, influencing productivity scales. These dynamics can drive changes in the ranks of countries on the productivity scale, altering global economic hierarchies. For instance, Abramovitz notes that institutional sclerosis or other metaphorical maladies associated with age and wealth can afflict economies (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This idea is supported by Allen et al.'s work, which shows that wage rates may serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group and the marginal productivity of labor in the economy as a whole (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). These dynamics are influenced by factors such as regional migration patterns and the integration of labour markets; they are not straightforward. Allen et al.'s research reveals that in early modern China, there was a vibrant and active labour market for short-term or day labour, indicating a high degree of integration between urban and rural areas (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This suggests that while technological advance and social change can drive economic growth, the specific institutional and economic contexts play a crucial role in determining how these dynamics unfold.

Bolt and van Zanden argue that accurate measurement is essential for understanding economic performance globally (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3; Feinstein 1998, p.28). They emphasize the importance of collaborative research to refine estimates and address uncertainties. This approach involves acknowledging margins of error in historical data and encouraging scholars to engage with these estimates, thereby improving them over time. Bolt and van Zanden highlight that Maddison's work on measuring economic performance was pivotal in this regard; his strategy of producing numbers even when the basis for them was not always solid induced many scholars to work on these themes and quantify long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This collaborative effort has led to a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than what was available when Maddison started his project in the 1960s. Feinstein builds on this by showing that real wages and the standard of living in Britain improved during and after the Industrial Revolution, countering pessimistic views (Feinstein 1998, p.28). The measurement challenges highlighted by Bolt and van Zanden qualify these improvements; they underscore that while progress was made, the accuracy of these measurements remains a subject of debate. This ongoing refinement is crucial for understanding historical economic performance and informing contemporary policy decisions aimed at sustainable growth.

Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Bryceson (2002, p.2) share a finding that economic restructuring in Africa has led to fundamental social changes (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). The introduction of money into subsistence economies transformed traditional economic institutions, shifting away from multicentric systems. This transition involved deep social transformations as well as being economic. In particular, the integration of African economies into global markets through colonial trade networks introduced new forms of currency and exchange mechanisms. For instance, brass rods became a general-purpose currency within specific spheres (Bohannan 1959, p.9). These changes were not uniform across all sectors; equivalency between traditional currencies like brass rods and modern economic exchanges did not always exist.

Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlights that the introduction of alien institutions disrupted local economic systems. This disruption was evident in the shift from traditional barter systems to monetary economies, influenced by colonial governance (Bohannan 1959, p.10). Bryceson (2002, p.2) elaborates on this by noting how neoliberal policies impacted rural social structures, leading to depeasantization and changes in gender roles. Rural men accepted that their wives and daughters needed to work outside the home to earn money, indicating a significant shift in societal norms (Bryceson 2002, p.8). This economic restructuring reshaped social dynamics within African communities as well as altering livelihoods.

These profound changes underscore how economic restructuring can lead to both intended and unintended social consequences.
