The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal as it highlights how formal and informal rules influence societal outcomes over extended periods. This question is central to understanding why some regions thrive economically while others lag behind. A key disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions shape economic trajectories by determining how resources are allocated and how incentives are structured (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). Others posit that institutional frameworks are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, influenced by historical contexts and external factors (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The impact of colonialism on the development of institutions in former colonies is a critical area of study. Acemoglu et al. highlight how Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, setting up either inclusive or extractive institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach underscores that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic development. The long-run impact of these institutional legacies is evident in contemporary economic performance, where former colonies with extractive institutions often struggle with poverty and underdevelopment.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonialism was pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth through institutional changes. The authors argue that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional framework had profound implications for long-term economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) note a direct correlation between these colonial-era institutions and current income per capita in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions highlights a significant mechanism through which colonialism shaped economic trajectories.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) both show that shifts in land abundance can drive institutional changes in former colonies. The scarcity of land led to an institutional revolution in Ghana during 1874-1939, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production even where European settlers were not present. This is because colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that this process was more nuanced than simply adopting institutions favourable for growth; it involved a complex interplay between local economic conditions, government policies, and historical legacies. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony exemplifies this dynamic (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional shift had an important economic dimension, as it facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour. This highlights the complex interplay between economic conditions, government policies, and historical legacies in shaping long-run economic growth.

The educational and structural impacts of colonial rule in Africa highlight how British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This is evident from the demand for missionary education, which depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that this dynamic was particularly pronounced under British rule, where the colonial administration actively promoted missionary activities which in turn fostered educational development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This gap underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) jointly show that 20th-century economic performance in India and Anglophone African economies is tied to periods of growth and decline. The latter's economic development was driven by export production, with variations due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). In India, the per capita GDP decline relative to Britain began during the early modern period, preceding colonial rule (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This suggests that the state of institutional development at any given time may influence economic growth in a non-linear fashion. The Mughal Empire's collapse and subsequent British revenue increases indicate that institutions can both facilitate and hinder long-term economic growth. Broadberry et al note that India lacked key state institutions to support investment and innovation (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This underscores the importance of specific institutional factors in underpinning economic development, particularly those that enable technological progress and productivity gains.

The literature on African economic history has made significant strides in understanding how institutions and governance influence development, but it remains fragmented and lacks consensus on key issues such as precolonial production and the impact of colonial rule. Hopkins (2019, p.3) notes that despite initial skepticism, African economic history has progressed significantly over the past half-century, while Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights how new approaches to institutional reforms can inform development policy and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. One specific mechanism through which institutions shape long-run growth is by providing incentives for investment; 'good' institutions are crucial because they enable productive activities and foster economic development. This incentive structure underpins the significance of "bringing the state back in" as a developmental policy, which allows institutional and organizational factors to come to the fore (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins emphasizes that while some scholars have suggested East Asian experiences might be relevant to Africa, this perspective does not account for the diversity of African societies (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Bryant (2006, p.12) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) share the posture that economic and social changes in Europe and Asia can be understood through long-term institutional developments. This perspective highlights the importance of historical context in shaping economic outcomes, which Bryant illustrates by critiquing revisionist views that dismiss Eurocentric narratives as empirically suspect (Bryant 2006, p.1). The shared findings indicate that major Eurasian societies experienced cumulative institutional developments that enhanced coercive and productive power (Feinstein 1998, p.28)(Bryant 2006, p.12), but these changes did not necessarily lead to uniform economic progress. Allen et al.'s examination of real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries demonstrates that, despite vibrant labour markets, wage rates stagnated for agricultural day labourers (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This stagnation suggests that regional migration and market integration did not translate into widespread economic improvement (Allen et al. 2011, p.22), illustrating how specific institutional contexts can shape long-run economic outcomes. Feinstein (1998, p.28) further qualifies this pattern by showing that, in Britain, real wages and living standards improved during the Industrial Revolution, highlighting the role of industrialization in driving economic growth (Feinstein 1998, p.28). This implies that while institutional changes are necessary for economic development, they must be accompanied by structural transformations to yield significant long-run benefits.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can lead to profound changes and shifts away from multicentric economies, challenging the notion that institutions are the sole fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Domar 1970, p.4). Bohannan (1959, p.10) demonstrates how the integration of a new road network in Northern Nigeria facilitated increased trade and a greater number of markets. This transition perturbed the internal marketing system and aligned local economies with the global economy, illustrating the transformative power of economic transitions on societies. This suggests that economic transitions can fundamentally alter social structures and labor dynamics, independent of institutional frameworks. However, the transition is not without challenges: Bohannan (1959, p.10) notes that while brass rods served as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere, they could not fully replace traditional exchange systems such as marriage or food rights outside this sphere. This highlights the potential pain points and complexities in economic transitions, where new monetary systems may coexist with older exchange mechanisms. The introduction of money can thus lead to hybrid economies, where traditional and modern economic institutions interact in complex ways, shaping long-run economic outcomes beyond institutional frameworks alone.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect investment and development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that European colonial policies led to varied institutional frameworks in former colonies, which significantly impacted their long-term economic trajectories. Similarly, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the evolution of institutions is crucial for understanding economic growth patterns, highlighting how different paths of institutional development can lead to divergent economic outcomes. In this view, whether these institutional arrangements are inclusive or extractive determines their impact on long-run economic performance.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic development extends beyond immediate policy effects. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often hindered long-term growth in former colonies, as these institutions were not oriented towards fostering local economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) shows how colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting a dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional design that shaped economic outcomes over time. This interaction underscores the importance of understanding both the historical context and the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate.

Institutional quality also influences contemporary economic performance by affecting investment incentives and governance structures. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, a critical factor in development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) provide evidence of how variations in export production tied to political institutions influenced growth patterns across different African economies. This linkage between institutional quality and economic performance is further supported by the observation that even ethnically heterogeneous societies can develop progressive institutions capable of upholding the rule of law, as shown by Hopkins (2009, p.18). The key takeaway is that the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth is multifaceted, involving both historical legacies and contemporary governance practices.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the shared finding (Austin 2008, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) that institutional frameworks introduced during colonial rule had enduring effects on economic development. A key point of disagreement remains over whether all colonial powers had similarly negative impacts on African education systems. Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This specific historical evidence of differential educational outcomes under varying colonial institutions settles the debate, showing that the nature and impact of colonial institutions can vary significantly across different contexts.
