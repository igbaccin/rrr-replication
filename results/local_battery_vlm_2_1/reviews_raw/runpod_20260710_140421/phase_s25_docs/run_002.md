The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The debate revolves around whether the nature of institutions, such as property rights and political stability, determines a nation's economic trajectory over centuries. One side posits that these institutions are crucial for sustained development; another argues that geography or other factors hold greater sway. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) represent two poles of the debate on institutions' role in long-run growth. In the former, institutional change is a gradual process influenced by multiple historical forces, while the latter argues that initial colonial institutions have enduring effects on economic outcomes. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors or endogenous dynamics. This question matters because it affects how policymakers view economic development: are current policies constrained by centuries-old institutional legacies? Or can they chart a new path regardless of historical context? The answer lies in understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, and if these mechanisms can be altered. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may not encourage economic development because they are shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize their own interests.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institution established depended on European mortality rates; high mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction for the metropole, while low mortality resulted in settler colonies with institutions fostering investment and growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional divergence was not merely a function of geography or initial endowments but a direct result of colonial policies. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these extractive institutions persisted, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The mechanism here is clear: European colonizers established institutions that served their immediate interests, often at the expense of long-term economic development in the colonies. This institutional legacy has had lasting effects on economic outcomes, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. The implication is that institutional change, even if beneficial for long-run growth, may not occur spontaneously because it challenges the interests of those in power.

The literature on economic divergence and colonial impact explores how historical events and policies have shaped economic outcomes in different regions (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen 2001, p.8). BroadberryEtal argue that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period. Allen contends that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. These findings suggest that long-run economic growth is influenced by historical factors that precede colonialism. This pattern indicates that institutional changes, even if beneficial for long-run growth, may not occur spontaneously because they challenge the interests of those in power.

Austin’s work on Ghana during 1874-1939, together with (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20), identifies that the institutional revolution in factor markets played a pivotal role. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional transformation marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This change was driven by the 1874 British emancipation ordinance, which abolished slavery and pawning, thereby facilitating a transition from coerced to free labour (Austin 2007, p.20). Austin highlights that this institutional shift was a response to changing factor scarcities (Austin 2008, p.2). The colonial administration’s role in this process is crucial; it created incentives for indigenous economic and political action, which in turn influenced the institutional prescriptions of the colonial government. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis that lower production would be favoured (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin further notes that the imposition of British rule had significant market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional revolution in factor markets underscores how colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes by fostering a more dynamic and integrated market system.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). Hornbeck shows that this technological innovation allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively, which was crucial for preventing cattle from trampling crops. Domar's analysis of labor scarcity and property rights further illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic development. He argues that when land is abundant but labor is scarce, institutions like slavery or serfdom emerge to secure labor for agricultural production (Domar 1970, p.4). This dynamic is relevant to the American Plains context, where the availability of land was high and labor was relatively scarce; barbed wire fencing can be seen as an institutional mechanism that helped mitigate these labor scarcity issues by protecting farmers' investments in their land. However, Domar also notes that the development of serfdom or slavery requires a non-working class of agricultural owners, which suggests that the introduction of barbed wire fencing alone may not have been sufficient to drive economic growth without complementary institutional changes (Domar 1970, p.12). Thus, while barbed wire fencing played a crucial role in enhancing property rights and reducing externalities, its full impact on economic development was likely contingent on broader institutional frameworks that supported agricultural productivity.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels, which indicates that economic conditions were not uniformly dire across British African colonies during the colonial period (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The variability in export production growth patterns highlights the role of local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions in shaping economic outcomes. These factors influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic development. Broadberry and Gardner note that the lack of national accounting data for Africa has limited comparative historical work on economic growth and development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This gap in data availability suggests that while institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic performance, our understanding of their impact remains incomplete without comprehensive historical records.

Changes in women's employment and education have profoundly altered family dynamics and economic participation (Goldin 2006, p.3; Bryant 2006, p.12). Goldin identifies several exogenous changes that drove shifts in women’s roles. These include the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, which made desirable jobs available to women; the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from 1910s to 1940s, which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers; changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s, which altered female labor supply; and institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women. These factors altered labor supply and increased women's participation in the workforce. Concurrently, Bryant asserts that European political institutions provided a setting conducive to developmental modernization (Goldin 2006, p.10; Bryant 2006, p.1). Thus, changes in women’s roles can influence broader economic trends by increasing their participation in the workforce and thereby altering family dynamics and overall economic activity.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the direction of societal development and resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions across colonies; these institutions significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often resulted in extractive states that hindered economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The divergent paths of institutional development are crucial in understanding the varying levels of economic prosperity among former colonies. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, highlighting the importance of institutions in achieving economic milestones (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2).

The role of political institutions is also critical in fostering developmental modernization.
