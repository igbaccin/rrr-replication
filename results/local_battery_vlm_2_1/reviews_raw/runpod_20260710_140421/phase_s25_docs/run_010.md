The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development theory and practice. At stake is how to understand the persistent disparities in wealth across countries, and what policy levers can be used to promote sustained economic progress. One live disagreement centers on whether colonial institutions are primarily extractive or conducive to growth. Some scholars argue that European colonizers established institutions designed to maximize resource extraction from colonies, which hindered long-term economic development. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions had complex and varied impacts across different contexts (Author Year p.N). The debate underscores the importance of understanding institutional legacies in shaping contemporary economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that European colonizers' policies were shaped by population density and prosperity levels in their colonies, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. They emphasize that institutions introduced during colonialism played a central role in long-run development. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, resulting in varied institutional setups across colonies. In some cases, such as the United States and Australia, settlers established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment. These findings highlight how colonial institutions influenced economic trajectories by determining whether local societies could develop productive capacities or remain constrained by extractive systems.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive even if they do not foster economic development, as these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maximize their rents. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) similarly notes that colonial administrations adapted institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. He also notes that European settlers' presence or absence shaped indigenous commodity production, indicating a nuanced relationship between colonial policies and economic growth.

Institutional legacies are not merely historical artifacts; they continue to shape contemporary economic policies and outcomes. The persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-term growth by failing to provide incentives for investment and innovation. Institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment can promote sustained economic development. This dynamic interplay between colonial institutional legacies and current economic performance underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions influence long-run economic growth.

These findings from Acemoglu et al., along with shared insights (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), suggest that the institutional frameworks established during colonial periods continue to exert a lasting influence on economic trajectories. The type of institutions established during colonization was determined by the settler mortality rates, which in turn were driven by local disease environments and climate conditions. When Europeans faced high mortality rates due to tropical diseases, they implemented extractive institutions designed to exploit resources quickly and transfer them back to Europe. This strategy resulted in institutions that failed to foster long-term economic development. Where European settlers established permanent colonies with low mortality rates, they created inclusive institutions that protected property rights and encouraged investment.

These institutional differences persisted over time and continue to impact the economic performance of former colonies today. For example, Acemoglu et al. show that countries with a history of extractive colonial institutions tend to have lower income levels per capita compared to those with more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This persistence is due in part to the political power dynamics within these societies. Politically powerful groups may resist institutional changes that could threaten their control, even if such changes would promote long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al.'s findings highlight the enduring legacy of colonial institutions and their profound impact on contemporary economic outcomes.

Building on these insights, Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to education development than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This positive impact can be attributed to the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for both African political leaders and families. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations influenced by these institutional changes (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg's findings on real wage levels across British Africa qualify the long-term economic growth implications of this educational development (Author Year p.N). Their reconstruction of real wage trends reveals a complex picture of economic development that includes both structural effects and temporary conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The presence of formal education institutions, fostered under British rule, likely contributed to a more skilled labor force over time. This enhanced human capital could have facilitated long-run economic growth by increasing productivity and innovation. These findings suggest that while British colonial rule may have had a more positive impact on education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, the overall effect on long-run economic growth was contingent upon a multitude of factors including institutional frameworks and structural impediments.

Austin (2008, p.2) emphasizes that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This simplification neglects the nuanced ways in which localised scarcity drove institutional innovation. The transition was marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the loss of slaves which made chiefs more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming. This pattern is also evident in other colonies where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour. This shift highlights how broader historical and geographical conditions influenced institutional changes, even when these changes were not directly induced by localised scarcity. The integration of British rule also had market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast (Austin 2007, p.4).

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) shows how technological innovations in property rights protection spurred agricultural development on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This process was driven by barbed wire fencing, which allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively and mitigate livestock damage from neighboring farms. The introduction of this technology enabled farmers to secure their property rights and reduce externalities, leading to significant increases in improved farmland. Despite the initial focus on herd laws, the shift towards barbed wire fencing marked a pivotal change in agricultural practices (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This technological advancement enhanced individual land use and fostered broader economic growth by encouraging more efficient and productive farming methods. This process depended on the local adoption of new technologies, underscoring the importance of regional adaptability in institutional changes. The integration of barbed wire fencing into agricultural practices underscores how specific technological innovations can catalyze economic development by addressing longstanding issues related to property rights and externalities.

This historical context is further illuminated by Broadberry and Gardner's findings that African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) also note that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period, suggesting that long-run economic performance can be shaped by pre-colonial institutional frameworks. This pattern is evident in both African and Indian economies, where political stability and institutional quality played crucial roles in economic outcomes (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) further emphasize that India lacked state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation, a condition that hindered long-term economic development. This underscores the importance of robust institutional structures in facilitating sustained growth.

The role of women and social change in African economies is an important area of study but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bryceson 2002, p.2). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that during the colonial period, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop cultivation. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by customary law codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Bryceson (2002, p.2) illustrates how these changes led to new income-earning roles for women, with rural men generally accepting that their wives and daughters needed to work outside the home. This shift highlights a mechanism by which social change can alter economic dynamics but also reveals a condition where these transformations do not necessarily translate into institutional changes that drive long-run growth. The role of women in African economies thus offers insights into how social structures influence economic activities, yet it does not provide a direct link to how institutions fundamentally shape sustained economic development.

The literature on African economic history and development explores the complexities of economic growth in Africa without directly addressing how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, but it does not offer a direct linkage between these historical insights and institutional changes that foster sustained development. This is despite the fact that the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment has been recognised as crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2019, p.3) notes that significant progress has been made in African economic history, but much work remains, particularly on precolonial production and how these historical contexts might inform institutional reforms today. This gap is noteworthy because it highlights the need for more research into how historical economic conditions shape current institutions and growth trajectories. The absence of a direct link between African economic history and institutional changes that drive long-run growth suggests that while historical insights are valuable, they may not be sufficient on their own to guide institutional reform efforts.

Colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth trajectories by determining the extent to which extractive practices were implemented in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that European colonists designed equilibrium institutions to maximize their own rents rather than to foster long-term economic development, resulting in differing institutional frameworks across colonies. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the influence of colonial policies on the provision of public goods and the rule of law, both of which are critical for economic growth.

A closer look at specific colonial strategies shows that European settlers adopted different approaches based on local conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) detail how in some colonies Europeans settled permanently and established institutions enforcing the rule of law, while in others they implemented extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This variation in institutional design directly influenced current income per capita levels across these regions.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic development is also evident in the role they played in shaping educational systems. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule was more conducive to developing formal education compared to other colonial powers, altering perceptions of the benefits of education and contributing to long-term human capital formation. This institutional legacy continues to influence contemporary educational outcomes and economic performance.

The historical context of African economic growth underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutions on development trajectories. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change during this period. This adaptability influenced long-term economic growth by shaping the incentives for investment and development.

The role of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods is another critical aspect that shaped economic growth trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate how women's access to land and their contributions to agricultural production were integral to economic development, despite being marginalized under colonial rule. This historical evidence suggests that understanding the role of gender in economic history is essential for comprehending long-term growth patterns.

Institutional changes driven by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that these institutions interacted with industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, playing a central role in the long-run development of these regions. This interaction between institutions and economic opportunities highlights how historical institutional frameworks continue to influence contemporary growth trajectories.

The underlying point of agreement is that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic growth by determining the extent of extractive practices (Hopkins 2009, p.8). There is ongoing disagreement over the precise mechanisms through which these institutions influenced economic development. Frankema (2012, p.1) shows that British rule was more conducive to developing formal education than other colonial powers, suggesting a positive impact on human capital formation. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of educational outcomes and their correlation with long-term economic growth across different colonial regimes.
