The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The stakes are high intellectually because the causal mechanisms that link institutions to economic performance can inform both historical explanations and contemporary policy debates. A central disagreement revolves around whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive or conducive to development, with some scholars arguing that extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers hindered long-run growth in former colonies. Others posit that institutional changes during colonialism had a more nuanced impact on economic trajectories. This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how specific institutional arrangements influence economic outcomes over extended periods. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The nature of these institutions was determined by the Europeans' settlement patterns and their intended duration of stay. Where Europeans settled permanently, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Where Europeans focused on rapid resource extraction and created extractive states to transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these equilibrium institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, showing that colonial policies had enduring effects on institutional development and economic outcomes in former colonies. This suggests that while initial colonial institutions were crucial, their impact was further shaped by subsequent economic opportunities and policies.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This innovation allowed farmers to more effectively demarcate and defend their land, which was crucial for the expansion of agriculture in regions where open-range grazing had previously dominated. The ability to clearly delineate property boundaries not only facilitated the transition from nomadic herding to settled farming but also fostered a more stable and productive agricultural sector. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) shows that the adoption of barbed wire led to significant increases in improved farmland, with herd-law counties experiencing a notable 20 percentage point increase from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This period coincided with the widespread use of barbed wire, indicating that the technology played a pivotal role in transforming agricultural practices. Domar (1970, p.4) further elaborates on this by highlighting how land ownership rights can shape economic structures, asserting that government policies facilitating land ownership can create a non-working class of agricultural owners (Domar 1970, p.4). This dynamic is particularly relevant to the American context, where the scarcity of labor and the abundance of land created conditions favorable for the development of serfdom or slavery. However, the introduction of barbed wire altered this landscape by making it possible for individual farmers to secure their property rights more effectively, thereby reducing the need for large-scale labor exploitation. This shift had profound implications for economic growth, as it encouraged investment in agriculture and facilitated the development of a more diversified economy. The economic benefits extended beyond agricultural productivity to broader infrastructure and market development, as farmers could now invest in their land with greater confidence. By protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage, barbed wire fencing played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth on the American Plains.

This shift in resource availability necessitated institutional changes that would support the new economic realities, as demonstrated by Austin and his colleagues (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20) in their analysis of African economies. This shift was marked by the emergence of factor markets, particularly in land use rights and hired labour. In Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to scarcity drove an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional change facilitated widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, as chiefs sold land to strangers to farm cocoa, driven by debt incurred after losing slaves. This nuance highlights that while institutional changes in African economies were driven by shifting resource availability, they were also influenced by broader political and social factors. The complex interplay of these elements shaped the long-run economic growth trajectories in Africa, as localised scarcity and institutional adaptations interacted with colonial policies and labor dynamics.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies has led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). This monetary transition facilitated the integration of local economic systems with broader global markets, marking a significant departure from traditional exchange mechanisms. Bohannan documents that the Tiv economy shifted as brass rods, once a general-purpose currency within prestige spheres, became integrated into broader market transactions (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bohannan 1959, p.9). This shift underscores how monetary systems can transform traditional economic frameworks by altering the functions and roles of currency in social exchanges. Bryceson builds on this insight by highlighting that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social changes, including depeasantization (Bryceson 2002, p.2; Bryceson 2002, p.8). This process involves significant social transformations, as rural populations adapt to new income-earning roles. Despite these challenges, the integration of monetary systems into traditional economies continues to drive long-term economic changes in Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) both argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. For instance, colonial institutions influenced the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa, with British rule being more conducive to educational progress than other forms of colonial administration. This was due to a complex interplay of factors including cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were altered by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These dynamics highlight how the implementation of specific institutional frameworks can drive long-term economic changes. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that while these patterns of growth are tied to foreign trade and export markets, they also vary significantly between countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional contexts in which economic reforms are implemented.

The dynamics of technological advance and social change play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, Feinstein 1998). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and endogenous forces in driving economic convergence. The process involves profound social changes that alter a nation's productivity landscape. This perspective is echoed by Feinstein (1998, p.28), who underscores that real wages and living standards can improve significantly during periods of industrialization, challenging pessimistic views about the Industrial Revolution. These improvements are driven by enhanced technical competence and the development of large-scale enterprise management capabilities, both of which are critical for mobilizing capital effectively.

Abramovitz (1986, p.5) further elaborates that social capability, encompassing political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions, is crucial for economic growth. However, this capability is challenging to quantify, often relying on qualitative assessments rather than precise metrics. This complexity suggests that while technological advance is pivotal, the institutional framework that supports it is equally important. Feinstein (1998, p.5) provides historical context by examining how wars and other factors influenced British economic growth, illustrating that external shocks can either impede or accelerate institutional change and economic development. Technological progress alone does not guarantee sustained growth; it must be accompanied by robust institutions that facilitate adaptation and innovation. This interplay between technological advance and social capability underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic growth, where both technological and institutional factors are intertwined in a dynamic process.

The great divergence in economic performance between Europe and Asia was already underway well before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8), with the maintenance of incomes in European cities playing a crucial role. Broadberry and Gupta argue that merchant distribution and finance were key drivers of productivity growth in northwest Europe, highlighting the importance of the service sector during this period. Parthasarathi attributes the high agricultural productivity in southern India to investment (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Allen's analysis shows that real incomes in European cities remained stable despite fluctuations in grain prices and other commodities, suggesting that economic leaders maintained their living standards through institutional changes (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen 2001, p.23). The gains observed between 1815 and 1850 were part of a cyclical pattern. This cyclicality underscores the complex interplay between economic performance and institutional factors, where short-term fluctuations can mask underlying structural changes. The maintenance of incomes in European cities during this period highlights the role of institutions in supporting economic stability amidst broader economic shifts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their impact on the development trajectories of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, significantly influenced the economic outcomes of these regions. This perspective aligns with Austin's observation (Austin 2008, p.15) that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in shaping the long-term development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The historical evidence shows that institutions influenced economic outcomes and shaped societal structures. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that patterns of growth and decline in many African economies were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, with political institutions playing a crucial role. This aligns with Hopkins' view (Hopkins 2009, p.18) that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but also by the effectiveness of their institutions. The development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule further illustrates how institutional frameworks can foster economic growth by altering cost-benefit calculations for African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15).
