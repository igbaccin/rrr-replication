The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding how institutional frameworks influence the trajectory of economies over extended periods. Two competing positions dominate this debate. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutional factors, arguing that the nature and quality of institutions are pivotal determinants of sustained economic development. In this view, institutions that enforce property rights, ensure rule of law, and promote investment are critical for long-term growth. The opposing position contends that geographic or other exogenous factors play a more significant role in shaping economic outcomes, often overshadowing the impact of institutional frameworks. This disagreement underscores the need to examine how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, affect economic performance over time.

This debate is particularly salient when considering the historical experiences of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the differential outcomes in these regions can be attributed to the institutional legacies left by colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The nature of institutions established during colonization, whether extractive or inclusive, has had profound and lasting effects on economic development. For instance, colonies where European settlers introduced rule-of-law-institutions saw higher long-term growth compared to those where extractive states were established for resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic performance.

The idea that institutional legacies shape long-run economic outcomes is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner's work, which demonstrates that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding underscores how historical institutions influenced economic development trajectories in these regions. Moreover, Frankema and van Waijenburg note that African poverty has not been a persistent phenomenon due to structural impediments but rather fluctuated over time in response to institutional changes and other factors (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This perspective aligns with the view that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes, as they can either facilitate or hinder growth depending on their characteristics.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks depending on the mortality rates they faced. In regions where Europeans experienced high mortality, such as tropical Africa and Central America, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas with lower mortality rates, such as North America and Australia, settlers established institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional design had profound implications for long-run economic development. The persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era has been a significant factor in the economic underperformance of many former colonies. This institutional legacy highlights how colonial policies continue to shape economic trajectories in former colonies, even after political independence has been achieved.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge conventional narratives about economic development by re-evaluating the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. These authors argue that the 'reversal of fortune' oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century.

This reassessment highlights how colonial policies influenced economic trajectories through local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This dynamic suggests that local economic and political action could influence institutional prescriptions, contrary to previous hypotheses. For example, Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) expand on this by noting that patterns of growing and shrinking in export production were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, which varied both between and within countries.

This perspective shifts attention from monolithic institutional frameworks to more nuanced interactions between local conditions and colonial policies. For example, colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which influenced institutional prescriptions. This interaction highlights how economic outcomes were shaped by a complex interplay of factors that went beyond simple institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15).

Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight that colonial rule in Africa was marked by specific educational developments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema 2012, p.15), which shaped local institutions and economic outcomes. The British administration fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers did, as African political leaders and families calculated the benefits of this new social institution. This process altered how Africans perceived the advantages of formal education, influencing their decisions to pursue it. Frankema (2012, p.15) explores a related mechanism:  demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The impact of these educational developments on long-run economic growth is unclear. This finding suggests a more nuanced relationship between colonial institutions, educational developments, and long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) argue that the early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia was driven by productivity gains in traded goods sectors. These gains were facilitated by merchant distribution and finance, in addition to production alone (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The service sector thus played a crucial role in economic growth during this period, similar to its role in modern economic development. This perspective is supported by Allen (2001, p.8), who highlights that real incomes in Europe began to diverge significantly between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.8). However, BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) point out that this divergence was not solely due to institutional changes in Europe. India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was exacerbated by a lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation. This suggests that while productivity gains in traded goods sectors were important, the presence or absence of supportive institutions also played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing illustrates how institutional changes can enhance productivity by protecting property rights and reducing externalities. This technological innovation allowed for more effective demarcation of land boundaries, which in turn facilitated the adoption of more intensive farming practices (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). The ability to secure property rights through fencing led to increased investment in agriculture, as farmers could now safeguard their investments against encroachment by neighboring herds. This mechanism is supported by Bryant's observation that agricultural productivity gains were often tied to improvements in land tenure security and the ability to enforce property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). The introduction of barbed wire fencing protected individual plots and fostered a broader environment conducive to agricultural growth, as farmers could now plan for long-term investments with greater certainty. The impact of these institutional changes varied across different regions. For instance, differences in land-use outcomes between herd-law counties and non-herd law counties highlight how local conditions can modulate the effectiveness of property rights institutions (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports, which ceased detailing herd laws after 1884, suggest that the institutional landscape was dynamic and responsive to technological advancements. This dynamism underscores the importance of adaptive institutions in supporting long-term economic growth, as they can evolve in response to new challenges and opportunities.

Bryant challenges traditional views of economic progress by examining the roles of non-Western societies and women's contributions to economic development (Goldin 2006, p.3; Bryant 2006, p.12). Bryant argues that revisionist scholarship has overlooked crucial institutional differences between Western Europe and other Eurasian societies. These differences have significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes, as Goldin illustrates with the example of women’s education and employment in the U.S.. The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, such as increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century which made "nice" jobs available. These changes also included growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.10). The institutional changes that facilitated these shifts highlight how adaptive institutions can support long-term economic growth by responding to new challenges and opportunities. Bryant's assertion that European political institutions provided an advantageous setting for developmental modernization is not universally accepted (Bryant 2006, p.12). This debate underscores the importance of considering multiple perspectives when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through the lens of colonial strategies and their enduring impacts. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that European colonists established varied institutional frameworks depending on local conditions, with extractive institutions often persisting in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates or sought rapid resource extraction. These institutions, designed to maximize colonial rents rather than long-run growth, underscore the importance of initial institutional setups in determining economic trajectories over centuries (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This is evident in the divergent paths of former colonies like the United States and the Congo, where differing institutional frameworks led to vastly different economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15).

The persistence of these institutions highlights their role in maintaining power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, benefiting politically powerful groups at the expense of broader economic development. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) analysis, which shows how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, further emphasizing the adaptive nature of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in the development of former colonies, illustrating how institutional changes can drive or hinder economic progress (Frankema 2012, p.1). This is evident in the varied levels of per capita income across Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century, demonstrating that many regions had already achieved subsistence levels before independence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2).

The debate on the role of institutions extends to the impact of colonial rule on education and development. Frankema (2012, p.1) shows how British rule was more conducive to formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, highlighting the differential impacts of institutional policies on long-term economic growth. This is further supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who argues that European settlers' presence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production and the adoption of institutional prescriptions that encouraged material interest in the status quo. The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits a shift in relative incomes among former colonies due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, is thus grounded in the adaptive nature of institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15).

The consensus across these studies is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on resource allocation and political dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different colonization strategies with distinct institutional frameworks, which had lasting effects on current income per capita. The debate continues over whether these institutions were universally extractive or if some colonial powers, such as Britain, fostered more development-oriented policies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) settle this by demonstrating that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments but rather a result of specific historical and geographical conditions. The measurement of real wage levels across British African colonies over their period of colonial rule provides empirical evidence for the adaptive nature of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth, showing how institutional changes can drive or hinder economic progress depending on local conditions and political dynamics.
