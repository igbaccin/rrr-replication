The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians alike. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around whether colonial-era institutions, particularly those imposed by European powers, have had enduring impacts on the economic trajectories of former colonies, or if other factors such as geography or cultural differences play a more significant role.

One perspective posits that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has had lasting effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and growth or extractive institutions designed to transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional legacy is seen as a critical factor in explaining current income disparities among former colonies.

The argument about colonial institutions' enduring impact on economic performance is further supported by the work of Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, may be extractive and not conducive to long-run growth. This perspective highlights how institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists have persisted (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty, underscoring the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks in addressing contemporary economic challenges (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Broadberry and Gardner present a different angle by challenging previous characterizations of sub-Saharan Africa's economic performance. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that the region's economic trajectory was more complex than often portrayed (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This view aligns with Frankema’s analysis, which contends that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This indicates that the impact of colonial institutions varied and was not uniformly negative across different regions and contexts (Frankema 2012, p.1).

The complexity of institutional impacts is further illustrated by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood within patterns and paths of institutional evolution going back to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors such as policy experience and investment.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the mortality rates of Europeans in different colonial settings determined whether they established extractive or inclusive institutions. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. In areas with lower mortality rates, like the United States and New Zealand, settlers implemented institutions that enforced rule of law and encouraged investment.

Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these colonial institutions persisted over time, shaping the long-run development trajectories of former colonies. The extractive nature of institutions in high-mortality regions meant that they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote economic growth. This institutional legacy hindered the development of private property and investment, which are critical for sustained economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in the current income per capita levels of former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, Acemoglu et al. note that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), suggesting that while institutional frameworks are crucial, they interact with other factors such as technological advancements and economic policies to shape growth trajectories.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). In Ghana during 1874-1939, this transition was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The abolition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 was a pivotal moment that drove economic changes beyond legal reforms; it also had important implications for the labour market (Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2007, p.20) underscores the importance of this transition, noting that "the loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, which made them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa" (Austin 2007, p.4), illustrating how economic pressures led to institutional adaptations. Colonial administrations often responded to these changes by altering their policies to accommodate new economic realities. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). However, Austin (2007, p.20) notes that this was not always the case; the British emancipation ordinance of 1874 did not directly respond to changing factor scarcities but had significant economic repercussions. This highlights that while institutional changes were driven by economic pressures, they also shaped long-term growth trajectories through complex interactions with colonial policies and local actions. The interplay between these factors underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping African economies during this period.

The introduction of money to African subsistence economies has led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). Bohannan (1959, p.10) describes how the integration of colonial markets disrupted traditional economic institutions among the Tiv people. The influx of alien institutions, such as new road networks and increased trade, fundamentally altered local economies by connecting them to global markets (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This shift was both economic and social, as traditional systems of exchange were disrupted by the introduction of money.

The integration of monetary systems into African subsistence economies led to a complex interplay between economic restructuring and social change. Bryceson (2002, p.2) highlights that neoliberal policies have impacted rural social structures, leading to deep-rooted social transformations. This depeasantization process is evident in the changing roles of women in rural economies, where they increasingly engage in income-earning activities outside the home (Bryceson 2002, p.8). The introduction of money thus altered economic frameworks and restructured social dynamics and gender roles within African communities.

The transformative impact of monetary systems varied across all spheres. Bohannan (1959, p.10) notes that brass rods, a form of general-purpose currency, fulfilled specific functions within the prestige sphere but did not equate to other forms of value, such as rights in women or food (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This suggests that while money facilitated certain types of exchange, it also created new economic disparities and social tensions. The economic restructuring brought about by monetary systems thus had both enabling and constraining effects on traditional African economies.

The transformative impact of monetary systems on traditional African economies highlights the complex interplay between economic restructuring and social change in Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that historical analysis can reveal how specific institutional arrangements have influenced Africa's development trajectory, including the distribution of income and welfare. For example, colonial legacies and post-independence policies have shaped contemporary economic outcomes in ways that can inform current policy debates.

The new economic history of Africa suggests that institutions matter because they create incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective helps to explain why some African countries have experienced rapid growth while others remain mired in poverty. For instance, the presence of "good" institutions can foster an environment conducive to private sector investment and economic diversification. This relationship between institutions and growth is not straightforward; it depends on various factors such as historical context, geographical conditions, and social dynamics.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the persistence of poverty in Africa should be understood through a lens that considers both temporary and structural impediments. They highlight that real wage levels and trends across British African colonies during colonial rule can provide insights into long-term economic development and welfare. This approach underscores the importance of historical data in understanding contemporary economic challenges. Moreover, it suggests that institutional reforms must be tailored to address specific historical and geographical conditions rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach.

This nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history is crucial for designing effective poverty reduction strategies.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) show that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to educational development than other colonial powers. This is due to the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). Moreover, the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era demonstrates that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This suggests that British rule fostered conditions that supported economic growth. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). While this variation indicates that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played crucial roles, it also underscores the complexity of African economies under colonial rule.

The educational and economic developments during British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa highlight the importance of understanding the specific institutional frameworks imposed by different colonial powers. These frameworks shaped educational outcomes and broader economic conditions that influenced long-term development trajectories. This nuanced perspective challenges simplistic views of colonial impacts, emphasizing instead the need for a detailed examination of historical and geographical contexts.

The great divergence in economic performance between Europe and Asia was well underway by 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). This early onset of divergence highlights the importance of historical contexts that predate the Industrial Revolution. Broadberry and Gupta show that high productivity in northwest European traded goods sectors arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as production, emphasizing the role of service sectors in economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The maintenance of incomes in European cities during this period was crucial for the eventual divergence. Allen argues that real income gains observed in mid-19th-century Britain were part of a minor cycle rather than a sustained trend, suggesting long swings reflecting population changes and decisive breaks with past economic patterns (Allen 2001, p.23). Allen's focus on urban centers does not fully account for regional variations or rural economies. Broadberry and Gupta note that the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates could have reinforced economic differences (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that geographic factors played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes during this period. The implications of these findings are that long-term economic growth is influenced by multiple factors including historical context, urban maintenance, and regional variations.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is highlighted by Acemoglu et al.'s work on colonial institutions and their lasting impacts (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The authors argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than purely geographic factors. This perspective underscores how initial institutional frameworks set during colonization can have profound long-term effects on economic development. Broadberry and Gardner build on this framework by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This shows that institutional legacies from colonial rule significantly influenced economic outcomes, even in regions characterized by challenging environmental conditions.

The impact of institutions is further elucidated by Hopkins' analysis of Africa's new economic history (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins emphasizes that the quality of public goods and the rule of law are crucial for development. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that extractive institutions, which prioritize rent extraction over long-run growth, can hinder economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence from both sources underscores that institutional frameworks designed to maximize rents for colonial powers often resulted in suboptimal economic outcomes. This is particularly relevant in contexts where European settlers were absent and indigenous commodity production was encouraged by colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15).

The debate on the role of institutions also involves the effectiveness of different colonial administrations. Frankema argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.1). This suggests that certain institutional practices, such as investing in education, can foster economic growth by enhancing human capital. Acemoglu et al.'s work complements this by showing how the adoption of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment can lead to sustained economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding shows that the quality of institutions is crucial for long-term economic growth.

A central point of agreement among these sources is that institutional frameworks established during colonial rule significantly influence long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights how extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists can impede economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.15). This disagreement is settled by Broadberry and Gardner's evidence showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Their use of national accounting data from the colonial era demonstrates that economic performance was tied to institutional factors, such as political institutions and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).
