The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that engages scholars across multiple disciplines and theoretical frameworks. At stake intellectually is understanding the mechanisms by which institutional structures influence economic performance over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are exogenous determinants of growth or endogenous outcomes shaped by historical, geographic, and political factors. Some scholars argue that the nature of colonial institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, plays a pivotal role in determining long-term economic trajectories. Others emphasize the importance of pre-colonial conditions and the interaction between these conditions and institutional changes introduced during colonialism. The extent to which current economic outcomes are path-dependent, shaped by historical institutional legacies, remains a contentious issue.

One stream focuses on the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions ranged from inclusive ones that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed for rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies, which in turn influenced the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) . These studies highlight that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not promote long-term economic growth, illustrating how historical institutional choices have lasting effects on economic outcomes. This stream argues that colonial institutions are a primary driver of current economic disparities among former colonies.

Another stream explores the role of pre-colonial conditions and indigenous economic action in shaping institutional development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions, imposing uniform extractive institutions across all colonies. For instance, colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This stream underscores the importance of considering pre-colonial conditions and indigenous agency in understanding the development of institutional structures during colonialism.

Yet another line of inquiry examines how historical economic data can inform our understanding of long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This perspective highlights the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, which can be used to reconstruct historical trends in economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg also emphasize the importance of understanding real wage levels and trends across British Africa as a way to assess the possibilities of future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). These studies suggest that historical economic data provides valuable insights into the long-term effects of institutional changes on economic outcomes, offering a more nuanced view of African economic development. This stream argues that historical economic data is crucial for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Acemoglu et al. illustrate this dynamic through their comparative analysis of settlement patterns and institutional outcomes in different colonies. This contrasts sharply with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutional differences had profound effects on current income per capita, highlighting how colonial legacies continue to influence economic development trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was intricately linked to industrialization opportunities during the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interplay suggests that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists were a critical factor in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The legacy of these extractive institutions persists, influencing contemporary economic development and underscoring the enduring significance of historical institutional changes.

Broadberry and Gardner share with BroadberryEtal that economic performance varied greatly across regions during specific historical periods (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This divergence is particularly evident in the patterns of growth and decline tied to foreign trade and export markets. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner highlight how growth in export production was influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), demonstrating that external factors significantly shaped economic outcomes. Allen's work complements this perspective by emphasizing that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century had roots in earlier periods, notably between 1500 and 1750 when incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in leading economies (Allen 2001, p.8). This suggests that long-term economic trends are deeply influenced by both local conditions and broader economic dynamics. BroadberryEtal caution that institutional factors, such as the absence of state institutions needed to support investment and innovation, played a crucial role in India's decline relative to Britain (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16), illustrating how specific institutional deficiencies can hinder economic progress over extended periods.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights into institutional reforms that could alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by ethnic fragmentation and the effectiveness of institutions. This perspective is crucial because it shifts focus from cultural determinants to institutional ones, suggesting that even ethnically homogeneous societies can develop progressive institutions if they prioritize good governance and rule of law. This divergence underscores the importance of historical context in shaping current institutional frameworks. They emphasize the need for detailed studies on long-term economic development, including income distribution, to assess future growth possibilities and understand the impact of historical or geographical factors on African welfare. This approach suggests that by identifying and addressing these specific conditions, Africa can achieve sustainable economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The institutional changes in Africa varied across regions and time, shaped by local conditions and responses to factor scarcity. For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4; Austin 2007, p.20). The colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic and political action further influenced these institutional changes. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15).

This nuanced view of institutional change highlights the importance of local contexts and specific historical conditions in shaping long-run economic growth. Colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic and political action varied based on local incentives and actions. This variability suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to understanding institutional change and its impact on economic growth is insufficient. Instead, detailed studies of specific regions and periods are necessary to grasp the complex interplay between institutions, historical conditions, and economic outcomes. The institutional changes in Africa were shaped by local dynamics and adaptations (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20).

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). The adoption of barbed wire technology allowed for more effective enclosure of farmland, mitigating the challenges posed by open-range grazing. This technological innovation facilitated a shift in land management practices, enabling farmers to invest more confidently in improvements to their property. This institutional adaptation is consistent with Hornbeck's findings that the introduction of barbed wire coincided with significant increases in improved farmland, suggesting a direct link between technological innovation and institutional change. The effectiveness of these changes varied across all regions. The variability in land-use outcomes indicates that local conditions and adaptations played a crucial role in shaping the impact of new technologies on agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This underscores the importance of considering regional-specific factors when analyzing the relationship between institutional change and economic growth.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies has led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). Bohannan (1959, p.10) illustrates this through the Tiv people's experience, where the advent of colonial rule brought new trade routes and markets, integrating local economic institutions with the global economy. This integration was economic and social, as traditional exchange systems were disrupted by the introduction of alien institutions. Bryceson (2002, p.2) extends this analysis by highlighting that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social change and depeasantization. The shift from subsistence farming to cash-based economies transformed rural social structures, with women increasingly taking on income-earning roles outside the home. Bryceson (2002, p.2) notes that while international financial institutions and donors have recognized the importance of the noneconomic sphere, there has been a reluctance to consider how neoliberal policies impact African rural social structures. This gap in understanding underscores the need for a more holistic approach to economic development that acknowledges both the economic and social dimensions of change (Bryceson 2002, p.8).

Bryant (2006, p.12); (Goldin 2006, p.3) examine how economic and social transformations can be driven by institutional changes. The rise of Western European powers was contingent on the evolution of political institutions that fostered modernization in Europe. These institutions facilitated coercive and productive power, enabling Europe's ascent to global hegemony in the early modern period (Bryant 2006, p.1). Goldin elaborates on this dynamic by showing how exogenous changes reshaped women’s employment and education during the Industrial Revolution. Institutional shifts made part-time work accessible and removed barriers for married women, enhancing their labor supply (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation was not merely economic; it involved profound social changes in household production technology and educational institutions. Feinstein further underscores this point by demonstrating that real wages improved during the Industrial Revolution, reflecting a higher standard of living (Feinstein 1998, p.5). Goldin's analysis also reveals that these transformations varied across all sectors. The quiet revolution in women’s employment and education highlights how specific institutional changes can have varied impacts on different segments of society (Goldin 2006, p.10).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and encourage investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita; this effect was due to the institutional frameworks set up by European colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical context of colonial rule highlights how different colonization strategies led to varied economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that in some colonies, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they set up extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; he underscores how colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This adaptation shows that institutions evolved in response to local conditions, further influencing long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, showing that it was related to industrialization and the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Broadberry and Gardner reveal that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that institutional frameworks were already influencing economic outcomes well before decolonization. This historical evidence suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run growth was a continuous process shaped by colonial policies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had lasting effects on economic development in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions were likely designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth; this institutional design has persisted and continues to affect current economic conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Hopkins highlights how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This shows that understanding the historical role of institutions is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at reducing poverty and promoting long-run growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is agreed upon: they influence development by enforcing property rights, encouraging investment, and setting up frameworks that maximize rents to colonizers. The disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutional effects are due to colonial policies or local conditions (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins shows that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still be capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions; this challenges the notion that ethnic fragmentation inherently leads to poor economic outcomes. Thus, the historical evidence of colonial institutional frameworks and their long-term effects settles the disagreement by demonstrating that both colonial policies and local adaptations have played significant roles in shaping long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
