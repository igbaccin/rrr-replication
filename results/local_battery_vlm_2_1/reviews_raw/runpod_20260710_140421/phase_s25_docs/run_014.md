The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics and economic history. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, or if they merely reflect deeper underlying factors such as geography, culture, or initial endowments. This debate pits those who argue that institutional quality directly influences economic outcomes against those who see institutions as secondary to other determinants. The disagreement centres on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they can be effectively engineered for development.

The literature on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is divided between those who emphasize the causal power of institutions and those who view them as less determinative. On one side, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The authors emphasize the role of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, where Europeans established states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole rather than fostering local economic development. On the other side, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The paper suggests that European settler presence was not a uniform determinant of institutional quality. Instead, colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political dynamics.

The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how institutional reforms can alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective challenges the notion that ethnic heterogeneity inherently leads to poor institutions and economic underdevelopment. Instead, it suggests that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also contribute to this stream by arguing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Their work highlights the importance of understanding long-term economic development trends in Africa, including the distribution of income. This perspective underscores the need for deeper insights into the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by studies that examine the relationship between institutional changes introduced by colonialism and subsequent economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective emphasizes the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by Europeans, highlighting how these factors shaped the long-run development trajectories of former colonies. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) contribute to this stream by arguing that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This perspective underscores the crucial role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period, suggesting that institutional changes brought about investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has had persistent effects on economic development. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These extractive institutions, characterized by poor property rights and weak rule of law, hindered investment and productivity gains that could have driven economic growth in the colonies over time. For example, Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on whether they intended to settle permanently or merely extract resources (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions shaped the long-term development trajectories of former colonies by influencing their ability to industrialize and achieve sustained economic growth.

This institutional legacy is evident in the stark differences in income levels among former colonies today. The persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies has limited their ability to achieve high levels of economic growth, trapping them in a cycle of poverty. The institutional framework established by colonial powers continues to shape economic outcomes in these regions, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on long-run development trajectories.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Domar (1970, p.4) share that institutions protecting property rights can significantly enhance economic development in agricultural sectors. They argue that the introduction of barbed wire fencing allowed farmers to better protect their land from livestock damage, which was a critical externality on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This institutional change enabled more efficient use of farmland. Farmers in herd-law counties increased improved farmland by 20 percentage points after barbed wire's introduction. He argues that when governments facilitate the creation of a non-working class of agricultural owners by granting them sole ownership rights over land, it can lead to significant changes in agricultural production and development (Domar 1970, p.4; Domar 1970, p.12). The introduction of barbed wire fencing altered how farmers managed their land and influenced the broader economic landscape by reducing externalities and enhancing property protection. This institutional innovation illustrates a mechanism by which property rights can drive long-term economic growth in agricultural settings.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrate that institutional changes in Africa during colonial rule were driven by shifts in resource availability and local economic conditions (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). These shifts led to the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor, illustrating how institutional adaptations can drive long-term economic growth. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the introduction of British colonial rule, which integrated markets by removing tariffs and administrative barriers within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4). This market integration facilitated the development of commodity production, as local economies adjusted to new factor scarcities.

Institutional responses varied across different regions. In areas where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often supported indigenous commodity production to secure fiscal stability and align with local economic interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutional changes in Africa were influenced by both external colonial policies and internal economic pressures, leading to varied outcomes depending on local conditions.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights for institutional reforms aimed at alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, highlighting that the quality of public goods provision is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation. This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) argument that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that current institutional frameworks can be adapted to promote economic growth. The focus on 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment essential for development, underscores the importance of effective governance in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that a deeper understanding of long-term economic development is necessary to assess future growth possibilities. The new economic history of Africa can therefore inform targeted institutional reforms that address poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa by focusing on governance structures that promote investment.

BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) and Allen et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) examine economic divergence through various historical and regional factors without explicitly linking these to institutional frameworks. They establish that the economic decline of India relative to Britain began during the early modern period, a trend that cannot be solely attributed to colonialism. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) highlight that "India lacked the state institutions needed to underpin the investment and innovation which allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap" (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This lack of institutional support is a critical factor in understanding India's economic trajectory, as it prevented the necessary investment and innovation for sustained growth. Allen et al. provide further context by showing that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, indicating a broader pattern of economic stagnation across Asia during this period (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This highlights the importance of institutional factors, such as merchant networks and financial systems, in driving economic development. However, BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) qualify the impact of these institutions by noting that the Mughal Empire's revenue declined significantly during its collapse, which had negative consequences for agricultural labour productivity (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This decline underscores how institutional changes can have cascading effects on various sectors of an economy.

Bryant (2006, p.1) and Goldin (2006, p.3) challenge traditional Eurocentric views of historical progress by highlighting alternative perspectives on societal advancement. They jointly establish that the transformation in women's economic roles was driven by exogenous changes, such as shifts in labor demand and technological advancements. The increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century made 'nice' jobs available (Goldin 2006, p.3), while changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s altered female labor supply. These factors, coupled with institutional changes that facilitated part-time work and removed barriers to married women's employment, significantly reshaped women's economic participation. Bryant (2006, p.12) qualifies this perspective by noting that revisionist scholarship challenging Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. Bryant points out that while Goody asserts the existence of 'democratic' regimes in the East, his references are limited to ancient Mesopotamia and India, which may not fully capture the broader historical context. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional changes and their varied impacts on economic development across different regions and time periods.

The shift in women's education around 1970 further illustrates this complexity (Goldin 2006, p.10). The significant increase in women entering professional and graduate schools during this period highlights how educational institutions can drive long-term economic growth by enhancing human capital. The varied experiences of different societies suggest that the impact of such institutional changes depends on broader historical and cultural contexts.

The interplay between exogenous factors and institutional changes in shaping women's economic roles offers a nuanced understanding of long-run economic growth, one that acknowledges the multifaceted nature of societal advancement.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with historical and cultural contexts, which influence how institutional changes impact societal advancement. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to varying economic outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled and created institutions enforcing the rule of law, long-term economic growth was encouraged (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In extractive states like the Congo or Gold Coast, resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole, hindering economic development. This dynamic highlights how initial institutional frameworks set by colonial powers significantly influenced future economic trajectories. Austin also emphasizes that European settlers' presence shaped local economies and institutions, with colonial administrations adapting their policies based on indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

Acemoglu et al. further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. These institutional legacies persist because they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Hopkins' assertion that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins highlights the importance of understanding Africa's economic history to inform current institutional reforms and reduce poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Broadberry and Gupta support this view by showing that high productivity in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, underscoring the role of institutions in fostering economic growth. They argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, with institutional changes driving investment and agricultural productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9).

The shared finding is that institutions play a crucial role in long-run economic growth by shaping incentives for investment and development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions operate remain debated. While Acemoglu et al. emphasize the extractive nature of colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), Hopkins points out that good institutions provide incentives for investment, essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence from real wage levels and trends in British African colonies during their period of colonial rule shows that specific structural factors have had a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development. This empirical comparison reveals that the persistence of poverty is not an inevitable consequence of structural impediments but rather a result of specific historical and institutional factors (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).
