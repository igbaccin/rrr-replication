The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has sparked debates among economists and historians alike. At the heart of this controversy lies the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or inclusive, determines a nation's economic trajectory, with inclusive institutions fostering growth and extractive ones hindering it (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). Others contend that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent on other factors such as geography, culture, and historical context. This disagreement underscores the need to delve into how different institutional frameworks have influenced economic outcomes over time.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the lasting effects of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. contend that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions, as seen in settler colonies like the United States and Australia, encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, thereby promoting economic development. Conversely, extractive institutions, prevalent in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, stifling local growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). Austin challenges this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic but evolved in response to indigenous economic and political actions, with some colonial administrations adapting their policies to encourage commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation indicates a more nuanced relationship between colonial institutions and economic outcomes, where local dynamics played a crucial role in shaping institutional evolution. The debate highlights the complexity of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The interplay between institutions and economic development is further illustrated by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that this reversal was primarily driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). They emphasize that equilibrium institutions in many former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Broadberry and Gardner provide a counterpoint by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance and suggests that institutional factors alone do not fully explain economic outcomes in these regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The debate underscores the need to consider multiple factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, when examining long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic development is also evident in the context of agricultural modernization. Hornbeck demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This example illustrates how institutional changes, such as the enforcement of property rights, can drive economic growth by creating a more stable and predictable environment for investment. Broadberry and Gardner's work on Sub-Saharan Africa further supports this idea, highlighting that export production in many African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This interaction underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes, even in regions with diverse historical and geographic contexts.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled. While some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions, others argue for a more nuanced understanding that considers multiple factors. The evidence from colonial histories, agricultural modernization, and regional economic development all point to the critical role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth. These studies also highlight the complexity of institutional change and its interaction with other factors such as geography, culture, and historical context. This interplay suggests that while institutions are crucial, they operate within a broader framework of economic and social dynamics.

The Europeans adopted different colonization strategies which resulted in distinct institutional frameworks; these were tailored to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than promote long-term growth. These extractive institutions persisted over time and had a profound impact on current income per capita in these regions. One specific mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes is the establishment of extractive states by Europeans in colonies with high mortality rates. This strategy was designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, leading to long-term negative consequences for the colonized regions. These institutions were not designed to encourage economic development but rather to maximize short-term gains for the colonizers. This perspective is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who emphasize the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies, which in turn influenced the institutional frameworks established in colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in many parts of the world today, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic growth.

Austin argues that historical shifts in land abundance led to changes in economic institutions in Ghana (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2). His work highlights how specific historical contexts shaped economic institutions. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 in the Gold Coast Colony, which later became Ghana, marked a significant institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin argues that this transition was driven by the abolition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti, which had important economic dimensions as it changed labour dynamics. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 played a crucial role in driving Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional change was induced by changing factor scarcities, illustrating how historical contexts can shape economic institutions. This indicates that institutional changes in response to local conditions could lead to economic growth, even under colonial rule.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Domar (1970, p.4) show that property rights are critical to economic development, as they enable farmers to protect their investments and reduce externalities. This suggests that well-defined property rights can drive economic development by encouraging investment in agriculture.

The relationship between property rights and economic growth is complex; it can be influenced by other factors such as government policy and social structure. For example, Domar (1970, p.12) discusses how governments can facilitate the creation of a non-working class of agricultural owners, which in turn shapes the institutional structure. This highlights that while property rights are crucial for economic development, their impact can be modulated by broader political and social contexts.

The historical context of economic divergence between Europe and Asia reveals that the great divergence was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). The maintenance of incomes in European cities during this period is critical to understanding long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta emphasize merchant distribution and finance as key drivers of productivity in Europe's traded goods sector, highlighting that the service sector played a crucial role in early modern economic growth; Allen supports by noting that real wages in British cities were maintained despite economic development gathering strength in the seventeenth century (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Allen 2001, p.23). This suggests that while institutional change is often seen as driving investment in agriculture and leading to high agricultural labour productivity, it was also instrumental in maintaining urban living standards through diverse mechanisms. These findings imply that institutions' role in shaping economic growth extends beyond direct impacts on production; they also influence the distribution of economic benefits across different sectors and populations.

These insights into institutional change and its multifaceted impact on economic growth suggest a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic performance. Specifically, this indicates that institutions can facilitate economic development by influencing the service sector's role in distributing goods and maintaining living standards, not just through direct impacts on agricultural productivity. This perspective underscores the importance of considering both production and distribution mechanisms when examining the relationship between institutions and economic growth, as it highlights how institutions can drive economic development by encouraging investment in multiple sectors.

Abramovitz's exploration of economic growth and technological advancement highlights that these processes are influenced by various factors including social change and technological dynamics (Bryant 2006, p.12; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex, driven not only by exogenous factors but also by the intricate interplay of technological advance and social change. This perspective underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development, where institutions play a role alongside other influences. Bryant (2006, p.12) further elaborates on this complexity by examining the institutional settings that support developmental modernization. Bryant (2006, p.12) suggests that European political institutions provided an advantageous setting for such modernization, though he acknowledges that historical differences have been overdrawn (Bryant 2006, p.1). The shared claim of Abramovitz and Bryant is that institutional factors are interwoven with broader social and technological dynamics in shaping economic growth. This framework implies that the role of institutions in economic development is intertwined with other societal changes. This underscores a nuanced view where institutional effects are context-dependent and require careful consideration of historical and social dynamics.

Feinstein (1998, p.28) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) focus on improvements in real wages and the standard of living during and after the Industrial Revolution, without directly addressing how institutions might have influenced this outcome (Feinstein 1998, p.28; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Feinstein's analysis highlights that the improvement in British workers' living standards was significant despite various economic challenges. This suggests that structural changes in the economy, such as increased productivity and industrialization, played a crucial role in enhancing real wages. Allen et al. corroborate this view by showing that wage rates can serve as a proxy for broader economic conditions, indicating that labor market dynamics were integral to improvements in living standards (Feinstein 1998, p.5). The shared finding is that the Industrial Revolution brought about substantial economic gains, particularly in terms of real wages and living standards. However, Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that these improvements were not uniform across regions or socio-economic groups, as evidenced by variations in wage rates and labor market conditions (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This qualification underscores the importance of regional economic integration and the role of local labor markets in shaping wage outcomes.

The implication is that while broad economic trends drove improvements in living standards, local factors moderated these effects.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities within societies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder economic development. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political actions, thereby influencing long-term growth trajectories. These findings highlight the importance of understanding how institutions evolve in response to changing economic conditions and power dynamics.

Moreover, the role of institutions extends beyond mere economic efficiency; they also shape social structures and labor markets. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was tied to industrialization and institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This aligns with Austin's (2008, p.2) observation that European settlers' presence significantly influenced the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, thereby fostering economic growth. These insights underscore the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.

The broader implications of these findings are profound. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that institutional factors were at play well before modern economic development theories began to take shape. This is further supported by Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.15) analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia, which shows that high productivity in traded goods sectors was crucial for economic growth during the early modern period. These studies collectively show that institutions are pivotal in determining long-run economic trajectories.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions significantly influence long-run economic growth by shaping resource allocation and economic opportunities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement centers on whether institutional changes were primarily driven by exogenous factors such as colonial policies or endogenous factors like local economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This disagreement is settled by the specific historical evidence of how colonial administrations adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic conditions, as documented by Austin. The empirical comparison of different colonial strategies and their long-term economic impacts, as shown by Acemoglu et al. provides a clear measurement of this effect.
