The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate. At stake intellectually is an understanding of why some nations thrive economically while others lag behind, with implications for policy-making and development strategies. The disagreement centers on the extent to which institutional frameworks, shaped by historical and colonial influences, determine economic trajectories. One key position argues that institutions designed to maximize rents for colonizers often hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Another position suggests that the impact of colonial rule varied significantly depending on the specific policies and institutional changes introduced by different colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The tension between these views underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic development, where historical context and policy specifics are crucial. This debate extends to the nuances of how institutions interact with other factors such as population density, prosperity, and the opportunity for industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), shaping the long-term economic prospects of former colonies.

The influence of European policies on colonial institutions, as detailed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33), highlights how external forces can shape the economic trajectory of former colonies. The specific strategies adopted by Europeans, whether they settled and established inclusive institutions or set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources, had profound effects on the institutional frameworks of these colonies, ultimately shaping their economic trajectories. These strategies varied based on factors such as mortality rates among European colonizers. This pattern was evident in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where Europeans implemented policies that prioritized immediate gains over sustainable development. Regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, saw the establishment of more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutional differences were crucial in determining the long-term economic outcomes of former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which interacted with local conditions such as population density and industrialization opportunities. This interaction underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) shows that Ghana experienced an institutional revolution in factor markets during 1874-1939 (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This revolution was marked by significant changes in land use rights and the emergence of hired labour markets. The shift from abundant to scarce local land resources drove chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2007, p.20) further notes that British rule had market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional change facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, opting for higher production equilibria when it suited their interests (Austin 2008, p.15). Thus, while the institutional revolution in factor markets was driven by localised scarcity and colonial policies, the specific outcomes were also shaped by the adaptive nature of colonial governance. This dynamic interplay between local conditions and colonial policies highlights the complex role institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Hornbeck (2010, p.8) establish that property rights institutions can catalyse agricultural development by reducing externalities from livestock damage. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their land, delineate boundaries, and mitigate disputes over grazing rights. This technological innovation significantly enhanced agricultural productivity by providing a stable framework for investment in land improvements. For example, herd-law counties experienced a notable increase in improved farmland after the adoption of barbed wire, with a 20 percentage point rise from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This period marked a significant shift in agricultural development, as farmers could now invest more confidently in their land.

Despite the extensive literature on African economic history and development, there remains a gap in directly linking institutional frameworks to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa has begun to inform institutional reforms, shifting focus towards "good" institutions that provide incentives for investment. This emphasis on institutions aligns with broader development policy shifts in the 1990s, where governance and organizational structures gained prominence (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The role of institutions is crucial because they shape the environment in which economic activities occur, influencing everything from property rights to regulatory frameworks.

This development includes a deeper understanding of how precolonial production systems laid the groundwork for modern economic structures (Hopkins 2019, p.13). The importance of multiple approaches and techniques in studying economic development underscores the need for comprehensive institutional frameworks that can adapt to diverse historical contexts. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness depends on contextual factors such as cultural heterogeneity and historical legacies.

The implications of this literature point towards a dynamic relationship between institutions and economic growth in Africa. This underscores the need for continued research and policy interventions that recognize the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in regions with complex historical and cultural landscapes.

The Great Divergence between regions like Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, driven by factors that influenced real incomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue for the high productivity in north-western Europe's traded goods sector, noting that merchant distribution and finance played a crucial role. This aligns with Allen’s (2001) findings on urban real incomes during this period. The productivity gains in Europe were unevenly distributed among workers of different skill levels (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). BroadberryEtal further explain that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period, long before colonial rule. This decline was marked by a collapse in revenue and a lack of state institutions needed for investment and innovation (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) also highlight that India's workers remained predominantly agrarian, which had negative consequences for agricultural labour productivity and the relative size of the industrial and service sectors. The absence of robust state institutions in India during this period is a critical factor in understanding its economic trajectory compared to Britain (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This institutional weakness meant that India did not have the necessary foundations to break out of the Malthusian trap, where population growth was constrained by limited resources and low productivity.

The impact of British colonial rule on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa was more positive than that of other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education for both African leaders and families. This shift fostered a greater demand for missionary education, as local communities recognised the potential advantages of acquiring formal skills within the colonial framework. This educational development was not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa; instead it varied according to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variation in growth patterns of export production and related economic activities underscores the complex interplay between colonial policies and indigenous responses.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Frankema 2012, p.1). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, which significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions ranged from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This variation in institutional design highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and indigenous responses, which Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates by noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political actions. For instance, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior underscores the dynamic nature of institutional change in response to local conditions.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that this reversal was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), who note that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. These economies benefited from British rule, which fostered educational development and economic activities tied to foreign trade and export markets (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The variation in growth patterns of export production and related economic activities further underscores the complex interplay between colonial policies and indigenous responses.

The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not promote long-run growth. Instead, they created extractive states that hindered economic development. This is evident in the case of the Congo or the Gold Coast, where institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole rather than invest in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education and economic activities tied to export markets. This highlights the importance of institutional design in shaping long-run economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in per capita income levels across former European colonies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies achieved higher per capita income levels by the early twentieth century, demonstrating the economic benefits derived from British rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). This historical comparison provides a clear measurement of how different institutional frameworks influenced long-run economic growth.
