The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures and governance systems influence economic trajectories over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes driven by historical events, political dynamics, and strategic choices. Some scholars argue that institutions' impact on growth is contingent upon their ability to foster investment and secure property rights (Austin 2008, p.2). Others contend that the type of institutional framework, whether extractive or inclusive, determines economic outcomes over the long run (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The nature of colonial institutions, particularly those imposed by European powers, has been a focal point in this debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These studies indicate that extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which aimed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote long-run growth, have had lasting effects on current economic performance. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutional frameworks can persist and shape contemporary economic conditions.

The legacy of colonial rule is further explored by Frankema (2012, p.15), who argues that British colonial rule was more conducive to formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This finding suggests that the type of colonial institution matters significantly, as it can influence critical factors such as education and human capital development. The influence of these institutions goes beyond educational outcomes; it affects broader economic development pathways. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide evidence that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that institutional frameworks established during colonial rule played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The influence of colonial institutions on economic growth extends beyond Africa, as seen in the significant impact of European policies on former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the colonization strategies adopted by Europeans varied widely, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns.

This institutional design was a direct result of the political power dynamics between colonizers and local populations, where the former sought to extract resources efficiently while maintaining control (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The impact of these institutions on economic development is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, which can be traced back to the industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. This perspective underscores the enduring legacy of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, suggesting that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule continue to influence contemporary economic performance.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This institutional change allowed farmers to reduce externalities from livestock damage and thereby increase productivity. The effectiveness of property rights in driving economic growth depends on the broader institutional framework. For instance, Domar (1970, p.4) argues that serfdom emerges when labor scarcity increases pressure on land, suggesting that the benefits of secure property rights may be offset by other institutional factors such as labor market conditions (Domar 1970, p.12). This highlights how specific institutional changes can drive economic growth in agriculture.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, as shown by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). The new economic history of Africa sheds light on how specific institutions have shaped long-run economic outcomes, offering insights into how targeted reforms could alleviate poverty. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This focus on institutional quality aligns with the broader theme that effective institutions are crucial for fostering economic growth. They argue that a better grasp of historical and geographical conditions can help assess future growth possibilities. This historical perspective is vital because it reveals how specific institutional changes have influenced African economies over time. For example, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This finding underscores that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily impede economic development; rather, it is the quality of institutions that matters most. Frankema and van Waijenburg's call to explore determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa implies that a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics is necessary for effective poverty reduction strategies. Future research should build on these insights by examining how specific historical conditions have shaped contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

Austin examines how shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes in Ghana during 1874-1939 (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This dynamic is evident in the transition from coerced to free labour and the emergence of markets for land use rights. The shift from abundant to scarce land resources drove a transformation in factor markets, as local chiefs increasingly sold land to strangers to farm cocoa after being driven into debt by the loss of slaves (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional change was further facilitated by British colonial policies such as the 1874 emancipation ordinance and the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908. These changes indicate that local economic conditions, not exogenous policy imposition, were crucial to institutional innovation.

The integration of markets through administrative measures also played a significant role in shaping Ghana's economic landscape during this period. The removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony under British rule highlights how colonial policies could integrate markets (Austin 2007, p.4). Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in maintaining the status quo, which led them to change institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic illustrates how local economic conditions influenced colonial governance, leading to a higher production equilibrium than might otherwise have been expected (Austin 2008, p.15). The interplay between land scarcity, labour market changes, and colonial policies underscores the complex nature of institutional development in Ghana during this period.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies led to profound changes and shifted these economies away from multicentric structures, as jointly shown by Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Frankema (2012, p.15). The shift towards monetization was driven by both the establishment of new trade routes and markets, as well as the integration of local economic institutions with global systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15). The introduction of money into African subsistence economies altered the benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This change resulted from a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, who responded to new social and political institutions under colonial rule (Frankema 2012, p.1). The integration of local economic systems with global markets led to the development of new exchange networks that altered traditional economic practices in Northern Nigeria. This process was facilitated by trading companies that introduced governing powers and alien institutions into the region (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The transition from a multicentric economy to one based on monetary exchange was complex, as brass rods fulfilled only limited functions within the prestige sphere of Tiv society; equivalency between these rods and other rights or goods could not exist (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This complexity highlights how institutional changes under colonial rule transformed economic structures in Africa.

Economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was tied to foreign trade and export markets, as jointly asserted by BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This dynamic is further supported by BroadberryEtal's observation that the absence of state institutions capable of underpinning investment and innovation in India hindered its ability to break out of the Malthusian trap (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16), despite periods of growth tied to foreign trade. The role of merchant distribution and finance was crucial for economic development during the early modern period, as high productivity in traded goods sectors could drive overall economic performance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This highlights the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth through trade.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia is evident in long-run trends of real wages and living standards, as jointly argued by Bryant (2006, p.12) and Allen et al. (Bryant 2006, p.12; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This divergence was driven by institutional developments in Europe that enhanced coercive and productive power, as well as by the vibrant labour markets in early modern China. These include political institutions that provided an advantageous setting for developmental modernization, which allowed Europe to achieve greatly enhanced capacities in coercive and productive power.

The economic progress of Britain during and after the Industrial Revolution is a notable example of this divergence. Feinstein (1998, p.28) argues that real wages and living standards improved significantly in Britain during this period (Feinstein 1998, p.28). This improvement is evident from quantitative estimates of wages and prices, which reveal a sustained increase in British economic growth despite various factors such as wars (Feinstein 1998, p.5). Allen et al. (2011, p.22) show that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This stagnation resulted from institutional and economic continuities that hindered wage growth.

The implications of these findings are significant for understanding long-run economic growth. The divergence in real wages between Europe and Asia highlights the importance of institutional developments in shaping economic outcomes. While Europe's political institutions facilitated modernization and economic growth, China's stagnation suggests that institutional factors played a crucial role in limiting economic progress. This underscores the need to consider the historical context and specific institutional developments when analyzing long-run economic growth.
