The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centering on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if they merely facilitate growth initiated by other factors. The crux of this disagreement lies in understanding which causal mechanisms are most influential. One position posits that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Another perspective, however, argues that the impact of institutions is contingent on broader historical and geographical contexts, suggesting that their effects can be overstated when considered in isolation from other factors (Austin 2008, p.15).

This tension underscores a fundamental question: how do institutions interact with other variables to produce long-term economic outcomes? Addressing this question requires examining both the direct effects of institutional frameworks and their interactions with historical legacies. This review synthesizes evidence on the causal role of institutions in shaping long-run growth, focusing on how different institutional arrangements have influenced economic trajectories across various regions.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in determining the economic paths of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). European settlers established distinct institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and population density, which significantly impacted long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions ranged from extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole to more inclusive ones that enforced property rights and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

This perspective is supported by Austin's work, which highlights the importance of institutional evolution in shaping economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). For instance, colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating the dynamic nature of institutional change (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction between institutions and local contexts underscores the complexity of institutional effects on long-run growth.

The mortality rates of Europeans in colonized territories determined the type of institutional framework established. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, while low mortality rates fostered settler institutions that encouraged investment and enforced property rights. These institutional differences persisted over time and significantly affected current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these colonial institutions is crucial as it highlights how initial conditions set by European colonizers shaped long-term economic trajectories. For instance, Acemoglu et al. document how population density and prosperity in colonies interacted with European policies to shape institutional outcomes. This interaction underscores the complexity of colonial institutions' effects on economic growth, as local factors could modify or resist the imposed institutional frameworks.

Frankema argues that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education development more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This is evident in the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it a more attractive investment. Austin (2008, p.2) further qualifies this impact, noting that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which in turn influenced institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.1). This dynamic highlights how local economic and political actions could shape colonial policies, demonstrating the complex interplay between external institutions and internal responses. The nuanced view of colonial impacts on education thus suggests that while British rule had some beneficial effects on formal education development in sub-Saharan Africa, these benefits were contingent upon specific local conditions and interactions with indigenous populations. This interaction underscores the importance of understanding how colonial institutions adapted to local contexts, influencing long-run economic growth through educational outcomes.

The early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with significant differences in real incomes observed between European cities and their Asian counterparts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). This divergence was driven by the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Allen notes that the maintenance of high real wages in European cities after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century was a decisive break with the past, suggesting that institutional changes led to sustained investment in agriculture and high agricultural labor productivity (Allen 2001, p.23).

Institutional factors thus played a pivotal role in determining long-run economic growth. The service sector's influence on economic growth during the early modern period mirrors its role in the modern period, indicating a continuity in the importance of institutions over time. Broadberry and Gupta argue that high levels of investment in southern Indian agriculture during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries were crucial for maintaining productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This investment was driven by institutional changes that fostered agricultural development, leading to both high levels of economic development and low silver wages. The reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in a warm climate further reinforced this effect, highlighting how geographical factors interacted with institutions to shape economic outcomes. The gains from 1815 to 1850 were minor cycles that did not constitute long-term trends (Allen 2001, p.23). This indicates that while institutional changes can drive significant economic development, their effects are not always sustained over the long term.

The economic decline in India and China during the early modern period is examined through various lenses, including revenue fluctuations and labor productivity (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Broadberry et al. (2015) observe that India's revenue declined significantly after the collapse of the Mughal Empire in the mid-eighteenth century, highlighting a critical institutional failure. This decline began under the Mughal Empire and continued through colonial rule (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). The absence of state institutions capable of fostering investment and innovation exacerbated this economic downturn. Allen et al. note that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, suggesting a broader pattern of economic stagnation driven by institutional constraints on productivity growth (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). The lack of robust institutions hindered both India and China's ability to break out of the Malthusian trap, where population growth and per capita incomes remained intertwined. Thus, the absence of effective institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes in both countries by limiting productivity gains and hindering structural transformation. This dynamic underscores how institutional failures can perpetuate economic decline over extended periods, even in the absence of exogenous shocks like colonialism.

The institutional revolution in factor markets during periods of shifting land abundance and scarcity led to significant structural changes in economic systems (Austin 2007, p.20; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). Austin (2007, p.20) demonstrates that the transition from coerced to free labor in Ghana during 1874-1939 was driven by localised scarcity of land, which prompted chiefs to sell land and adopt hired labor. Similarly, Hornbeck (2010, p.32) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage by 1880. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) notes that these institutional changes took time to manifest fully.

The downstream implication of these institutional changes is that they facilitated structural transformation by enabling more efficient use of resources. These shifts in factor markets allowed for greater economic efficiency and productivity gains. Austin (2007, p.20) highlights how the integration of British rule in Ghana removed tariffs and administrative barriers, further integrating markets (Austin 2007, p.4). Yet, the relationship between these institutional changes and long-run economic growth is complex; while they enabled structural transformation, they do not directly address growth. This complexity underscores that while institutional changes in factor markets are crucial for resource allocation and productivity, their impact on sustained economic growth over extended periods remains less direct.

Women have been instrumental in driving economic growth and development, both historically and contemporarily (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show how women's contributions to agriculture and labor markets have propelled economic transformations. In the early twentieth century, increased demand for female office workers created new employment opportunities that altered household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.3). This shift was not just about evolving social norms but also about structural changes in education and workplace policies that made part-time work available and removed barriers to married women's employment. This dynamic is mirrored in colonial Africa, where women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight how these institutional constraints shaped economic activities, as seen in the migration of Krobo and Akuapem farmers seeking farmland for cocoa production. This pattern underscores how institutional changes can both enable and constrain women's contributions to economic growth. The impact of these institutional frameworks on long-term economic growth was less direct than their effects on resource allocation and productivity (Goldin 2006, p.10).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in how colonial rule influenced African development trajectories (Frankema 2012, p.1; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Colonial powers introduced legal frameworks that significantly altered resource allocation and productivity. For instance, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in some colonies, while in others they set up extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.1). The impact of these institutional frameworks is clear: European settlers' presence or absence shaped economic activities. In areas where Europeans were present, such as the United States and Australia, institutions encouraged investment and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), whereas in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions hindered long-term growth by prioritizing resource extraction over local development (Frankema 2012, p.1). These differences highlight how institutional changes can both enable and constrain economic growth.

The colonial era also saw significant shifts in land use and agricultural practices. In some African colonies, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production, reflecting broader gender dynamics influenced by colonial institutions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched these gender disparities, as seen in the migration patterns of Krobo and Akuapem farmers seeking farmland for cocoa production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). These institutional constraints shaped economic activities and had long-term implications for resource allocation and productivity. For example, the de-privileging of women in access to land under colonial rule affected both pre-colonial and colonial economic development trajectories (Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The broader impact of these institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. This reversal was due to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, as well as geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in shaping the long-term development paths of these colonies, emphasizing the enduring influence of early institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. For example, the British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shows how different colonial institutions shaped educational outcomes and, by extension, long-term economic growth.

The precise historical evidence that settles the remaining disagreement between Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis lies in the varying institutional frameworks established by European colonial powers (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The reversal of relative incomes among former colonies is directly tied to these institutional differences, which influenced both economic activities and long-term development paths.
