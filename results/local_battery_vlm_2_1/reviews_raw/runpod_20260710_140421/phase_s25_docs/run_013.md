The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake are the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty across nations. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, with some arguing that institutional quality is shaped by underlying geographic factors such as climate and disease ecology (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while others posit that institutions are more malleable and can be shaped by historical events and political decisions. Additionally, there is a dispute over the extent to which colonial institutions have influenced contemporary economic outcomes in formerly colonized regions.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly due to mortality rates and disease environments (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies where mortality was high, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction and foster long-term growth. This institutional legacy persists today, affecting current income per capita levels. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who had incentives to maintain the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In contrast, Austin highlights the complexity of causal pathways in economic development and argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that institutional changes were not solely driven by exogenous factors but also by endogenous processes influenced by local economic and political dynamics.

These institutions were not uniform but varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates; in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development.

The persistence of these extractive institutions has been a significant factor in the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. also highlight that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions, illustrating how institutional legacies can shape economic outcomes over extended periods (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. acknowledge that while extractive institutions were detrimental, they did not entirely preclude economic growth; rather, they influenced the pace and nature of development.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This view is supported by the argument that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political action, adjusting their institutional prescriptions accordingly (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). For example, in Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4; Austin 2007, p.20). The British emancipation ordinance in 1874 and subsequent prohibitions on slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal moments that drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.4; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2007, p.20) notes that these changes were significant but did not entirely displace existing institutions. Colonial administrations often maintained a degree of continuity with pre-existing systems, integrating them into new frameworks rather than replacing them outright. This integration had important market-integrating effects, as tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the colonies were removed (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional changes in factor markets during this period highlight how shifts in resource availability can drive significant economic transformations over time.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can lead to significant economic transitions, altering traditional systems and property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Bohannan (1959, p.10) provides a vivid example of this transition in the Tiv economy, where the integration with the world economy through colonial institutions led to increased trade and a greater number of markets. This shift was driven by the introduction of brass rods as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere, but these rods could not fully replace traditional exchange systems such as marriage and food distribution (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The limitation in equivalency between money and traditional rights highlights the complexity of economic transitions, where new monetary systems must coexist with established institutions. This coexistence can lead to significant transformations in property rights and economic structures. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) illustrates a similar dynamic in the American context, showing how barbed wire fencing allowed farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. The adoption of herd laws and the introduction of barbed wire led to a 20 percentage point increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). However, the impact of these changes was not uniform; the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped detailing herd law decisions after 1880 and entirely ceased reporting the law status after 1884, indicating a shift in institutional focus and highlighting how economic transitions can alter administrative priorities (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The integration of new technologies and institutions into existing frameworks can drive long-run economic growth by enhancing property rights and market efficiency.

The literature on economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa highlights specific historical and educational developments that have shaped regional economies over time; Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa were influenced by colonial rule. This educational development was tied to broader economic trends; Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was closely linked to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These institutional frameworks not only shaped educational outcomes but also influenced the broader economic landscape, driving long-run economic growth by enhancing market efficiency and integrating new technologies into existing frameworks.

The literature on African economic history and development explores the complexities of economic growth in Africa without directly addressing how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa has shifted focus toward institutional reforms. The role of 'good' institutions is crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective aligns with broader discussions on governance and the state's role in economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2019, p.3) further notes that significant progress has been made in African economic history despite initial skepticism, emphasizing the need for multiple approaches to fully understand economic development (Hopkins 2019, p.13). The literature acknowledges that much work remains, particularly on precolonial production. This indicates that while institutions are recognized as important, their specific mechanisms and long-run effects on economic growth require further investigation.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), together with Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), examine specific factors contributing to African economic development and poverty. They focus on the role of women in economic growth and historical wage trends, which are important for understanding the evolution of Africa's economy without directly addressing the institutional framework that underpins long-run economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize the importance of understanding real wage divergence across British Africa to assess future growth possibilities. This focus on wages highlights a mechanism through which economic development can be traced over time, providing insights into the broader patterns of economic change in Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014) work shows that women were integral to this process, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial periods, where they contributed significantly to agricultural production despite facing marginalization under customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This suggests a pattern of economic development driven by both gender dynamics and broader structural changes. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) also note that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances, which limited women's access to land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic development in Africa, where historical legacies continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), a view shared by BroadberryEtal in their analysis of India's economic trajectory (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). Both sources highlight that this divergence was driven by industrialization as well as differences in agricultural productivity, institutional frameworks, and the role of service sectors. Allen supports this perspective by noting that real income disparities between European cities and other regions emerged as early as 1500-1750 (Allen 2001, p.8). This period saw a maintenance of high real wages in leading economic centers despite fluctuations in grain prices and other commodities, suggesting that institutional factors played a crucial role in sustaining these wage levels. However, BroadberryEtal qualify this by noting that India's decline relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was exacerbated by the lack of state institutions needed for investment and innovation (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This qualification underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth, as these factors influenced both agricultural productivity and the development of industrial sectors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. 2001 show that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This argument aligns with Austin's view that institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience are key to understanding long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). These perspectives highlight that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks, which in turn affected economic outcomes. For instance, settler colonies like the United States saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas extractive states in regions like the Congo focused on rapidly transferring resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The interaction between institutions and industrialization during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in the long-run development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. 2002 demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was closely tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by Hopkins' assertion that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The specific historical evidence of industrialization and institutional changes during the nineteenth century shows that these factors were central to the long-run development trajectories of former colonies. This interaction highlights how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic growth over extended periods.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further complicated by the influence of colonial legal systems on gender dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite being marginalized by customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective aligns with Frankema's view that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. Frankema shows that demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were altered by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These findings illustrate how institutional changes under colonial rule affected economic outcomes as well as gender dynamics and educational development.

The remaining disagreement focuses on the extent to which European settlers' presence influenced indigenous commodity production. Austin shows that in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on the role of extractive institutions in maximizing rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The specific historical evidence of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher production equilibrium settles this disagreement by showing that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action.
