The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in development economics and economic history. The stakes are high because the answer informs policy decisions that can either perpetuate cycles of poverty or catalyze sustained prosperity. At its core, this debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, is crucial for long-term economic development. Another view posits that while institutions matter, their impact is mediated by historical contexts and external influences, making them less deterministic in shaping economic outcomes. These differing perspectives underscore the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks evolve and interact with other factors over time.

The debate on whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth is central to understanding global economic disparities. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies has been extensively studied by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who argue that colonial policies shaped different institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced long-run development paths. This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further elaborates on this point, noting that the quality of public goods provision and institutional development are closely linked to economic growth, suggesting that effective institutions are essential for fostering investment and development.

The role of colonial policies in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions depending on the context. In colonies where Europeans settled, such as the United States and Australia, inclusive institutions were established, fostering economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to quickly transfer resources to the metropole, impeding long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dichotomy highlights how colonial policies directly influenced institutional frameworks and, consequently, economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) add that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that while colonial institutions were extractive, they did not entirely stifle economic development in some regions.

The impact of colonial policies on long-run economic growth is further complicated by the role of education and social institutions. Frankema (2012, p.1) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, indicating that the type of colonial administration influenced institutional outcomes. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) argument that European colonization strategies varied significantly and had lasting impacts on economic development. The interaction between colonial policies, educational institutions, and long-run economic growth underscores the multifaceted nature of this relationship.

The endurance of these institutions is significant because it indicates that even post-decolonization, many former colonies retained institutional frameworks that impeded economic growth. This process illustrates how colonial policies influenced immediate economic outcomes and had lasting effects on the long-term economic performance of these regions. This perspective underscores the profound and lasting influence of colonial institutions on economic development, illustrating how historical policies continue to shape contemporary economic realities.

In a similar vein, Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This innovation allowed for more effective land management and cultivation, as evidenced by the substantial increase in improved farmland following its adoption (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). The effectiveness of barbed wire in securing property rights is further supported by evidence that shows how it facilitated the enforcement of herd laws, which were crucial for managing livestock and preventing damage to crops. This mechanism highlights how institutional changes, such as the introduction of new technologies, can have profound effects on agricultural productivity and land use patterns (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). Barbed wire's impact varied by region; its advantages were more noticeable in areas where property rights were already somewhat secure. This suggests that while technological innovations can drive economic growth, their effectiveness is often contingent upon pre-existing institutional frameworks.

This transition in African economies highlights how institutional changes can be driven by shifts in resource availability, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights how the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, especially where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour. This dynamic underscores that colonial administrations encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, thus altering institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour in Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates how institutional changes can be driven by shifts in factor scarcity. Austin (2007, p.20) shows that the loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.20). This transition from coerced to free labour had significant economic implications, as it created new incentives and opportunities within the local economy. Local political dynamics also played a key role in driving this institutional revolution. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal legal actions that facilitated this transition (Austin 2007, p.4). These changes modified labor practices and land use patterns, giving rise to new economic structures.

The service sector played a crucial role in this economic growth by facilitating the distribution of manufactured goods and contributing to an uneven spread of development benefits. This divergence was not solely about income levels; it also reflected differences in institutional frameworks that supported investment and innovation.

The implications are significant: the Great Divergence highlights how specific institutional arrangements can either facilitate or impede long-run economic growth by influencing productivity, investment, and innovation.
