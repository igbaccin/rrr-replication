The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that intersects multiple disciplines, including economics, political science, and history. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder sustained economic development over generations. The live disagreement centers around whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive and detrimental to growth, or if they could also be conducive to development under certain conditions. Some scholars argue that European settlers established institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Others posit that the impact of colonial institutions varied significantly depending on local factors such as population density and prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The debate extends beyond colonial legacies, encompassing broader questions about how institutions evolve and their effects on economic outcomes. Some argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were more influential than geographic factors in determining relative incomes among former colonies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance. However, other scholars contend that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary to explain long-term economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.1; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly impacted long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers implemented different strategies depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development; former colonies with extractive institutions experienced slower growth compared to those with more inclusive frameworks. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutional differences were directly shaped by colonial policies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced long-term growth can be seen in the persistence of extractive states, which maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. also note that the interaction between these institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-term development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact was mediated by other economic factors and historical contexts. The enduring effects of colonial institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary economic performance, highlighting the importance of understanding these historical processes for explaining long-run growth patterns.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing and money into subsistence economies led to significant economic and social transformations, highlighting how institutional changes can drive long-run economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrates that the adoption of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enabled farmers to protect their property rights more effectively. The impact of this institutional change is evident in the substantial rise in total improved land from 1880 to 1890. This period saw an 11 percentage point increase, underscoring the transformative effect of enhanced property rights on agricultural development. The transition, however, is not without challenges. For example, the equivalence between monetary currency and other forms of value, such as rights in women or food, could not be established (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This highlights that while institutional changes can drive growth, they also necessitate significant social and economic adaptations. Bryceson (2002, p.2) shows that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social change and depeasantization, emphasizing the interconnectedness of economic shifts and accompanying social transformations (Bryceson 2002, p.2). The impact on social structures is evident as rural men have generally accepted that their wives and daughters need to work outside the home to earn money (Bryceson 2002, p.8). This underscores how institutional changes can reshape economic activities and broader social dynamics, ultimately influencing long-run growth patterns.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. They highlight that understanding historical economic paths requires more than just structural impediments; it necessitates a deeper examination of specific institutional factors. These authors argue that institutional changes, such as those related to labor and commodity production, played crucial roles in shaping long-run economic growth. For instance, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating incentives for local economic activity (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores how institutions can foster economic development by responding to local conditions and incentives.

The complexity of institutional impacts extends beyond encouraging production; it involves nuanced responses to economic and political actions. This perspective is crucial because it reveals that institutions influence immediate economic outcomes and shape broader socio-economic structures over time.

This nuanced view of institutional impacts suggests that future research should delve deeper into the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic development. By examining real wage levels and trends across different colonial periods, scholars can gain insights into the structural effects of historical conditions on African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach helps in understanding past economic trajectories and provides valuable lessons for future policy-making.

The great divergence between Asia and Europe was already well underway before 1800 (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen 2001, p.8). This divergence is evident in the economic trajectories of regions like India. This narrative shows that institutional changes are usually seen as bringing about investment in agriculture, but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. This leads to high agricultural labour productivity and can reinforce the uneven spread of early modern development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9).

The decline in India's per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism; this is evident from the revenue trends under different regimes, including Mughal rule and British administration. The Mughal Empire experienced a revival in revenue under Aurangzeb but declined again as it collapsed (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This pattern suggests that pre-colonial economic conditions played a crucial role in shaping India's long-term economic trajectory; however, the absence of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation was a significant factor. The lack of such institutions prevented India from breaking out of the Malthusian trap, which allowed Britain and Holland to increase both population and per capita incomes (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic development, even as it highlights that colonialism is not the sole determinant of long-run economic growth.

The role of women in economic growth and development is significant, as seen through historical perspectives that highlight their contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Goldin 2006, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) describe how African women were integral to economic activities. The marginalization of women's access to land during the colonial period, particularly for cash crops, was reinforced by customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Women in Africa were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men controlled the more profitable cash crop farming. This gendered division of labor constrained economic growth because it limited the productive potential of women and reduced overall agricultural productivity.

Goldin (2006, p.3) illustrates a similar dynamic in the United States, where institutional changes facilitated shifts in women's employment and education. The increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century created new job opportunities, while educational expansions from the 1910s to the 1940s supplied potential workers (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes altered female labor supply by making part-time work available and removing barriers to married women's employment. The transformation of household production technology further influenced women's participation in the labor market. These institutional shifts enhanced women’s economic roles and introduced new challenges, such as balancing work with family responsibilities.

This legal framework limited women’s economic activities and reinforced gender inequalities. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched these disparities, creating a cycle where women’s contributions to economic growth remained undervalued and underutilized.

Domar's work on labor scarcity and serfdom (Domar 1970, p.4; Abramovitz 1986, p.21) reveals that economic growth is influenced by various factors, including labor availability and social capability. Domar argues that when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, societies tend to develop institutions like slavery or serfdom. This perspective highlights how economic forces shape institutional structures. Abramovitz builds on this idea by emphasizing the complexity of the catch-up process in economic growth, which is influenced not only by exogenous factors but also by technological advance and social change. He notes that institutional changes can limit the growth of economies, suggesting that institutions play a crucial role in long-run economic performance. Abramovitz acknowledges the challenge of measuring social capability, which includes technical competence and the organization of large-scale enterprise (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This uncertainty underscores the need for further research into how specific institutional factors contribute to economic growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) argues that institutional changes in Ghana during 1874-1939 were driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity, leading to significant economic transformations. This view is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), who demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during this period led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This was marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the 1874 British emancipation ordinance and subsequent legal actions that addressed changing factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.4; Austin 2007, p.20). The extension of British rule in Ghana also had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This indicates that while institutional changes were crucial, they also interacted with broader social dynamics, influencing the benefits perceived from formal education (Frankema 2012, p.1).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Hopkins (2019, p.3) share an emphasis on the progress made in African economic history and the role of governance structures. This literature highlights significant advances despite initial skepticism, particularly focusing on precolonial production and governance mechanisms (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). These studies underscore that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) elaborates on this point, noting that "good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8), which aligns with the broader theme of institutional impact on economic growth. This perspective does not directly address how specific institutional changes fundamentally cause long-run economic growth, thus leaving room for further exploration. Hopkins (2019, p.3) acknowledges that multiple approaches and techniques are necessary to fully understand the history of economic development (Hopkins 2019, p.13). This implies that while institutions are crucial, their impact on long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of various factors.

Institutional frameworks significantly influence long-run economic growth by shaping incentives for investment and development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established varied institutional structures in their colonies, with some promoting property rights and others extractive states. Austin (2008, p.2) also highlights the long-term impact of these institutions, noting that they are key to understanding economic growth across different historical periods. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Austin 2008, p.15) underscores how institutional changes during colonialism set the stage for divergent economic trajectories in former colonies.

The role of institutions in economic development is further elucidated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions often maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth. This perspective aligns with Hopkins' (2009) (Hopkins 2009, p.18) emphasis on the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) work shows that institutional changes also affected gender dynamics, with colonial rule codifying customary laws that marginalized women's access to land. This highlights how institutions shape economic outcomes and have profound social implications.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement about whether institutions are conducive to development is the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies during industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical comparison demonstrates that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule was more conducive to formal education, which altered the benefits of formal education for African families and political leaders. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, as documented by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), reveals how specific institutional changes fundamentally cause long-run economic growth by altering incentives and access to resources.
