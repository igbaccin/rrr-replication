The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake is the understanding of why some regions prosper while others stagnate over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural attributes, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Others posit that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), while still others suggest that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.1). The interplay between these perspectives is crucial for comprehending the complex dynamics of economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. and others ( (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This divergence in institutional frameworks resulted from European powers establishing systems that maximized their own rents rather than fostering long-run growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. note that "equilibrium institutions" could be extractive as they were designed to benefit the colonizers ( (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) ) . The implications of these institutional differences are profound, as they directly affected the economic trajectories of the colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that regions with extractive institutions faced significant barriers to development, while those with inclusive institutions experienced more robust economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This dynamic underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic outcomes.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the transition from coerced to free labour was a pivotal moment in this process. The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour transformed economic structures during periods of scarcity by incentivising indigenous commodity production, which colonial governments encouraged (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional revolution in factor markets was driven by the need for fiscal security and the response to changing economic conditions. Austin (2008, p.2) provides evidence that colonial administrations altered their policies in response to these incentives, opting for higher production equilibria, showing how localised scarcity could drive institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). However, the integration of markets within colonies, such as those later administratively integrated into Ghana, was also influenced by British rule which removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade (Austin 2007, p.4). This suggests that while localised scarcity drove institutional changes in factor markets, broader colonial policies also played a role in shaping economic outcomes.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights for institutional reforms aimed at poverty alleviation among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasises that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, but rather by the broader institutional framework. This aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) reconstruction of real wage levels in British Africa, which underscores the importance of historical conditions in shaping long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The new economic history of Africa thus highlights that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development. However, Bryceson (2002, p.2) cautions that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social change and depeasantization, suggesting that institutional reforms must also address the accompanying social transformations (Bryceson 2002, p.2). This implies that while historical conditions and institutional frameworks are pivotal, successful poverty alleviation strategies must also consider the broader socio-economic context.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated increased investment in agriculture. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrates that the adoption of barbed wire led to a substantial increase in improved farmland, particularly after 1880 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). Domar (1970, p.4) complements this by illustrating how property rights can shape agricultural structures (Domar 1970, p.12). The scarcity of labor and the need for secure land ownership drove the development of serfdom in some historical contexts, highlighting the importance of property rights in economic development. This underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth.

British rule in Africa fostered formal education more than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15)(Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This development was driven by the demand for missionary education, which African political leaders and families perceived as beneficial due to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of these institutions altered the cost-benefit calculations related to formal education. This suggests that British rule's impact on economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa operated through a different mechanism than solely through institutional frameworks. The colonial period saw significant variations in educational development across African countries, influenced by both local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors shaped the timing and scale of growth in export production, which was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner underscore that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous views on Africa's economic performance during this era. This historical context highlights how colonial rule, particularly British rule, influenced educational development and economic outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa through mechanisms beyond institutional frameworks alone.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10) examine the early onset of the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. The economic performance of India relative to Britain during this period was influenced by several factors such as agricultural productivity, investment patterns, and state institutions. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) highlight that Indian workers remained predominantly in agriculture, which had negative consequences for labour productivity and the growth of industrial and service sectors. This pattern is consistent with broader global trends at the time, where many economies were constrained by limited institutional support for investment and innovation. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that high agricultural productivity in India was not solely due to geographical factors but also resulted from significant investments during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, they were not the only drivers of economic development. Consequently, understanding the great divergence requires considering multiple factors beyond institutional frameworks alone.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented across various historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies significantly impacted the institutional frameworks established in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly influenced economic outcomes by determining the extent to which investment and innovation were encouraged (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores that institutional changes are not isolated events but part of a complex interplay with other economic and political factors. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15). This aligns with Austin's observation that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting a dynamic relationship between institutions and local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence from African economies further illustrates the complex role of institutions. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that institutional frameworks were already influencing economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This finding is supported by Frankema's argument that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The variation in educational outcomes across different colonial regimes highlights how institutional differences shaped long-run economic trajectories. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people, emphasizing the practical implications of understanding institutional impacts on economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective underscores the importance of studying historical institutions to address contemporary economic challenges.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic development remain a subject of debate.
