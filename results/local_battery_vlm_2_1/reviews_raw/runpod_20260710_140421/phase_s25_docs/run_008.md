The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians alike. At stake intellectually is the question of whether institutional frameworks can be designed to promote sustained economic development, or if other factors such as geography and culture play more determinative roles. This disagreement centres around competing views on the causal mechanisms driving economic outcomes: one side posits that institutions are primary drivers, while the other argues for a more complex interplay with additional variables. The debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering growth in developing economies.

A key position emphasises the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial policies influenced by population density and prosperity shaped the development trajectories of former colonies, which resulted in a reversal in relative incomes due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) provide further support for this perspective by asserting that equilibrium institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth.

The debate extends beyond the nature of institutions to their interaction with other factors such as geography and culture. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of economic growth in African countries were tied closely to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development, where institutions interact with various factors to shape long-term outcomes.

The role of historical context is also crucial in understanding how institutions influence economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic relationship between external policies and local conditions. This dynamic is further illustrated by Frankema (2012, p.15), who discusses how the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. These insights highlight that institutions evolve in response to changing contexts, affecting economic development over time.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate this through their examination of European colonization strategies and the resulting institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates among Europeans, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole; these institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. also support this pattern by arguing that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth. This institutional framework, characterized by its focus on short-term gains and sustainable development, had profound implications for the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies.

The impact of colonial institutions is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al., who document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and link this to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. They provide evidence that the interaction between institutions and the ability to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights how colonial institutions shaped immediate economic outcomes and influenced the trajectory of industrialization, which is crucial for sustained economic growth. This variability in institutional implementation highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local contexts, suggesting that the economic impact of colonial institutions was mediated by these factors.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that institutional changes in colonial economies were driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This dynamic was evident in Ghana during 1874-1939, where the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity led to significant institutional transformations in factor markets. The emergence of widespread markets for land use rights and hired labour was a direct response to these changing economic conditions (Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2007, p.20) notes that the shift from coerced to free labour, driven by legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance, had profound economic implications. This interplay between local conditions and colonial policies highlights how the trajectory of long-run economic growth was shaped by a complex interplay of factors that included shifting resource availability and evolving social structures.

The literature on African economic history has made significant strides in elucidating the role of institutions and governance in development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, which are crucial for eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people. This literature underscores how "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, thereby fostering development. This multifaceted approach is essential because it allows for a comprehensive analysis of the complex factors that influence long-run economic growth. Significant work remains to be done, particularly on precolonial production (Hopkins 2019, p.13). The implication is clear: a deeper understanding of Africa's historical economic conditions could provide valuable insights into contemporary development challenges.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and van Waijenburg (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that British colonial rule in Africa played a pivotal role in shaping the development of formal education systems. These institutions altered perceptions of the benefits associated with formal education, thereby fostering its growth under British rule. Frankema (2012, p.15) emphasizes that this development was more pronounced in regions where the demand for missionary education was high. This dynamic is crucial because it highlights how colonial institutions could catalyze educational advancement despite historical impediments to long-term African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that the impact of these educational developments on poverty reduction is complex. The lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates complicates efforts to quantify the persistence of poverty during colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2), suggesting that while education was a significant factor, its effects on long-term economic growth were contingent on broader historical and geographical conditions.

The literature on sub-Saharan Africa and China provides valuable insights into historical economic conditions by focusing on per capita income levels (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) and real wages (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Allen et al. argue that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, whereas Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. These findings highlight the regional differences in economic performance during this period. This variation suggests that economic growth was influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These studies do not directly address how institutions influence long-run economic growth, leaving this aspect under-explored in the literature on sub-Saharan Africa and China.

The divergence in economic performance between Europe and Asia during the early modern period was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This divergence is evident in the productivity of traded goods sectors and the role of merchant distribution and finance. Similarly, BroadberryEtal show that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonial rule. This decline is attributed to several factors, including the lack of state institutions needed to support investment and innovation, which allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). The absence of these institutions in India led to stagnation in agricultural labour productivity and hindered the growth of industrial and service sectors. BroadberryEtal also note that while colonial rule exacerbated these issues, the decline had already begun during the Mughal Empire (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This indicates a deeper institutional failure that predates European intervention.

The implications of this early divergence are significant for understanding long-run economic growth. The institutional differences between Europe and Asia highlight the critical role of state institutions in fostering investment and innovation, which are essential for sustained economic development. These findings suggest that the institutional framework in place during the early modern period significantly influenced the trajectory of economic growth in both regions. This divergence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors. This perspective aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. In this regard, Acemoglu et al. argue that "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores how political power and rent-seeking behaviors can shape institutions in ways that maximize short-term gains and long-run growth.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes is further highlighted by the contrasting experiences of different regions. Frankema argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This institutional legacy had lasting effects on economic development, as shown by Broadberry and Gardner's findings that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The importance of institutions in fostering investment and innovation is also evident in the experiences of European colonies. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective highlights how historical institutional frameworks continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. All agree that the institutional framework in place during the early modern period significantly influenced the trajectory of economic growth in both regions. This view is shared by Acemoglu et al., who argue that "Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions" (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which these institutional frameworks operate. Austin argues that colonial administrations that sought ﬁscal security, and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights how local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions can shape long-run economic outcomes.
