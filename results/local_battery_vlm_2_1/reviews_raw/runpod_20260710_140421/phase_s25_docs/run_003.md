The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question for understanding development trajectories over centuries and across regions. The debate pivots on the extent to which institutional frameworks, shaped by historical events and colonial legacies, influence sustained economic prosperity. A central disagreement centers around whether extractive versus inclusive institutions are the primary drivers of economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the establishment of extractive institutions, often rooted in colonial policies, has led to persistent economic underdevelopment in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Others contend that a more nuanced understanding is needed, one that acknowledges the complex interplay between historical context, institutional evolution, and economic growth. This perspective challenges simplistic narratives about institutional impacts and emphasizes the importance of contextual factors such as population density, prosperity, and the interaction between institutions and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The stakes are high: understanding these dynamics could inform policy reforms aimed at fostering inclusive economic development in regions still grappling with the legacies of colonialism. By examining how different institutional frameworks have shaped economic outcomes over time, scholars can provide valuable insights into the mechanisms that drive long-run growth and development.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the legacy of European colonization and the institutions it imposed on colonies. Acemoglu et al argue that European colonizers established very different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates and settlement patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development trajectories. Hopkins argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). He notes that ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, while ethnically heterogeneous ones can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that institutional quality is determined by ethnic composition as well as the broader socio-political context. The implication is that effective reforms must consider historical contexts and local dynamics to achieve sustainable economic growth.

This insight underscores the importance of understanding historical context when evaluating the role of institutions in economic development.

They establish that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and other factors. This pattern is contrasted with extractive states set up in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was rapid resource transfer to the metropole. These varying institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. The persistence of these institutions over time meant that they significantly affected current income per capita. The interaction between these institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.

The development of property rights institutions can significantly impact agricultural productivity, as shown by Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Domar (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrates this through the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which enhanced farmers' ability to protect their land and livestock on the American Plains. This innovation led to a substantial increase in improved farmland, with herd-law counties experiencing a notable 20 percentage-point rise from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). Domar (1970, p.4) complements this by illustrating how land ownership rights can shape agricultural structures. In regions where labor is scarce and the land-to-labor ratio is high, such as colonial America, property rights institutions emerge that facilitate the creation of a non-working class of agricultural owners (Domar 1970, p.4). This pattern underscores how the establishment of secure property rights can drive long-term economic growth by encouraging investment in agriculture. Domar (1970, p.4) also highlights that this process can be influenced by broader historical contexts, such as government policies or cultural factors (Domar 1970, p.12). The interaction between property rights institutions and other socio-economic factors thus plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. They demonstrate this through a focus on how African institutions have evolved over time, particularly under colonial rule. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. This suggests a key mechanism: as 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, they are essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) extend this by noting that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial. This implies that a deeper grasp of long-term economic development trends can inform future growth strategies, suggesting that specific historical conditions may have had a structural effect on African welfare development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) also point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty. This qualification underscores the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. The new economic history of Africa thus offers valuable insights into how institutional reforms can be tailored to address persistent poverty on the continent.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2007, p.20) show that the institutional changes in African economies, particularly the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, led to significant transformations in factor markets. These changes were marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The transition from coerced to free labour was a critical aspect of this institutional revolution (Austin 2007, p.4). The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 further drove these changes, as chiefs were compelled to sell land to strangers due to debt, fostering cocoa farming. This shift was driven by changing factor scarcities as well as legal actions (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political incentives, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution in Africa. This adaptation was evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. The removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast colony further integrated markets, facilitating economic growth. These institutional shifts underscore the importance of understanding historical contexts in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

These historical factors shaped the economic trajectories of African and Asian economies through the colonial period, as shown by Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). The institutional context was pivotal in determining educational outcomes in Africa, as British rule encouraged formal education more than other colonial powers did. This is because demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were altered by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). Thus, the institutional framework underpinning British rule facilitated a unique pathway to economic development in Africa. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) qualifies this by asserting that India's decline relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This suggests that while institutions played a role, other factors such as state capacity and agricultural productivity were also crucial. The implications of these findings underscore the importance of considering multiple historical and economic factors when examining long-run economic growth in Africa and Asia.

The historical roots of economic divergence challenge Eurocentric views and highlight complex social formations that influenced global hegemony (Bryant 2006, p.12; Allen 2001, p.8). Bryant (2006, p.12) and Allen (2001, p.8) argue that the early modern period saw significant shifts in economic power, with Europe's ascent to global dominance driven by cumulative institutional and cultural developments. One key mechanism was the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity gains in traded goods sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This highlights how service sector innovations contributed to economic growth, a pattern seen both in early modern and contemporary periods. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) qualify this by noting that living standards rose unevenly across Europe due to differing impacts on skilled versus unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that while institutional changes were pivotal, their effects varied significantly based on socio-economic conditions. Consequently, understanding long-run economic growth requires acknowledging the diverse and sometimes conflicting roles of institutions and social structures in shaping economic outcomes.

Women significantly contributed to economic growth both historically and contemporarily (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Goldin argues that women's increased education and employment opportunities in the early twentieth century were driven by various exogenous changes. This includes the rise of female office workers due to shifting demand, educational expansion at secondary levels, and technological advancements in household production. These factors altered labor supply dynamics, making part-time work more accessible and removing barriers for married women (Goldin 2006, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women also played pivotal roles during pre-colonial and colonial periods. For example, Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated to secure farmland for cocoa production, driving economic growth and making the region a global leader in cocoa by 1911 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This progress faced challenges; patriarchal alliances and customary law often reinforced women's subordinate status under colonial rule. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that the codification of customary law during colonial times further marginalized women's access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This underscores how institutional changes, while enabling growth, can also create barriers that disproportionately affect certain groups.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly impacted economic development trajectories. For example, colonies with extractive states often saw resource transfer to the metropole rather than local investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where colonial policies prioritized rapid resource extraction over sustainable growth.

The role of institutions in economic development is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on how equilibrium institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Austin's observation that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical processes, ignoring the nuanced ways in which institutions evolve over time (Austin 2008, p.2). Institutions are not static; they adapt to changing political and economic landscapes. For instance, colonial administrations sometimes altered their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, illustrating how institutional changes can be influenced by local dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15).

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is also evident in the context of African women's contributions to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, reinforcing their subordinate status under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional marginalization was further exacerbated by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified during the colonial era (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Frankema shows that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing demand for missionary education among African communities (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that institutional changes introduced by colonial powers had varying impacts on economic development, with some fostering growth more effectively than others.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is clear. All sources agree that institutions are key determinants of economic outcomes, as shown by the varied impacts of European colonization strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). There remains a disagreement over whether these institutional changes were primarily driven by external factors such as colonial policies or internal dynamics within the colonies themselves. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). This disagreement is settled by Hopkins' argument that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.
