The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study that seeks to understand how societal rules and norms influence economic development over extended periods. The central intellectual debate revolves around the extent to which institutional frameworks can either propel or hinder economic progress. One key position emphasizes that institutions designed to enforce property rights and the rule of law are pivotal for sustained economic growth, while a rival perspective argues that geographic factors and resource endowments play a more significant role in determining long-run economic outcomes. Another stream contends that colonial legacies, particularly the type of institutions imposed by colonial powers, have had enduring effects on economic development.

Institutional frameworks can be either inclusive or extractive. Inclusive institutions foster economic growth by protecting property rights and ensuring secure investment environments. Extractive institutions, however, concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, often at the expense of broader economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They point out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and thus fail to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores how the nature of colonial institutions has had lasting impacts on economic development in these regions. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight that African poverty is a result of specific historical and geographical conditions.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide evidence that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance, suggesting that institutional factors played a crucial role in shaping these outcomes. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings align with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) argument that the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of former colonies. The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further complicated by the demand for missionary education, which depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic altered the perceived benefits of formal education, illustrating how institutional changes can influence broader economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. Institutional frameworks can be either inclusive or extractive, with significant implications for economic development. The type of institutions imposed during colonial rule has had enduring effects on economic outcomes in former colonies. Furthermore, institutions evolve over time in response to various factors, including political and economic changes. Understanding how institutional frameworks influence long-run economic growth is crucial for designing policies that promote sustainable development.

Colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans implemented divergent colonization strategies, setting up either inclusive or extractive states depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This institutional framework had enduring effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The type of institution, extractive or inclusive, determined the extent to which local populations could engage in productive activities and benefit from economic growth. These findings underscore the importance of considering the historical context of institutional development when analyzing long-run economic growth.

Frankema and others argue that British colonial rule had a more positive impact on education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This educational development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) both show that institutional changes in factor markets were driven by shifts in land abundance during Ghana's colonial period (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, as local chiefs sold land to strangers to farm cocoa. This institutional revolution was marked by a transition from coerced to free labour, which had significant economic dimensions (Austin 2008, p.15; Austin 2007, p.20). The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 further facilitated this transition. Austin (2007, p.20) shows that these legal actions were responses to changing factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.4). This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering both historical and geographical conditions in assessing long-term economic development.

The introduction of money into an African subsistence economy led to profound changes and a shift away from a multicentric economy (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The transition involved the integration of local economic institutions with global markets. This shift introduced alien institutions that disrupted existing internal marketing systems, as seen in colonial Northern Nigeria, which was initially controlled by trading companies with governing powers (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The introduction of brass rods as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere further complicated this transition, as these rods could not fully replace traditional exchange systems such as rights in women or food (Bohannan 1959, p.9; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This substitution highlights the complexity of integrating new economic innovations into existing social structures. The shift towards a monetary economy was thus fraught with challenges and necessitated significant adjustments to local institutions, which had profound implications for long-term economic development.

Bryant (2006, p.12) and Goldin (2006, p.3) both show that economic and social changes in specific regions are driven by complex interactions between cultural, institutional, and technological factors. These transformations often involve significant shifts in labor markets and educational systems (Bryant 2006, p.12; Goldin 2006, p.3). For instance, the increased demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century altered labor supply dynamics significantly, as did changes in household production technology and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). Bryant (2006, p.12) argues that these transformations involve a series of interdependent developments over time. While this perspective highlights the importance of local adaptations and institutional responses to economic changes, it also underscores the role of exogenous factors in shaping regional economies.

This interplay between local adaptation and exogenous changes is further illustrated by Allen et al.'s (2011, p.22) examination of real wages in China (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). Their findings reveal that wage rates can serve as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor, indicating a vibrant labor market. The integration of urban and rural labor markets varied significantly across regions, with commercialized areas near major urban centers showing higher degrees of integration compared to less developed regions (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This regional variation suggests that while exogenous factors like technological changes and educational growth can drive economic transformations, local institutional contexts play a crucial role in mediating these effects.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) document how historical economic performance in specific regions challenges conventional narratives about per capita income levels and growth patterns. They note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding that counters previous characterizations of Africa's economic history. This revision of historical economic data highlights the importance of local contexts in shaping long-run economic outcomes (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further elaborate on this point by noting that recent advancements in African economic history have revealed large amounts of economic data for both the colonial era and post-independence periods, enabling more nuanced comparative analyses.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the varied outcomes of European colonies, where different institutional frameworks were established by colonizers. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers implemented distinct colonization strategies based on mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In high-mortality regions like the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In low-mortality areas such as the United States and Australia, institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment were set up. These institutional differences significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin elaborates on this by noting that colonial governments often adapted their policies based on local economic conditions and political actions. For instance, in areas where European settlers did not compete for African labor, indigenous commodity production was encouraged by colonial administrations seeking fiscal security (Austin 2008, p.15).

These institutional frameworks evolved over time, shaped by both external shocks and internal dynamics of the colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and thus fail to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's analysis, which highlights how pre-existing conditions influenced the impact of exogenous shocks like colonial rule on long-term economic growth. Their work shows that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more nuanced economic performance than previously characterized (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This is consistent with Frankema and van Waijenburg's findings, which reveal that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They show that real wage levels in British African colonies varied significantly over the colonial period, reflecting diverse economic trajectories within the region (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how resources are allocated and how economic activities are regulated. Acemoglu et al.'s work emphasizes that different institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have had lasting impacts on current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement centers around the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate and their interaction with local conditions. Frankema's research shows that British rule was more conducive to developing formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, highlighting a positive impact of certain institutional frameworks (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the type of institution established during colonial rule can significantly influence long-term economic outcomes by shaping educational attainment and human capital development. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of real wage levels across different British African colonies, which reveals varied economic trajectories influenced by institutional frameworks (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).
