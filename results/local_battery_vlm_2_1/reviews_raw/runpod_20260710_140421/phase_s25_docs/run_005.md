The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic among scholars. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography and culture, play more significant roles. One key position argues that institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic growth. This perspective finds support in evidence indicating that colonies with extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and did not promote long-run growth, experienced stagnation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This view is complemented by studies emphasizing the importance of institutional quality in providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Another position suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth has been oversimplified. This perspective highlights the complexity of historical paths and the need to consider multiple factors beyond institutions alone. A central theme across these debates is the importance of understanding how different institutional frameworks influence long-term economic outcomes. These disagreements underscore the need for a nuanced examination of institutional effects, considering both their direct impacts on growth and the broader contexts in which they operate.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established distinct institutions based on their mortality rates and settlement patterns in colonies. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional frameworks, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and did not promote long-run growth, persisted over time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is exemplified by Acemoglu et al.'s documentation of the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. They show that this reversal was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interplay between colonial institutions and economic opportunities underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.

Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This innovation allowed farmers to delineate and defend their land more effectively. The ability to securely claim and protect land encouraged farmers to invest in crops and infrastructure that would have otherwise been vulnerable to damage from wandering livestock. The impact of these institutional changes varied across all regions. The effectiveness of barbed wire in protecting property rights varied depending on local conditions and pre-existing legal frameworks (Hornbeck 2010, p.8).

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the colonial period in Africa was marked by unique institutional developments that differed from the broader narrative of long-run economic growth driven by secure property rights. This shift was a response to both European settlers and indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, the expansion of commodity production in regions where European settlers were absent often aligned with colonial governments' interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This alignment highlights how local conditions shaped institutional responses, leading to varied outcomes across different African regions.

Austin (2007, p.20) further illustrates this dynamic through the case of Ghana during 1874-1939. The transition from coerced to free labour in Ghana was driven by the loss of slaves, which made chiefs more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional change was not a direct result of colonial policies but an adaptive response to changing factor scarcities. Administrative integration of colonies under British rule also played a crucial role in market integration by removing tariffs and barriers to trade within the region (Austin 2007, p.4). As such, the institutional changes in African economies were shaped by both local economic dynamics and colonial administrative policies, leading to a complex interplay that influenced long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) along with Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The economic performance of these regions was driven by export production, which was closely tied to foreign trade markets. This dynamic was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These conditions shaped the growth patterns in export production, leading to varying scales and timings of economic expansion across different countries. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era has enabled detailed historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa, revealing complex patterns of economic development that challenge previous characterizations of persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These findings highlight the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence and its relationship to broader economic trends in colonial Africa. This insight is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities and evaluating the long-term impact of historical conditions on African welfare development.

The literature on economic divergence and colonial impacts explores how historical events and policies have shaped economic trajectories (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen 2001, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) assert that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period, a finding supported by Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that high productivity in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance. This suggests that institutional changes, including those related to merchant activities and financial systems, played a pivotal role in driving economic growth. The specific mechanism here is the service sector's contribution to economic development, as BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) note that India lacked state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This institutional gap highlights how the absence of supportive frameworks can hinder economic progress. Allen (2001, p.8) qualifies this by showing that real wage maintenance in European cities between 1500 and 1750 was crucial for sustaining living standards, indicating that while institutional changes are important, maintaining high levels of consumption is also critical (Allen 2001, p.23). This implies that economic growth is not solely driven by institutional factors but also by the ability to maintain and improve living standards.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms highlights that governance and historical context play crucial roles in shaping development paths (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) shows that the quality of public goods provision is determined by both ethnic fragmentation and the capacity to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This nuanced view underscores how institutional effectiveness can vary widely within African societies regardless of their ethnolinguistic makeup.

The emphasis on governance is further supported by the broader shift in development policy towards incorporating institutions and organizations (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shift enabled new economic history to engage with development specialists, as "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This indicates that the historical context of institutional reforms is pivotal for long-run economic growth, as precolonial institutions may have laid the groundwork for current economic structures.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied significantly across different regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies with high mortality rates among Europeans, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional framework persisted and had a lasting impact on current income per capita. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He highlights how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more dynamic interaction between institutions and local contexts. This adaptive institutional change is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonial rule on African economies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not conducive to economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and did not promote long-run growth, illustrating the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic structures. Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this by showing how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks to address current economic challenges.

The economic performance of many Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century was more robust than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, challenging earlier characterizations (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Instead, they suggest that specific historical or geographical conditions have had temporary effects on long-term African welfare development. This perspective highlights the need for nuanced understanding of historical economic data to inform current policies.

A disagreement persists over the persistence of poverty and its causes. This disagreement can be settled by examining real wage levels and trends, as Frankema and van Waijenburg do in their study.
