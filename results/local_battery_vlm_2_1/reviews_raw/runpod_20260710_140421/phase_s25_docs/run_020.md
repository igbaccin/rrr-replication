The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with divergent views on how institutional frameworks influence development trajectories. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or exogenous drivers that fundamentally shape economic outcomes. The first stream of literature focuses on the enduring effects of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both argue that colonial institutions, particularly those established by European powers, have had lasting impacts on the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions were often extractive in nature, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions has significantly affected current income per capita levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional legacies in understanding contemporary economic disparities.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This innovation allowed farmers to reduce externalities from livestock damage and secure their landholdings. Domar elaborates on the historical context of land ownership and labor scarcity, noting how government policies can shape agricultural structures. This dynamic is further illustrated by Domar's (1970, p.4) observation that serfdom emerged in response to high land-to-labor ratios and labor scarcity. In European history, the increase in population in Western Europe by the end of the thirteenth century coincided with the decline of serfdom (Domar 1970, p.12). The interplay between property rights, labor availability, and institutional frameworks highlights how secure land ownership can foster economic growth through enhanced agricultural productivity.

The distinct colonization strategies employed by Europeans led to varying institutional frameworks in their colonies. Extractive institutions were established where mortality rates for Europeans were high, as these regions did not attract settlers and instead became exploitative outposts for resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) document that in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) show that shifts in land abundance can drive institutional changes. The transition from abundant to scarce land resources in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to a significant institutional revolution, marked by the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor. This transformation was a response to colonial policies and indigenous economic and political actions. Austin (2007, p.20) highlights that the loss of slaves due to emancipation ordinances drove chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.20). This shift in factor markets was crucial as it transitioned from coerced to free labor.

Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by these economic actions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to some hypotheses (Austin 2008, p.15). The adaptive behavior of colonial administrations underscores the dynamic nature of institutional changes which were responsive to local economic conditions and political actions. Austin (2007, p.20) notes that the imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what would become Ghana (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional shifts facilitated market integration and economic development, illustrating how changes in land abundance can drive broader economic transformations.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) argue that African institutions are not monolithic but rather shaped by complex dynamics. These dynamics include the roles of different groups such as women, and the impact of colonial rule on governance. The contributions of women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods highlight how gender dynamics interact with institutional frameworks (Hopkins 2009, p.18). These interactions were not straightforward; colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, codifying the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This codification had significant implications for land access and economic activities. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms; it is crucial to recognize the multifaceted nature of institutions and their interactions with various societal groups.

Economic divergence between Europe and Asia began well before 1800 and was influenced by pre-colonial economic conditions in India (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) argue that the decline of India's per capita GDP relative to Britain started during the early modern period. The collapse of the Mughal Empire led to a significant drop in revenue, which was only partially recovered under British rule (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This economic downturn was exacerbated by institutional factors; Allen shows that the maintenance of real wages in European cities during the seventeenth century was pivotal for economic growth. The divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century had been produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.23). Allen demonstrates that prices changed frequently, indicating a dynamic market environment (Allen 2001, p.8). The institutional changes in Europe allowed for investment in agriculture. Broadberry and Gupta note that high productivity in north-western Europe's traded goods sector was driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in a warm climate may have reinforced economic disparities (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, geographical factors also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that Anglophone African economies exhibited levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema (2012, p.15) supports this perspective with evidence from education outcomes under British rule, which were more conducive to development than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that British colonial institutions fostered economic growth through the development of formal education. The demand for missionary education was driven by African leaders' cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutional changes altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize that the timing and scale of growth varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This variation implies that while British colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth through education, other contextual factors also influenced outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through historical and comparative analyses that highlight the impact of colonial rule on institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that European colonizers introduced varied institutional frameworks across different colonies, with some promoting private property rights and others establishing extractive states to maximize rents for colonists. This variation in institutions had lasting effects on economic outcomes, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. Austin (2008, p.2) reinforces this by arguing that the institutionalist approach emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term growth, but also notes the complexity in explaining why certain favourable institutions were adopted in some countries but not others.

The influence of colonial institutions on economic development is further illustrated by the interaction between population density and prosperity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritized short-term rents over long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who highlights that poor institutional quality in Africa is linked to its dismal economic record, underscoring the importance of 'good' institutions for providing incentives for investment and development.

The impact of colonial rule on education also played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British colonial institutions were more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, altering the perceived benefits of education at both macro and micro levels. This institutional change influenced economic outcomes by fostering a demand for missionary education among African families and political leaders.

The role of women in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods has been marginalized in some views. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic development by migrating in search of farmland for cocoa production, propelling economic revolutions. This perspective challenges the notion that colonial rule uniformly hindered economic growth by highlighting the agency of local populations in responding to new institutional frameworks.

The precise historical evidence and empirical comparisons that settle disagreements about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth are found in the varied institutional frameworks introduced by European colonizers. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to industrialization opportunities and the interaction between institutions and these opportunities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations often altered their prescriptions based on indigenous economic action, reinforcing the importance of local context in institutional development. This evidence underscores that while colonial rule introduced varied institutional frameworks, local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions also played significant roles in shaping long-run economic growth.
