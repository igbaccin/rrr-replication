The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked significant debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures and governance frameworks can either facilitate or impede sustained economic development over extended periods. The live disagreement centres around whether institutional factors are primary drivers of economic growth, or if other elements such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more decisive roles. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and the rule of law are crucial for long-term economic prosperity shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Austin 2008, p.15). Others contend that the impact of institutions is nuanced, influenced by historical contexts and interactions with other factors such as population density and resource endowments. This debate underscores the need to delve into the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate and their interaction with broader economic and social dynamics to understand their role in economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin 2008 (Austin 2008, p.2) both focus on how European colonization strategies shaped institutional frameworks in colonies, but they differ in their emphasis on the causal mechanisms at play. Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood through patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights how institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political actions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that different colonization strategies led to varying economic outcomes in former European colonies by shaping the institutions established during colonial rule. These authors show how Europeans adopted distinct approaches based on mortality rates and settler motivations, which resulted in either extractive or growth-promoting institutions. For instance, high mortality rates in regions like the Congo led to extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer to the metropole, whereas lower mortality areas such as the United States saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show how these institutional differences significantly influenced long-run economic growth by affecting industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, demonstrating that colonies with extractive institutions struggled to industrialize and achieve sustained growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) both argue that shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes in Ghana, focusing on the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The transition from coerced to free labour had an important economic dimension. This phenomenon is particularly evident during the period between 1874-1939 when localised scarcity in Ghana drove an institutional revolution in factor markets. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874, followed by the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908, played a pivotal role in this transition. These legal actions were not merely induced institutional innovations but responded to changing factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.20). Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which led to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. Governor-General Clifford's decision illustrates this dynamic by opting for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule also had significant market-integrating effects, with tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within 'Ghana' being removed (Austin 2007, p.4).

Hopkins (2019, p.3) and Englebert (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3) provide valuable insights into the historical context and development of institutions in Africa. In particular, Hopkins highlights how African economic history has evolved from a marginal field to an area of increasing importance. While this literature does not directly address how these institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth, it illuminates the mechanisms that have shaped institutional reforms over time. For instance, Hopkins (2019, p.3) notes that the study of Africa's economic past requires multiple approaches and techniques to be fully understood (Hopkins 2019, p.13), suggesting that a nuanced understanding of African history can inform policy reform. This is consistent with Hopkins' (2009) assertion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments, implying that other factors such as governance and institutional frameworks play crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18). However, while Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of 'good' institutions for providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8), this perspective is qualified by the recognition that the history of economic development requires a multifaceted approach. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional reforms and their impact on long-run economic growth, suggesting that while institutions are pivotal, their effects are contingent on various historical, political, and socioeconomic factors.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can lead to significant economic transitions, altering traditional systems and property rights (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). Bohannan (1959, p.10) demonstrates that the integration of monetary systems in Africa led to a shift away from multicentric economic structures. This transition was not seamless; it involved complex interactions between local institutions and external economic forces, such as those introduced by colonial powers. The adoption of new technologies like barbed wire allowed for more effective enclosure and protection of land, reducing externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation facilitated a substantial increase in improved farmland, indicating that institutional changes can drive economic transitions when they align with technological advancements. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) notes that the impact of such innovations depends on broader socio-economic contexts, as evidenced by the varying adoption rates and effects of herd laws (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This underscores that while new institutions and technologies can catalyze economic growth, their effectiveness depends on historical and contextual factors.

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that women's contributions to economic development have been significant during pre-colonial and colonial periods. These contributions were often underpinned by access to land resources. Women were typically relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cultivation of cash crops such as cocoa. This gendered division of labor was reinforced through customary law codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional framework shaped agricultural production and influenced broader socio-economic dynamics.

The shifts in women's roles and economic contributions were also driven by broader institutional changes, including the growth of educational institutions and the availability of "nice" jobs. Changes in household technology further altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available (Goldin 2006, p.10). Goldin (2006, p.10) shows that these shifts were not uniform across different birth cohorts, indicating that generational factors also played a role in shaping women's economic participation. This suggests that while institutions can facilitate women's economic contributions, their impact is moderated by demographic and technological factors.

Bryant (2006, p.12) argues that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. This implies that the institutional frameworks supporting women's economic roles in Africa may have been influenced by broader global dynamics, including the ascendancy of Western powers and their colonial policies. The interaction between local and global institutions thus shaped the economic contributions of women during these periods.

Economic divergence between Europe and Asia predates colonialism. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) and Allen (Feinstein 1998, p.28; Allen 2001, p.8) argue that the economic divergence in real incomes became pronounced as early as 1500-1750, with European cities experiencing income declines while Asian economies maintained their levels. This period saw a complex interplay of factors including population changes and shifts in agricultural productivity. Allen (2001, p.8) notes that grain prices, which were wholesale, played a minor role in this divergence, whereas retail bread prices had a significant impact on cost-of-living indices (Allen 2001, p.8). The maintenance of high real wages after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century further underscores this divergence. This pattern varied across regions; for instance, Britain experienced rises in living standards between 1870 and World War I (Allen 2001, p.23). Feinstein (1998, p.28) supports this view by highlighting that real wages improved during and after the Industrial Revolution, challenging pessimistic views of economic stagnation. BroadberryEtal's findings indicate that India’s decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This suggests that while institutional frameworks may have played a role, broader economic and demographic factors were also at play. The absence of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation in India further complicated its economic trajectory (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16).

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa contributed to the development of formal education systems and helped Anglophone African economies achieve levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by local political leaders and families, influenced by new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational development fostered a skilled workforce that could support economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further underscore the importance of foreign trade and export markets in driving economic expansion in these regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, highlighting the multifaceted nature of colonial impacts on African economies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that poverty in Africa was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that the economic conditions under British rule were more dynamic than often portrayed (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). They also note the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates, which leaves some questions about long-term economic development unanswered. This underscores the need for further research to fully understand the complexities of colonial impacts on African economies and the role of institutions in shaping their growth trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is extensively documented across various historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism. These changes resulted from specific policies pursued by Europeans, which varied based on population density and local prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, essential for development. This shared finding (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), that institutional frameworks significantly influence economic trajectories, is further supported by Austin's argument that colonial policies often responded to indigenous economic and political actions. He also shows how European settlers' presence or absence shaped the expansion of commodity production and fiscal policies in colonies (Austin 2008, p.15).

Institutional changes during colonial rule were not uniform across Africa. Frankema argues that British rule was more conducive to developing formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, suggesting a more nuanced impact on African economies than often portrayed (Frankema 2012, p.1). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s observation that European colonists established different institutions based on local conditions and mortality rates, leading to varied economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, in regions where Europeans settled, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Extractive states were set up in areas with high mortality rates, focusing on rapid resource transfer to the metropole.

The debate over institutional impacts on long-run economic growth highlights a point of agreement: institutions matter for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). There is disagreement regarding the specific mechanisms by which colonial institutions shaped African economies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Frankema shows how British rule fostered formal education, suggesting a more positive institutional legacy in some contexts (Frankema 2012, p.1).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement involves the measurement of colonial policies and their differential impacts on local economies. For example, Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of varying colonization strategies and their long-term effects on income per capita provides clear empirical support for the impact of institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is further substantiated by Frankema's detailed examination of education outcomes under different colonial regimes, which shows that British rule had a more positive institutional legacy in terms of formal education development (Frankema 2012, p.1). These studies collectively demonstrate that while extractive institutions generally hindered growth, specific policies like those promoting education could mitigate some negative impacts.
