The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics and economic history. At stake is understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained economic progress over centuries. The live disagreement centers on whether colonial-era institutions were primarily extractive, designed to benefit European powers at the expense of local populations, or if they could also foster growth under certain conditions.

One side argues for an institutional explanation: that colonial institutions, particularly those established by settler colonies, encouraged investment and economic development through secure property rights and rule of law. This perspective posits that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists but also created environments conducive to long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15). Some scholars argue that colonial institutions were primarily extractive, concentrating on resource transfer and development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The question of whether institutional differences between settler and non-settler colonies significantly affected current income per capita remains contentious. The notion that European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, leading to distinct institutional outcomes, highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This interplay is crucial for understanding why some former colonies have experienced sustained growth while others have lagged behind.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic development. This reversal can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which interacted with local factors such as population density and prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These interactions shaped the policies pursued by Europeans and ultimately influenced the economic trajectories of the colonies.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of education. British rule, for instance, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth may vary depending on the specific policies implemented.

The debate extends to whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis accurately captures the causal mechanisms at play. The thesis posits that Europe's economic dominance resulted from its early adoption of institutions favorable for growth, but this perspective oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This critique highlights the need to consider a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution.

The role of women in economic development during colonial periods further illustrates the complexities of institutional impact. Women made significant contributions to economic growth, but their roles were often marginalized due to patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). This points to the importance of considering gender dynamics within the broader institutional framework.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The establishment of extractive institutions was driven by the mortality rates and settlement patterns of Europeans. These institutional differences had profound impacts on current income per capita, illustrating how colonial policies shaped long-term economic trajectories. The endurance of these institutions is attributable to both initial conditions and the continuous influence of politically powerful groups. The implications are clear: understanding the role of colonial institutions requires considering both the historical context of their establishment and the enduring effects of political dynamics on institutional persistence. This perspective underscores the need to examine how institutional changes can drive economic development over the long term, highlighting the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin argues that shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes, specifically an 'institutional revolution' in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This perspective underscores the impact of resource availability on the emergence of new institutions. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) supports this view by highlighting that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism, showing that institutional changes can occur independently of colonial influence (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). These findings collectively suggest that shifts in factor endowments like land abundance can drive significant institutional transformations. Austin adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by merging different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This implies that while institutional changes are driven by shifts in resource availability, the specific nature and timing of these changes can vary widely across different contexts. Austin elaborates on this complexity by discussing how colonial governments' responses to economic incentives created by indigenous action influenced their institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the interaction between local economic conditions and external governance structures is crucial in shaping institutional outcomes, highlighting the multifaceted nature of institutional change driven by resource shifts.

The introduction of money into traditional economic systems can lead to significant shifts in economic structures (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Bohannan's study of the Tiv people in Nigeria illustrates how the integration of monetary systems into subsistence economies can alter local economic dynamics. The influx of trade and markets, facilitated by colonial infrastructure like roads, transformed the Tiv economy from a multicentric system to one more integrated with global networks. This shift was not without challenges, as the introduction of money disrupted traditional exchange institutions such as marriage and food distribution. Hornbeck's analysis of agricultural development in the American Plains further elucidates how new technologies, like barbed wire fencing, enabled better protection of property rights and reduced externalities from livestock damage. The adoption of these technologies led to a notable increase in improved farmland, highlighting how institutional changes can drive economic growth by enhancing resource management and productivity.

The particular results of such changes hinge on local conditions and the character of the introduced institutions. For instance, Bohannan notes that while brass rods served as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere, they could not fulfill all functions of money outside this sphere. This limitation suggests that the effectiveness of monetary systems in driving economic growth is contingent on their compatibility with existing social and cultural frameworks. The introduction of new technologies or institutions may not always lead to immediate or uniform benefits; rather, their impact can vary widely based on how well they integrate with pre-existing structures. Hornbeck's findings support this notion by showing that the effects of barbed wire fencing were more pronounced after its widespread adoption, indicating a lag between institutional change and economic outcomes.

The complexities involved in integrating new monetary systems into traditional economies underscore the importance of understanding local contexts when assessing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Hopkins (2019, p.3) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. These findings are part of a broader reassessment of African economic performance. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era has enabled recent work to demonstrate significant progress in African economic history, challenging previous characterizations. Hopkins (2019, p.13) underscores this point by highlighting that the history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques if it is to be fully understood. This includes recognizing that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). For example, the timing and scale of growth in export production differed greatly between countries due to these factors, showing that institutional changes were context-dependent. Hopkins (2019, p.13) observes that much work remains, especially on precolonial production, indicating that while progress has been made, a comprehensive understanding of African economic history is still developing.

Bryant (2006, p.12) critiques revisionist scholarship for dismissing Eurocentric views, arguing that these views are both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. This institutional advantage is bolstered by the finding that real wages and the standard of living in Britain improved during and after the Industrial Revolution (Feinstein 1998, p.28). The literature on women's contributions to economic growth highlights significant roles played by women in various periods and regions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Goldin 2006, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack document how African women were integral to agricultural economies, particularly during the cocoa revolution in Ghana. Women migrated with men in search of land for cash crops, contributing significantly to economic growth despite being relegated to marginal lands. Goldin (2006, p.3) similarly notes that shifts in U.S. employment patterns were driven by increased demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology, which altered the supply of women in the labor force. These institutional changes, including the growth of educational institutions and the availability of part-time work, facilitated women's entry into the workforce (Goldin 2006, p.10). Goldin (2006, p.3) complicates this narrative by noting that while women began to further their education in professional and graduate schools around 1970, parity with men in fields like law was achieved much later. This suggests that while institutional changes can open new economic opportunities for women, the pace of these changes varies significantly across different sectors and regions.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) establish that understanding how institutions have shaped African economies is pivotal for designing effective policies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) demonstrates that the quality of public goods provision is influenced by institutional frameworks as well as ethnic fragmentation; a society can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional or heterogeneous but still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This indicates that institutional quality, along with ethnic composition, determines economic outcomes. This mechanism highlights the importance of educational institutions in shaping long-term economic development in Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) advise that while historical data on African GDP per capita is scarce, understanding real wage levels and trends can provide insights into the persistence or transience of poverty. They show that a lack of pre-1950 African GDP estimates leaves key questions unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This implies that future research must address these gaps to fully grasp the long-term economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income and the extent to which historical conditions have influenced contemporary welfare outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident from historical and contemporary analyses of colonial legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks depending on the mortality rates they encountered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This approach contrasts with settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where institutions enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These differences in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, as evidenced by current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further argue that population density and prosperity influenced European policies, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies due to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. This institutional divergence is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

The impact of colonial institutions on African development is also highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who notes that poor institutions are linked to Africa's dismal economic record (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Good institutions, which provide incentives for investment and development, are essential for economic progress. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) findings on the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes. However, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth, suggesting that multiple factors beyond initial colonial institutions are at play.
