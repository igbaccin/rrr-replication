The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, as it addresses how societies develop and sustain prosperity over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic development and those who argue that other factors, such as geographic or demographic conditions, play a more significant role. Some researchers emphasize the critical role of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth by providing stable property rights, enforcing contracts, and ensuring accountability. Some scholars posit that these institutional effects are frequently overstated and that historical paths taken by nations, including their colonial legacies and local adaptations, significantly influence economic trajectories (Author Year p.N). The tension between these views underscores the complexity of understanding how institutions interact with other factors to drive long-term economic development.

The centrality of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is evident in the work of Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn, who argue that colonial policies established enduring institutional frameworks that continue to affect income per capita. Their research highlights how European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varied institutional setups across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast experienced extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer to the metropole.

The impact of these colonial legacies is further elucidated by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how institutional changes introduced during colonialism drove the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their work highlights that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive and not conducive to economic development, were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This perspective is supported by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which argues that this framework oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin contends that the institutionalist approach, while emphasizing secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, struggles with explaining why some countries adopted these institutions while others did not. The divergence in historical trajectories highlights the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic development.

The interaction between colonial policies and local adaptations is further complicated by the role of women in African economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and resources crucial for cash crop production. This dynamic illustrates how institutional changes during colonialism interacted with local socio-economic conditions to shape economic outcomes.

The complexity of measuring long-term economic performance is highlighted by Bolt and van Zanden, who emphasize the importance of collaborative research in continuing and improving Angus Maddison's work (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). Their argument underscores that while estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error, collaborative efforts can enhance the accuracy and reliability of these measurements. This perspective aligns with Austin’s examination of Ghana's institutional revolution in factor markets during 1874-1939, which was driven by a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity (Austin 2007, p.4). The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour illustrates how institutional changes can adapt to evolving economic conditions.

Their work demonstrates that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies where they faced high mortality rates, prioritizing rapid resource extraction over long-term development. These institutions aimed to maximize rents for the colonists.

Acemoglu et al. identify a key mechanism: the establishment of extractive states in regions with high European mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional setup was not conducive to long-run economic growth, as it focused on immediate resource extraction rather than fostering investment and development. The persistence of these extractive institutions had profound effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among them.

Acemoglu et al. further argue that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The institutional framework set by colonial powers influenced the ability of these regions to industrialize, which in turn affected their economic growth trajectories. This perspective underscores how initial institutional conditions can have enduring effects on economic outcomes.

The legacy of these institutions persists, highlighting the critical role that institutional changes due to colonialism have had on shaping economic development trajectories over time.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which facilitated economic growth. One key mechanism driving this change involved legal reforms, specifically the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.20). This legislation played a pivotal role in transitioning from coerced to free labour, thereby enhancing economic productivity. The institutional changes spurred by these shifts were both reactive and proactive; colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15), (Austin 2007, p.20). This dynamic highlights the adaptive nature of institutions in responding to local conditions, which is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth trajectories. This process was not uniform; the transition from coerced to free labour varied across different regions and colonial administrations. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium illustrates how institutional prescriptions could be tailored to specific contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). This variability underscores the importance of considering local factors in assessing the impact of institutions on economic growth. The adaptive nature of these institutions, driven by both legal reforms and colonial policy adjustments, highlights their role in shaping long-run economic development.

Bolt and van Zanden argue that refining historical economic data is essential to understanding global economic performance (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). They underscore the significance of collaborative research in improving estimates of national accounts, particularly for periods with limited data. The accuracy of these estimates is crucial for assessing long-term economic growth and development. The margins of error inherent in historical economic data pose challenges. These errors are often based on subjective estimates of underlying data, which can introduce significant biases. For instance, Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even when a solid basis did not exist induced many scholars to work on these themes. Allen et al. while focusing on real wages, provide insights into labour market conditions and regional migration patterns. They note that wage rates can serve as a reasonable proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labour, highlighting the existence of vibrant labour markets in early modern China (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This suggests that real wages, despite their variability, offer valuable insights into economic performance over time. Allen et al.'s analysis also reveals regional disparities in wage rates, indicating that while commercialized regions near major urban centres exhibited integrated labour markets, rural areas with thin long-term labour markets faced lower income levels (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This implies that while real wages can provide a useful indicator of economic performance, their interpretation must account for regional variations and the specific institutional contexts in which they are embedded.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women significantly contributed to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The role of African women in agriculture is particularly notable. This dynamic highlights how gendered institutions influenced economic activity: women’s agricultural work supported broader economic development even as they faced institutional barriers. The introduction of money into African subsistence economies also drove significant changes, altering traditional economic structures. Bohannan describes how the Tiv economy transitioned from a multicentric system to one integrated with global markets, facilitated by increased trade and new road networks (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This integration was not seamless; monetary systems introduced pain points in exchange institutions outside the prestige sphere. The equivalence between brass rods, a form of currency, and other economic rights such as marriage or food production could not be maintained (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This indicates that while monetary systems can drive economic growth, their effectiveness depends on how well they integrate with existing social and cultural institutions.

Technological advance is a central driver of economic divergence, as Allen (2001, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argue. The dynamics of technological progress are crucial in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories, with technological changes influencing productivity and living standards over centuries. For example, Allen's analysis shows that the maintenance of high real wages in certain European cities between 1500 and 1750 was pivotal for sustaining economic leadership during this period (Allen 2001, p.8). This technological progress influenced the cost of living and fostered a cycle of gains that were decisive breaks with past trends. These dynamics are complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the internal capabilities of societies to adapt and innovate. The relationship between technological progress and economic divergence is intricate; it involves interactions with social institutions and market structures. For instance, Abramovitz notes that the concept of "social capability" encompasses technical competence as well as institutional frameworks (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This interaction suggests that while technological advance can drive growth, its impact is mediated by the institutional environment in which it occurs. Therefore, understanding long-run economic growth requires considering both the dynamics of technological change and the role of institutions in facilitating or hindering these changes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and complex, involving intricate interactions with social structures and market dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that 'equilibrium institutions' may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. These institutional frameworks can significantly hinder economic development by prioritizing short-term gains over sustainable progress. This perspective is supported by Austin's (2008, p.15) observation that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between institutional changes and local economic conditions.

Moreover, the impact of institutions on long-run growth is evident in how they mediate technological advancements. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that extractive institutions can impede the adoption of technologies that would otherwise drive growth. This aligns with Austin's (2007, p.20) discussion of institutional changes in factor markets, such as the emergence of land use rights and hired labour markets in Ghana, which were responses to shifting economic conditions.

The historical context further illuminates the role of institutions. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This shows that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence long-run growth trajectories. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonized regions.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles these disagreements is the documented reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This evidence shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in shaping long-run development.
