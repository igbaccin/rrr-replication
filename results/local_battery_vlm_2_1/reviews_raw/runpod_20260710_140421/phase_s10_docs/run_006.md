The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake intellectually is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate over extended periods. Central to this debate are differing views on the relative importance of institutional factors versus other determinants such as geography or culture. Some scholars emphasize that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic development. Others argue that these institutional frameworks are just one piece of a complex puzzle, influenced by historical contingencies and colonial legacies. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run growth or if they operate in tandem with other factors to shape economic trajectories.

This question is complicated by how institutions evolve over time; the same institutional framework that fosters growth in one era may hinder it in another, depending on broader historical and economic contexts. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to vastly different institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Settler colonies like the United States saw institutions that enforced rule of law and encouraged investment while extractive states in places like the Congo were designed for rapid resource extraction. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting impacts on income per capita, highlighting how initial colonial policies shaped long-term economic trajectories. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies these causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He posits that the behaviour of older industrial economies today should be seen in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional change over time.

The debate extends to how institutions interact with economic activities at different stages of development. Acemoglu et al. identify that equilibrium institutions may be extractive because they are shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from maintaining the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is particularly relevant in former colonies where institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run economic growth was not a priority. Austin provides a nuanced view on institutional change in Ghana during 1874-1939, showing how shifts from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4). The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour was driven by changing economic conditions and exogenous policy changes did not play a role. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic factors highlights the importance of considering both endogenous and exogenous influences on long-run growth.

This interplay is further complicated by the role of gender dynamics within institutional frameworks. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). They note how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming under colonial rule, reinforcing patriarchal alliances through customary law. This codification of women's subordinate status highlights how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequalities, affecting economic outcomes over the long term. The interaction between institutions and gender dynamics is crucial for understanding broader economic trajectories.

The measurement of economic performance adds another layer to this debate. Maddison's work on measuring economic performance in the world economy has been foundational, but collaborative research is essential to continue and improve upon it (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Bolt and van Zanden emphasize that estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error, particularly for distant past periods. These errors suggest a degree of objectivity but are based on subjective estimates of underlying data. This underscores the need for ongoing research and collaboration to refine our understanding of long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how resources are allocated and how economic activities are regulated. This role evolves over time in response to changing economic conditions and historical contingencies. Understanding the dynamic interplay between institutions and other factors is essential for comprehending why some economies thrive while others stagnate.

The interplay between technological advancement and social change significantly influences productivity and living standards over time (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights that the dynamics of technological advance are crucial in shaping economic growth trajectories. He suggests that institutional changes can both facilitate and hinder this process, noting that countries' social capabilities, including technical competence, political stability, and financial institutions, play a pivotal role in their ability to catch up economically.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) further elaborates on the complexities of measuring social capability. He argues that while education levels can serve as a proxy for technical competence, the qualitative aspects of institutional frameworks are equally important. This includes the organization and management of large-scale enterprises and financial institutions capable of mobilizing capital effectively.

Allen et al. (2011, p.22) offer a nuanced view by examining real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries. They demonstrate that wage rates can serve as a reasonable proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor, revealing a vibrant labour market during this period. This finding underscores the importance of understanding regional migration patterns and labour market conditions, which are influenced by broader institutional frameworks.

The implications of these findings extend beyond simple convergence hypotheses. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) show that even in regions with high degrees of integration between urban and rural labor markets, agricultural day laborers were at the lower end but not a marginal fringe of the income ladder. This suggests that while technological advances and social changes are critical drivers of economic growth, their impact is mediated by institutional factors that shape labor market dynamics.

This mediation highlights the need for institutions to adapt and evolve in response to changing economic conditions, ensuring that technological advancements translate into improved living standards for all segments of society.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can profoundly alter economic structures, shifting away from multicentric systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bohannan 1959, p.9). This transformation is evident in the Tiv economy, where new road networks facilitated increased trade and market integration. This shift was not seamless; traditional economic institutions were disrupted, altering how resources were exchanged and valued within society. The brass rods used as currency within the prestige sphere of the Tiv economy had limited functionality outside this context. This limitation underscores the complexity of integrating new economic institutions into existing social frameworks (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bohannan 1959, p.9). The pain points in transitioning to a monetary system can be significant, as traditional values and practices may not align seamlessly with modern economic principles. This dynamic highlights the broader implications for long-run economic growth: while technological advances and institutional changes are necessary for development, their success depends on how well they integrate with existing social structures and norms. The Tiv economy's experience shows that the introduction of money can catalyze economic transformation but also requires careful navigation of cultural and social complexities to ensure sustainable growth.

The economic contributions of African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods were significant drivers of growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Allen 2001, p.8). Women's roles in food farming and cash crop production were pivotal in shaping the economic landscape. The de-privileging of women in land access, reinforced by customary law under colonial rule, highlights how institutional frameworks can both enable and constrain economic activity (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These findings underscore the importance of considering gender dynamics within institutional analyses of long-run growth. Institutions such as customary law codified social structures and influenced resource allocation and productivity. The divergence in real incomes between European cities from 1500 to 1750, marked by falling incomes in most cities while leaders maintained their economic standing, further illustrates how institutions can shape long-term economic trajectories (Allen 2001, p.23). This period saw significant changes in living standards, with decisive breaks reflecting broader economic developments. The maintenance of high real wages after the seventeenth century and the rise in living standards between 1870 and World War I indicate institutional stability and adaptation over time. The gains from 1815 to 1850 were part of a cycle, not a trend, suggesting that institutional frameworks can either sustain or disrupt long-term economic progress.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) explore how the transition from an abundance to scarcity of land in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes. This shift drove a revolution in factor markets, particularly through the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 played a pivotal role by legally ending slavery and pawning, which in turn influenced the economic dimension of labor transition from coerced to free (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional change was a response to legal directives and changing factor scarcities. The loss of slaves due to emancipation pushed local chiefs into debt, making them more inclined to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming. As the British colonial administration sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects' material interest in the status quo, they adapted institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2007, p.20). This dynamic highlights how colonial administrations could alter their policies based on local incentives, contrary to simplistic views that assume uniform institutional imposition (Austin 2008, p.15).

This adaptive institutional framework underscores the importance of contextual factors in shaping long-term economic growth. The colonial period in Ghana illustrates how external interventions can interact with local conditions to produce distinct economic outcomes. This integration exemplifies how institutional changes can foster economic growth by creating more efficient market structures. However, it is crucial to recognize that these institutional shifts were not universal but rather tailored to specific historical and geographical contexts.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, prioritizing resource extraction over long-term economic development. High mortality rates discouraged settlement and led to the implementation of extractive states, which hindered local economic activity. This institutional framework persisted, shaping the long-run development trajectories of these colonies. The interaction between extractive institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, highlighting the significance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The significance of institutions is further underscored by their role in factor markets. In Ghana, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.15; Austin 2007, p.20). This transformation was driven by changes in factor scarcities, which necessitated legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance. These changes had important economic dimensions, facilitating the transition from coerced to free labour and integrating markets within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The interplay between institutions and factor market dynamics was crucial in shaping long-run economic growth.
