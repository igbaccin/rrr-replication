The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or climate, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce the rule of law and protect property rights are crucial for sustained economic growth. Others contend that historical and geographical factors have a more profound impact on institutional development and, consequently, economic outcomes. The debate centers on whether extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over local economic development, are attributable to geographic or climatic conditions or institutional design (Acemoglu et al 2001). This question is central to understanding the persistence of poverty in many regions and the pathways to economic prosperity.

Institutions are a key variable in the long-run economic growth of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates, which significantly impacted income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed for rapid resource transfer, while low mortality rates facilitated the establishment of institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law. These institutional differences persisted over time, shaping long-term economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, consistently show similar results in their regressions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Austin's work adds nuance to this view by suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships. He argues that different historical periods and paths need to be considered separately, as the institutional evolution and investment patterns vary significantly over time (Austin 2008, p.2).

A related stream of research emphasizes the role of population density and prosperity in shaping colonial policies. Acemoglu et al. highlight that European colonizers' policies were influenced by local conditions, such as population density and economic prosperity. These factors determined whether colonies would develop institutions conducive to long-run growth or extractive ones focused on resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is further supported by Austin's analysis of colonial administrations that sought fiscal security, which often led to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of women in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods has been significantly overlooked. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women made substantial contributions to economic growth but were marginalized by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional framework relegated women to marginal lands for food farming, further limiting their economic impact. Austin adds that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal structures, which had lasting effects on economic development.

The impact of colonialism on long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the institutional changes it introduced (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). High mortality rates among European colonizers in certain regions led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term resource extraction and foster long-term economic development. The result was a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, with those that experienced extractive colonial institutions lagging behind in economic growth.

These institutions were not designed to encourage investment or enforce the rule of law; they facilitated rapid resource transfer back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al 2001). This institutional framework had lasting effects on economic development, as it did not incentivize long-term growth or investment in local economies.

The persistence of these institutions is a critical factor in understanding the long-run economic disparities between regions.

The legacy of colonial institutions is thus not just about the immediate impact on economic growth but also about the long-term structural barriers they created. The extractive nature of these institutions meant that even after independence, many former colonies struggled to break free from the institutional frameworks that had been established during colonial rule. This enduring influence underscores the profound and lasting effects of colonialism on economic development.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. This perspective is complemented by Austin’s examination (Austin 2007, p.20) of Ghana during 1874-1939. During this period, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. The emergence of widespread markets for land use rights and hired labour was a direct response to these changes in resource availability. This institutional transformation is crucial as it highlights how economic factors can drive institutional change, even in the absence of exogenous shocks. This adaptability suggests that while institutional changes may be driven by resource shifts, they are also influenced by the responsiveness of governing bodies to local economic dynamics. The interplay between resource availability and institutional adaptation underscores the complex nature of long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (2005) both acknowledge that the catch-up process in economic growth is shaped by complex interactions among exogenous factors, technological advancements, and social changes. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) elaborates on this complexity, noting that institutional changes can significantly alter a country's position in the productivity scale (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This perspective aligns with Allen (2005), who argues that economic growth is contingent upon the development of institutions capable of mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprises. These institutions are not merely passive recipients of technological advancements but active agents shaping the catch-up process, particularly in countries with limited initial social capabilities . One specific mechanism through which these institutions influence growth is their role in fostering technological diffusion. Institutions that facilitate the adoption and adaptation of new technologies can accelerate economic convergence by reducing the gap between leading and lagging economies.

However, this process is not linear; Abramovitz (1986, p.5) highlights that measuring social capability, the aggregate of a country's technical competence and institutional strength, remains elusive. This measurement challenge underscores the importance of qualitative assessments in understanding how institutions drive long-run economic growth. The nuanced role of institutions in technological diffusion is further complicated by the endogenous nature of institutional change, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). They demonstrate that institutions can evolve in response to economic factors, even in the absence of exogenous shocks. This adaptability suggests that while institutional changes may be driven by resource shifts, they are also influenced by the responsiveness of governing bodies to local economic dynamics . Therefore, countries with adaptive institutions may experience more sustained growth trajectories as they can better respond to technological and economic changes over time.

The cluster examines real income divergence and wage stagnation in China without directly linking these phenomena to institutional factors (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Allen (2001, p.8) demonstrates that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This suggests that while institutional factors may not directly cause wage stagnation or income divergence, they can influence how economic shocks are absorbed and transmitted across regions. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) show that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, despite significant economic activity and labor market integration. This indicates that even without direct institutional intervention, economic conditions can lead to prolonged periods of wage stagnation. However, Allen (2001, p.8) notes that prices changed frequently, indicating that while institutions may not fix prices for long periods, they can still influence economic outcomes through their impact on price dynamics. This highlights the importance of considering institutional factors when examining long-term economic trends.

The literature on African women's economic contributions and the impact of money introduction does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land, particularly for cash crops. This institutional change had profound effects on economic development, as it altered the dynamics of agricultural production and trade. Bohannan (1959, p.10) similarly highlights how the introduction of money into a subsistence economy led to significant shifts, including increased trade and the establishment of new markets. The introduction of alien institutions perturbed the internal marketing system and integrated local economies with the world market (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This integration facilitated economic growth by expanding trade networks and creating new opportunities for exchange. However, Bohannan (1959, p.10) also points out that brass rods, used as currency within the prestige sphere, had limited functions outside of payment, indicating that not all forms of money introduction led to equivalent economic benefits (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The implications of these institutional changes are complex, as they both facilitated and constrained economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either facilitate or constrain development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies is linked to institutional changes introduced during colonialism. These institutions, often extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, significantly impacted economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin's work supports this view by demonstrating how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, thereby influencing the development of local economies (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation was not uniform; institutional changes varied based on the presence or absence of European settlers and the fiscal needs of colonial governments. For instance, in areas where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of legal and customary frameworks on resource allocation and market integration. Allen highlights that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.23). This period saw significant changes in institutional frameworks, including the codification of customary law under colonial rule. Akyeampong and Fofack show that this codification often marginalized women, reallocating resources and labor in ways that reinforced patriarchal structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Austin 2007, p.20). The shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor. This transformation was driven by the need for economic adaptation in response to changing resource availability (Austin 2007, p.4).

The evidence shows agreement that institutions shape long-run economic growth through their impact on resource allocation and market integration (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). There is disagreement over whether these institutional changes were primarily driven by exogenous factors such as colonial policies or endogenous factors like indigenous economic actions. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, while Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence of institutional adaptation in Ghana during 1874-1939, marked by the emergence of markets in land use rights and hired labor, settles this disagreement. This case demonstrates that while colonial policies played a role, endogenous factors such as changing resource availability and indigenous economic actions were crucial drivers of institutional change (Austin 2007, p.4).
