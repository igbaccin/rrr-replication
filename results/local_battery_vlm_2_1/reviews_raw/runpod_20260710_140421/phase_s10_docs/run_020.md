The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent development trajectories of nations over time. At stake intellectually are the mechanisms through which institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes and the extent to which these mechanisms can explain historical disparities in wealth and prosperity. A key disagreement revolves around whether colonial institutions, particularly those established by European powers, were instrumental in determining long-term economic performance. One position emphasizes the role of extractive institutions, which stifled growth by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few. Another position highlights the significance of localised institutional changes driven by factor market dynamics rather than exogenous colonial policies. The third position argues for broader interpretations that integrate gendered perspectives on economic development, focusing on how women's contributions were shaped by and influenced these institutions over time.

Acemoglu et al argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in different colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions, as seen in settler colonies like the United States and Australia, encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law. Conversely, extractive states in regions such as the Congo or Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, significantly impacting long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al's empirical evidence, which shows that variations in institutional quality, measured through indices like constraints on executive power and property rights, correlate strongly with income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Austin argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies the complex historical paths taken by different economies. He suggests that long-term economic growth is better understood through a nuanced view of institutional evolution over multiple historical periods rather than as a direct result of colonial institutions (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the dynamic nature of institutions and their interaction with local economic and political conditions.

Acemoglu et al further develop this argument by highlighting that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This extractive framework, while not conducive to economic development, persisted due to the political power dynamics of the time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to a significant institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by changes in factor scarcities rather than exogenous colonial policies (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin also notes that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a degree of flexibility and interaction between local conditions and colonial policies (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of women in shaping economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods is also significant. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women made substantial contributions to economic development despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further marginalized women's access to land, particularly in the context of cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective underscores the importance of considering gendered dimensions in understanding institutional impacts on economic growth. Austin discusses how legal actions, such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony, had significant economic implications by transitioning from coerced to free labour (Austin 2007, p.20).

These studies collectively illustrate that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on resource allocation, investment incentives, and political power dynamics. The interaction between local conditions and exogenous factors, such as colonial policies, further complicates this relationship. Understanding these dynamics is essential for comprehending the divergent development trajectories of nations over time.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers implemented different strategies depending on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This contrasts with settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans built institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutional differences shaped long-run economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting how political power dynamics influenced institutional design (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is linked to industrialization opportunities and the interaction between institutions and these opportunities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This implies that the economic divergence observed today can be traced back to the institutional frameworks established during colonial times, which either facilitated or hindered industrial development. These findings collectively illustrate that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was not uniform but varied significantly based on specific historical and geographic contexts, which influenced both the type of institutions introduced and their long-term effects.

This adaptive behaviour of colonial governments suggests that institutional changes resulted from complex interactions with local economic realities. The institutional revolution in Ghana during this period highlights how external policies can interact with local economic conditions to shape long-term economic growth, even if they do not directly address its fundamental causes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Bohannan 1959, p.10) establish that women played crucial roles in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods across Africa. This gendered division of labor, reinforced through customary law under colonial rule, highlights how institutional changes could reshape economic activities without necessarily promoting growth. Bohannan (1959, p.10) shows that the introduction of money and new infrastructure like roads facilitated trade and markets. The dual legal system introduced by colonial powers codified a subordinate status for women in the economy (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This shift from a multicentric economy to one integrated with global markets reveals how economic institutions can evolve through external influences, but may not always foster inclusive growth. Bohannan (1959, p.10) analysis of brass rods as currency illustrates that money can facilitate certain transactions while hindering others, depending on the existing social and cultural frameworks. This dynamic interaction between new economic institutions and pre-existing social structures underscores the complex nature of institutional change in shaping long-run economic growth.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) show that the gap in real incomes between economic leaders and laggards emerged as early as 1500-1750. This divergence was a gradual process where most European cities saw income declines while economic leaders maintained their levels. The maintenance of high real wages in economic leaders, such as Britain, was decisive in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering stable living standards and economic development. This pattern is evident in the British context: population changes influenced real wage trends through long swings in medieval times; thereafter, real wages were sustained at a high level after economic development took hold in the seventeenth century (Allen 2001, p.23). The trend of maintaining high real wages in Britain persisted until it was decisively broken by the sharp rise in living standards between 1870 and the First World War. This historical context underscores how economic leaders managed to maintain their income levels, which in turn supported long-run growth. However, Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). The existence of a vibrant labor market and regional migration in early modern China suggests that wage rates could serve as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor, indicating that economic conditions varied significantly between regions. This regional variation implies that while some areas may have experienced stagnation, others might have seen different trends. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not uniform across different regions, highlighting the need to consider local contexts when examining institutional impacts on economic development.

Abramovitz and Allen et al. argue that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex, influenced by technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Abramovitz posits that convergence of economies is shaped by various factors including institutional changes which can limit or enhance growth. In this view, as economies develop, their institutions evolve in ways that can either support or hinder further economic advancement. For instance, institutional changes might introduce rigidities that slow down technological progress and social adaptation (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). Allen et al complement this perspective by highlighting the significance of wage trends as indicators of broader economic conditions. They suggest that variations in real wages across regions reflect underlying differences in productivity and institutional frameworks. This implies that while some areas may experience stagnation due to institutional barriers, others might see growth spurts driven by favorable institutional environments (Abramovitz 1986, p.21).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the allocation of resources and opportunities for technological progress. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, establishing either extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists or inclusive institutions that encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional frameworks significantly influenced income per capita in colonies, demonstrating that the nature of institutions directly impacts economic outcomes over extended periods. Austin builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal relationships and compresses different historical paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of institutions extends beyond colonial contexts to encompass broader historical frameworks. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin's analysis of Ghana during 1874-1939, where shifts in factor markets led to an institutional revolution marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). These changes illustrate how institutional adaptations can drive economic growth by responding to changing resource scarcities.

Institutional frameworks also shape economic development through their impact on wage trends and productivity. Allen highlights that variations in real wages across regions reflect underlying differences in productivity and institutional settings, suggesting that favorable institutional environments can spur growth (Allen 2001, p.8). This finding is complemented by Acemoglu et al.'s work, which shows that institutions designed to maximize long-run growth are more likely to be adopted when they benefit the broader population rather than just a select few (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The precise role of institutions in shaping economic growth remains nuanced. Both Acemoglu et al. and Austin agree that institutions significantly influence long-term economic outcomes, particularly through their impact on resource allocation and technological progress (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous factors like political power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions are more likely to persist when they benefit politically powerful groups, even if they do not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin counters this by showing how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the role of endogenous factors (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence from Ghana during 1874-1939 provides a clear case study. The shift from land abundance to localized scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This empirical comparison shows that endogenous factors such as changing resource scarcities can drive institutional adaptations that promote economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15).
