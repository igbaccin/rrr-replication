The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures and rules influence economic performance over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions have persistently affected economic outcomes in former colonies. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers have had enduring negative effects on economic growth, while others contend that these institutions are but one factor among many, and that local actors and historical contexts also play significant roles. The debate underscores the need to disentangle the complex interplay between institutional legacies and long-term economic development. To grasp this interplay, it is essential to examine how colonial policies shaped institutional frameworks in former colonies and how these frameworks have influenced economic trajectories over time.

The role of extractive institutions in shaping economic outcomes has been extensively studied by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This approach contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these extractive institutions has significantly affected income per capita in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that a more nuanced understanding of institutional change is necessary to fully appreciate how colonial legacies have influenced long-term economic growth. The debate between these scholars underscores the importance of considering both the initial conditions set by colonizers and the subsequent adaptations made by local actors in shaping economic outcomes over time.

The interplay between institutions and economic development is further complicated by the role of local actors and historical contexts. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political action, adapting their institutional prescriptions accordingly (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is shaped by ongoing negotiations between colonizers and the colonized. In Ghana during 1874-1939, for example, shifting factor scarcities led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). This localised institutional change illustrates how economic conditions can drive institutional adaptation even within a colonial framework. Austin's work on Ghana provides valuable insights into how shifts in factor endowments can lead to significant institutional changes that influence economic outcomes. The interaction between institutions and economic development is thus a complex process involving both external impositions and internal adaptations.

The type of colonial institution thus becomes a critical determinant of long-term economic outcomes, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27). The differential mortality rates among Europeans led to varied colonization strategies; where mortality was high, extractive states were established. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-term growth, had enduring effects on economic development in former colonies. This pattern is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among these colonies, where those with extractive institutions experienced slower economic growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutional legacies highlights how colonialism's impact on long-run economic growth extends beyond immediate resource extraction.

Institutional changes introduced by colonial powers often prioritized the interests of colonists over local populations, leading to enduring economic disparities. This institutional framework created a political economy that favored European colonists at the expense of broader economic development in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The variation in institutions across colonies can be traced back to these differing colonization strategies; where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law-based institutions, long-run growth was more likely to occur (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence is a key factor in explaining why some former European colonies have achieved higher levels of economic development than others.

The interaction between colonial institutions and local conditions further complicates the picture. The legacy of these extractive institutions persists even today, influencing contemporary economic performance in many former colonies. This institutional path dependence underscores how initial colonial policies can have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory, shaping its development for generations to come.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) show that the transformation of land markets in Ghana during 1874-1939 was driven by shifts in factor scarcity. This institutional revolution led to widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which were critical for economic development. The abolition of slavery and pawning further facilitated this transition (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that the colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic actions by altering their policies to create a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive institutional change highlights how local economic dynamics can influence policy outcomes. Austin's work underscores that even within the constraints of colonial rule, endogenous factors played a significant role in shaping economic institutions.

Akyeampong and Fofack document that under colonial rule, men were instrumental in codifying customary laws that reinforced the subordinate status of women. The shift towards a monocentric economy, driven by European trading companies, disrupted local systems that had previously balanced multiple forms of exchange (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This disruption highlights how colonial interventions could reshape economic structures, often at the expense of indigenous populations' diverse economic practices. The specific impact on women's roles in these economies remains under-examined.

This influence of historical wage patterns and social capabilities on economic growth underscores the multifaceted nature of income divergence, as explored by Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights that the catch-up process in economic growth is intricate, driven by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. One specific mechanism involves the role of institutions in mobilizing capital for large-scale enterprise and managing financial markets effectively (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This institutional competence is crucial for technological advancement, as it facilitates the absorption and implementation of new technologies, thereby driving economic growth. Allen (2001, p.8) provides a historical perspective on this, noting that real wage patterns reflect broader economic trends and population changes over long periods (Allen 2001, p.23). For instance, the maintenance of high real wages in certain European cities during the seventeenth century highlights how institutional stability can support sustained economic development.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) illustrate that stagnant real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries suggest a different institutional landscape, one where labor market conditions did not translate into significant wage improvements (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This points to the importance of understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing both technological progress and social capabilities. The intricate interplay between these factors underscores that economic growth is a function of exogenous shocks and heavily influenced by institutional arrangements and their ability to foster long-term development.

The impact of historical wage patterns on economic divergence is significant, as illustrated by the differing experiences of European cities and China during the same period. This highlights how institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic progress through their influence on technological adoption and social capabilities.

Collaborative research is pivotal in refining measurements of economic performance over time. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) underscore the importance of collaborative efforts in improving historical economic data, noting that estimates are often subject to significant margins of error due to partial data and underlying assumptions about economic activities. These errors tend to increase as one delves further into history, though exceptions exist where more recent periods may have poorer data quality than older ones. The collaborative process allows for continuous improvement and updating of historical data, which is essential for accurate economic performance measurements. The Maddison Project's strategy of producing numbers even when the underlying basis was not always solid has been instrumental in encouraging scholars to engage in debate and produce new research (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This approach has led to significant advancements in understanding long-term economic growth, demonstrating the power of collaborative efforts in refining our knowledge of historical economies.

Institutions, particularly those established during colonial periods, have significantly influenced the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was closely tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a crucial role in long-run development.

The extractive nature of many colonial institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-term growth, highlights the enduring impact of these historical legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.15) argues that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This dynamic interaction between colonial policies and indigenous responses further underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes.

The codification of customary law under colonial rule also had profound effects on economic development, particularly for African women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The marginalization of women in access to land and their relegation to less productive activities significantly impacted economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This institutional exclusion highlights how gender dynamics within colonial institutions shaped long-term economic trajectories.

The debate over the reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19), continues to be a focal point for understanding institutional impacts on growth. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that scholars have little difficulty seeing the behavior of older industrial economies today within patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to original industrialization in Europe. The persistence of these institutions underscores their enduring influence on economic performance.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of colonial-era mortality rates and their correlation with long-term economic outcomes. This type of data-driven analysis demonstrates how initial institutional structures, shaped by factors like European mortality rates, have persisted and influenced economic growth over centuries (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
