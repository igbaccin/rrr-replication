The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in development economics, as it helps illuminate why some nations prosper while others lag behind. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the enduring impact of colonial institutions and those who highlight alternative factors such as shifts in factor scarcity and the contributions of marginalized groups. The former group argues that the type of institutions imposed during colonial rule, whether extractive or inclusive, has had lasting effects on economic development, often perpetuating poverty and inequality. This perspective is counterposed by the latter group, which posits that other dynamics, such as changes in resource availability and the economic activities of underrepresented populations, also play crucial roles in shaping long-term growth trajectories.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic development has been extensively studied. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. assert that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies and that these changes were more influential than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in determining economic outcomes, as they can either foster growth or hinder it based on their design. However, Austin challenges this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships, suggesting that different historical periods and paths must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic development is further complicated by the influence of other factors. Austin argues that where European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that local economic activities and political actions can shape institutional prescriptions, even under colonial rule. Additionally, Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These contributions underscore the importance of considering a broader range of actors and factors in understanding long-term economic development.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, linking it to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights the dynamic nature of institutional impact, as institutions can evolve in response to economic opportunities and challenges. The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic development is further illuminated by Austin's argument that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20). This dynamic underscores the importance of considering changes in resource availability when examining the impact of institutions on economic growth.

The differential European mortality rates across regions led to varied settlement strategies and corresponding institutional frameworks. The persistence of these extractive institutions, which were not conducive to long-run economic growth but rather maximized short-term rents for European colonists, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. The institutional legacies of colonialism, therefore, provide a compelling explanation for the divergence in economic outcomes among former European colonies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, but Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) both agree that technological change and social dynamics are crucial factors influencing the economic trajectories of countries (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) emphasizes that catching up in growth involves exogenous factors and the interplay between technological advances and social changes. These dynamics can lead to shifts in productivity rankings among countries, including those at the top, as institutional changes may limit or enhance economic performance (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). They argue that real wages can serve as a proxy for average earnings and the marginal productivity of labor, illustrating how social factors influence economic outcomes (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). Allen (2001, p.8) further elucidates this by showing how price fluctuations and institutional buying arrangements influenced living standards, particularly in the context of grain prices and retail information. However, Allen (2001, p.8) also notes that these price changes were frequent, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between institutions and economic performance than static models might suggest (Allen 2001, p.23). This interplay indicates that while technological change and social dynamics are essential for growth, the institutional framework within which they operate can significantly modulate their impact on long-run economic development.

This adaptive behaviour suggests that institutions evolved through external impositions and endogenous responses to changing economic conditions. The emergence of markets in land use rights and hired labour in Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates this point, as the shift from coerced to free labour had profound economic implications (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin (2007, p.20) qualifies that while these institutional changes were significant, they did not always result in immediate or uniform economic growth. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in African economies is nuanced, reflecting both exogenous and endogenous factors.

Therefore, understanding these nuances is essential for accurately measuring and interpreting economic performance over time. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) emphasize that historical estimates of national accounts are essential for this task, though they are subject to margins of error due to partial data and assumptions about the links between different data points. The further back in time one goes, the larger these errors become; however, exceptions exist where more reliable data is available. For example, data from medieval England may be better than that from nineteenth-century Sub-Saharan Africa or pre-Colombian Latin America. These margins of error are often subjective estimates based on underlying data, which can lead to a false sense of objectivity in the measurements. Maddison's approach was to produce numbers even when a solid basis did not always exist (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This strategy has been highly successful in inducing many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development.

Patriarchal alliances and customary law were pivotal mechanisms by which colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women's access to land and resources. This codification of customary law under colonial rule systematically relegated women to less productive lands. Bohannan highlights that the introduction of money into African subsistence economies led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10), which may have further disrupted traditional gender roles. The transition was not seamless; the integration of local economies with global markets introduced significant challenges in maintaining economic stability and equity.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their influence on economic outcomes and development trajectories. In particular, the nature of colonial institutions established by European powers significantly impacted the economic performance of colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that extractive institutions, characterized by the exploitation of resources for the benefit of the colonizing power rather than local development, were common in regions with high mortality rates among Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions often persisted post-independence and hindered long-run economic growth by failing to foster investment and innovation. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the reversal of relative incomes between former European colonies is attributable to institutional changes introduced during colonialism, which influenced industrialization opportunities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores how institutions shaped by colonial policies continue to affect economic outcomes today.

The interaction between local economies and global markets further complicates the role of institutions in economic growth. Austin (2007, p.20) shows that the shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes, including the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional revolution was driven by economic necessities and not direct colonial policies. It highlights how local economic conditions can prompt institutional adaptations that support growth. Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutional change requires considering multiple historical factors, including local economic conditions and colonial policies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the experiences of African women during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that women significantly contributed to economic growth and development, often overlooked by colonial policies that prioritized male-dominated cash crops over subsistence farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization of women's contributions was reinforced through the codification of customary law under colonial rule. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) emphasize the importance of collaborative research in measuring economic performance, which is crucial for understanding long-term economic growth (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The measurement of national accounts, while subject to margins of error, provides valuable insights into historical economic trends. This underscores how accurate institutional analysis requires robust data and interdisciplinary approaches.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate that extractive institutions hinder development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise nature of this influence remains debated. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the institutional changes in Ghana during 1874-1939, which were driven by local economic conditions and not direct colonial policies. This case study highlights how institutional adaptations can support growth when they are responsive to local economic needs and opportunities (Austin 2007, p.4).
