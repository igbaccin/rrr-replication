The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development studies. This question matters because it helps explain why some regions have prospered economically while others have stagnated. One key disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-term economic outcomes, or if other factors such as geography and climate play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for sustained economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Conversely, another perspective posits that colonial policies and their interaction with local conditions significantly influenced institutional development and subsequent economic trajectories. This debate is critical because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth in developing regions.

The causal role of institutions in long-term economic growth has been extensively studied through the lens of colonial history. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu and his colleagues' work, which emphasizes that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes are seen as more influential than geographic factors in determining long-term development outcomes. Austin's work also highlights how colonial policies interacted with local conditions to shape economic institutions and growth patterns. Specifically, he notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15), suggesting a dynamic interplay between external policies and local developments.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic outcomes is further illustrated by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. attribute this reversal to the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies, which interacted with institutional frameworks to drive industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in mediating the effects of colonial policies on economic development. Austin's analysis complements this view by showing how colonial administrations responded to local economic incentives, thereby shaping institutional outcomes that influenced long-term growth trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of women in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods is another critical aspect of institutional influence. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth despite their marginalization under customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This perspective underscores how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic participation, especially for marginalized groups. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal structures that limited women's access to land and other resources (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), illustrating how institutions can shape economic outcomes by determining who has access to productive assets.

The interplay between colonial policies and local conditions in shaping institutional frameworks is further complicated by the role of factor markets. Austin's study on Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates how shifts from land abundance to localized scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor (Austin 2007, p.4). This transformation was driven by changes in factor endowments and local economic conditions, which in turn shaped institutional responses. The legal actions taken during this period, such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance, had significant economic dimensions that influenced long-term growth trajectories (Austin 2007, p.20).

The measurement of economic performance in the world economy is another area where institutions play a crucial role. Bolt and van Zanden emphasize the importance of collaborative research in continuing and improving Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This perspective highlights how institutional frameworks for data collection and analysis are essential for understanding long-term economic growth patterns. The collaborative efforts to quantify economic development, as exemplified by the Maddison Project, underscore the need for robust institutional mechanisms to ensure accurate and reliable economic measurements (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

These studies collectively illustrate that institutions play a multifaceted role in shaping long-run economic growth. They influence not only how resources are allocated but also who has access to these resources, thereby determining the trajectory of economic development over time.

The paper argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transformation was driven by the need for chiefs to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa due to debt incurred from the loss of slaves. The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule also played a crucial role in market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within 'Ghana' (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2). This institutional revolution was not merely a top-down process but was influenced by indigenous economic and political action, which led colonial administrations to adapt their policies. This responsiveness suggests that the institutional framework in colonial Ghana was more flexible and adaptive than often portrayed, with significant implications for long-term economic growth patterns.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. They argue that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and rule of law, impeded economic development and persisted even after decolonization.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established during colonialism was largely determined by European settlers' mortality rates. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they implemented extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. In contrast, in colonies with lower mortality rates, like the United States and Australia, Europeans settled permanently and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This differential institutional setup had profound long-term effects on economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) illustrate how these extractive institutions created barriers to economic development by failing to incentivize investment or innovation, while simultaneously concentrating power in the hands of a few. This institutional legacy hindered post-colonial economic performance and contributed to persistent income disparities among former colonies.

They underscore that while geographic factors may have initially influenced colonial policies, it was the institutional frameworks established by these policies that ultimately determined economic outcomes. This suggests that even when initial conditions were similar, differences in institutional design could lead to vastly different growth paths.

This perspective highlights how the legacies of colonial institutions can persist and shape long-term economic trajectories even after the end of direct colonial rule.

The historical divergence in real incomes and wage stagnation are pivotal phenomena that help elucidate long-term economic trajectories. Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) explore these patterns without directly attributing them to institutional factors, focusing instead on the economic dynamics of the period. For instance, Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that real wages in Britain showed significant fluctuations but maintained a high level after economic development gained momentum in the seventeenth century. This pattern is mirrored in China during the same era, where wage rates served as proxies for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor, indicating a relatively integrated labor market (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). The stagnation in real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries suggests that economic conditions were stable but not improving significantly. This stability, however, does not directly imply institutional stasis, as Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that labor markets were vibrant and active, particularly for short-term or day labor. The integration of urban and rural labor markets in commercialized regions near major urban centers further supports the idea that economic conditions were complex and multifaceted (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This complexity suggests that while real incomes and wages may have stagnated, underlying economic structures were dynamic and responsive to local needs. Allen (2001, p.8) also notes that prices changed frequently, indicating a flexible economic environment rather than one locked into rigid institutional frameworks (Allen 2001, p.23). These observations collectively imply that the long-term economic trajectories of regions like Britain and China were influenced by a mix of stable and dynamic factors, with institutions playing an indirect but crucial role in shaping these outcomes.

This interplay is further illuminated by the literature on African women's economic contributions and the introduction of money in subsistence economies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack detail how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. The introduction of money into subsistence economies similarly transformed economic structures, as seen in Bohannan's (1959) study of the Tiv people. The new road network and increased trade led to a shift away from a multicentric economy, integrating local markets with the broader colonial economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This integration was not without challenges, as the transition to a monetary system required significant adjustments in social and economic institutions. However, the introduction of money did not uniformly disrupt traditional institutions; rather, it co-existed with them in complex ways. For instance, brass rods served multiple functions within the prestige sphere but had limited equivalence outside it (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This coexistence suggests that while institutional changes were profound, they did not entirely displace existing economic structures. The role of women and the introduction of money both illustrate how institutions can drive economic growth by facilitating trade and land use, even as they create new social dynamics that require adaptation.

The Maddison Project’s collaborative approach has advanced understanding of long-term economic development by highlighting the subjectivity inherent in estimates of national accounts. This is particularly true for distant historical periods, where data may be limited or incomplete (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). Maddison himself acknowledged these uncertainties and encouraged scholars to build on his work, generating new data and fostering an iterative process crucial for continuous improvement in the accuracy of economic performance measurements.

The necessity of collaboration becomes even more apparent when considering the challenges posed by incomplete or partial data. For instance, early estimates of national accounts often rely on limited datasets and assumptions about their broader economic implications (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Collaborative efforts can mitigate these issues by pooling expertise and resources to develop more robust methodologies for estimating margins of error. This collaborative approach not only refines existing data but also fosters a dynamic research environment where new insights and corrections are continually incorporated into the historical record.

While some scholars may disagree on specific methodologies or interpretations, the collaborative framework allows for these debates to be resolved through evidence-based discussions rather than subjective opinions. This process ensures that the field remains dynamic and responsive to new findings, ultimately leading to a more comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz argues that economic growth convergence is complex and influenced by technological advancements and social changes (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Abramovitz 1986, p.5). The catch-up process in economic growth is not merely a function of simple convergence but is deeply intertwined with the dynamics of institutional change. Abramovitz's work highlights how institutions, particularly those related to political, commercial, industrial, and financial sectors, play a crucial role in mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprises (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This perspective underscores that technological progress alone cannot drive economic growth; rather, it is the synergy between technology and institutional frameworks that facilitates sustained development. The social capability of a country, encompassing its technical competence and institutional robustness, is pivotal in this process. However, measuring these capabilities remains challenging, as they involve qualitative aspects that are hard to quantify (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This complexity suggests that while technological advancements spur economic growth, the effectiveness of institutions in adapting to and integrating these advancements determines the extent and sustainability of such growth. This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions highlights that economic convergence is not a linear process but one influenced by multifaceted factors, including the adaptability of institutions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and geographical contexts of colonies and their interactions with European powers. Acemoglu et al. argue that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas in others, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This divergence highlights how initial institutional frameworks, shaped by colonial policies, significantly influenced long-term economic trajectories. Austin further elaborates on this theme, noting that indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial governments' interests (Austin 2008, p.15). The interaction between local economic dynamics and colonial policies underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional development.

The persistence of these institutions over time is crucial for understanding current economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, can persist because they benefit politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin's observation that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15), suggesting a dynamic interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes. The role of women in this context is particularly notable, as Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Their contributions were often marginalized by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20), illustrating how gender dynamics also influenced institutional outcomes.

The measurement of long-term economic performance is essential for understanding these historical processes. Bolt and van Zanden argue that collaborative research is crucial for continuing and improving Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance in the world economy (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This collaborative approach allows for a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth, as seen in the integration of colonial Ghana into a unified market system under British rule (Austin 2007, p.4), which had significant economic implications. The codification of customary law and the emergence of markets in land use rights and hired labour further illustrate how institutional changes responded to shifting factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.20).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). However, the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. For instance, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), suggesting that a more nuanced understanding is needed. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the detailed measurement of economic performance over time, as advocated by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2). This approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of how institutional changes have influenced long-term economic growth across different regions and periods.
