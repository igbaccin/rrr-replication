The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy debates. The stakes revolve around identifying the causal mechanisms through which institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over extended periods. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are endogenous to broader socio-economic processes or exogenous drivers of economic change. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those ensuring property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained growth, while others contend that these institutions are often shaped by deeper historical and political forces.

Acemoglu et al., in two studies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), and Austin (2007, p.20; 2008, p.15), examine the impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, leading to extractive or inclusive institutions. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, were prevalent in high-mortality colonies. Colonies with low mortality rates saw the establishment of more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods are highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack. They argue that women played a significant role in agricultural production and economic development, despite being marginalized under customary law codified during colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin's work on Ghana further illustrates how shifts in factor markets, driven by changes in land abundance and scarcity, led to institutional innovations such as the emergence of land use rights and hired labor markets. These developments were crucial for economic growth during the late 19th and early 20th centuries (Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al.'s work on colonial institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be explained by institutional changes introduced during colonialism. The different colonization strategies of Europeans led to the establishment of either extractive or growth-promoting institutions, depending on factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in areas where Europeans settled permanently, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were created. This institutional divergence significantly impacted long-run economic growth, with former colonies characterized by extractive institutions experiencing slower development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). For instance, Acemoglu et al.'s analysis reveals that the interaction between these colonial institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century played a pivotal role in shaping the long-term development trajectories of these regions. However, Acemoglu et al.'s findings are qualified by the recognition that institutional changes are not solely driven by colonial legacies but also influenced by local political dynamics and power structures. This highlights the complex interplay between historical institutional changes and contemporary development outcomes, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of these factors is essential for fostering long-run economic growth in former colonies.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2007, p.20) argues that the transition from coerced to free labour, driven by British emancipation ordinances and subsequent prohibitions on slavery, had important economic dimensions. The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour illustrates how localised scarcity can drive significant institutional changes. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) supports this view by highlighting that the catch-up process is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change, suggesting that institutional changes are part of a broader economic transformation (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This perspective underscores how institutional dynamics can be both a cause and an effect of economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) points out that social capability, which includes technical competence and well-functioning institutions, is crucial for economic growth but challenging to measure quantitatively (Abramovitz 1986, p.5).

The implication is that while institutional changes driven by scarcity can spur economic growth, the effectiveness of these changes depends on the broader social and technological context. This nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics highlights the complexity of long-run economic growth, where institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes but are themselves influenced by a myriad of factors including legal frameworks, factor scarcities, and socio-technological advancements. This interplay suggests that fostering long-run economic growth requires institutional reforms as part of a comprehensive approach addressing the broader social and technological environment.

The economic impact of institutional changes in Africa illustrates how growth and development are influenced by various factors, but institutions themselves are not always framed as the primary driver (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule reinforced gender disparities in land access. The introduction of a dual legal system, comprising European courts based on common and civil law alongside native courts, further entrenched these inequalities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The introduction of money and new road networks increased trade and market activity, but this transition was not without challenges (Bohannan 1959, p.10). Despite these profound changes, the shift from a multicentric economy to one more integrated with the world economy did not uniformly benefit all segments of society. The economic institutions of groups like the Tiv in Northern Nigeria were perturbed by alien institutions introduced through colonial rule (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This dynamic underscores that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, their impact is mediated by existing social structures and power dynamics, such as gender relations and legal frameworks.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) both point to a critical period between 1500 and 1750 when real incomes diverged across European cities. This divergence was marked by falling incomes in most urban centers while economic leaders managed to maintain their income levels. Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that this period saw significant shifts in prices, with grain prices playing a minor role compared to the broader cost of living indices, which were heavily influenced by retail information from noninstitutional sources (Allen 2001, p.8). This price dynamics likely reflected institutional differences in how cities managed their economies and responded to changes in supply and demand. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) complements this view by noting that the maintenance of high real wages in economic leaders was a result of sustained economic development, which gathered strength during the seventeenth century. The institutional frameworks in these leading cities likely facilitated long-term supply arrangements and bulk buying, contributing to their resilience against income declines observed elsewhere (Allen 2001, p.23). This suggests that robust institutions can buffer economies from adverse shocks, ensuring sustained growth over extended periods. The pattern of long swings reflecting population changes in the medieval period further underscores the dynamic nature of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over centuries.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical periods and paths (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis compresses diverse historical experiences into a narrow framework, failing to account for the complexities of institutional evolution. This critique is shared by Allen et al. (2011, p.22), who observe that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, challenging the view of significant improvement during this period. By focusing on wage rates as a proxy for economic conditions, Allen et al. highlight the need for nuanced analyses that consider local labor market dynamics and regional variations. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions adapt over time in response to specific economic contexts rather than assuming uniform outcomes across different regions. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this point by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional policies in reaction to indigenous economic activities, illustrating a more dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.2; Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This dynamic nature of institutions suggests that long-run economic growth is shaped by adaptive processes rather than static frameworks. The complexity of historical institutional development requires detailed analysis of local contexts and regional variations to fully understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on resource allocation and economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced subsequent economic trajectories. In colonies where Europeans settled and implemented inclusive institutions, such as the United States or Australia, these institutions encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions where extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, long-term growth was hindered (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The dynamic nature of institutional development is further highlighted by Austin's analysis. Colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic activities, illustrating a more nuanced interplay between local conditions and institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive process shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political pressures, influencing long-run growth trajectories.

The role of women in African economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods underscores the complexity of institutional impacts. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that women significantly contributed to economic growth, despite being marginalized by colonial policies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This contribution was often overlooked but crucial for understanding the broader economic dynamics in African societies.

Austin shows that this period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by changes in factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional adaptation highlights how economic conditions can drive institutional change, which in turn shapes long-run growth.

Both Acemoglu et al. and Austin underscore that institutions are shaped by powerful groups who may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. For example, European colonists often designed institutions to maximize their own rents rather than promote sustainable economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is reinforced by Austin's observation that colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional policies (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth lies in the detailed analysis of local contexts and regional variations. Acemoglu et al.'s argument about the impact of extractive versus inclusive institutions, grounded in mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), is complemented by Austin's emphasis on the adaptive nature of institutional policies in response to indigenous economic activities (Austin 2008, p.15). The measurement of these interactions through case studies and empirical data shows that long-run economic growth is shaped by a complex interplay between local conditions, institutional changes, and the actions of powerful groups.
