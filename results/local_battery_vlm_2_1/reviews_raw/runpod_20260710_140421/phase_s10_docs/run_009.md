The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that lies at the heart of debates about development and prosperity. Institutions are widely understood as the rules of the game in society, which can either facilitate or hinder economic progress. A central disagreement revolves around whether colonial institutions laid the foundation for enduring disparities in income levels between former colonies, or if other factors, such as geography, climate, or cultural differences, were more decisive. One position argues that extractive institutions imposed by colonizers have had lasting negative effects on economic development, while another perspective suggests that institutional changes during colonial rule were more complex and multifaceted, with varying impacts across different regions and periods.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth highlights the importance of understanding how historical legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. This issue is particularly salient for countries that were once colonized, as it raises questions about whether their current economic trajectories are largely determined by past institutional frameworks or if there are other factors at play. The nuances of this debate underscore the need for a comprehensive analysis of how institutions have evolved over time and their specific impacts on economic growth in different contexts.

The causal relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with some scholars emphasizing the enduring impact of extractive institutions imposed by colonizers while others point to more complex interactions between institutional changes and local conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks in colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were shaped by the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in different regions, which influenced their settlement patterns and the types of institutions they implemented. European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, with one extreme being the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). In contrast, extractive states were set up in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had significant long-term effects on income per capita in these colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further emphasize that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not limited to their immediate effects but persists over generations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These authors highlight that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, have had lasting negative consequences on the economic development of former colonies. The interaction between colonial institutions and local conditions is further illustrated by the role of indigenous commodity production and its impact on institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, leading them to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic highlights how local conditions and economic activities influenced the evolution of institutions during colonial rule.

The role of women in shaping long-run economic growth is an important aspect that has often been marginalized in discussions about institutional legacies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, particularly in regions like the Gold Coast where they played a crucial role in agricultural production. The de-privileging of women in access to land for food farming was reinforced by the central role of men in codifying customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dynamic highlights how gendered power structures and institutional changes during colonial rule affected economic growth and development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly impacted long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that these institutions were shaped by European colonizers to extract resources efficiently from their colonies rather than to foster local economic development, and that this dynamic led to a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27); the reversal was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. One specific mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced long-run growth is via the establishment of extractive states. This focus on short-term extraction meant that investments in infrastructure and education were often neglected, leading to persistent underdevelopment. However, the impact of these institutions was not uniform across all colonies. This variation highlights how specific environmental factors influenced the type of institutions established by colonizers, which in turn shaped economic outcomes. The legacy of these colonial institutions persists today, as many former colonies still grapple with the effects of extractive governance structures.

Austin (2007, p.20) and (Akyeampong and Fofack (Austin 2008, p.2)) demonstrate how shifts in resource availability can drive institutional changes that fundamentally alter economic trajectories. By examining Ghana during 1874-1939, Austin shows that the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity led to a significant institutional revolution. This shift was marked by the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional changes were driven by the need to adapt to new economic realities, such as the loss of slave labor and the subsequent demand for free labor (Austin 2007, p.20). These developments highlight how institutional adaptations can be induced by changing factor scarcities, aligning with broader historical patterns of institutional evolution observed in other contexts. However, the specific outcomes of these adjustments could vary widely depending on local conditions and governance structures. This variability underscores the complex interplay between environmental factors, institutional responses, and economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Abramovitz 1986, p.5) highlight that the catch-up process in economic growth is subject to complex dynamics, influenced by both exogenous factors and the interplay of technological advancement with social change. This implies that countries aiming to achieve rapid economic growth must navigate a landscape shaped not only by their own efforts but also by global technological trends and institutional changes. One key mechanism through which these factors operate is the development of 'social capability', technical competence, educational attainment, and robust institutions capable of mobilizing capital effectively (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This social capability is crucial for absorbing and implementing new technologies, thereby facilitating economic growth. However, the challenge lies in quantifying and measuring this capability accurately, as it encompasses both tangible and intangible elements such as experience with large-scale enterprise management and financial institutions (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Abramovitz 1986, p.5). The implication is that countries must invest not only in physical capital but also in building institutional frameworks that support technological advancement and social change. This investment includes fostering educational systems, developing strong governance structures, and creating financial markets capable of mobilizing resources efficiently. Without these foundational elements, the catch-up process can be hindered by institutional sclerosis or arthritis (Abramovitz 1986, p.21).

Allen et al. along with Allen (2001, p.8) (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Allen 2001, p.8), examine real wages in historical contexts, focusing on the stagnation of Chinese wages from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries. They find that China's stagnant real wages during this period were indicative of a broader economic and institutional environment that prevented significant improvements in living standards. The paper by Allen (2001, p.8) points out that prices, including those for grain and other essential goods, changed frequently, suggesting dynamic market conditions rather than fixed prices over long periods. This dynamism implies an active labor market where wages could respond to changing economic conditions; however, this responsiveness did not translate into sustained improvements in real wages. Allen et al (2011) (Allen et al. 2011, p.24) further substantiate that the vibrant and active labour markets in early modern China indicate a relatively high degree of integration between urban and rural areas, suggesting that wage rates could serve as proxies for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor. However, despite this market integration, real wages remained stagnant, highlighting the limitations of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic growth during this period. The implications are profound: even with active markets, the absence of supportive institutions can hinder economic development.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2), along with the Maddison Project's research, emphasize that accurate measurements are crucial for understanding historical economic performance. They argue that collaborative research is essential to continue and improve Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance in the world economy. Their approach involves estimating margins of error and refining national accounts, which are often based on partial data and assumptions about economic activities. This method acknowledges that while estimates may have subjective components, they can still provide a degree of objectivity. The collaborative nature of this research not only enhances the accuracy of historical economic measurements but also fosters a community of scholars dedicated to refining these estimates over time. However, the success of such collaborative efforts hinges on the willingness of scholars to engage with and build upon existing work, as well as the availability of reliable data sources.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15)  and Bohannan (1959, p.10) show that economic changes in African economies involved the introduction of new institutions. The integration of colonial legal systems and monetary frameworks into traditional structures led to significant shifts in economic activities, with specific impacts on different segments of society. These institutional changes were not merely imposed but interacted with existing social norms and practices. This legal codification affected access to land and economic opportunities, particularly for women engaged in cash crop farming. Bohannan highlights similar disruptions in the Tiv economy, where the introduction of a new road network and trade routes altered traditional exchange systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The economic implications of these changes were profound: they shifted power dynamics within societies and altered the ways in which resources were distributed. However, these shifts were not uniform across all groups. Bohannan points out that while brass rods served as a general-purpose currency within certain spheres, they did not fulfill all functions of money outside those spheres (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This suggests that the introduction of new monetary systems did not entirely replace traditional economic institutions but rather coexisted with them in complex ways. The downstream implication is that the effectiveness of these new institutions depended on how well they integrated with existing social and economic frameworks, a pattern that can be observed in other contexts where institutional changes have led to varied outcomes based on local conditions.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on income per capita, with extractive institutions leading to lower economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This shows that institutions matter greatly in the long run.

The role of gendered institutions is also crucial in understanding how colonial policies affected economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite facing marginalisation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This underscores the importance of considering gendered dimensions when examining institutional impacts on long-run economic growth. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal structures, which had profound effects on land access and economic activities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).
