The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods, with implications for policy and development strategies. This debate pivots on two key positions: one that emphasizes institutional structures as primary determinants of economic outcomes; another that highlights the complexity of interactions between institutions, geography, and historical contingencies. The former view posits that certain types of institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce contracts, are essential for sustained growth. The latter acknowledges these factors and argues that their effects are mediated by various other variables including cultural practices, political dynamics, and technological changes. The disagreement hinges on whether institutional frameworks can be isolated as independent variables or must be understood within a broader web of interdependent factors.

Acemoglu et al., in 2001 and 2002, present a strong case for the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes. They argue that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional frameworks in colonies, with some establishing extractive states focused on resource transfer while others developed more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores how initial institutional setups can have lasting impacts on a region's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions are actively shaped by political and economic interests which in turn influence long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin also engages with the institutional perspective, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. He highlights how colonial governments adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between institutions and local conditions (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This view complements Acemoglu et al.'s argument by showing that institutional frameworks evolve in response to various factors. Institutions can be shaped by the need for fiscal security or indigenous economic activities, demonstrating a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with local contexts and influence long-term growth.

A complementary perspective is provided by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who focus on the contributions of African women to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women played significant roles in agricultural production, often being relegated to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This highlights how gender dynamics within institutional frameworks can influence economic outcomes, adding another layer of complexity to the understanding of institutions' role in growth. Austin further elaborates on this by discussing how shifts in factor markets, such as land abundance and scarcity, led to an institutional revolution marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This suggests that institutional changes are not just driven by exogenous factors like colonization but also by endogenous economic pressures. Allen provides a different temporal focus, arguing that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.8). This indicates that institutions' role in shaping long-run growth has deeper historical roots beyond colonial or post-colonial periods.

The interplay of institutions with economic, social, and political factors underscores their multifaceted role in shaping long-term economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely depending on mortality rates and other factors, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In many cases, European colonizers set up states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, rather than fostering long-term economic development in the colonies themselves. These institutions persisted even after decolonization, continuing to shape economic outcomes in these regions.

Institutional legacies from colonial rule have had profound effects on post-colonial economies. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to affect the economic performance of former colonies today.

The shift from abundant to scarce land in Ghana during 1874-1939 drove an institutional revolution. The emergence of these markets was driven by internal dynamics such as the loss of slaves driving Akyem chiefs into debt which made them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming. The removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within 'Ghana' further reinforced this market integration, highlighting how internal factors shaped institutional changes that facilitated long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by various factors beyond simple convergence hypotheses (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). He highlights social capability as pivotal for facilitating technological change and institutional development (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Abramovitz points out that this social capability includes technical competence, robust political institutions, commercial frameworks, industrial management, and financial markets capable of mobilizing capital. These factors are crucial for managing large-scale enterprises and fostering innovation. The qualitative nature of social capability makes it challenging to measure, suggesting that its impact on economic growth may not always be straightforward (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This complexity implies that countries with similar educational levels might still exhibit different growth trajectories depending on the effectiveness of their institutions in mobilizing capital and fostering innovation. For instance, a country's ability to adapt its institutions to changing circumstances can significantly influence its growth trajectory.

The stagnation of real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries underscores a period of economic stasis (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Allen 2001, p.8). Allen et al. argue that this period saw minimal improvement in living standards, challenging narratives of significant economic progress during these years. The stagnation can be attributed to several factors, including the limited integration of labour markets and the prevalence of day labour, which constrained wage growth (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This economic environment suggests that structural barriers, such as institutional rigidities and market inefficiencies, played a crucial role in maintaining low real wages. For instance, Allen (2001, p.8) notes that prices for essential goods like grain and bread were influenced by both wholesale and retail markets, indicating complex supply chains that could affect wage levels (Allen 2001, p.8). The stagnation of real wages also reflects broader economic trends, including population changes and long-term supply arrangements. Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that real wage history in Britain featured significant breaks with the past marked by periods of high real wages and rises in living standards (Allen 2001, p.23). This comparison suggests that institutional factors, including those related to labour markets and economic development, are pivotal in shaping long-term economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack examine how African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The paper by Akyeampong and Fofack argues that the role of women in Africa's economic development has been marginalized, while Bohannan documents how the introduction of money into an African subsistence economy led to profound changes. A key mechanism through which this occurred was the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced patriarchal alliances and limited women's access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This shift in economic institutions, driven by colonial intervention, had a downstream implication on the gendered division of labor and resource allocation. However, Bohannan (1959, p.10) also notes that the introduction of money and new trade networks led to increased markets and internal trade, suggesting that while institutional changes under colonial rule had significant impacts on women's economic roles, they also facilitated broader economic growth (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The complexity of these shifts highlights how social and institutional changes in Africa were both driven by and contributed to long-term economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that "equilibrium institutions" can be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-term growth. This perspective is supported by Austin's analysis (Austin 2008, p.15), which shows how colonial administrations tailored institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. The interplay between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the 19th century was crucial in determining the long-run development of former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). The enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes is evident in how they shaped resource allocation and economic policies.

The role of institutions in long-term economic growth is further illuminated by examining their effects on specific sectors and groups within society. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite being marginalized in access to land for cash crops under colonial rule. This dynamic is also reflected in Austin's work on Ghana, which shows how the shift from land abundance to localized scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20). The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor was a direct response to changing economic conditions, underscoring the adaptive nature of institutions.

The historical evidence reveals that institutions evolve in response to economic changes. Austin's work on Ghana demonstrates how institutional changes were driven by factors such as the loss of slaves and the need for chiefs to sell land to strangers (Austin 2007, p.20). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may resist change if it threatens their rents or political power. The historical evidence shows that institutional changes under colonial rule had significant impacts on women's economic roles and facilitated broader economic growth, as shown by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15). This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth through their influence on resource allocation and economic policies.
