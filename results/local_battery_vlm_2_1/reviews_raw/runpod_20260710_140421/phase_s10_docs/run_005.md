The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At the heart of this disagreement lies the tension between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that geographic factors play a more significant role. The former position, championed by scholars like Acemoglu et al., emphasizes how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This perspective posits that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote growth, were a direct result of colonial policies. Meanwhile, Austin's work challenges this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Allen 2001, p.23). This debate underscores the complexity of understanding how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic trajectories. The stakes are high, as resolving this disagreement could provide critical insights into the mechanisms behind sustained economic development, offering valuable lessons for contemporary policy-making.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The type of institutions established by European colonizers varied greatly depending on factors such as mortality rates and the settler population. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they tended to establish extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach was evident in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was resource extraction. The persistence of such institutions is attributed to the influence of politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo, even if it meant forgoing long-term economic growth. This dynamic was further elaborated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argued that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote sustained development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between these institutional frameworks and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. It is important to acknowledge that while institutions are crucial, they do not operate in isolation; other factors such as population density and prosperity also influenced the policies pursued by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interplay between institutional changes and broader socio-economic conditions underscores the complex nature of colonial impact on economic growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that shifts in land abundance and scarcity can drive institutional changes. These shifts alter the economic landscape by influencing the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor. The historical context of Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates this dynamic, where localised land scarcity prompted significant institutional transformations. This process was driven by the need to adapt to changing factor scarcities, which in turn influenced economic policies and market integration. For instance, the British emancipation ordinance in 1874 and subsequent prohibitions on slavery and pawning in Ashanti (Austin 2007, p.4) reflect how legal changes responded to evolving labor dynamics. These institutional adaptations were proactive, as colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This interplay between land scarcity, institutional innovation, and market integration highlights the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The introduction of money into traditional African subsistence economies triggered significant shifts in economic structures and social dynamics, as demonstrated by Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Akyeampong and Fofack. The integration of monetary systems disrupted the multicentric economy, replacing it with a more centralized one. This transition was facilitated by colonial infrastructure developments such as new road networks, which enhanced trade routes and market accessibility (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This economic transformation introduced challenges to traditional institutions, including marriage and exchange practices, which were historically intertwined with the prestige sphere of brass rods currency (Bohannan 1959, p.9).

The monetary integration into African economies had profound implications for local economic systems. The colonial introduction of money altered traditional exchange mechanisms and disrupted existing social structures. By examining the Tiv economy in Northern Nigeria, Bohannan shows that this change was not merely a superficial shift; it fundamentally altered how value and wealth were perceived and exchanged (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This disruption underscores the broader impact of economic transitions on indigenous systems, highlighting the transformative power of monetary integration.

The transition to a monetary-based economy also affected the roles and functions of traditional currencies within different spheres of society. This limitation underscores how economic transitions can selectively impact certain aspects of a society while leaving others relatively unaffected. The inability to establish equivalency between brass rods and other forms of exchange highlights the complexity of integrating new monetary systems into traditional economies.

The broader implications of these findings are significant for understanding long-run economic growth in developing regions. The introduction of money and the subsequent shift away from multicentric economies highlight how economic transitions can reshape institutional frameworks, influencing growth trajectories over extended periods. This insight underscores the importance of considering institutional adaptations when analyzing economic development in historically subsistence-based societies.

The transformative impact of monetary integration on African economies illustrates how institutional changes can drive long-run economic growth by reshaping traditional systems and fostering new forms of exchange.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20), along with Lawrance et al., find that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging marginalizing views. Their contributions were multifaceted, including significant agricultural labor and entrepreneurial activities. This transition was a proactive strategy that propelled regions like the Gold Coast to become global leaders in cocoa production. Colonial rule introduced legal and social structures that reinforced patriarchal norms. This legal framework, while restricting women's economic autonomy, also underscores the resilience of African women in navigating institutional constraints to maintain their economic contributions. Despite these challenges, women continued to drive agricultural and economic development, illustrating how their agency shaped long-run growth trajectories even within restrictive institutional frameworks. This dynamic interplay between institutional changes and women's economic roles underscores the complexity of African economic development during colonial rule.

The divergence in real wages between regions like China and Europe during the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries highlights significant differences in economic development trajectories (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Allen 2001, p.8). This divergence is evident in Allen et al.'s analysis of real wage trends. They show that while European cities experienced a decline in incomes from 1500 to 1750, China maintained relatively stable wages during this period. The stagnation in Chinese real wages can be attributed to the lack of significant technological advancements and institutional changes that drove economic growth in Europe (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). For instance, Allen et al. note that the vibrant labour market in early modern China facilitated the integration of urban and rural areas, but this did not translate into substantial wage increases. The maintenance of high real wages in Europe after the seventeenth century indicates that institutional changes, such as those driven by colonial powers or internal political developments, likely supported economic development. This points to the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, as seen in the divergence between China and Europe.

Abramovitz and others highlight that the catch-up process in economic growth is intricate and shaped by multiple factors beyond mere convergence (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This complexity arises from the interplay between technological advancements and social capability. Abramovitz defines social capability as encompassing technical competence and the institutional frameworks that support large-scale enterprise and financial markets (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). These factors are crucial for mobilizing capital and managing complex economic activities, which in turn drive growth. The role of institutions is particularly evident in the context of technological change; social capability includes the ability to absorb and implement new technologies effectively. This absorption process is not uniform across countries; it depends on the institutional environment that supports innovation and adaptation.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to influence technological adoption and adaptation. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks; these frameworks shaped the subsequent economic trajectories of colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, countries where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment saw significant growth, while those with extractive states experienced stagnation or decline. This is echoed by Austin who argues that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic actions, which influenced long-term economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation was not uniform; it depended on the specific colonial policies and the interactions between European settlers and indigenous populations.

The interaction between institutions and technological change is crucial for understanding growth patterns. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights how institutions can either facilitate or hinder the absorption of new technologies. Austin supports this view by noting that institutional changes in response to local economic conditions were pivotal in shaping development paths (Austin 2008, p.15). The interplay between these factors shows that robust educational systems and well-developed financial institutions are essential for leveraging technological advancements effectively.

The historical evidence from colonial periods provides a clear lens through which to view the impact of institutions on growth. Acemoglu et al.'s work demonstrates that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by Austin, who argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Together, these sources show that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutional frameworks and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), which played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.
