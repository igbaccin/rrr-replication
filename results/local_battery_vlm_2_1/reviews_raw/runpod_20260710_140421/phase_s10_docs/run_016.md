The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures influence economic trajectories over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or colonial policies, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacts long-term economic development. Others contend that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographical factors.

Institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are key drivers of economic growth, according to Acemoglu et al., who argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies based on mortality rates. In areas with high mortality, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a crucial role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Colonial policies and their impact on economic growth are complex. Austin argues that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of these interactions (Austin 2008, p.15). This view is complemented by Akyeampong and Fofack's observation that patriarchal alliances and customary law codified during colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, affecting economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between institutional changes and local factor markets. Austin notes that the shift from land abundance to scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional change was driven by the loss of slaves and the need for chiefs to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa.

This underscores the need for rigorous and collaborative efforts to improve the accuracy of historical economic data.

The complexities of economic growth and technological advance are underscored by the role of social capability and institutional change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Abramovitz posits that exogenous factors influence the catch-up process in economic growth along with technological advance and social change, which complicates simple convergence hypotheses. He identifies a country's social capability with technical competence and its institutional framework, including political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). These capabilities are crucial for organizing and managing large-scale enterprise and mobilizing capital, factors that can significantly impact economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden highlight the challenges in measuring economic performance, noting that estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error, especially in the distant past. This uncertainty underscores the need for rigorous and collaborative efforts to improve the accuracy of historical economic data (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The further one goes back in time, the larger these margins of error become, which can obscure the true extent of institutional change and its impact on growth. Collaborative research can mitigate some of these errors by refining methods and assumptions over time. This is particularly important for understanding how institutional changes have influenced economic growth in different historical contexts (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

The institutional revolution in factor markets is pivotal for understanding economic development in Africa (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. Austin also notes that this institutional change was a complex process. Colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local incentives, which could either align with or contradict broader colonial strategies (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive nature of institutional change highlights the complex interplay between local and colonial forces in shaping economic development in Africa.

Allen et al. (2011, p.22) and Allen (2001, p.8) show that real income divergence in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries was marked by stagnation rather than improvement. This finding is supported by evidence of a vibrant but segmented labour market, where wage rates served as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). The integration of urban and rural labour markets in commercialized regions near major urban centres suggests that real wages reflected broader economic conditions across all sectors. However, the stagnation was not uniform across all regions or socio-economic groups: Allen (2001, p.8) notes that real wage trends in Britain showed significant fluctuations, with decisive breaks occurring during periods of economic development and population changes (Allen 2001, p.23). This pattern indicates that while there were cycles of improvement, these did not constitute a sustained upward trend. The implications are nuanced by the fact that price changes, including those for grain and other essential commodities, were frequent and influenced by both wholesale and retail dynamics. Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that prices were not fixed for long periods (Allen 2001, p.8). The volatility in real wages and prices underscores the complexity of economic conditions during this period.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlight that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods. This dynamic was further reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary laws, which solidified the subordinate status of women during this era. Bohannan notes that the introduction of money into subsistence economies led to increased trade and market activity as well as disruption of traditional economic institutions. This shift, driven by colonial rule and the influence of trading companies, altered the multicentric economy of regions like Northern Nigeria, integrating them more closely with the global economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). Consequently, the economic transition brought about by the introduction of money had profound implications for gender roles and economic structures in Africa. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched patriarchal norms, which had significant impacts on women's access to resources and their economic contributions.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Specifically, Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions led to persistent economic underdevelopment. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The mechanism by which this occurred is through the political influence of powerful groups who resist institutional changes that might threaten their power or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). A pattern emerges where extractive institutions, shaped by colonial powers, persist over time and hinder economic development. This persistence is further compounded by the reluctance of politically dominant groups to initiate institutional reforms that could lead to broader economic benefits but do not directly serve their immediate interests. In addition, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that these institutions are robustly associated with lower income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). However, the persistence of extractive institutions is not solely due to colonial legacies; it is also reinforced by contemporary political dynamics where elites resist reforms that might diminish their control. The interplay between historical institutional changes and current political structures creates a complex web that continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies.
