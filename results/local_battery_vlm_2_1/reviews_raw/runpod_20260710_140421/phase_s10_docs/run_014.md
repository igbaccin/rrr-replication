The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is our understanding of how societies develop and why some prosper while others stagnate. This question intersects with debates about the origins of inequality; the extent to which historical factors can explain modern disparities; and the effectiveness of policy interventions aimed at fostering growth. The central disagreement revolves around whether institutions, as defined by rules and norms governing economic activity, are indeed a primary driver of long-term economic performance or if other factors such as geography, culture, and technological change play more significant roles. One side argues that institutions matter profoundly because they determine the incentives for investment and innovation; the opposing view contends that institutions are merely one factor among many and that their impact is often overstated. The debate extends to how institutions evolve and whether they can be engineered to promote growth or if they are largely path-dependent, shaped by historical contingencies. This question of institutional influence on long-run economic growth is particularly salient in the context of colonial legacies.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes has been a focal point for scholars examining the legacy of European colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates; high-mortality colonies received extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected income per capita in former colonies. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s thesis that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The role of institutions is further underscored by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2).

The evolution of institutions in response to changing economic conditions is another critical aspect. This perspective aligns with Austin's observation that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The interplay between institutions and economic development is further illustrated by the shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2).

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth extends beyond extractive practices. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This contribution was often shaped by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule, which reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land and other resources.

These scholars contend that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes, with extractive institutions in high-mortality regions leading to lower income per capita. This argument hinges on the premise that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and population density. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole; local economic development was not fostered. However, Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that equilibrium institutions may be shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from maintaining the status quo, which can further entrench extractive practices. The implications are significant: even if institutions were designed to maximize rents for colonial powers, they can still persist and hinder economic development long after decolonization.

This transformation resulted from indigenous economic and political actions compelling colonial administrations to adjust their institutional frameworks; it was not merely a response to colonial policies. Colonial administrations seeking fiscal security encouraged their subjects to develop a material interest in maintaining the status quo, leading to adaptive changes in institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political dynamics. This adaptability is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to hypotheses that predicted otherwise (Austin 2008, p.15). The shift from coerced to free labour played a crucial role as seen in the 1874 British emancipation ordinance and the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti. This institutional adaptation highlights how local conditions and indigenous actions could influence colonial policies, shaping long-run economic growth in Ghana through dynamic interactions between local and colonial interests.

This income divergence underscores the critical role of institutions in sustaining or eroding economic advantages over extended periods. Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) jointly find that this divergence was not driven by institutional changes but rather by shifts in population and economic development, with real wages stagnating in regions like China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries. These findings highlight a pattern of significant income maintenance or improvement in specific areas despite broader trends, suggesting that local economic conditions and historical developments played crucial roles in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories. This is evident in Allen's (2001, p.8) analysis of British real wage history, where long swings reflecting population changes and decisive breaks with the past are noted as key features (Allen 2001, p.23). This focus on local economic conditions does not fully account for broader institutional frameworks that might have influenced these trends. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains underexplored in this context, despite its potential significance in understanding regional economic disparities and stagnation over time.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) both underscore that the catch-up process in economic growth is shaped by exogenous factors and technological advancements (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). Abramovitz identifies social capability as a pivotal factor, encompassing technical competence and institutional frameworks crucial for mobilizing capital on a large scale (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This aligns with Allen et al.'s analysis of British real wage history, which highlights long-run economic growth shaped by broad economic changes, including technological advancements. Abramovitz notes that institutions can act as subtle yet complex limiting factors on growth. This complexity is evident in Allen et al.'s identification of decisive breaks with the past, which may imply institutional shifts or changes in social capability.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Bohannan 1959, p.10) examine how African women contributed to economic development and the impact of introducing money into subsistence economies. They show that women played crucial roles in agricultural production and trade, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. This dynamic is further complicated by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced men's central role in economic activities (Bohannan 1959, p.10). Bohannan demonstrates how the introduction of money into a subsistence economy can lead to profound changes. He notes that the Tiv people experienced increased trade and a greater number of markets due to peace and improved road networks, integrating their economy with the world market despite potential pain points in this transition (Bohannan 1959, p.10). Bohannan also points out that brass rods, used as currency within the prestige sphere, did not fulfill all functions of money outside this sphere. This nuance suggests that while monetary introduction can drive economic changes, it does not uniformly enhance economic growth across different spheres of activity.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profound and multifaceted, as evidenced by various historical and empirical studies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the institutional frameworks established in colonies, which in turn affected long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These strategies ranged from settler colonies with institutions enforcing rule of law to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin further elaborates on the complexity of institutional change by highlighting that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15).

The impact of institutions is not uniform across different spheres of activity. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Austin's observation that colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could lead to higher production equilibria despite initial extractive policies (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic development is further illuminated by the experiences of African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack show that women significantly contributed to economic growth, often through agricultural activities such as cocoa farming, which were integral to the economic revolution in regions like the Gold Coast (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This contribution was sometimes hindered by institutional changes under colonial rule, particularly through customary law codifications that marginalized women's access to land and resources (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 also highlights the role of institutions in adapting to changing economic conditions. Austin notes that the transition from coerced to free labour, driven by institutional changes such as the emancipation ordinance and the prohibition of slavery and pawning, had significant economic implications (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, underscores how institutions can facilitate economic adaptation and growth.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutional change occurs and its impact on different economic activities. Austin argues that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating a more dynamic process of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from Ghana, where institutional changes facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour, provides a clear case for how institutions can drive economic adaptation and growth.
