The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy debates. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement over whether colonial-era institutional legacies are key determinants of current economic performance. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions established during colonial periods have persisted and significantly hindered economic development. Others contend that this perspective oversimplifies complex historical processes, ignoring the role of local agency, pre-existing institutions, and evolving contexts in shaping long-term economic outcomes. This disagreement underscores the need to understand how different institutional frameworks interact with other factors over time to influence growth trajectories.

A central claim is that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional paths among colonies, which in turn significantly affected their long-run economic performance. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates shaped colonial institutions: high-mortality areas saw the establishment of extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, while low-mortality regions developed more inclusive institutions that fostered investment and growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings are supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions in colonies were likely designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The relationship between colonial institutions and economic development is further complicated by the role of local factors and historical contexts. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that former colonies with initially favorable geographical conditions have experienced lower economic growth due to extractive institutions, oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who highlight the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies in colonies, suggesting that local conditions significantly shaped institutional outcomes and long-run development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in economic growth cannot be fully understood without considering the contributions of marginalized groups, such as women, whose economic activities have often been overlooked. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how different social groups interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes.

This discussion shows that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, but their impact is mediated by complex interactions with local contexts, historical trajectories, and the contributions of diverse social groups.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that the institutional changes in Ghana during specific historical periods were driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity. The transition from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This shift resulted from indigenous economic and political actions as well as a response to colonial policies (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, the British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land for cocoa farming. This transition from coerced to free labour had significant economic implications (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2007, p.4). Colonial administrations were not passive recipients of these changes; they adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous actions. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations based on Nunn's hypothesis. Understanding these nuances is crucial for appreciating the complex interplay between colonial policies and indigenous actions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Social and cultural changes in Africa have significantly influenced economic outcomes by reshaping traditional roles and introducing new economic systems (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that women played a crucial role in African economic development, both pre-colonially and during colonial rule. They were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming; however, their contributions were pivotal in driving agricultural revolutions, such as the cocoa boom in the Gold Coast. This dynamic is further illuminated by Bohannan (1959, p.10), who describes how the introduction of money into subsistence economies transformed economic structures. The influx of trade and markets disrupted traditional systems, integrating local economies with global networks. Despite these changes, customary laws and patriarchal alliances often reinforced gender inequalities, as seen in the codification of women's subordinate status under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dual legal system created by European courts based on common and civil law versus native courts exacerbated economic disparities. The introduction of money, while fostering trade, also led to a shift away from multicentric economies, creating new economic institutions that altered traditional exchange systems. However, the transition was not without challenges; the equivalence between brass rods as currency and rights in women or food highlights the complexities of integrating new economic systems into existing social structures (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The profound changes brought about by these social and cultural shifts underscore the multifaceted ways in which African economies adapted to and were shaped by external influences.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) emphasize the importance of collaborative research in refining economic measurements, particularly in extending Angus Maddison's work on historical national accounts. This collaborative approach has not only improved the accuracy of economic estimates but also fostered a scholarly environment where continuous refinement is possible. Estimating margins of error for historical data remains a significant challenge due to its subjectivity (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This highlights that while collaborative efforts enhance the precision of economic measurements, they also require ongoing critical evaluation to mitigate biases and uncertainties.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (2001, p.8) (Abramovitz 1986, p.21), among others, examine the complex dynamics of economic growth by focusing on technological change and its influence on long-run development. They jointly highlight that economic growth is marked by periodic shifts driven by exogenous factors and technological advancements. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) suggests that these forces can alter the productivity rankings among countries, including those at the top (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). Allen (2001, p.8) elaborates on this dynamic by showing how economic development in Europe was shaped by fluctuations in real wages and living standards over centuries (Allen 2001, p.8).

Allen et al. (2011, p.22) illustrate this pattern through China's historical experience, where real wages stagnated despite significant economic activity and market integration, thereby demonstrating that growth is not always reflected in immediate wage improvements (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This finding underscores the complexity of long-run economic trends, where periods of stability can coexist with underlying structural changes. However, Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that measuring social capability, an essential component of technological advancement and institutional change, remains challenging due to its qualitative nature (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This challenge complicates the analysis of economic growth, as it requires a nuanced understanding of both quantitative data and qualitative institutional dynamics. The intricate interplay between technological progress and long-run economic development necessitates a multidimensional approach that considers both immediate impacts and underlying structural changes in society.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and geographical contexts in which they operate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European colonization strategies led to diverse institutional outcomes, ranging from inclusive rule-of-law-enforcing systems to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. These institutions significantly influenced income per capita and long-run economic development. The variation in colonial institutions highlights how initial conditions, such as mortality rates and settlement patterns, shaped the trajectory of economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes, with extractive institutions often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions over time further emphasizes their lasting impact on economic development.

The complexity of measuring social capability and technological progress adds another layer to understanding institutional impacts (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2007, p.20; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods. This approach highlights how institutions evolve in response to local economic and political actions, rather than being static entities. The shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4). This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor, illustrating how institutional changes can drive economic growth by adapting to changing resource availability. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further complicated these dynamics, as it often reinforced patriarchal alliances that marginalized certain groups, particularly women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The interplay between institutional change and economic development is thus multifaceted, involving both immediate impacts and underlying structural changes in society.

The disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth hinges on whether institutions are primarily shaped by initial conditions or evolve in response to local actions. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced patriarchal alliances and marginalized certain groups (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This historical process illustrates how institutions can evolve in response to local actions while also being shaped by broader colonial strategies.
