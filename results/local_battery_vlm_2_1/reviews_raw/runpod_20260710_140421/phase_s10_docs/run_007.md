The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development research. The stakes are high because it speaks to the efficacy and sustainability of policy interventions designed to foster prosperity. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement between scholars who view institutions as exogenous determinants of economic outcomes, and those who see them as endogenous products of historical and political processes. One position argues that certain institutional frameworks inherently promote growth by protecting property rights and enforcing contracts, while another contends that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and historical contingencies. A third perspective focuses on the role of colonial legacies in shaping contemporary economic trajectories through enduring institutional effects.

A key insight shared across this debate is that institutions matter for long-run economic performance, but their influence is mediated by complex interactions with other factors such as geography and demographic characteristics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They illustrate this with the contrast between settler colonies like the United States and extractive states such as the Congo or Gold Coast, where Europeans established institutions to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole instead of fostering local investment. The nature of these colonial institutions was shaped by European settlers' expectations of mortality rates in different regions. In areas with high mortality, Europeans were less likely to settle permanently and more inclined to establish extractive institutions that facilitated resource extraction rather than long-term economic development. This institutional legacy persisted even after decolonization, continuing to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies.

These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They emphasize that equilibrium institutions in colonial contexts were often extractive, serving the interests of politically powerful groups at the expense of broader economic development. This institutional design limited the potential for industrialization and sustained economic growth in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document how the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was closely tied to their ability, or lack thereof, to industrialize during the nineteenth century, highlighting the critical role of institutions in this process. The interaction between colonial institutions and the opportunity to industrialize thus played a central role in shaping the long-run development paths of former colonies.

The persistence of these institutional legacies is crucial for understanding why some former colonies have struggled with economic growth while others have thrived.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) jointly argue that historical institutional changes in Ghana during 1874-1939 were driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity. This shift led to significant transformations in factor markets, including the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). The Gold Coast ordinance of 1874 exemplifies how legal frameworks adapted to changing economic conditions, facilitating a transition from coerced to free labour. This institutional revolution was influenced by local political and economic dynamics as well as exogenous forces. For instance, colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to indigenous actions, opting for higher production equilibria rather than lower ones (Austin 2008, p.15). However, the integration of these colonies under British rule had market-integrating effects that went beyond immediate institutional changes, as tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within Ghana were removed (Austin 2007, p.4). This broader economic integration suggests that while local institutional adaptations were crucial, they were part of a larger framework shaped by colonial governance.

The integration of local economies into broader frameworks underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth during colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) underscore the critical role of women in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as the impact of introducing money into subsistence economies. Women were instrumental in driving agricultural productivity, particularly through their involvement in food farming and the cultivation of cash crops like cocoa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic was influenced by colonial rule, which codified customary laws that often marginalized women's access to land for cash crops (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The introduction of money into subsistence economies, as detailed by Bohannan, led to significant changes in economic institutions and trade networks (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). This transition was facilitated by the establishment of peace and improved road infrastructure, which increased trade and market activity. However, this process also involved potential pain points, such as the disruption of traditional economic systems and the challenges of integrating local economies into a global framework (Bohannan (1959, p.9). These changes underscore how institutional frameworks can both facilitate and complicate economic development in African contexts.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) focus on historical contexts of real income and wage stagnation without directly addressing institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. One specific mechanism through which this plays out is the influence of population changes on wages, where long swings in real wages reflect broader demographic shifts rather than institutional factors. For instance, Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that significant gains in British real wages occurred between 1870 and the First World War, marking a decisive break from previous trends (Allen 2001, p.23). This period of wage growth was driven by population changes and economic development, suggesting that demographic factors can overshadow institutional influences. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further elaborate on this by noting that in early modern China, real wages stagnated despite a vibrant labour market and regional migration (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This indicates that even with active labour markets, wage stagnation can persist independently of institutional changes. The integration of urban and rural labour markets in commercialized regions near major urban centres suggests that local economic conditions can mitigate some aspects of wage stagnation.

Collaborative research is essential for improving estimates of historical economic performance (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Bolt and van Zanden note that national accounts from the distant past often rely on partial data and assumptions about economic activities, leading to significant margins of error. For example, while medieval England's data might be more reliable than nineteenth-century Sub-Saharan Africa’s, this variability underscores the need for collaborative efforts to enhance accuracy (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Maddison’s approach to presenting numbers even without a solid basis has been highly successful in stimulating further research and refining estimates of long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This strategy not only enhances the precision of historical data but also fosters an ongoing dialogue among economists.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. both argue that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by various factors beyond simple convergence hypotheses. They highlight that technological change and social capability play crucial roles in this process, suggesting that countries with advanced institutions and high levels of education are better positioned to absorb new technologies and achieve sustained growth. The complexity arises from the interplay between exogenous factors and endogenous dynamics within economies. This social capability is evident in countries with robust educational systems and well-developed institutions (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality and human capital development in fostering economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the trajectories of different economies. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies significantly impacted institutional development in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These strategies ranged from establishing settler societies with strong property rights and rule of law, as seen in the United States and Australia, to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer, such as those in the Congo or Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced long-term economic outcomes. The presence of extractive institutions, which prioritized the interests of colonial powers over local development, hindered economic growth by failing to foster investment and innovation.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. Colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between exogenous factors and endogenous institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.15). For example, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, illustrating the adaptive nature of institutional responses.

Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is crucial because it underscores the importance of understanding the political and economic motivations behind institutional design. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. was closely linked to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women played a significant role in economic growth both pre-colonially and under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This contribution challenges views that marginalize their economic impact. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and resources crucial for economic activities such as cocoa farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The historical evidence shows that the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities is pivotal in determining long-term development trajectories. Austin highlights how the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). This transformation illustrates how changes in factor scarcities can drive institutional innovation, underscoring the dynamic nature of economic growth. The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced patriarchal structures and limited women's access to land and resources crucial for economic activities such as cocoa farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).
