The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake are the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty across nations. One key disagreement centres around whether colonial institutions have played a decisive role in determining the economic trajectories of former colonies. One position argues that extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers have stunted growth, while another posits that local factors, such as geography or indigenous practices, have been more influential. Additionally, there is contention over whether the reversal of fortune thesis accurately captures the complex interplay between institutions and economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20), who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, suggest that indigenous factors played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, suggesting that external factors also shaped long-run growth. This tension underscores the need to examine how institutions interact with local conditions and historical contexts.

A key aspect of this debate involves the nature of the institutions themselves: whether they are extractive or growth-enhancing. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, rather than to foster long-run economic development. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions. These findings suggest that the impact of institutions on growth depends on their inherent characteristics and how they evolve in response to local dynamics.

Institutional changes can be driven by shifts in factor markets, as seen in Ghana during 1874-1939. Austin (2007, p.4) notes that the imposition of British rule had market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what would become Ghana. This period also saw an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, highlighting how institutional changes can be driven by localised scarcity (Austin 2007, p.20). These shifts underscore the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which institutions operate.

The role of geography and climate in shaping institutions is another critical area of inquiry. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that European colonizers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to varying institutional outcomes. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution going back at least to Europe's original industrialization.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth highlights the complexity of this relationship. While some argue that extractive institutions imposed by colonizers have stunted growth, others point to the significance of local factors and historical contexts. This tension underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with geography, climate, and indigenous practices to shape economic outcomes over the long run.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions are key drivers of long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than foster long-term development; this extractive approach significantly affected income per capita. The differential mortality rates among Europeans led to varied colonization strategies. In regions with high mortality, Europeans established extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework contrasts sharply with the settlement colonies like the United States and Australia, where Europeans implemented rule-of-law institutions that encouraged investment. The enduring impact of these colonial institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. The divergence in economic trajectories can be attributed to the differing institutional frameworks imposed during colonization. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that this institutional legacy has persisted, shaping long-term economic outcomes and industrialization patterns.

The influence of colonial institutions on economic development extends beyond immediate resource extraction; it also affects the broader socio-economic fabric. The persistence of these extractive institutions means that former colonies often struggle to transition to more inclusive and growth-oriented policies, further entrenching their economic disadvantages. This institutional legacy underscores the profound and lasting impact of colonial rule on long-term economic development.

In Ghana's case, the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes. These changes included the emergence of widespread markets for land use rights and hired labour, marking an institutional revolution in factor markets. This shift was influenced by both external policies, such as British emancipation ordinances, and internal dynamics, like the economic pressures faced by local chiefs who were driven into debt due to the loss of slaves (Austin 2007, p.20). The colonial administration's response to these changes further shaped institutional prescriptions, illustrating how indigenous economic and political actions could influence colonial policies. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium contradicted Nunn's hypothesis, demonstrating that colonial administrations were not monolithic in their approach but responded to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability in colonial governance highlights the complexity of institutional changes during this period. The removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within Ghana also played a crucial role in market integration, further illustrating how institutional shifts could facilitate economic growth. This nuanced view of institutional change in colonial Africa underscores the importance of understanding local dynamics and adaptive governance in shaping long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Bohannan 1959, p.10) examine specific economic changes in African societies by highlighting the significant contributions of women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They note that the introduction of money into subsistence economies led to profound shifts, including increased trade and a greater number of markets, which in turn perturbed the internal marketing system (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This economic transformation was driven by external factors and internal dynamics such as migration for farmland and the emergence of cash crops. This gendered division of labor was reinforced through customary law, which codified the subordinate status of women and further entrenched patriarchal structures. This process underscores how institutional changes, even those not directly aimed at economic growth, can have significant downstream effects on economic development by altering the roles and opportunities available to different segments of society.

Similarly, Allen (2001, p.8) and Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that income divergence across European cities between 1500 and 1750 was driven by institutional changes. The economic leaders maintained their high income levels due to the rise of urban markets and improved trade networks, which were facilitated by robust institutions that supported commerce. These institutions included legal frameworks that protected property rights and enforced contracts, enabling economic leaders to sustain their advantages over time (Allen 2001, p.8). While other cities experienced declines in real incomes, this disparity was not merely a result of natural resource endowments or geographic factors but was fundamentally shaped by the institutional frameworks that either promoted or hindered economic activity. The maintenance of high real wages after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century (Allen 2001, p.23). This divergence highlights how institutions can create and sustain economic advantages over long periods, even when other factors such as population changes or resource availability might suggest different outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of urban markets. These factors collectively enabled economic leaders to sustain their advantages over time. The gains from 1815 to 1850 were a cycle-and a minor one at that-not a trend (Allen 2001, p.23). This period of maintenance was crucial in laying the groundwork for later economic developments, including the rise in living standards between 1870 and the First World War. Each of these periods represented decisive breaks with the past but were shaped by underlying institutional structures that facilitated or hindered economic progress.

Abramovitz and Allen et al. explore how economic growth is shaped by complex dynamics that extend beyond simple convergence hypotheses (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). They jointly describe the intricate interplay between technological advancement, social change, and institutional structures that drive economic progress. Abramovitz highlights that the catch-up process in economic growth involves not just technological adoption but also profound social and institutional changes. These shifts can alter a country's position in the productivity scale, influencing long-term economic trajectories (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). One specific mechanism Abramovitz identifies is the role of social capability, which encompasses technical competence and robust institutions that facilitate large-scale enterprise and capital mobilization (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Allen et al. substantiate this by documenting vibrant labour markets in early modern China, where wage rates served as a reasonable proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This indicates that institutional frameworks supporting labour market integration can significantly influence economic outcomes. The downstream implication is qualified by the recognition that these institutions must be adaptive to changing circumstances. For instance, Allen et al.'s analysis shows that even in regions with high labour market integration, certain socio-economic groups remained at the lower end of the income ladder (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This suggests that while institutional structures are crucial for economic growth, their effectiveness depends on their ability to evolve and address emerging challenges.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either foster or hinder development. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks, with some colonies developing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This contrasts with other colonies where extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Austin reinforces this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution, emphasizing that economic historians see patterns going back at least to Europe's original industrialization (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) indicates that the type of institutions introduced during colonialism significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. Austin further highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in how they address emerging challenges and adapt to changing circumstances. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Austin's observation that colonial administrations adjusted their policies based on the economic incentives created by indigenous action, suggesting a responsive institutional framework (Austin 2008, p.15). Akyeampong and Fofack add to this discussion by noting that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, highlighting how institutions can shape gender dynamics within economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The institutional framework's adaptability is crucial for addressing socio-economic challenges. Allen et al.'s analysis shows that even in regions with high labour market integration, certain socio-economic groups remained at the lower end of the income ladder (Allen 2001, p.8). This underscores the importance of institutions that can evolve to address emerging inequalities and ensure more inclusive economic growth. Austin's work on Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates how a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). This historical evidence shows that institutions can adapt to changing resource scarcities, fostering economic development. Austin's analysis of the Gold Coast ordinance further demonstrates how legal actions influenced economic dimensions, such as the transition from coerced to free labour (Austin 2007, p.20).

Institutions are central to long-run economic growth because they create frameworks that either foster or impede development. Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups can be extractive and hinder growth is supported by Austin's observation that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous action (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding indicates the importance of adaptive institutions. There remains disagreement on how these adaptations occur. Acemoglu et al.'s argument suggests that institutional changes are driven by powerful groups seeking to maximize rents, while Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.2). This discrepancy is settled by Allen's historical evidence of labour market integration, which shows that even with adaptive institutions, certain socio-economic groups remained at the lower end of the income ladder, highlighting the need for more inclusive institutional frameworks (Allen 2001, p.8).
