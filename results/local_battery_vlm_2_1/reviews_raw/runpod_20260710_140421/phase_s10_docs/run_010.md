The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks determine economic trajectories over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and climate, or if they emerge from endogenous processes driven by historical events and political dynamics. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonial powers, have persistently hindered economic development in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2007, p.20). Conversely, other researchers emphasize the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20), suggesting that institutions can be shaped by both exogenous and endogenous factors. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how different types of institutions influence long-term development outcomes.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is explained by institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutions were shaped to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a dynamic that was influenced by population density and prosperity in the colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions was evident in regions where Europeans established states primarily for resource extraction. In contrast, colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand saw the development of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment due to lower mortality rates among European settlers. These differences in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were not merely a response to geographic factors but were actively designed by colonial powers to serve their interests, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on long-term economic outcomes.

This institutional legacy is particularly evident in the interaction between colonial policies and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to their ability or inability to industrialize. This dynamic underscores how institutions influenced not just immediate economic conditions but also long-term development trajectories, shaping the economic landscape of these regions for centuries. While Acemoglu et al. provide a robust framework for understanding this phenomenon, it is important to recognize that the specific mechanisms by which these institutions operated varied across different colonial contexts (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The role of population density and prosperity in influencing European policies adds another layer of complexity to the institutional dynamics at play during colonial times.

Austin (2007, p.20; 2008, p.2) demonstrates that shifts in resource availability can trigger significant institutional changes. Austin's examination of Ghana during 1874-1939 reveals how the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity drove an institutional revolution in factor markets. This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, a pattern that highlights the adaptive nature of institutions in response to changing economic conditions (Austin (2008, p.2). Austin further elaborates on this dynamic, showing how colonial governments adjusted their policies based on local economic incentives. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium despite Nunn's hypothesis suggesting otherwise. This indicates that institutional changes were both imposed and shaped by the interplay between colonial administrations and indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The implications of these findings are significant: they suggest that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of how institutions evolve in response to specific historical contexts, rather than assuming a uniform path driven solely by exogenous factors.

Allen argues that historical trends in real incomes reveal significant regional disparities (Allen 2001, p.8). Allen et al. concur, noting that economic leaders maintained their income levels while others experienced declines (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Allen 2001, p.8). This divergence was not merely a function of exogenous shocks but was also influenced by institutional factors that shaped labor market conditions and wage rates. For instance, the maintenance of high real wages in certain regions can be linked to vibrant labour markets and economic development gathering strength. One specific mechanism is the role of long-term supply arrangements and bulk buying, which could stabilize prices and influence real incomes (Allen 2001, p.8). This pattern suggests that institutional frameworks supporting efficient market operations were crucial for sustaining income levels. Allen et al. qualify this by noting that while labour markets in early modern China were active, the proportion of short-term or day labourers varied significantly across regions (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This variation implies that the impact of institutions on real incomes was not uniform but contingent on local economic structures and market integration.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al (Abramovitz 1986, p.5; Abramovitz 1986, p.21) highlight that the trajectory of economic growth is complex and multifaceted. The catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by exogenous factors and social change, as well as technological advances. Abramovitz's work underscores that institutional changes can either facilitate or impede long-run economic growth. This dynamic is evident in how institutions shape a country's ability to absorb new technologies and manage large-scale enterprises (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Allen et al. expand on this by noting the variability of labor markets across regions, which suggests that local economic structures and market integration play crucial roles in determining how institutions influence real incomes (Abramovitz 1986, p.21).

This complexity is further illustrated by the role of social capability, which includes technical competence and institutional frameworks. This perspective aligns with Allen et al.'s observation that while labor markets were active in early modern China, regional variations in short-term or day laborers indicate that institutional impacts on real incomes were contingent on local economic conditions.

However, Abramovitz also acknowledges the challenge of measuring social capability, which includes technical competence and institutional frameworks. The complexity of economic catch-up processes means that institutions must be adaptive and responsive to changing economic conditions. This adaptability is crucial for sustaining long-run economic growth, as it allows countries to leverage their institutional strengths while addressing weaknesses in their social capability.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) share the finding that economic transitions in Africa have been significantly influenced by social and institutional changes. These authors underscore how shifts in gender roles and the integration of monetary systems into subsistence economies have driven economic growth, even if these processes are not directly causal for long-run economic growth. The introduction of money into African economies, as described by Bohannan (1959, p.10), led to increased trade and market activity, aligning with Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014, p.15) observation that women played crucial roles in agricultural production during colonial periods. These changes highlight the adaptability of institutions in response to new economic opportunities. However, Bohannan's (1959, p.9) analysis reveals potential pain points in this transition, as the introduction of money did not seamlessly integrate with existing social institutions like marriage and food exchange, suggesting that the institutional adaptation process is complex and fraught with challenges. This complexity underscores the need for nuanced understanding of how institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic growth during periods of significant change, including the shift from subsistence to market economies.

Institutions significantly shape long-run economic growth by influencing investment decisions and resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates persisted and significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This effect is not confined to colonial contexts; similar dynamics are evident in pre-colonial Africa, where institutions determined economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that women's contributions to economic growth were shaped by institutional frameworks, including access to land for food farming and the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). These findings indicate that institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic growth by determining who benefits from economic activities.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by their interaction with factor markets. Austin argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This transformation was driven by changing factor scarcities, highlighting how institutions adapt to economic conditions. Allen provides a complementary perspective by showing that real incomes diverged between 1500 and 1750 as European cities maintained high living standards while other regions experienced declines (Allen 2001, p.23). The maintenance of high living standards in some regions indicates the importance of institutional stability, which supports long-term economic growth.

The historical evidence shows that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining who benefits from economic activities and how resources are allocated. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin counters this by showing that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6).
