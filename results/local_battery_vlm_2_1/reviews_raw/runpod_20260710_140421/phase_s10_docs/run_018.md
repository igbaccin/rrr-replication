The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question for understanding why some societies thrive while others stagnate. Institutions are the rules that shape and constrain human interaction, and their influence on economic outcomes is profound. The debate centres around whether institutions are exogenous factors that determine growth independently or if they emerge endogenously from historical, political, and social contexts. Some scholars argue that certain institutional frameworks, such as those enforcing property rights and rule of law, are universally beneficial for long-term economic development. Others contend that the effectiveness of institutions is contingent on local conditions and historical legacies. This disagreement underscores the need to explore how specific institutional arrangements interact with broader socio-economic factors to drive growth over extended periods.

The study of colonial institutions provides a rich terrain for examining this relationship. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27)  argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, which significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, such as the United States and Australia, income per capita was higher (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states established in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, led to lower economic performance. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who highlights that colonial institutions were not monolithic but varied based on local conditions and interactions with indigenous populations (Austin 2008, p.2). Colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional change.

The interaction between colonial policies and local socio-economic conditions is further elaborated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These policies, in turn, shaped the long-run development trajectories of former colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was closely linked to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. This perspective is complemented by Austin (2007, p.20), who examines the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4). The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour reflects how changing economic conditions can drive institutional innovation. This dynamic interplay between institutions and socio-economic factors underscores the complexity of long-run economic growth.

The role of women in African economic development during colonial periods offers another lens through which to view institutional impacts. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced the subordinate status of women, illustrating how institutions can both enable and constrain economic participation. This perspective is supported by Austin (2007, p.20), who notes that legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony had important economic dimensions (Austin 2007, p.20). The transition from coerced to free labour, driven by institutional changes, highlights how shifting factor scarcities can influence economic outcomes. This interplay between institutional frameworks and socio-economic conditions underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Acemoglu et al., (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Abramovitz 1986, p.5), assert that economic catch-up processes are complex. They involve both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This complexity is evident in the institutional changes affecting long-run growth, where institutions' roles can be both direct and indirect. The direct role involves institutions shaping incentives for investment and innovation. Colonial institutions influenced social capability, which includes a country's technical competence, political stability, and financial market development. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) suggests that these factors are crucial for mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprise (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Austin (2007, p.20) further illustrates this by showing how colonial institutions adapted to local conditions in Africa, leading to varied production equilibria. The interplay between institutional changes and socio-economic factors underscores the complex role of factor markets in shaping long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz's work (Abramovitz 1986, p.21) points out that forces of institutional change can act as a limiting factor on growth. This is particularly relevant for countries experiencing institutional sclerosis or stagnation, where established institutions may hinder progress. The implication here is that not all institutional changes are beneficial; some may even be detrimental to long-run economic growth. Thus, the quality and nature of institutional change matter significantly. Acemoglu et al., (Abramovitz 1986, p.5) highlight how poor institutions can lead to underdevelopment by failing to provide adequate incentives for investment and innovation.

These disparities are evident in historical trends of real incomes, where leading economies maintained their levels while others saw declines. Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) both argue that these diverging paths were shaped by underlying economic structures and labour market conditions. For instance, Allen (2001, p.8) highlights how grain prices and other commodities played a crucial role in maintaining high real wages in certain regions, suggesting that the stability of these prices was instrumental in sustaining economic growth (Allen 2001, p.8). These findings underscore the importance of stable economic conditions and efficient labour markets in maintaining real income levels over extended periods. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further qualify this by noting the significant regional variations in China's labour market integration, where commercialized regions near urban centers showed higher degrees of integration compared to other areas (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This qualification suggests that while stable economic conditions and efficient labour markets are beneficial, their impact can vary significantly based on regional specificities. Allen (2001, p.8) also points out that the gains in real wages observed between 1815 and 1850 were part of a cycle rather than a sustained trend (Allen 2001, p.23), indicating that long-term economic growth requires more than just cyclical improvements.

The role of women in Africa's economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods is significant, as highlighted by the literature on economic transitions and gender roles (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) identify the codification of customary law under colonial rule as a critical mechanism that reinforced patriarchal structures, marginalizing women's access to land for cash crops. This legal framework constrained women’s economic participation and redirected labor towards male-dominated sectors. Bohannan shows how the introduction of money into subsistence economies led to profound changes in economic institutions, shifting away from multicentric systems toward more centralized market structures. The transition to a cash-based economy altered traditional exchange mechanisms, where brass rods were previously used as a general-purpose currency within specific spheres (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This shift also brought pain points, such as the inability of brass rods to facilitate equivalency between monetary value and rights in women or food, thereby complicating traditional economic relationships. The introduction of alien institutions, including European courts and trading companies, further perturbed internal marketing systems, integrating local economies into a global framework (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This integration had significant implications for gender roles, as it often reinforced existing patriarchal alliances through legal frameworks that codified women's subordinate status (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, where cocoa production became dominant, illustrates how these changes could propel economic growth while simultaneously marginalizing women’s contributions to agriculture.

For instance, in regions where European settlers were absent, the expansion of indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial government interests, further shaping economic development paths (Austin 2008, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).
