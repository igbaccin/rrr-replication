The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of why some regions have prospered while others have lagged behind over extended periods. The disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. Another perspective suggests that the impact of institutions is often overstated and that other variables, including local economic dynamics and policy responses, are equally important.

The debate involves the interplay between institutional frameworks and economic performance. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly across different regions, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, in turn, had profound effects on income per capita, with extractive states often resulting in lower economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial in determining the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights how institutions shaped by colonial powers influenced long-term development trajectories.

The role of women in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods is another critical aspect. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This contribution was often overlooked but was integral to the economic development of regions like the Gold Coast. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dynamic underscores how institutional changes during colonialism not only affected economic outcomes but also had gender-specific impacts.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex, involving multiple factors that interact over time. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary to fully grasp how institutions influence economic growth. Additionally, Austin (2007, p.20) highlights that shifts in factor markets, such as the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity, can drive institutional changes that facilitate economic development (Austin 2007, p.20). This indicates that while institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by broader economic and social dynamics.

The debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth continues to evolve. Collaborative research efforts, as emphasized by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), are essential for improving our understanding of historical economic performance (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). By continuing Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance, researchers can provide more accurate and detailed overviews of long-term economic growth. This collaborative approach is vital for advancing the field and resolving ongoing debates about the role of institutions in economic development.

These authors show that as land became scarce, markets for land use rights and hired labour emerged. This institutional transformation was not merely a response to external pressures but also an endogenous process driven by local economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). The shift from coerced to free labour, facilitated by legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony and the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti, played a pivotal role in this transition. These changes were not isolated events but part of a broader institutional revolution that reshaped factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin (2007, p.20) observes that these legal reforms significantly interacted with changing factor scarcities to create new economic dynamics; they did not solely drive the institutional innovation. The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule further intensified market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within 'Ghana' (Austin 2007, p.20). This dynamic underscores how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, illustrating the complex interplay between external governance and local institutional evolution. This interplay indicates that the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour was influenced by both top-down policies and bottom-up economic pressures.

Allen et al. (2011, p.22) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that measuring economic performance over time requires navigating significant challenges, particularly when relying on historical data. The construction of national accounts for past economies often involves partial data and assumptions about the links between available information, such as tax proceeds, and broader economic activities (Bolt & van Zanden 2014, p.2). This process introduces margins of error that can be substantial, especially as one delves further into history. For instance, estimates for medieval England might be more reliable than those for nineteenth-century Sub-Saharan Africa or pre-Colombian Latin America due to variations in data availability and quality (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The work of Allen et al. on real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries highlights these challenges by showing that wage rates can serve as a proxy for average earnings and labour productivity, provided there is an active labour market (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). However, this approach assumes regional integration and economic continuity; Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) note that Angus Maddison’s strategy of producing numerical estimates despite data limitations has spurred extensive scholarly engagement. This collaborative effort has led to a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than was possible in the 1960s, demonstrating how ongoing research can refine and enhance our understanding of historical economic performance.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economies (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The integration of alien institutions under colonial rule transformed the economic landscape by fostering increased trade and market activity. This transition, however, was not without its complexities. Bohannan describes how money introduced new exchange mechanisms within traditional societies, but these changes were often partial: brass rods served as currency for prestige but did not equate to rights in women or food (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The introduction of cash crops further complicated this economic shift; Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the role of men in codifying customary law under colonial rule, which marginalized women's access to land for cash crop farming. This led to a significant reallocation of labor and resources as farmers migrated in search of suitable farmland (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The economic revolution driven by cocoa production in the Gold Coast underscores how these institutional changes could propel rapid growth despite the underlying gender disparities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Thus, understanding this process requires a multifaceted approach that considers both the immediate economic impacts and the broader social context. Abramovitz's analysis shows that these forces can alter the ranks of countries on the productivity scale (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This dynamic is evident in Allen’s observation that maintaining high real wages post-economic development gathered strength during the 17th century, marking a decisive break with past economic trends (Allen 2001, p.23). Institutional changes can also limit growth in Western countries due to what many economists describe as 'institutional arthritis' or other age-related afflictions (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). These factors suggest that while technological advance and social change drive long-run economic growth, the specific institutional contexts in which these processes occur are crucial.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions established by European settlers significantly impacted income per capita in former colonies. In particular, extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth were a key factor in shaping economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) complements this view by emphasizing the importance of institutional evolution and its role in long-run economic growth.

While extractive institutions can stifle growth, the interaction between institutions and industrialization is crucial for understanding long-run development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. This highlights how institutional changes introduced by colonialism influenced economic trajectories.

The role of institutions extends beyond economic policies; they also shape social structures and gender dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) reveal that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, both pre-colonially and during the colonial period. This contribution was often marginalized by patriarchal alliances and customary laws codified under colonial rule. Austin (2007, p.20) provides a detailed case study of Ghana, showing how institutional changes in factor markets, such as the emergence of land use rights and hired labor markets, were driven by shifts from land abundance to localized scarcity.

The measurement and interpretation of economic performance are also critical aspects of understanding long-run growth. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that collaborative research is essential for improving estimates of national accounts and measuring economic performance accurately. This collaborative approach builds on Angus Maddison's pioneering work, which encouraged scholars to quantify long-term economic development despite margins of error in historical data.

The consensus across these studies is that institutions are foundational to long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show how extractive institutions can limit growth by maximizing rents for colonists rather than promoting long-term development. The remaining disagreement centers on the precise mechanisms through which these institutions operate and their interaction with other factors such as industrialization and social structures. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, illustrating how local dynamics can influence institutional outcomes. The specific historical evidence of institutional changes during the nineteenth century settles this disagreement by demonstrating how these changes interacted with industrialization opportunities to shape long-run development trajectories.
