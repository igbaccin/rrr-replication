The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder sustained economic progress over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial-era institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, with some scholars emphasizing the enduring impact of extractive institutions introduced during colonial rule. Other scholars argue that these institutional effects are often oversimplified and do not fully account for the complex interplay of historical factors that shape long-run growth trajectories.

For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) contend that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Austin's argument (Austin 2008, p.15) that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between institutions and economic growth.

The role of women in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods has been significantly marginalized. Akyeampong and Fofack argue (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) that African women played crucial roles in economic activities such as food farming and cash crop production, which were essential for the economic revolution in regions like the Gold Coast.

These perspectives collectively highlight the multifaceted nature of institutional impacts on long-run economic growth. The interplay between colonial institutions and local economic dynamics reveals a complex landscape where institutional legacies are not monolithic but rather evolve in response to various historical and contextual factors, shaping economic outcomes over time.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to varied economic outcomes in their colonies. For instance, high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and rule of law, hindered long-run economic growth. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies has been a critical factor in shaping their economic trajectories over time.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. document that this reversal was closely tied to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the ability to industrialize played a central role in determining long-run development outcomes. This suggests that while colonial institutions initially shaped economic trajectories, subsequent institutional changes and industrialization opportunities further influenced growth patterns.

The legacy of these extractive institutions remains evident today, as many former colonies continue to grapple with the challenges posed by weak property rights, rule of law, and institutional frameworks that do not foster long-term economic development.

Austin's re-evaluation of historical economic transitions in Africa provides a nuanced perspective on institutional changes and their impacts (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. This approach reveals that colonial institutions, far from being monolithic, evolved in response to local economic and political dynamics. This adaptive behavior challenges the notion of static, extractive institutions and highlights the importance of contextual factors in shaping institutional outcomes. Austin's analysis is complemented by a focus on Ghana during 1874-1939, where shifts from land abundance to scarcity drove an institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor, illustrating how institutional changes can be induced by local economic conditions (Austin 2007, p.4). However, it is important to note that while these adaptations occurred, they did not necessarily lead to long-term economic growth but rather reflected immediate responses to changing circumstances. This suggests that while institutions can adapt to local pressures, their impact on long-run economic development remains complex and contingent on various factors.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlight that African economies have seen significant contributions from women to economic development. The introduction of money into traditional subsistence economies has led to profound changes in local economic systems. Women were integral to this process, as they were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to privilege men's roles in the economy (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The introduction of money facilitated increased trade and market activity but also brought about a shift away from traditional economic institutions (Bohannan 1959, p.10).

Economic changes driven by these shifts were not uniform. The transition to a monetized economy introduced new challenges as well as opportunities. This dynamic illustrates that economic development in Africa was shaped by both external forces, such as colonial rule, and internal adaptations, such as women's continued contributions to food production. The complex interplay between these factors suggests that while institutions can drive significant changes, their impact on long-run economic growth is mediated through local economic conditions and social structures.

The interplay between technological advance and economic growth is complex, influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights that this interaction can lead to shifts in productivity rankings among countries. For example, technological advancements may initially benefit leading economies but eventually spread to others, altering their relative positions in the global productivity scale. This dynamic is evident in the historical data on real wages and prices. Allen (2001, p.8) shows that while grain prices fluctuated frequently, other commodity prices exhibited long-term stability due to bulk buying arrangements (Allen 2001, p.8). These price patterns reflect broader economic trends, such as the maintenance of high real wages after significant economic development in the seventeenth century and the sharp rise in living standards between 1870 and the First World War. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further elaborate on this by noting that wage rates can serve as proxies for average earnings and labour productivity, particularly in regions with vibrant labour markets (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This suggests that technological advances and social changes, such as improved labour market integration, can drive economic growth by enhancing productivity. However, Allen (2001, p.8) qualifies this by pointing out that the gains from 1815 to 1850 were part of a cycle rather than a sustained trend (Allen 2001, p.23). This indicates that while technological advancements and social changes can spur economic growth, their effects may be cyclical rather than linear.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonists established varied institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, which significantly impacted income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical paths and periods (Austin 2008, p.2). The establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies contrasts sharply with the development of inclusive institutions in settler colonies, highlighting how institutional choices directly affect economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that even when institutions are designed to maximize rents for colonizers, they still shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin's observation that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, further emphasizing the dynamic role of institutions in economic development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The interaction between institutions and economic opportunities is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional perspective is complemented by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women's roles in agriculture and commodity production were pivotal, despite being marginalized by customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The codification of customary law reinforced gender disparities, but it also reflects the broader institutional changes that shaped economic development.
