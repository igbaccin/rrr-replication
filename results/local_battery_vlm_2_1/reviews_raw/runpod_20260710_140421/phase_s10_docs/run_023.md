The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers behind a country's prosperity over extended periods. The debate revolves around two main positions: one that emphasizes the primacy of institutional factors in determining economic outcomes, and another that highlights alternative causes such as geographic or demographic factors. These rival explanations offer competing narratives about how societies evolve economically over time.

A central argument is that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of either extractive or growth-promoting institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, in regions with high mortality rates among Europeans, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, colonies were characterized by extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach differs from settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences had lasting effects on income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development.

The significance of these institutions is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002) emphasis on how they influenced industrialization during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). They contend that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced through colonialism, as well as geographic factors. This perspective is supported by Austin's (2007) analysis of Ghana, where the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4). The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour during 1874-1939 illustrates how changing economic conditions can spur institutional innovation. These findings collectively underscore the role of institutions in adapting to and mitigating economic challenges, thereby shaping long-run growth trajectories.

However, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that focusing solely on institutional factors may obscure the complex interplay between economic, political, and social dynamics. For example, colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more nuanced relationship between institutions and growth. This perspective challenges the notion of a linear causal link between institutions and economic development, suggesting instead a more intricate web of influences. The debate highlights that while institutions are crucial, they operate within a broader context of historical and socio-economic factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that European colonial institutions were established to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth, and that this institutional framework played a crucial role in determining the economic trajectory of former colonies. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated varied widely across different regions. For example, the mortality rates experienced by Europeans influenced their settlement patterns and subsequent institution-building efforts. This pattern was driven by the institutional design that prioritized short-term gains over sustainable development. The implications of these colonial institutions persisted long after independence, as they shaped the economic policies and governance structures in former colonies. This nuanced view suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is mediated by historical and socio-economic contexts, indicating that institutional effects are not uniform across different settings. The complex interplay between colonial institutions and local conditions underscores the need for a more holistic understanding of economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2007, p.20),  argue that in African economies, particularly Ghana during the period of 1874-1939, institutional change in factor markets did not directly address long-run economic growth. This institutional revolution was marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change was driven by both legal actions and broader economic pressures, as Akyem chiefs sold land to strangers to farm cocoa. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal moments that reshaped the economic landscape (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional changes did not necessarily translate into long-run economic growth. This nuanced perspective highlights the complexity of institutional effects on economic development, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by various historical and socio-economic factors. The fact that colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to local economic conditions further underscores this point (Austin 2008, p.15).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Bohannan 1959, p.10) examine how economic changes in African societies were shaped by the contributions of women during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their analysis underscores that women significantly contributed to economic growth through their agricultural labor, despite facing marginalization from access to land for cash crops under colonial rule. The central role of men in codifying customary law reinforced this gendered division of economic resources (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic was not limited to agricultural practices; the introduction of money into subsistence economies also reshaped economic institutions. Bohannan's work on the Tiv economy illustrates how the integration of a new road network and increased trade altered traditional economic structures, bringing local markets into contact with global economic systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This transition was not seamless; the equivalence between new monetary forms and traditional rights in women or food could not be easily established within the prestige sphere. This suggests that while economic changes driven by women's labor and new trade networks propelled growth, institutional shifts were complex and often disruptive to existing social norms (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Allen et al. (2011, p.22) and Abramovitz share that real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries provide an important indicator of economic growth, but this period saw stagnation rather than improvement. The existence of a vibrant labor market and significant regional migration suggest that wage rates could serve as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This dynamic is evident in early modern China where day labor was common, particularly during peak seasons, indicating a relatively integrated labor market between urban and rural areas. The income distance of 20% from the mean per capita income shows that agricultural day laborers were at the lower end but not marginal to the income ladder (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This integration suggests that while there was economic activity, it did not translate into significant wage growth over the long term. The stagnation in real wages contrasts with periods of economic growth where wage increases are typically observed, suggesting a lack of broad-based productivity improvements during this period. Allen et al. (2011, p.24) also point out that while there was economic activity, it did not translate into significant wage growth over the long term, highlighting the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks shape economic performance over extended periods.

Economic divergence and technological advance are pivotal factors influencing long-run economic growth (Allen 2001, p.8; Abramovitz 1986, p.21). The dynamics of this process involve complex interactions between institutional frameworks and technological progress. This dynamic is crucial because it underscores the role of institutional frameworks in managing and responding to these changes. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) elaborates on this by identifying social capability with technical competence and institutions capable of mobilizing capital for large-scale enterprise, indicating that robust institutional structures are essential for leveraging technological advancements effectively. This complexity is further highlighted by Allen's observation that the gains from 1815 to 1850 were a cycle-and a minor one at that-not a trend, indicating that technological advancements do not always translate into sustained economic growth without supportive institutional frameworks.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either facilitated or hindered development. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of colonial institution imposed by European powers was crucial for economic outcomes in their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The distinction between extractive and inclusive institutions is key; extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonizers, often impeded long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical processes by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This simplification overlooks the nuanced institutional changes that occurred during colonial rule. Austin highlights the importance of local economic and political action in shaping institutional outcomes, demonstrating that colonial administrations sometimes adjusted their policies in response to indigenous initiatives (Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical context of Ghana illustrates this dynamic. Allen notes that technological advancements did not always translate into sustained growth without supportive institutions (Allen 2001, p.23). Austin further elaborates on the institutional revolution in factor markets during 1874-1939, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which were responses to changing factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.4). Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role. They show how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming under colonial rule, reinforcing patriarchal structures through customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The precise impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is nuanced and context-dependent. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, as well as geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin's work on Ghana shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to significant institutional changes in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4), which aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persisted and significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths, leading to an incomplete understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Austin highlights that colonial administrations sometimes adjusted their policies in response to indigenous initiatives, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between local actors and colonial institutions than Acemoglu et al.'s focus on extractive versus inclusive frameworks allows (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence of institutional changes in Ghana, as documented by Austin, settles this disagreement.
