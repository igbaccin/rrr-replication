The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that underscores the complex interplay between historical context and contemporary economic performance. The debate centers around whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or exogenous determinants that shape economic trajectories over centuries. One position posits that institutions are shaped by geographical factors, such as mortality rates and resource endowments, which in turn influence long-run growth. This perspective emphasizes the persistence of institutional legacies from colonial times, arguing that these legacies significantly impact current economic outcomes. Some scholars posit that institutions evolve dynamically under the influence of local socioeconomic conditions and historical events, with initial geographical factors playing a role. These positions conflict over whether institutions are static determinants or dynamic adapters to changing circumstances.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the colonial experiences of different regions, where varying institutional frameworks were established based on European settlement patterns. Acemoglu et al. argue that high mortality rates in certain colonies led Europeans to adopt extractive institutions, which focused on resource extraction and did not foster local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional framework persisted and significantly affected income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions can be seen in the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often reinforced patriarchal alliances and marginalized certain groups, such as women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional legacy continues to influence contemporary economic dynamics, underscoring the importance of historical contexts in shaping long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key factors in explaining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their research shows that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates and settler motivations. In colonies with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted over time and significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled permanently, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were established. This divergence in institutional frameworks highlights how colonial policies shaped long-term economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis underscores the importance of institutions in determining economic development trajectories, showing that extractive states designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings are further supported by evidence from the Polity III data set and other measures of institutional quality. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions continues to influence contemporary economic dynamics, underscoring the enduring impact of historical contexts on long-run growth.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions. The shift was a response to external pressures as well as internal dynamics within colonial administrations. For instance, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating incentives for local economic action (Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional change had profound implications for long-run growth, as it facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour, which was integral to economic development (Austin 2007, p.20). The removal of tariffs and administrative barriers within 'Ghana' further integrated markets, enhancing economic activity. This institutional revolution underscores how changes in factor markets can drive long-term economic growth by fostering more efficient and productive economic structures.

Technological advancement and social changes are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing productivity and living standards over time (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is intricate. This complexity arises from both exogenous factors, such as institutional changes, and endogenous dynamics like technological advancement and social shifts. He posits that these forces can alter a country's productivity rank over time (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). Social capability, encompassing technical competence and robust institutions, plays a crucial role in this process. Measuring social capability remains challenging, often requiring qualitative assessments of institutional frameworks (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Allen et al.'s examination of China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries highlights the significance of labour markets. Their analysis reveals that wage rates can serve as proxies for average earnings and marginal productivity of labour. This is particularly evident in regions with vibrant labour markets, where day labourers were integrated into the income ladder (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). The integration of urban and rural labour markets further supports the idea that institutional frameworks facilitating market efficiency are key drivers of economic growth. Allen et al.'s focus on specific socio-economic groups in China qualifies this by showing how regional variations can influence these dynamics (Allen et al. 2011, p.24).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) along with Bohannan (1959, p.10), focus on specific economic changes due to the introduction of money and women's role in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). This cluster identifies that the introduction of modern institutions, such as colonial legal systems and monetary economies, significantly altered traditional economic structures. The shift from subsistence economies to market-based systems, facilitated by new road networks and trade routes, also brought about profound changes in economic institutions. This transition was not seamless; it involved complex negotiations between traditional practices and newly introduced systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10).

The introduction of money into African societies, for example, transformed local economies from multicentric structures to more centralized ones. Brass rods served as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere but struggled to fulfill all functions of money, such as payment, in broader economic contexts (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This shift had significant implications for women's roles in the economy. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production under colonial rule. This dynamic underscores how institutional changes can have far-reaching effects on economic activities and social structures, even when they are not the primary drivers of long-run growth.

The implications of these institutional shifts go beyond immediate economic impacts. They reveal that while new institutions can drive significant changes in economic behavior, their effects are often mediated by pre-existing social norms and power dynamics. This interplay between institutional change and social structure highlights the complexity of economic development in colonial contexts. The introduction of money and the codification of customary law altered economic practices as well as reshaped gender roles and access to resources. This dynamic underscores how institutions can both facilitate and constrain economic growth, depending on their interaction with local socio-economic conditions.

This regional focus is crucial as Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2), along with Feinstein and Thomas, emphasize the necessity of collaborative research for accurate measurements of economic performance. Their work highlights that estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error, which increase as one goes further back in time due to partial data and assumptions linking these data to economic activities. These errors are based on subjective estimates of underlying data. The authors' collaborative approach aims to refine historical economic data by inviting scholarly disagreement and new research, a strategy that has significantly improved the detailed overview of long-term economic growth since Maddison's pioneering work in the 1960s. This ongoing debate and updates to historical economic data underscore the dynamic nature of economic performance measurements, where continuous collaboration is essential for refining our understanding of past economies. Bolt and van Zanden note that these margins of error suggest a certain degree of objectivity, whereas they are actually based on subjective estimates of the possible margins of error of the underlying data (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This subjectivity in measurement highlights the need for rigorous collaborative efforts to enhance the accuracy and reliability of economic performance data over time.

High-mortality colonies often had extractive institutions, while low-mortality areas saw the establishment of rule-of-law-based systems. This divergence in institutional frameworks directly impacted long-run economic outcomes. Austin highlights the dynamic nature of institutional evolution and its influence on economic growth over time (Austin 2008, p.2). Colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous actions, illustrating how institutions could be shaped by local conditions and interactions. The interaction between colonial powers and local populations thus played a crucial role in determining the long-term development paths of former colonies.

These institutions, often designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth, had lasting effects on economic development. This perspective is complemented by Austin's analysis, which shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies to local conditions and indigenous actions, illustrating how institutions can evolve in response to changing economic and political landscapes (Austin 2008, p.2).
