The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that cuts to the heart of debates about why some countries prosper while others stagnate. At stake is understanding how formal and informal rules influence economic outcomes over extended periods. Two prominent positions exist within this debate: one view argues that institutional differences, particularly those rooted in colonial legacies, are pivotal in determining long-term growth patterns; another perspective questions the simplistic causal relationships attributed to institutions, emphasizing instead the complex interplay of historical factors and local adaptations.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote economic development. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) observation that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from rule-of-law-enforcing systems in places like the United States to extractive states in regions such as the Congo and Gold Coast. These differing institutions significantly influenced income per capita in these areas. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical processes by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative.

The complexity of institutional evolution is further highlighted by Austin's (2008, p.2) critique of the new institutionalist economic historiography. He notes that this approach, while emphasizing secure private property rights as key to long-term growth, struggles with quantitative testing and explaining why some countries adopted favourable institutions while others did not. This points to a need for more nuanced analyses that consider local adaptations and historical contingencies. Austin (2008, p.15) also illustrates how colonial administrations sometimes adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions, indicating that institutional changes could be influenced by local dynamics.

Austin (2007, p.20) provides a case study of Ghana during 1874-1939, showing how the shift from land abundance to scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This included the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by factors such as the loss of slaves and the need for chiefs to sell land to strangers. Austin (2007, p.4) further notes that British rule had significant market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what became Ghana. This historical context reveals how institutional changes can be driven by shifts in local factor endowments and external influences.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) thesis emphasizes the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies during colonial times, which in turn influenced long-run development outcomes in former colonies. This perspective aligns with Austin's (2008, p.2) argument that institutional evolution is a complex process shaped by multiple factors, including local adaptations and historical contingencies.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly based on mortality rates and the settlers' intentions to establish institutions that either enforced the rule of law or created extractive states. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as parts of Africa and Latin America, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted over time, hindering economic growth by discouraging investment and limiting opportunities for local development. In colonies with lower mortality rates, like Australia and New Zealand, Europeans settled permanently and implemented institutions that protected property rights and encouraged investment, fostering long-term economic prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these colonial-era institutions continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This historical legacy underscores how initial institutional frameworks set during colonial times can have enduring effects on a region's economic trajectory.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and economic divergence are discussed through various historical lenses, but these discussions do not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Allen 2001, p.8). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods. Allen (2001, p.8) adds to this perspective by noting that real income divergence occurred between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This suggests that the institutional frameworks which supported these leaders played a pivotal role in maintaining their economic status (Allen 2001, p.23).

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (Abramovitz 1986, p.5; Abramovitz 1986, p.21) discuss the complexities of the catch-up process in economic growth, highlighting exogenous factors and technological advancements as key drivers. Abramovitz identifies social capability, including political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions, as a critical factor influencing a country's ability to absorb and implement new technologies, which is pivotal for catching up economically (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This capability encompasses technical competence as well as the organization and management of large-scale enterprises. Allen elaborates on this by emphasizing how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on their adaptability and effectiveness in mobilizing resources. This evolution is crucial for sustaining long-run economic growth, as it allows institutions to remain relevant and effective despite changing circumstances. This highlights the need for further research into how institutional changes interact with other factors in driving economic growth over the long term.

Allen et al. show that real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries remained stagnant (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Europe saw significant improvements in living standards during this period. One mechanism driving this stagnation is the segmented labor market described by Allen et al. which was characterized by vibrant but localized day labor markets (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). These conditions suggest that while there was a robust demand for labor, it did not translate into broader economic dynamism or productivity growth, likely due to institutional barriers hindering efficient resource allocation. This perspective is supported by the work of Allen et al. which indicates that despite the presence of day laborers in significant numbers, long-term labor market integration remained thin (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). The lack of technological innovation and industrialization in China during this period was also influenced by global economic dynamics and trade patterns Austin (2008, p.2). This underscores how institutional factors, such as those affecting labor markets, play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Austin (2007, p.20) share the finding that institutional changes in Africa significantly altered economic dynamics during colonial rule. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transformation was driven by the emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony, which abolished slavery and pawning, thereby facilitating a transition to free labor (Austin 2007, p.20). The loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This shift had profound economic implications, propelling Ghana's economic growth in the early 20th century.

This institutional change varied across Africa. Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlights that the introduction of money into subsistence economies led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economies. This transition was facilitated by peace and new road networks, which increased trade and the number of markets. The economic institutions of groups like the Tiv were integrated into the world economy, leading to significant economic shifts (Bohannan 1959, p.10). Despite these changes, Bohannan notes that brass rods, a form of currency within the prestige sphere, did not fully integrate with other economic functions such as marriage and food exchange, indicating a partial integration of new institutions into existing economic structures. This suggests that while institutional changes drove economic growth in some regions, the extent and nature of these changes varied significantly across Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who notes that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). The role of institutions in economic development is further highlighted by the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, which significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the type of institutions introduced during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic outcomes.

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that this reversal was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This view is supported by Austin (2007, p.20), who shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). The codification of customary law under colonial rule further reinforced the subordinate status of women, highlighting how institutions can marginalize certain groups and affect economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The de-privileging of women in access to land, reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule, shows how institutions can shape economic outcomes by influencing resource allocation and incentives (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).
