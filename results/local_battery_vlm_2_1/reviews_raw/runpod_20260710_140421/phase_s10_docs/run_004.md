The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake intellectually is an explanation for why some societies have thrived economically while others have stagnated. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography and climate play a more significant role. One position argues that institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is challenged by views that emphasize the role of geographic endowments and climatic conditions in determining long-term economic outcomes. The complexity of this debate highlights the need to disentangle the effects of institutions from other potential determinants of growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both engage with the idea that colonial legacies have significantly influenced institutional frameworks, but their approaches differ in emphasis. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time, taking into account the dynamic interactions between colonial policies and local economic conditions. For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to localized scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.4), marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This transformation underscores how changes in resource availability can drive institutional adaptations that support economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that European settlers implemented varied colonization strategies, resulting in either extractive or inclusive institutions. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutional legacies highlights their enduring impact on long-run economic development, even after centuries have passed since colonial rule ended.

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further illustrated by the experiences of former European colonies and the dynamics of post-colonial development. Acemoglu et al (2001) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) both explore how institutional legacies from colonial times continue to influence contemporary economies, but they focus on different aspects of this relationship. Acemoglu et al argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions, frequently crafted to boost rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), have left a lasting mark on economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) observe that patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule often relegated women's roles in economic development to the margins, demonstrating how institutional frameworks can either bolster or impede economic progress. This interplay between institutions and economic outcomes underscores the complex nature of long-run economic growth.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of property rights and legal frameworks. Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'new institutionalist' approach for its difficulty in testing hypotheses quantitatively and its inability to explain why some countries adopted growth-promoting institutions while others did not (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that a more comprehensive understanding of institutional evolution is necessary to fully grasp how institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al (2001) provide empirical support for the importance of property rights and rule of law by showing that different measures of institutions yield similar results in regressions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This finding underscores the robustness of institutional frameworks as key determinants of long-run economic growth, regardless of the specific metrics used to assess them. The enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economies highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical legacies continue to influence economic development today.

Colonial powers established diverse institutions based on their mortality rates and settlement patterns, leading to either inclusive or extractive frameworks. The enduring impact of these colonial institutions is evident in their persistent influence on contemporary economies, shaping patterns of income and development. One downstream implication of this dynamic is that former colonies with extractive institutions often face significant challenges in achieving sustained economic growth. This trajectory is further complicated by the fact that politically powerful groups within these societies may resist institutional reforms that could undermine their existing privileges (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

These shifts led to the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor, transforming factor markets. The transition from coerced to free labor was a pivotal moment that reshaped economic dynamics in colonial Ghana. This institutional revolution was influenced by both external pressures and local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, the British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and subsequent prohibition of slavery in Ashanti in 1908 were critical legal actions that facilitated this transition. These changes had significant economic implications, driving Akyem chiefs into debt and prompting them to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were adaptable enough to respond to indigenous economic activities, indicating that colonial institutions could adjust to local contexts beyond mere extraction, even if they did not always align with the interests of European settlers. This flexibility highlights how colonial administrations could be responsive to local economic conditions and political actions, thereby shaping long-run economic growth in nuanced ways.

Akyeampong and Fofack examine how women significantly contributed to economic growth in Africa during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). They show that African women were integral to the economic revolution of the Gold Coast, where they farmed marginal lands for food while men focused on cash crops like cocoa. Bohannan argues that the introduction of money into subsistence economies led to profound changes, shifting away from a multicentric economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The new road networks and increased trade disrupted local economic institutions, integrating them with the world economy. This integration was not without its challenges; the transition involved significant pain points, such as the inability of brass rods to serve all functions of money within the Tiv society (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This highlights how economic changes driven by colonial institutions could reshape societal structures and gender roles, but also created tensions that influenced long-run economic growth. The codification of customary law under colonial rule exemplifies how institutional changes could both facilitate and complicate economic development in Africa.

In a different context, Allen et al. (2011, p.22) argue that real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries did not significantly improve, challenging the notion of broad economic progress during this period. This stagnation is evident across different socio-economic groups and regions, with wage rates serving as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labour. Allen et al. (2011, p.24) further substantiate that real wages in China were relatively low compared to other parts of the world at the time, particularly Europe. This indicates that while China had a vibrant labour market, especially for short-term or day labour, the overall economic conditions did not translate into significant wage improvements. The stagnation of real wages suggests that institutional factors, such as limited integration of labour markets and low productivity in agriculture, played a crucial role in constraining economic growth during this period. Thus, the stagnation of real wages in China highlights how institutional factors can shape long-run economic growth trajectories, even in the presence of a dynamic labour market.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21)(Allen 2001, p.8) show that economic divergence is driven by technological advancement rather than institutions per se. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change; this view contrasts with simple convergence hypotheses, which do not account for such complexities (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Allen's analysis of real incomes between 1500 and 1750 reveals that technological advancements in economic leaders were pivotal in maintaining high living standards while other regions experienced stagnation or decline (Allen 2001, p.23). These findings suggest that the dynamics of technological advance are fundamental to understanding long-run economic growth, as they shape productivity gains and real income trends. This highlights that while technological advancement is a key driver of economic divergence, institutions play a crucial role in mediating its effects on long-run growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their impact on economic development and income disparities across different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with Allen's findings, which highlight how institutional factors such as bulk buying arrangements influence price dynamics and living standards (Allen 2001, p.8). Therefore, while technological advancement drives economic divergence, institutions mediate its effects on long-run growth.

The reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. Moreover, he notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions is further illustrated by their influence on factor markets and economic development. Austin (2007, p.20) shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), meanwhile, argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution was often marginalized due to patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The specific historical evidence of institutional changes in factor markets in Ghana settles the disagreement over whether institutions or geographic factors drove long-run economic growth. The measurement of institutional changes in factor markets, such as land use rights and hired labour, shows that these changes were pivotal to economic development during the period 1874-1939 (Austin 2007, p.4). This evidence supports Austin's argument against Acemoglu et al.'s thesis by showing that institutions designed for indigenous economic action can drive long-run growth.
