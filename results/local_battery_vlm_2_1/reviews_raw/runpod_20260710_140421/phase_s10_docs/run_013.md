The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians alike. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures and governance frameworks influence sustained economic development over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on the extent to which institutional factors, as opposed to geographic or climatic conditions, drive economic outcomes. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions in determining growth trajectories, while others argue for a more nuanced view that incorporates multiple factors beyond just institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.6) present a strong case for the institutional perspective by demonstrating how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes in European colonies. They illustrate that where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law institutions, economic growth was encouraged; conversely, extractive states that prioritized resource transfer to the metropole stifled development. This argument is supported by evidence showing that institutions shaped by colonial powers significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective underscores the critical role of institutional design in economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) adds complexity by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits a direct link between colonial institutions and modern economic outcomes, oversimplifies historical paths. Austin notes that European settlers' presence or absence influenced indigenous commodity production and institutional changes in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by local contexts and historical contingencies.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the role of women in African economies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. This perspective shows how institutional changes under colonialism affected economic outcomes as well as social structures.

The institutional framework's influence on long-run economic growth is evident in the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana. Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) describes an institutional revolution in factor markets during 1874-1939, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transition was driven by legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance and the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional changes had profound economic implications, demonstrating how shifts in governance can reshape economic landscapes.

Institutional frameworks shape economic growth and influence interpretations of historical data. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) emphasize the importance of collaborative research in measuring economic performance, building on Angus Maddison's work. This perspective underscores the institutional underpinnings of economic data collection and analysis.

Institutional frameworks play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing governance structures, social dynamics, and economic policies. The evidence from various studies highlights how different institutional designs can either foster or hinder development, depending on historical contexts and local contingencies. Understanding these dynamics is essential for policymakers seeking to promote sustainable economic growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. These extractive institutions persisted long after colonial rule ended, significantly impacting income per capita in these regions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further support this claim by demonstrating that alternative measures of institutions, including constraints on executive power and property rights indices, consistently show similar results to those reported (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This consistency underscores the robust relationship between institutional quality and economic outcomes. This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of former colonies, where institutional designs were often tailored to benefit European colonists at the expense of local populations.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) document this reversal and link it to industrialization opportunities during the 19th century, suggesting that the interaction between institutions and the ability to industrialize played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding underscores how institutional legacies from colonial times continue to influence economic trajectories.

This period saw the emergence of widespread markets for both land use rights and hired labor, driven by a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity. This institutional evolution was further bolstered by legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti. These changes transformed economic structures and had profound implications for local governance and social dynamics. The integration of these colonies under British rule removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade, fostering a more integrated market (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin's work underscores the critical role of institutional shifts in shaping long-run economic growth by demonstrating how changes in factor markets can drive economic development.

Bohannan's exploration of the Tiv economy reveals that the introduction of money altered traditional economic institutions (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This shift was a profound transformation driven by the integration of African economies into global markets. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, for instance, systematically limited women's access to land and resources. This de-privileging was particularly pronounced in the context of cash crops, where men dominated the legal frameworks governing land use (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). While these institutional changes facilitated economic growth by integrating local economies into global markets, they also entailed significant social costs, particularly for women who were systematically excluded from key economic activities. This dynamic underscores how institutions can both drive and constrain long-run economic growth, with profound implications for social equity.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) (Allen et al. 2011, p.22), along with Allen et al., highlight the critical role of collaborative research in enhancing historical economic data. By refining national accounts, scholars can better estimate economic performance over time, even when data is partial or assumptions are necessary. For instance, real wages in China during the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries illustrate this challenge, as Allen et al. show that these wages remained stagnant despite institutional and economic continuities with later periods (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This finding underscores how accurate measurement is essential for understanding long-term economic trends.

Collaborative efforts also reveal regional variations in economic conditions. For example, Allen et al. document a vibrant labour market in early modern China, where wage rates served as proxies for average earnings and marginal productivity (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This regional specificity suggests that while national accounts provide a broad overview, local data is crucial for capturing the nuances of economic performance. Collaborative research improves historical economic data and highlights the importance of regional context in assessing long-run economic growth.

The dynamics of economic divergence and convergence are driven by complex interactions between technological progress and social changes (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen 2001, p.8). Abramovitz posits that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance; Allen's analysis of real wage trends in Europe reinforces this perspective, showing how technological developments and population shifts have historically driven changes in living standards. The long swings in British real wages reflect broader patterns of economic development and social change (Allen 2001, p.23). These findings suggest that the institutional framework, which includes political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions, plays a crucial role in mobilizing resources for technological advancements (Abramovitz 1986, p.21), but this framework is not static. The institutional context can either facilitate or hinder the absorption of new technologies, thereby influencing long-run economic growth. For instance, Allen's work on grain prices highlights how bulk buying arrangements and long-term supply contracts could affect price stability and economic conditions over time (Allen 2001, p.8). This interplay between technology, institutions, and social changes underscores the multifaceted nature of economic divergence and convergence.

Institutions are central to economic growth because they provide the framework for resource allocation and technological absorption. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions set up by European colonists varied widely across different regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences were pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as extractive institutions in some colonies hindered growth while inclusive ones fostered it (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin's work highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic paths, showing that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions is further underscored by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that this reversal was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by Austin, who demonstrates how shifts in factor markets and land use rights in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution marked by the emergence of widespread markets in hired labour and land use rights (Austin 2007, p.4).

The gendered dimensions of economic growth are also crucial. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This perspective challenges the view that marginalizes women's role in economic development. The interaction between institutions and social changes underscores the multifaceted nature of economic divergence and convergence.

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of colonial institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the detailed analysis of colonial administrative policies and their adaptation to local economic conditions, as documented in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin's study on Ghana (Austin 2007, p.20).
