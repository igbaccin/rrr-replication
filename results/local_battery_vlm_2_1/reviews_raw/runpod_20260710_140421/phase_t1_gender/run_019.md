The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a complex interplay that has significant implications for understanding historical and contemporary economic trajectories. The live disagreement centers on whether gender dynamics are primarily driven by institutional constraints or by deeper cultural and technological factors that influence human capital investment and economic growth. One position argues that institutions, such as those governing property rights and political power, play a pivotal role in determining how resources are allocated and who benefits from economic growth. Another perspective suggests that cultural norms and historical technologies, such as plough use, have lasting effects on gender roles and economic development by influencing societal attitudes towards women's workforce participation and educational access. These perspectives highlight the multifaceted nature of gender and development, where institutional frameworks and cultural practices intertwine to influence long-term economic outcomes. The interplay between these factors is crucial for understanding how historical legacies continue to shape contemporary economic disparities. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) report, “Fewer works have sought to analyze the origins, dynamics and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn 2013), or what happens to gender discrimination in the context of economic growth (Stotsky 2006).”

Lindert and Williamson's work (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) highlights the substantial role of African women in economic growth throughout pre-colonial and colonial eras. These women's contributions were central to production and reproduction processes that supported broader economic development. This perspective challenges views that marginalize women's roles, instead emphasizing their significant impact on economic outcomes. The specific mechanism through which these contributions are realized is the labor market. This is because gender discrimination tends to lower average productivity of workers. This dynamic does not necessarily establish a direct causal link between gender and development as the primary driver of economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack point out that historical plough use correlated with unequal gender roles today, suggesting that cultural factors may mediate the relationship between women's economic contributions and long-term economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The downstream implication is that while African women have been crucial to economic development, the specific mechanisms through which their contributions translate into broader economic outcomes remain complex and multifaceted. This highlights the need for further research on how gender roles and economic development interact over time.

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Komlos (1998, p.7) share a focus on how structural changes in employment and well-being affect broader economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.3; Komlos 1998, p.7). In the U.S., women’s employment shifted from piecework and domestic labor to more professional roles, marking a transition from jobs to careers. This shift involved both economic necessity and a redefinition of personal identity and societal worth through increased human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). The downstream implication is that gendered labor dynamics and health disparities are intertwined with long-run economic outcomes. Komlos (1998, p.7) observes that modern economic growth benefits can be unevenly distributed, affecting different occupational groups differently (Komlos 1998, p.2). Therefore, understanding the interplay between women's employment patterns and broader economic trends requires considering both the structural changes in labor markets and the distribution of health outcomes.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments, which Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain as a consequence of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.4). Colonialism's impact on long-run economic outcomes occurs through institutional changes and its effect on human capital and ethnic fractionalization. Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades, a significant aspect of colonial history, had an adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.4). The insecurity caused by the slave trades confined people within ethnic boundaries, constructing spheres of interaction and leading to high levels of ethnic fractionalization in Africa today. Austin (2008, p.23) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This critique suggests that the impact of colonialism on long-run economic outcomes is more complex than simply institutional changes and requires a nuanced understanding of various historical factors. The interplay between colonial institutions, human capital, and ethnic fractionalization shapes long-run economic outcomes in profound ways.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) argue that the political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework was critical for economic growth because it enabled secure contracting across time and space, a necessary condition for modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor. They point out that the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and the enforcement mechanisms were crucial factors driving this growth. North and Weingast focus on the institutional framework itself, North (1989, p.1) acknowledges Coase's insights into transaction costs, which underline how positive transaction costs necessitate an understanding that institutions matter in economic growth.

This perspective underscores the importance of credible commitments by regimes to secure property rights. The evolution of English political and economic institutions illustrates how these commitments fostered a stable environment conducive to long-term economic development. This stability was crucial for enabling impersonal exchange, which is essential for modern economies. North (1989, p.1) also highlights that the neoclassical model's assumption of zero transaction costs is restrictive. The broader implications of this historical context suggest that institutions governing public choice are a dynamic force shaping economic outcomes over the long run.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (Temin 2002, p.4; de Vries 1994, p.17) examine economic growth through the lens of labor allocation, focusing on historical episodes where shifts in labor distribution drove significant economic changes. Temin posits that post-WWII European growth was fueled by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, not just policy or ordinary factors. This perspective highlights how resource misallocation can create developmental deficits, distinct from the low income associated with conditional convergence. De Vries similarly argues for the Industrious Revolution as a precursor to industrialization, driven by increased labor input and altered consumption patterns. The Industrious Revolution transformed household production systems, making them more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers. This shift had profound economic consequences, including improved nutrition, lower morbidity, mortality, and enhanced educational levels.

The Industrious Revolution's impact on household production systems illustrates how changes in labor allocation can drive broader economic transformations. Temin (2002, p.4) qualifies that while resource misallocation can create developmental deficits, it is not the sole determinant of growth. He argues that differences in education between countries eliminate the prediction of unconditional convergence. This suggests that while labor allocation and household production patterns are crucial, they operate within a broader context shaped by factors such as human capital and educational levels.

The interplay between labor allocation and economic growth underscores the complex dynamics at work in long-run economic outcomes. These dynamics highlight how shifts in labor distribution can drive significant economic changes, but also how these shifts are influenced by broader socio-economic factors. This perspective offers valuable insights into how gender and development may have shaped long-run economic outcomes through their influence on labor allocation and household production systems.

Mokyr (2005, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) jointly argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological advancements have been pivotal in driving long-term economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This process involves collective societal knowledge, which encompasses the sum of all pieces of individual knowledge. Greater specialization and professionalization within societies have significantly expanded this collective knowledge base, making it vastly larger than ever before.

The expansion of useful knowledge has profound implications for economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.4) notes that modern economic growth differs from pre-modern periods in its reliance on the accumulation of knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This dynamic interaction between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is crucial for efficient use of innovations.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) builds on these ideas. McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies are primary drivers of economic change (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This perspective underscores how the intellectual environment, shaped by beliefs about the physical world and natural phenomena, can influence economic growth trajectories. The role of ideas extends beyond technological innovation to include broader societal changes. It is this interplay between knowledge accumulation, institutional adjustments, and ideological shifts that collectively shapes long-run economic outcomes, making it a complex and multifaceted process.

The collective expansion of useful knowledge influences the structure of societies in ways that extend beyond mere economic growth; these changes also affect family formation, urbanization, and societal views on individual roles.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19) challenge the notion that European economic development between 1500 and 1800 was accompanied by significant improvements in living standards. They argue that real wages declined during this period, particularly in the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, and that there is no clear connection between grain wages, standard of living, and economic growth. This perspective highlights the complexities of early modern European development, where economic progress did not necessarily translate into improved nutritional status or overall well-being.

The lack of a straightforward relationship between economic growth and living standards can be attributed to several factors. For instance, mortality crises such as plague epidemics led to increased availability of agricultural land and farm capital, which could temporarily ameliorate nutritional quality (van Zanden 1999, p.3). This effect was not uniform across different regions or social groups, indicating broader structural changes were at play. Gender inequality also played a significant role in shaping these outcomes; it had a notable impact on height differences between high and low socioeconomic statuses, with the effects of gender inequality being about half as strong as those of social inequality (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). These findings underscore the importance of considering both economic and social factors when examining long-run economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. Their analysis reveals that unequal distributions of wealth and income were heavily influenced by factor endowments, such as mineral resources and labor availability. The economic structures that evolved in these colonies were shaped by the abundance of low-skilled labor and valuable natural resources, which reinforced existing inequalities. This pattern was particularly pronounced in societies where large plantations dominated the economy and slave populations were prevalent (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This challenges the notion that inequality in New World societies was exclusively driven by colonial policies and suggests a more complex interplay between local factors and external influences. This dynamic underscores how historical economic structures continue to shape long-run economic outcomes by perpetuating inequalities through institutional frameworks that favor elites.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is deeply intertwined with historical and institutional factors that have influenced development trajectories. Goldin shows that women's employment and education significantly transformed over time, shifting from "jobs" to "careers," where occupation and employment came to define one's fundamental identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women contributed substantially to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3), highlighting the centrality of women to production and reproduction. These contributions underscore how gender dynamics have been integral to long-run economic outcomes, with historical shifts in women's roles driving broader economic changes.

The persistence of inequality in New World societies is closely linked to institutional frameworks that favored elites. Sokoloff and Engerman illustrate how the initial allocation of property and tribute created a legacy of unequal wealth distribution (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions in these societies advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interplay between gender and institutional factors is evident in how historical economic structures have shaped long-run outcomes. Goldin's analysis of women's employment transformation aligns with Lindert and Williamson's argument that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality, as shifts in the structure of occupations can drive economic changes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This shared finding suggests that historical economic structures continue to shape long-run economic outcomes by perpetuating inequalities through institutional frameworks that favor elites.

Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how local factors, such as factor endowments and initial property allocations, played a crucial role in shaping inequality in New World societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). It shows that while colonial institutions had a significant impact on economic outcomes, local factors such as factor endowments and initial property allocations were equally influential in perpetuating inequality.
