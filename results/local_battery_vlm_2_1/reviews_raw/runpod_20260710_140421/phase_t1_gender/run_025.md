The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether historical gender dynamics can explain enduring disparities in prosperity across regions. One position holds that gender roles have played a pivotal part in shaping economic institutions and human capital investment, with these factors then feeding back into economic growth. The opposing view attributes economic disparities primarily to geographic or institutional factors independent of gender. This debate hinges on the causal mechanisms linking historical gender dynamics to present-day economic outcomes, particularly how these dynamics might influence institutions and human capital accumulation over time. Understanding this relationship is crucial for grasping why certain regions have lagged behind in development while others have thrived.

The role of gender dynamics in shaping economic institutions has been explored through various lenses. Historical changes in women's employment and education offer insights into broader shifts in societal norms and economic structures. Goldin (2006, p.3; 2006, p.1) documents a significant transformation in women's roles from "jobs" to "careers," marking a shift towards occupations that foster identity and human capital investment. This shift aligns with findings on the impact of gender norms on economic productivity, where historical plough use correlates with contemporary gender inequality and labor force participation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). These studies suggest that deeply embedded gender roles can influence long-term economic outcomes by affecting both individual opportunities and societal structures.

The literature on gender and economic development demonstrates that shifts in women's employment patterns have significant implications for long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). Lindert and Williamson assert that changes in the structure of occupations drive earnings inequality, suggesting that occupational shifts can influence broader economic dynamics. Goldin observes that women's roles transitioned from "jobs" to "careers," indicating a profound change in how employment defines identity and fosters human capital investment.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) argue that African women have played an underappreciated role in economic growth and development. The authors show that women's contributions to production and reproduction were central during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This highlights how women were crucial in establishing the foundation for long-term economic growth. These contributions have often been overlooked due to biases in historical and economic analyses that marginalize women's roles (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

These findings underscore the importance of recognizing the multifaceted contributions of African women to economic development. The underappreciation of their role has implications beyond historical accuracy; it affects contemporary understandings of gender dynamics in economic growth, potentially leading to misguided policies that do not adequately support women's economic participation.

Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Temin 2002, p.4; Austin 2008, p.23). This perspective aligns with Temin's analysis of Europe's post-WWII Golden Age, which attributes unusually rapid economic growth to a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. The mechanism driving this growth is rooted in resource misallocation, distinct from the conventional narrative of low income due to insufficient physical and human capital relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9). This emphasis on resource allocation highlights the significance of structural factors in economic development. Austin's critique highlights that such simplifications can obscure the complex interplay of historical forces shaping long-run economic outcomes including those related to gender and development. The implications are that understanding the role of gender dynamics requires a nuanced approach that accounts for various historical periods and their specific economic contexts. This is especially important when considering how gender roles have influenced economic growth in different regions over time.

Mokyr (2005, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) both assert that advancements in technology and knowledge are pivotal drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge is a key factor in economic development. This accumulation leads to increased specialization, professionalization, and an overall expansion of collective societal knowledge, which in turn fuels economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets argues that technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These changes are both technical and deeply social affecting family structures urbanization and individual views on achievement. This dynamic highlights the need for societies to adapt their institutions and ideologies to harness the full potential of new technologies (Kuznets 1973, p.4). de Vries's work complements this view by emphasizing that increased labor input and new consumption patterns, which he terms the Industrious Revolution, preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This shift in household production systems led to profound economic changes, including increased capital intensity and a focus on quality over quantity in reproduction (deVries 1994). These insights suggest that technological advancements alone do not drive growth; they must be accompanied by societal adjustments that facilitate their efficient use. De Vries qualifies this view by noting that the Industrious Revolution's impact was achieved more through household productive systems than formal economies suggesting a nuanced interplay between technology and social structures (de Vries 1994, p.18).

The literature on economic growth and biological well-being does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). It offers valuable insights into the uneven distribution of benefits from economic growth. This decline was particularly pronounced among those with inelastic human or physical capital supplies, such as handloom weavers (Komlos 1998, p.7). Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) further illustrate this point by showing that both gender inequality and social inequality negatively impacted nutritional status, with social inequality corresponding to a significant height difference between high and low strata. This suggests that the benefits of economic growth were not uniformly distributed, and certain groups, particularly those at the lower end of the income distribution, faced diminished biological well-being (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). The implication is clear: while economic growth can lead to overall development, it does not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. Indeed, the uneven distribution of these benefits may exacerbate existing inequalities, particularly along gender and social lines.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), establish that the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutions that favored elites through government policies. These policies restricted competition and ensured that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed. The unequal distribution of wealth and income was exacerbated by the abundance of low-skill labor and the scarcity of human capital, which led to the concentration of power in the hands of a small elite (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework often resulted in large-scale plantations dominated by slave labor, further entrenching inequality. The efficiency of these large plantations, combined with the overwhelming majority of enslaved populations, created extremely unequal distributions of wealth and human capital (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutional arrangements restricted economic opportunities for the broader population while ensuring that the elite maintained their dominance through secure property rights. Consequently, the majority of the population risked being held up by the powerful elite after they undertook investments. The implications of these findings extend to long-run economic outcomes, suggesting that without inclusive institutions, economic growth may not translate into improved living standards for all segments of society.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with broader developmental processes. The historical contributions of women to economic growth and development highlight their significant role in both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). African societies, for instance, saw substantial participation from women in agriculture and other productive activities, which drove economic growth during these eras (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This dynamic is evident in the U.S. as well, where a quiet revolution transformed women's employment patterns, shifting them from mere piecework to more professional roles that required significant human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.3; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28).

The interplay between gender and development is further complicated by institutional factors. In societies with extractive institutions, the majority of the population, including women, often lack effective property rights protection, which hinders their ability to invest in education and other forms of human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This institutional barrier perpetuates economic inequality, as those without secure property rights are at risk of being held up by the powerful elite after undertaking investments. The persistence of these inequalities is evident in both historical and modern contexts, where gender discrimination tends to lower average productivity and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).

The economic structures that evolved in New World societies with greater inequality were greatly influenced by the factor endowments. These economies often had extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income, which were further exacerbated by policies that restricted competition and advantaged elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The dynamics of these inequalities are evident in the historical context of Latin American colonies, where large slave plantations dominated the economy and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is complex but clear. Gender discrimination and unequal access to education and productive assets have historically lowered productivity and adversely affected long-run growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The significant contributions of women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods underscore their crucial role in development processes. The historical evidence of women's participation in agriculture and other productive activities shows that inclusive institutions can mitigate some of these inequalities. This is evident in the U.S., where women shifted from piecework to more professional roles, indicating a positive correlation between gender equality and economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).
