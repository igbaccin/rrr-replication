The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history and growth literature. The stakes are high because the relative importance of these factors influences how economists understand the roots of inequality and the prospects for sustainable development. One live disagreement centers on whether gender dynamics are integral to the processes that determine economic trajectories or if they merely reflect other underlying causes, such as institutional frameworks or technological advancements. Another debate revolves around the extent to which historical contributions by women have been overlooked in traditional economic narratives, suggesting a need to reassess the role of gender in economic development.

Historical perspectives on women's roles in economic growth reveal significant contributions that are often marginalized in mainstream analyses. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) highlight that African women played crucial roles in both pre-colonial and colonial periods, contributing to economic growth and development. This perspective challenges views that downplay the historical significance of women's labor and underscores their centrality in production and reproduction processes. Goldin (2006, p.1) similarly argues that a quiet revolution transformed women’s employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S., shifting from 'jobs' to 'careers.' This transformation involved economic participation as well as a deeper change in societal perceptions of women's roles. The recognition of these historical contributions suggests that gender dynamics are integral to understanding long-run economic outcomes, as they reflect deeper structural changes in societies.

The interplay between institutions and gender dynamics further complicates the understanding of long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a pivotal role in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies. This perspective emphasizes how extractive institutions can concentrate power in the hands of elites, potentially marginalizing broader segments of society including women. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.5) also discuss how economic structures influenced by factor endowments led to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income in New World societies.

The role of human capital in economic growth further highlights the importance of gender dynamics. Temin (2002, p.9) argues that misallocation of resources, including human capital, is a critical factor in long-run economic outcomes. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.48) argument that industrial technologies were appropriate only for societies with sufficient human capital and that there were systematic cross-country differences in human capital correlated with institutional differences. The correlation between institutions and human capital likely reflects the fact that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This underscores how gender dynamics intersect with broader economic factors to shape long-run outcomes.

The relationship between growth and equality further complicates the role of gender in economic development. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.28) argue that changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and changes in the rate of skills deepening are two critical forces driving inequality across the 19th century. This dynamic suggests that gender dynamics are integral to understanding both growth and equality, as they reflect broader structural changes in societies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) and Goldin (2006, p.3) highlight that African women played pivotal roles in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They were integral to production and reproduction processes, challenging views that downplay their contributions. The role of African women in the economy was multifaceted: they engaged in various forms of labor, including agriculture, trade, and craftsmanship. These activities supported household economies and contributed significantly to broader economic development. This perspective is reinforced by Goldin (2006, p.3), who notes that women often worked as piece workers in manufacturing or labored in service sectors such as domestics and laundresses, roles that required significant skill and effort. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) caution that these contributions were substantial but often undervalued and underrecognized. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between gender roles and economic development, where women's labor was essential yet frequently marginalized. The significance of African women's economic activities during these periods highlights their critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, even as their contributions remain largely unacknowledged in traditional narratives.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes in colonies, particularly contributing to the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic was evident through the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite. The elite's secure property rights allowed them to exploit the majority population who risked being held up after investments, thereby reinforcing inequality. This pattern was particularly pronounced in societies where mineral resources were abundant, such as those under Spanish colonial rule. These institutional frameworks maintained existing inequalities and exacerbated them, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of wealth concentration and political power centralization. However, the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies this process by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that colonial institutions alone do not fully account for contemporary economic disparities (Austin 2008, p.1).

Economic transformations and labor dynamics significantly influence long-run economic outcomes by reshaping resource allocation and productivity (de Vries 1994, p.17; Temin 2002, p.4). De Vries describes the Industrious Revolution as a precursor to the Industrial Revolution, marked by increased labor input and altered consumption patterns. This shift intensified capital use and reoriented market demand towards goods consumed by families rather than individuals, emphasizing human capital investment over quantity of children. The Industrious Revolution thus laid the groundwork for industrial society's achievements in health, nutrition, and education. These changes were driven by technological advancements as well as shifts in household production systems (de Vries 1994, p.17). Temin further elaborates that post-WWII economic growth was fueled by labor misallocation between agriculture and industry, highlighting how resource distribution affects growth trajectories. This disequilibrium, separate from income levels driving conditional convergence, underscores the role of resource allocation in economic development. Komlos notes that despite overall economic growth during industrialization, biological well-being declined for certain groups due to uneven income and nutritional gains. This suggests that while economic transformations drive long-run outcomes, their benefits may not be universally distributed (de Vries 1994, p.17; Komlos 1998, p.7).

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (Pamuk 2007, p.15), along with Nunn (2008, p.27), argue that historical events significantly shaped long-term economic outcomes. These scholars highlight how the Black Death and Africa's slave trades influenced economic trajectories across different regions. The Black Death triggered structural changes, including a decline in the skill premium due to severe labor shortages (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This shift encouraged human capital formation, which was further supported by falling interest rates post-Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.15), but urbanization rates diverged significantly between northwestern and southern Europe. In Italy, for instance, the skill premium rebounded with population recovery in the 16th century, indicating regional variations in economic development trajectories (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The African slave trades also had profound effects on economic development. Nunn's work underscores that these trades increased ethnic fractionalization by creating an environment of insecurity, which confined people within ethnic boundaries and hampered long-term growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). This ethnic fragmentation, in turn, affected the distribution of resources and labor, influencing economic outcomes. These findings collectively illustrate how historical events like the Black Death and slave trades influenced long-term economic outcomes through mechanisms such as labor shortages, skill premiums, and ethnic fractionalization.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and Simson (2019, p.27) examine how distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe, and the budget policies of post-independence East Africa. Both authors highlight that institutions often emerge from power struggles rather than efficiency considerations alone. This perspective is crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes because it shows that institutional arrangements can be deeply influenced by the distribution of political and economic power within a society. These institutions emerged from power struggles between different social groups. For example, serfdom was maintained because it served the interests of landowners who had the political power to enforce it. This dynamic led to poor contract enforcement and property rights, high risks, and limited investments in physical and human capital. Simson (2019, p.6) extends this analysis to post-independence East Africa, where budget policies were similarly influenced by distributional conflicts. The narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services overlooks the broader economic context: many countries ran persistent deficits during the postwar era, and public expenditures grew rapidly until around 1980. The region faced significant challenges, including economic headwinds and military instability. This qualification underscores the importance of considering both internal power dynamics and external economic factors when examining long-run economic outcomes. The broader implication is that distributional conflicts can significantly shape economic institutions and, consequently, long-term economic performance.

Research on pre-industrial economies has often focused on understanding economic performance without explicitly linking it to gender dynamics or long-run economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) both focus on the complexities of measuring economic performance in pre-industrial contexts, but neither directly engages with gender roles. One specific mechanism that emerges from their work is the challenge of data availability and interpretation. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that early Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income above subsistence levels by the early 20th century, challenging earlier narratives that attributed economic growth solely to colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding underscores the importance of re-evaluating historical data to uncover pre-industrial economic dynamics. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies this perspective by highlighting the lack of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards in early modern Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.3). This qualification suggests that while economic performance can be measured, its impact on long-run outcomes is not straightforward. The broader implication is that understanding pre-industrial economies requires accurate data as well as a nuanced interpretation of how economic performance translates into long-term development.

The growth of useful knowledge, technology, and ideas/ideologies are key drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). The expansion of useful knowledge facilitates greater specialization and professionalization. This results in a vast increase in the total amount of social knowledge available to societies over time. The process is cumulative: as more individuals contribute specialized knowledge, society's overall knowledge base grows exponentially. Kuznets (1973, p.4) elaborates on this mechanism by describing how technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.4). These adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, enabling societies to adapt to new economic realities and maintain growth momentum. McCloskey (2015, p.9) qualifies this perspective by noting that ideas and ideologies can sometimes impede economic growth if they prioritize rigid cultural or political agendas over pragmatic adaptation (McCloskey 2015, p.8). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between knowledge expansion, institutional adaptability, and ideological influences on long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutional adjustments are pivotal for economic growth, as they underpin secure property rights and facilitate market development. This view is shared by Lindert and Williamson, who also emphasize the role of institutions in fostering economic growth through secure contracting across time and space (North 1989, p.5). Together, these scholars highlight how institutions governing public choice and property rights can drive economic growth by enabling secure transactions and investments (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). North's work further elaborates that changes in political or economic institutions, particularly those affecting the specification and enforcement of property rights, can significantly enhance productivity and thus economic growth. This perspective underscores how institutional frameworks can foster long-term economic development by reducing transaction costs and promoting efficient resource allocation.

North posits that neoclassical theories often overlook the critical role of institutions in economic growth, instead focusing primarily on population and savings as key determinants (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast suggest a more nuanced view: secure property rights are essential for economic development but not sufficient on their own. The political regime's commitment to these rules is equally important, as it ensures that the institutions governing economic exchange remain stable and predictable over time (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional stability, secure property rights, and long-term economic outcomes, suggesting that a holistic approach to institutional development is necessary for sustained growth.

The role of gender in development is multifaceted; it shapes long-run economic outcomes through complex interactions between institutional frameworks and societal dynamics. Goldin shows that women's employment opportunities have evolved significantly over time (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation has altered the labor market as well as broader social structures, influencing how societies view work and identity. Akyeampong and Fofack underscore this point by demonstrating that African women played crucial roles in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Their contributions were essential for production and reproduction, highlighting gender's pivotal role in development across diverse historical contexts.

The institutional framework governing property rights and political commitment is another critical factor. Acemoglu et al. argue that secure property rights are fundamental but not sufficient on their own; the political regime's commitment to maintaining these institutions ensures long-term stability (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis, which shows how institutional structures in New World societies often advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). These findings suggest that the interplay between gender dynamics and institutional stability is crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes. The historical evidence from both colonial and post-colonial contexts supports this view, indicating that gender roles and institutional frameworks are deeply intertwined in shaping economic development.
