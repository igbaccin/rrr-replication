Technological advancements and institutional changes are key drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets argues that the modern economy requires rapid shifts in labor force structure and occupational relations to efficiently adopt new technologies. This process extends beyond the economic realm, influencing family formation, urbanization, and societal views on personal achievement. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting how collective knowledge expansion fuels economic growth. Greater specialization and professionalization have exponentially increased societal knowledge, driving economic development. Kuznets (1965) notes that technological advancements are crucial for effective implementation and require significant institutional and ideological adjustments. This means that the benefits of new technologies are not automatically realized; societies must adapt their structures and ideologies to fully harness these innovations.

This implies that while technological progress is a powerful engine of economic growth, its impact on long-run outcomes depends heavily on societal readiness to embrace change. Kuznets's emphasis on institutional adjustments suggests that without concomitant changes in societal structures, even groundbreaking technologies may not yield their full potential benefits.

Acemoglu et al. together with Austin (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.23), highlight that colonialism's institutional legacies have shaped long-term economic outcomes by altering property rights and human capital development. Colonial institutions often secured the property rights of a small elite, leaving the majority vulnerable to exploitation after investing in their communities. This dynamic created a barrier to widespread investment and entrepreneurship (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, colonial policies frequently hindered human capital formation through systematic discrimination and reduced market flexibility, as seen in South Africa under apartheid (Austin 2008, p.23). These institutional changes had profound effects on economic growth. Austin (2008, p. 1) warns that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causality by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative. This suggests that while colonial institutions significantly impacted long-term economic outcomes, their effects varied across regions and time periods, indicating that other factors may also have played crucial roles. Therefore, understanding the complex interplay between colonial institutions, human capital development, and economic growth requires a nuanced approach that considers diverse historical contexts.

Government policies that restrict competition have played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes by perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) show that the economic structures of New World societies with greater inequality were significantly influenced by factor endowments, leading to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income. They argue that a regime's commitment to rules underpins economic development by enabling secure contracting across time and space (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast (1989, p. 2) also recognize the complexity of economic growth, noting that robust property rights are necessary but may not be sufficient on their own. This suggests that while restrictive government policies can perpetuate inequality, other factors such as technological advancements and market dynamics may also influence long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between these factors underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of how institutions shape economic development over time.

The Industrious Revolution and post-WWII Golden Age growth are distinct phenomena that do not directly address gender and development as causal factors in long-run economic outcomes (Temin 2002, p.4; de Vries 1994, p.17). Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) both argue that these periods of rapid economic growth were driven by specific labor allocation mechanisms. This shift was marked by a more capital-intensive production system oriented towards full-time male workers and a market demand focused on family-consumed goods (de Vries 1994, p.17). The implication of these mechanisms is that changes in labor allocation and productivity can drive significant economic growth, independent of gender dynamics. de Vries (1994, p. 17) observes that this period saw a focus on quality over quantity in human capital formation, suggesting that while gender roles were influential, they were not the primary driver (de Vries 1994, p. 18). This nuanced view highlights that even when labor allocation is a key factor, underlying social structures, including gender dynamics, can shape economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), focus on historical economic growth and divergence in Africa and Asia. They demonstrate that both African and Asian economies had significant economic activity before European colonial interventions, challenging the prevailing notion of stagnant pre-colonial economies. This perspective underscores the importance of local economic dynamics, where "economies of localization" played a crucial role by enabling specialized production and trade within regions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). These localized economies were characterized by dense networks of producers, skilled labor, and thick markets for inputs and outputs, all of which facilitated economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the pre-colonial economies of Africa and Asia, where local industries thrived through merchant distribution and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p. 25). This finding suggests that local economic factors were instrumental in driving growth. However, Broadberry and Gardner also note that the limited availability of data on long-run African economic performance has shaped research questions, which often focus on identifying sources of slow growth rather than acknowledging pre-existing economic activities (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This points to the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the role of local economic dynamics in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Van Zanden's work on European economic development and standard of living between 1500 and 1800 shows that there is no clear relationship between economic growth and improvements in the standard of living. This shared finding (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). The benefits of economic growth during this period were not uniformly distributed, leading to disparities in nutritional resources and physical stature across different regions and occupations (Komlos 1998, p.7). For instance, handloom weavers at the lower end of the income distribution experienced a decline in biological well-being despite overall economic growth. This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that while some groups may have experienced improvements in their standard of living, others were made worse off by the onset of industrialization (Komlos 1998, p.7). The interpretation of this pattern is central to understanding the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period. This finding underscores the importance of considering the distribution of benefits when evaluating the impact of economic growth on long-term outcomes.

These findings suggest that the economic roles of women were influenced by broader institutional frameworks that shaped their opportunities for education, employment, and societal worth.
