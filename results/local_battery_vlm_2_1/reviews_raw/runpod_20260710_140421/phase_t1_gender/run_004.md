The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue where different scholars propose competing explanations. At stake intellectually is the extent to which historical gender dynamics influence contemporary economic structures and growth trajectories. Some scholars emphasize that women's contributions to economic growth have been historically marginalized, while others argue that institutions shaped by colonialism and inequality are more determinant of long-run outcomes. The disagreement centers on whether gender roles or institutional factors are primary drivers of economic development. A related but distinct question is whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis adequately explains the divergence in incomes among former European colonies.

One key position argues that women's historical contributions to economic growth have been systematically underestimated. For instance, African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This perspective challenges views that marginalize women's roles in economic history by highlighting their centrality to production and reproduction. Furthermore, the transformation of women's employment and education patterns in the U.S. reflects a broader shift from 'jobs' to 'careers,' where occupation and employment define one's fundamental identity (Goldin 2006, p.1). This quiet revolution involved changes in both economic participation and societal worth, as well as human capital investment. The evidence suggests that gender dynamics are deeply intertwined with long-run economic outcomes, shaping both historical trajectories and contemporary economic structures.

In the same vein, the role of institutions in shaping economic development is a critical factor to consider. Institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, along with geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in determining economic outcomes, highlighting how extractive institutions can hinder human capital formation and economic growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize may capture the possible interaction between industrialization and some country characteristics correlated with institutions themselves.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis offers a compelling narrative but oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This perspective argues that the stagnation of certain economies, such as South Africa's during apartheid, owed much to systematic racial discrimination in hindering both human capital formation and market flexibility (Austin 2008, p.23) . This highlights how institutional factors, including those rooted in colonial legacies, can shape long-run economic outcomes. Yet there is also a shared finding that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance and suggests that pre-colonial factors may have played a more significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes than previously thought.

Goldin argues that women's employment and education significantly transformed long-run economic outcomes in the U.S., challenging views that see this change as merely evolutionary (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This quiet revolution began with women entering manufacturing and service sectors. They were often piece workers or labored in domestic roles, experiencing little on-the-job learning and minimal gains from formal education beyond elementary school. This dynamic underscores how gender roles can drive long-run economic outcomes by reshaping societal values around work. The transformation of women's employment from mere economic necessity to a defining aspect of identity suggests that cultural shifts in gender norms can have profound economic implications. For example, the historical use of ploughs in certain cultures has been linked to more unequal gender roles today (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This indicates that pre-colonial factors, including gender dynamics, may have played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes by influencing contemporary labor force participation and productivity. This perspective underscores how deeply ingrained cultural norms around gender can persist over time.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutions established during colonial rule, which either protected private property or facilitated rent extraction. These institutional changes created a framework where the masses often lacked effective property rights protection (Austin 2008, p.23; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The resulting inequality in property rights had significant implications for economic development, as it concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite, thereby limiting investment opportunities for the majority. This dynamic is further complicated by the role of human capital, where societies with extractive institutions typically had limited access to education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The adverse effects of these institutional legacies are evident in the long-term economic stagnation observed in many former colonies, particularly in Africa. Nunn (2008, p.27) adds that the slave trades exacerbated ethnic fractionalization, a key determinant of economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.4). This historical context underscores how colonial institutions and practices have shaped contemporary economic outcomes by influencing both institutional frameworks and social dynamics over centuries.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (North 1989, p.5), along with McCloskey (2015, p.8), focus on how institutional changes shaped long-run economic outcomes in Europe. They argue that the development of secure property rights and effective governance structures were pivotal for fostering economic growth, particularly through enabling secure contracting across time and space. This institutional framework allowed for the specialization and division of labor necessary for modern economies. North (1989, p.1) further underscores this by noting that institutions matter in economic growth, as they affect the specification and enforcement of property rights which can increase productivity. He argues that the success of economic growth in Northwestern Europe was due to both institutions and new ideas about trade-tested betterment, which were shaped by successful examples from neighboring countries. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, the broader cultural and ideological context can significantly qualify their impact on long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between institutional frameworks and ideological shifts thus emerges as a critical factor in understanding economic development over centuries.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) show that economic structures and factor endowments can significantly shape institutional development and inequality. The authors argue that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in colonies where abundant mineral resources and low human capital labor supplied by natives or slaves led to extremely unequal wealth distributions. Pamuk (2007, p.15) adds that long-term structural changes initiated by events like the Black Death can also lead to significant wage gaps, with a decline in the skill premium and a drop in interest rates encouraging human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.16). These findings suggest that exogenous shocks, such as pandemics or resource discoveries, can alter economic structures and factor endowments, thereby influencing institutional development. This interplay between economic structures, factor endowments, and institutional frameworks highlights how initial conditions can set economies on divergent paths, with long-lasting effects on inequality and economic outcomes.

The relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe during specific historical periods was complex, with some studies finding that economic growth did not always translate into improved biological well-being for the population. van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Komlos (1998, p.7) both examine this period and find that despite economic growth, real wages declined, especially during the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. This decline in real wages was accompanied by a deterioration in nutritional status, as evidenced by changes in physical stature across different social groups. For instance, van Zanden (1999, p.3) notes that there is no clear connection between the development of grain wages, the standard of living, and economic growth in Europe during this period, suggesting that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed. This uneven distribution of benefits was particularly pronounced among groups with inelastic supplies of human or physical capital, such as handloom weavers, who experienced a decline in their nutritional status (van Zanden 1999, p.3).

The impact of economic development on living standards varied across different regions and social groups. Komlos (1998, p.2) argues that both economic and demographic growth were ubiquitous in late eighteenth-century Europe, indicating regional variations in the relationship between economic development and living standards. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) support this idea by showing that nutritional quality might have been ameliorated during mortality crises due to enhanced availability of agricultural land. This variation suggests that factors such as population density and urbanization played a significant role in shaping the relationship between economic development and living standards. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.28) also highlight the negative impact of gender inequality on nutritional status, suggesting that social inequalities exacerbated the uneven distribution of benefits from economic growth.

The analysis of this stream thus shows that while economic development did not uniformly improve living standards across Europe during these historical periods, specific mechanisms such as population density and urbanization could mitigate some of the negative effects. The downstream implications for gender inequality were complex: even when economic development occurred, its benefits might not have reached all segments of society equally, particularly those already marginalized by social inequalities.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) along with Simson (2019, p.27), present Anglophone African economies and East Africa's post-independence budget policies as having higher per capita incomes than previously thought; their budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist. One mechanism that explains this is the role of colonial infrastructure, which facilitated trade and economic activity. Simson's findings about budget policies further support this, indicating that post-independence governments in East Africa managed their budgets more effectively than often portrayed. Simson also highlights the challenges these countries faced, including military instability and economic headwinds, which qualified the overall economic performance (Simson 2019, p.27). This implies that while these economies showed resilience and capability for growth, external factors such as political instability and global economic conditions played a significant role in shaping their long-run economic outcomes.

The significance of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is evident across different historical contexts. The role of women in economic growth and development has been substantial, particularly during pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Women's contributions to production and reproduction were central to the economy, challenging views that marginalize their role (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical evidence highlights that gender dynamics have played a crucial part in economic development trajectories. The quiet revolution in women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. further illustrates how changes in gender roles can transform economies over time (Goldin 2006, p.1). Similarly, the impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes shows that unequal distributions of wealth and human capital were influenced by broader societal structures shaped by historical contexts (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3).

The debate over the reversal of fortune thesis highlights the complexities in understanding how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes. While Acemoglu et al.'s argument emphasizes the role of colonial institutions, Austin's critique points to a more nuanced view, arguing that the compression of history oversimplifies causation (Austin 2008, p.1). This debate underscores the need for a comprehensive analysis that considers multiple historical periods and paths. The findings from Broadberry and Gardner on Anglophone African economies further support this, showing that these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Austin 2008, p.23). These insights collectively demonstrate that the interplay between gender dynamics and institutional frameworks is pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
