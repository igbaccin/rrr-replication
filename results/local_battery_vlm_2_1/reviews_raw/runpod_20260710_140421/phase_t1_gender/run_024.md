The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question that intersects with broader debates about economic growth and institutional change. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how historical patterns of gender roles and economic development intertwine to influence contemporary economic performance. One key point of contention revolves around whether institutional changes introduced by colonialism, as argued by Acemoglu et al. or the persistence of inequality due to factor endowments, as posited by Sokoloff and Engerman, are more determinative in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Another live disagreement centers on the extent to which historical gender roles, particularly those influenced by agricultural practices such as plough use, continue to affect modern economic development. These debates highlight the complex interplay between gender dynamics, institutional frameworks, and economic trajectories over extended periods. The interplay of these factors is crucial for understanding why some regions have achieved sustained economic growth while others lag behind (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; van Zanden 2009, p.25).

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) argue that gender roles have significantly transformed long-run economic outcomes by shifting women's employment patterns. Goldin describes how women in the U.S. transitioned from piece workers and domestic laborers to professional occupations, marking a shift from "jobs" to "careers" (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation involved changes in occupation as well as societal perceptions of women's roles, moving from economic necessity to identity and worth. Women's contributions were central to production and reproduction, countering the notion that gender discrimination solely hinders economic development.

The quiet revolution in women’s employment had profound implications for human capital investment. This shift was not merely evolutionary; it represented a fundamental change in how societies valued women's work. This transformation varied across different regions and cultures. For instance, historical plough use has been linked to unequal gender roles today, where women from cultures that historically used the plough have less equal gender norms and participate less in the workforce (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This suggests that while significant progress has been made, historical agricultural practices continue to influence modern economic dynamics.

Acemoglu et al. and Nunn's work together establish that colonialism shaped long-run economic outcomes by influencing institutional structures, which in turn affected human capital formation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). When colonial powers introduced extractive institutions, they concentrated power in the hands of a small elite, leaving the majority of the population vulnerable to exploitation. This concentration of political and social power hindered the development of property rights for the broader population, thereby limiting investment opportunities and entrepreneurial activities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The adverse effects of colonialism extended beyond immediate economic exploitation; they also disrupted traditional social structures and human capital formation. Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades, a legacy of colonial practices, exacerbated ethnic fractionalization and insecurity, which further impeded long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). This historical disruption meant that the masses typically did not or could not obtain education in societies with extractive institutions, thereby limiting human capital formation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin's work on South Africa shows how systematic racial discrimination under colonial rule hindered both human capital formation and market flexibility, providing a concrete example of these broader institutional effects (Austin 2008, p.23). Therefore the institutions established by European colonialists played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

de Vries's work on the Industrious Revolution highlights how increased labor input and new consumption patterns influenced economic performance during Europe's pre-industrial era (van Zanden 2009, p.27; de Vries 1994, p.17). This revolution was marked by a shift in household production, which became more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers. The structure of market demand also evolved to focus on goods consumed by the family, reflecting a broader economic transformation. de Vries claims that this new household production pattern had profound consequences for the entire economy, leading to lower morbidity and mortality rates, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort (de Vries 1994, p.17). This shift in household dynamics suggests that the Industrious Revolution laid crucial groundwork for later industrial developments by fostering human capital formation within families. However, van Zanden also notes that low-income countries typically have higher skill premiums than richer ones, indicating that the relationship between skill premiums and economic development is complex and multifaceted.

Broadberry and Gardner note that economic performance in specific regions was often characterized by urbanization rates and historical budget policies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Simson 2019, p.6). These patterns were shaped by the region's structural changes such as the Black Death. Pamuk argues that this plague initiated long-term structural changes leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.15). The skill premium began to decline under conditions of severe labor shortages, continuing with a drop in interest rates across Europe after the Black Death. Simson shows that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were shaped by economic headwinds and military instability (Simson 2019, p.27). Despite the structural changes and wage gaps, a clear pattern emerges: long-run economic outcomes are fundamentally shaped by historical factors and policy responses to regional challenges.

The literature on nutritional status and economic growth does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Komlos (1998, p.7) shows that industrialisation led to unequal distribution of nutritional resources. For instance, handloom weavers were made worse off by the onset of industrialisation because they possessed human or physical capital whose supply was inelastic. This suggests that the benefits of modern economic growth accrued unevenly across different groups.

The authors note that both gender inequality and social inequality have negative signs in their analysis, indicating that these inequalities contribute to reduced nutritional outcomes. This implies that the economic significance of social inequality is more pronounced than that of gender inequality in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) also show that the effect of gender inequality was about half as strong as that of social inequality (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). They attribute fairly large credibility to these results because they are similar to those of many other studies for the eighteenth to twentieth centuries. This suggests that while gender inequality plays a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, it is not the primary driver compared to broader social inequalities.

The role of technological progress in driving economic growth is well-established (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets and Mokyr agree that the expansion of useful knowledge is fundamental to long-term economic development. For example, Mokyr describes how societies with greater consensus on useful knowledge experience more rapid economic growth because they can collectively harness this knowledge for innovation. This suggests that a society's capacity to generate and disseminate useful knowledge directly influences its economic trajectory. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that the dynamic drives of modern economic growth necessitate significant changes in societal structure, including urbanization and shifts in occupational status groups. These institutional and ideological adjustments are essential for efficiently utilizing technological advancements.

The pace at which societies can adapt to these changes varies significantly. Kuznets highlights that high aggregate growth rates are associated with rapid changes in economic structure as well as other aspects of society such as family formation and urbanization. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its impact is contingent upon a society's ability to adapt institutionally and ideologically. Mokyr further qualifies this by noting that the expansion of useful knowledge does not necessarily mean that each individual has more knowledge than their ancestors; rather, it signifies an increase in social knowledge. This collective knowledge base allows for greater specialization and professionalization, driving economic growth. The downstream implication is that societies with robust mechanisms for generating and disseminating useful knowledge are better positioned to achieve sustained economic growth.

Thus, technological progress and the dissemination of useful knowledge play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, although their impact is moderated by societal adaptations.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show that institutions governing public choice shaped economic outcomes through secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This focus on institutional frameworks is shared with Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.6) argument about inequality in New World societies, where the economic structures were heavily influenced by factor endowments (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions, which include secure property rights and rule enforcement mechanisms, are crucial for fostering economic growth. The development of these institutions is pivotal because they enable secure contracting across time and space (North and Weingast 1989, p.30), thereby facilitating specialization and division of labor, key drivers of economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) argues that the efficiency of these institutional frameworks can be constrained by distributional conflicts. Poor contract enforcement and property rights, high risks, and limited investments in physical and human capital can choke off economic dynamism (Ogilvie 2007, p.25). This suggests that while secure property rights are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own; the broader institutional environment must also support efficient contracting and protect against violence and theft. The interplay between these factors underscores the complexity of how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is intertwined with broader institutional frameworks that influence human capital formation and investment opportunities. Goldin shows that women's transition from "jobs" to "careers" marked a significant shift in human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift was a quiet revolution that transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack extend this perspective by highlighting African women’s contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10), emphasizing their centrality in production and reproduction. This underscores the importance of gender roles in historical economic development.

The interplay between institutions and long-run economic outcomes is further illuminated by Acemoglu et al. who argue that secure property rights are crucial for protecting entrepreneurial investments, especially in societies where political power is concentrated among a small elite (van Zanden 2009, p.25). This concentration can lead to the majority of the population being held up by powerful elites after they undertake investments. Van Zanden argues that skill premiums are related to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation and the absence or presence of barriers to entry in these professions (van Zanden 2009, p.27), reinforcing the idea that institutional frameworks play a critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Van Zanden's argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on property rights, showing that efficient institutions are necessary for economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (van Zanden 2009, p.25). This suggests that while secure property rights and efficient institutions are important, they are not sufficient on their own to explain long-run economic outcomes; broader institutional environments must also support human capital formation and investment. Austin's critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how gender and development interact within these broader frameworks.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of skill premiums as a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This measurement shows that while secure property rights are necessary, they must be supported by efficient institutions for human capital formation. The absence of barriers to entry in these professions further underscores the importance of institutional efficiency in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
