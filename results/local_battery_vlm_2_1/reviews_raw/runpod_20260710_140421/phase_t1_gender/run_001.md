The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is an intricate question that intersects with broader debates on economic growth. This topic has been the subject of extensive research and lively intellectual disputes about whether gender equality promotes economic development, or if other factors are more determinative. One key debate revolves around the extent to which gender dynamics can be understood through existing theories of economic development such as human capital theory and institutional efficiency. A second debate concerns how historical institutions shape gender roles. The first debate pivots on whether the role of women in economic activities is a driver or a consequence of broader economic growth, while the latter focuses on whether institutions are shaped by gender dynamics or vice versa. This literature review addresses these debates by synthesizing insights from multiple streams of scholarship that engage with this question directly or indirectly.

A first stream argues for the importance of human capital and institutional efficiency in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Van Zanden argues that the skill premium, which reflects both the relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of capital markets, is a crucial predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that secure property rights for elites can lead to unequal distribution of opportunities, thereby affecting long-run economic outcomes by stifling human capital investments among the broader population (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The efficiency of institutions in forming human capital and reducing barriers to entry into skilled professions plays a pivotal role in determining economic performance. These perspectives underscore that gender dynamics may be intertwined with institutional frameworks.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are key drivers of economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets argues that modern economic growth is defined by a long-term rise in capacity to supply diverse goods, underpinned by advancing technology and the necessary institutional adaptations. He emphasizes how high aggregate growth rates correlate with rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn drive societal transformations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This dynamic aligns with Mokyr's assertion that the growth of useful knowledge is central to economic progress. Mokyr posits that the expansion of collective social knowledge has fueled the West’s economic success (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This suggests that while technological change is pivotal, its benefits are contingent on effective institutional adjustments.

Goldin and Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women's contributions to economic development have been historically significant yet often overlooked (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Goldin's analysis of the U.S. labor market reveals how women were predominantly employed in sectors with limited opportunities for skill acquisition or career advancement, such as manufacturing and domestic service. This marginalization stemmed from societal structures that restricted women's access to higher education and professional careers, as well as individual choices (Author Year p.N). The transformation from "jobs" to "careers" marked a shift in how women perceived their roles within the economy, moving towards occupations that offered long-term growth and identity (Goldin 2006, p.1). This change was gradual, with significant gains beginning around the 1910s as more women entered professional fields. They note that gender discrimination can adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, particularly for female workers. This historical perspective underscores the importance of recognizing women's economic roles beyond traditional narratives, suggesting that addressing gender disparities could yield substantial benefits for overall economic performance. Akyeampong and Fofack also point out that cultural practices, such as plough use, have historically influenced gender norms and labor force participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This implies that while women's contributions are crucial, the path to achieving gender equality in economic development is complex and multifaceted.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes in former colonies, particularly through their impact on inequality and economic structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The colonial policies that concentrated wealth and power in the hands of a small elite often led to institutions that favored extraction over investment. This concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of unequal distributions of wealth and income, influenced by factor endowments such as valuable mineral resources and abundant labor with low human capital, further entrenched these inequalities. This dynamic is evident in economies dominated by large plantations and slave labor, where the distribution of wealth and human capital was extremely unequal (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). In such societies, the economic structures evolved to favor the elite, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. Austin challenges this narrative by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, noting that systematic racial discrimination hindered both human capital formation and the growth of demand (Austin 2008, p.23). This suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, the complex interplay of factors such as racial discrimination and market flexibility also played significant roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) argue that institutions governing public choice played a crucial role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is supported by North's (1989) argument that economic growth can occur through increasing productivity, driven by changes in institutions that affect the specification and enforcement of property rights. This institutional framework allows for specialization and division of labor, which are essential for modern economies dependent on impersonal exchange. North further elaborates that institutions matter in economic growth because the neoclassical model's assumption of zero transaction costs is unrealistic; with positive transaction costs, institutions significantly influence economic outcomes (North 1989, p.1). The credibility of these institutions is reinforced by their enforcement and adaptation mechanisms, which ensure long-term economic stability. However, McCloskey (2015, p.9) qualifies this institutional focus by emphasizing that ideas and ideologies also played a significant role in economic change; she argues that the success of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven more by cultural shifts than by institutions alone (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This suggests that while institutions are necessary for economic growth, they may not be sufficient without complementary ideological support.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17), along with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1), examine various aspects of economic growth and labor allocation but do not directly address how gender and development shaped long-run economic outcomes. This perspective emphasizes that economic growth can be influenced by the efficient distribution of labor across different sectors, as well as policy interventions or technological advancements (Temin 2002, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further illustrate this point by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. De Vries (1994, p.17) qualifies this view by arguing that the Industrious Revolution, which involved increased labor input and new consumption patterns, was a distinct phenomenon preceding and facilitating the Industrial Revolution. De Vries (1994, p.17) suggests that this revolution had profound consequences for the entire economy, including changes in household production systems and market demand structures (de Vries 1994, p.17). This implies that while efficient labor allocation is crucial for economic growth, it is also important to consider the broader socio-economic context in which these allocations occur.

van Zanden (1999, p.1); (Komlos 1998, p.7) and Komlos (1998, p.7) argue that economic development in Europe did not necessarily translate into improved standards of living. Van Zanden highlights the lack of a clear connection between economic growth and improvements in real wages or material wealth during the early modern period, challenging optimistic views of this era (van Zanden 1999, p.1). Komlos extends this argument to the Industrial Revolution, showing that despite overall economic growth, certain segments of society experienced declining biological well-being due to uneven distribution of nutritional resources (Komlos 1998, p.7). This disparity suggests that economic development can exacerbate inequalities, particularly when gains are not evenly distributed. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) qualify this view by demonstrating that while population density and urbanization might improve access to certain goods like milk, these factors do not uniformly enhance nutritional status (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Additionally, they find that both social inequality and gender inequality significantly impact height differences, indicating persistent disparities in health outcomes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This underscores the importance of considering broader socio-economic factors when examining economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) show that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe during the Black Death. The plague triggered structural changes by causing severe labor shortages. Ogilvie argues that these shortages led to a decline in the skill premium, which encouraged human capital formation by lowering interest rates across Europe. Pamuk (2007, p.15) elaborates on this mechanism by noting that the recovery of the population in the sixteenth century reversed the skill premium's decline in Italy. This implies that while labor scarcity might have initially boosted investment in human capital, subsequent demographic shifts could undermine these gains. However, Pamuk (2007, p.16) qualifies this pattern by highlighting that urbanization rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, differed significantly between northwestern and southern Europe during this era. This discrepancy suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions varied regionally, with some areas more resilient to demographic changes than others. These findings underscore the complex interplay between labor dynamics, human capital investment, and regional economic trajectories in shaping long-run outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6; 2000, p.5) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality often restricted competition and advantaged elites through government policies, which also influenced the distribution of wealth and human capital.

These dynamics are further underscored by Temin's work on post-WWII Europe. He argues that unusually rapid economic growth during this period was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.9). This suggests that the redistribution of labor, influenced by gender roles and development policies, contributed significantly to long-run economic outcomes.

Goldin (2006, p.3; 2006, p.1) demonstrates that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. Women moved from being agents who worked primarily out of necessity to those who pursued careers as part of their identity and societal worth. This shift involved significant human capital investment and altered labor market participation.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the centrality of women to production and reproduction in Africa. The historical role of women in economic development is also evident in Acemoglu et al.'s work, which argues that institutions introduced by colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This underscores the importance of institutional changes and human capital investment in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that human capital investment, influenced by gender dynamics, has been crucial for long-run economic growth. Van Zanden's work on skill premiums shows that differences in human capital investment were key factors (van Zanden 2009, p.25). The precise remaining disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms through which these investments translated into economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al.'s work on institutional changes highlights the importance of property rights and political power distribution in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This suggests that while human capital investment is crucial, the institutional framework governing its deployment matters significantly. The measurement type used by these sources, such as skill premiums and institutional efficiency metrics, settles this disagreement. Skill premiums reflect the value of skilled labor and the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This shows that the economic outcomes are determined by both the investment in human capital and the institutional framework that governs its use, highlighting the complex interplay between gender, development, and long-run economic growth.
