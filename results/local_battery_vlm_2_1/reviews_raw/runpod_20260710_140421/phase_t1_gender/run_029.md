The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue among scholars, with disagreements centering around the causal mechanisms at play. One position argues that gender dynamics significantly influence economic growth by affecting human capital investment and labor market participation. The opposing view posits that other factors, such as institutional frameworks or technological advancements, drive economic development independently of gender roles. The disagreement hinges on whether gender is a primary driver of economic outcomes or merely a secondary factor influenced by more fundamental forces. Understanding this relationship is crucial for grasping the complexities of economic growth and development, as it informs policies aimed at fostering sustainable progress.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) and van Zanden (2009, p.27; 2009, p.25) highlight how institutional frameworks and human capital formation are pivotal in determining long-term economic performance. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that secure property rights and the protection of entrepreneurial investments are essential for economic growth, as they enable broader participation in productive activities. Van Zanden extends this by linking the skill premium to institutional efficiency and human capital formation, suggesting that societies with robust institutions and high levels of education tend to have lower skill premiums and better long-term economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the importance of inclusive institutions in fostering economic development.

The role of gender is more directly addressed by Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3). Goldin documents a significant shift in women's employment patterns, moving from piece work and domestic labor to more professional roles. This transition reflects broader changes in societal attitudes towards women's work, where occupations became careers defined by long-term investment and identity (Author, Year). Akyeampong & Fofack emphasize that African women have been integral to economic growth and development, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. They note that gender discrimination can lower productivity and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This highlights the potential for gender dynamics to influence economic outcomes by shaping labor market participation and human capital investment.

These findings suggest that gender roles are a critical component of economic development (Author, Year). The interplay between institutional frameworks, human capital formation, and gender dynamics creates a complex web of influences on long-term economic performance.

These scholars document how women transitioned from piecework or domestic labor to more stable and career-oriented employment, marking a shift from "jobs" to "careers." This transition involved substantial human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). As women moved into professional roles, particularly in teaching and clerical work beginning in the 1910s, they experienced greater opportunities for on-the-job learning and formal education. This transformation underscores how changes in gender roles can drive economic growth by enhancing productivity and human capital formation.

However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) qualify this narrative by highlighting historical and cultural factors that have influenced women's participation in the workforce, noting a positive correlation between historical plough use and unequal gender roles today. This suggests that while structural changes in employment patterns can drive economic growth, deeply rooted cultural norms may constrain or accelerate these shifts.

The key mechanism is the development of credible commitments by rulers to uphold these property rights, which facilitated long-term contracting and investment. North also acknowledges that institutions are crucial, operating within a broader context where transaction costs play a significant role (Author, Year). These advancements are crucial for understanding how technological progress and institutional changes have historically driven productivity gains through effective resource allocation (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets’s assertion that modern economic growth relies on advancing technology and subsequent adjustments aligns with van Zanden's work which shows how the skill premium is linked to efficient human capital formation, reflecting a high level of institutional efficiency. This connection suggests that technological progress and institutional frameworks are interdependent drivers of long-term economic performance. Technological advancements create new opportunities for resource allocation, while effective institutions ensure that these resources are used efficiently. Mokyr further emphasizes the role of useful knowledge in economic growth, arguing that societies with a higher degree of consensus-shaping mechanisms are better equipped to harness new technologies. This implies that the growth of useful knowledge is facilitated by institutions that promote consensus and confidence in technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.4; van Zanden 2009, p.27). However, Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the impact of new knowledge on economic growth is not immediate but accumulates over time, suggesting a lag between technological innovation and its economic effects.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and de Vries (1994, p.17) show that economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa was more robust before the European colonial period than previously assumed. This finding is supported by de Vries who argues that the Industrious Revolution, which preceded the Industrial Revolution, involved increased labor input and new consumption patterns. The Industrious Revolution was characterized by a shift towards capital-intensive production systems (de Vries 1994, p.17). This economic transformation also led to a focus on quality over quantity in terms of human capital investment, particularly in education and health outcomes for children. However, this increased focus on market goods that minimized home labor inputs had implications for household dynamics, as it reduced the redistribution within households and the formation of human capital in children (de Vries 1994, p.18). This suggests that while the Industrious Revolution brought about significant economic changes, it also had complex effects on social structures and family dynamics. The economic growth observed during this period highlights the potential for sustained development in regions like Sub-Saharan Africa, even before European interventions.

The literature on biological well-being and economic growth does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Both Komlos (1998, p.7) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) focus on physical stature as a proxy for nutritional status, arguing that despite aggregate economic growth, biological well-being can decline due to uneven distribution of resources. This suggests that while overall economic indicators may improve, certain segments of the population, particularly those with inelastic supplies of human or physical capital, may experience diminished well-being. This finding underscores the importance of social and gender inequalities in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as these disparities can exacerbate the negative effects of uneven resource distribution. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) note that while population density is economically significant, it lacks statistical significance. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between biological well-being, economic growth, and social inequalities, suggesting that addressing gender and development issues requires a nuanced understanding of these dynamics to ensure sustainable improvements in long-run economic outcomes.

The role of gender in economic development is pivotal to understanding long-run outcomes. Women's contributions have been significant throughout history, but their roles often remain under-recognized due to institutional barriers and discriminatory practices (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). In both pre-colonial and colonial Africa, women played crucial roles in production and reproduction, significantly contributing to economic growth and development. This contribution challenges views that marginalize women's role in historical economies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). Women often worked in labor-intensive sectors like manufacturing or service industries, experiencing limited opportunities for human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.3; van Zanden 2009, p.27). The skill premium, a key predictor of long-term economic performance, is influenced by the efficiency of institutions that form human capital and by barriers to entry in these professions. This highlights how historical gender roles and institutional frameworks shaped broader economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.25).

Institutional frameworks that favor elites often restrict competition and hinder market flexibility, perpetuating inequality. These frameworks affect economic structures and influence long-run economic performance through their impact on human capital formation. The persistence of these inequalities underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). For example, in societies with extractive institutions, the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments, further stifling broader economic growth. The relationship between gender and development is evident in the historical roles women played in various economies. Women's employment patterns shifted from agents who worked out of necessity to those who sought careers, reflecting a change in societal perceptions of work (Goldin 2006, p.1; Temin 2002, p.4). The skill premium, which reflects the relative abundance of capital and efficiency of human capital formation institutions, is closely linked to long-term economic performance. This relationship suggests that historical gender roles and institutional frameworks significantly influenced broader economic outcomes by shaping how societies valued and utilized human capital.

The underlying point of agreement is the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The specific disagreement lies in whether the impact of these institutions on economic development is primarily due to their effect on human capital formation or through other mechanisms such as market flexibility. The historical evidence of women's contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa settles this disagreement by showing that institutional frameworks not only restricted competition but also hindered human capital formation, ultimately stifling broader economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).
