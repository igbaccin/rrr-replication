The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a complex interplay that has been subject to extensive scholarly inquiry. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how historical factors related to gender dynamics influence contemporary economic structures and growth trajectories. Scholars have debated whether these influences are primarily driven by institutional frameworks, cultural norms, or economic policies. The disagreement lies in whether institutions and economic policies are the primary drivers of long-run economic outcomes through their impact on gender roles, or if cultural norms and human capital formation play a more decisive role. This debate is crucial for comprehending how societies evolve economically over time. A deeper understanding of this interplay can inform policies aimed at fostering inclusive growth and addressing persistent inequalities.

The quiet revolution in women's employment and education during the 20th century, as described by Goldin (2006, p.3; 2006, p.1), is a testament to how shifts in gender roles can transform economic landscapes. The transformation from "jobs" to "careers, " where occupation and employment define one's fundamental identity and societal worth, marks a significant shift in women's economic participation (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift has implications for human capital investment and long-term economic growth. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) note that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, suggesting that technological and cultural factors significantly influence contemporary gender norms and economic participation. The persistence of these norms can affect long-run economic outcomes by shaping the structure of occupations and human capital formation.

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that secure property rights for elites can lead to a concentration of political and social power, potentially holding back investments by those without effective protection. This institutional framework can influence long-run economic outcomes by affecting the distribution of wealth and human capital. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6; 2000, p.5) further illustrate how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These institutional factors are crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes as they shape the distribution of resources and opportunities.

The skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Van Zanden (2009, p.25) shows that differences in the price of human capital can help explain significant economic divergences, such as the "Great Divergence" between Europe and other regions. This link between the skill premium and economic performance highlights the importance of human capital formation in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.28) also emphasize the role of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening in driving changes in earnings inequality, suggesting that investments in human capital are critical for fostering economic growth.

The interplay between gender dynamics and development encompasses both institutional frameworks and cultural norms, as well as human capital formation. This perspective aligns with van Zanden's observation that a low skill premium generally reflects a high level of human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Together, these insights suggest that addressing persistent inequalities requires institutional reforms and a focus on cultural shifts along with investments in human capital.

Lindert and Williamson argue that changes in women's employment, education, and family dynamics have significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Goldin also highlights this shift, noting how women transitioned from being "agents who work because they and their families 'need the money'" to those seeking careers for personal identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This transformation involved a shift from jobs to careers where human capital investment became crucial. Women's roles in manufacturing, service sectors, and professional fields evolved significantly over time. The majority of women workers were initially piece workers or laborers with limited opportunities for skill development. In the 1910s, there was an enormous expansion in professional roles such as teaching and clerical work (Goldin 2006, p.3). Lindert and Williamson's analysis underscores that changes in occupational structure can drive economic inequality, suggesting that as women moved into higher-skilled occupations, they likely contributed to shifts in the broader labor market. The expansion of professional roles for women indicates a significant increase in human capital investment, aligning with Lindert and Williamson’s argument that changes in occupational structure can drive economic inequality. The extent to which these shifts directly translate into long-run economic outcomes remains complex, given that other factors such as institutional reforms and cultural attitudes also play critical roles.

Economic growth and institutional frameworks are crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes, but they do not directly address how gender and development shape these outcomes (Temin 2002, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Temin (2002, p.4) highlights that rapid economic growth can be driven by disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, a mechanism separate from traditional factors such as physical and human capital (Temin 2002, p.9; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). While this view emphasizes the importance of resource misallocation in driving development deficits, it does not directly consider how gender dynamics might influence these allocations. This perspective highlights the role of institutional frameworks in promoting economic development, yet it remains silent on how gender-specific factors might interact with these institutions to shape long-run economic outcomes. The implication is that while institutional stability and resource allocation are pivotal, their impact on long-run economic outcomes may be qualified by unaddressed factors such as gender roles and labor market participation patterns.

Acemoglu et al. (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Austin 2008, p.23) examine how institutional changes introduced by colonialism shaped long-run economic outcomes, focusing on the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This pattern suggests that colonial institutions shaped immediate economic conditions and had lasting effects on inequality and development trajectories. Austin (2008, p.23) qualifies this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3); (Nunn 2008, p.27) examine African economic development through various lenses, including the contributions of women and the historical impact of slave trades. They jointly highlight that gender discrimination can have long-term effects on economic growth by disinvesting in human capital. This dynamic is particularly relevant for understanding how colonial institutions shaped immediate economic conditions and had lasting effects on inequality and development trajectories. Nunn (2008, p.4) adds a nuanced perspective to this discussion, suggesting that the slave trades contributed to ethnic fractionalization which is known to hinder long-term economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.27). This pattern suggests that African women played a significant role in pre-colonial and colonial periods.

Komlos (1998, p.7) and Koepke and Baten (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19) share the finding that economic growth does not always translate into improved biological well-being, suggesting a need to explore mediating factors. Komlos (1998, p.7) demonstrates this by showing that during the Industrial Revolution, while aggregate purchasing power increased, physical stature declined in some populations due to uneven distribution of nutritional resources and insufficient income gains. This suggests that even when there is overall economic growth, certain groups may not experience improvements in their biological well-being. The mechanism here is inequality: as economic growth occurs without equitable distribution of resources, some groups do not benefit from the increased wealth, and their physical development suffers. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) corroborate this by finding that social inequality had a significant negative impact on height, implying poorer nutritional status for those at the lower end of the socio-economic spectrum (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this pattern when he notes that groups with inelastic supply of human or physical capital were particularly affected by these trends. This suggests that the impact of economic growth on biological well-being may be mediated by factors such as occupational status and access to resources.

Economic growth and technological advancements shape long-run economic outcomes by altering the skill premium and necessitating institutional adjustments (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Van Zanden (2009, p.27) shows that the skill premium is influenced by both factor endowments, particularly interest rates reflecting capital abundance and efficiency of capital markets, and human capital formation, which in turn affects long-term economic performance. This means that as economies grow, they must adapt their institutions to support the new technologies and skills required for continued progress. Mokyr (2005, p.4) builds on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth, suggesting that the expansion of social knowledge, the collective understanding of a society, is crucial for sustained economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This implies that investments in education and research are essential for maintaining high levels of human capital and technological innovation. However, Mokyr (2005, p.4) also notes that the benefits of economic growth are not evenly distributed, as the rate of knowledge expansion can vary significantly across different societies (Mokyr 2005, p.3). Therefore, while economic growth and technological advancements are key drivers of long-run economic outcomes, their impact is mediated by factors such as institutional efficiency and the distribution of useful knowledge.

Gender played an essential role in shaping long-run economic outcomes by influencing human capital investments and labor market participation. Women's entry into the workforce transformed employment dynamics, as seen in the quiet revolution of women’s employment in the U.S. where women transitioned from piece workers to career-oriented professionals (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift was significant and altered societal perceptions of work and identity. Concurrently, African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role; they were central to production and reproduction in various historical contexts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). These contributions highlight the broader impact of gender on long-run economic outcomes, as women's participation in the economy drove both technological advancements and human capital investments.

Gender dynamics also influenced institutional efficiency and the distribution of useful knowledge. Institutions in societies with greater inequality often advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These institutions shaped economic structures by influencing factor endowments and the distribution of wealth and human capital. The skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance, with institutional efficiency playing a crucial role in determining the price of human capital (van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.25). This relationship between gender, institutions, and economic outcomes underscores how gender dynamics can mediate the impact of technological advancements on long-run economic growth.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The security of property rights for elites in these societies often left the majority of the population at risk of being held up after undertaking investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic highlights how institutions can shape economic outcomes by influencing human capital investment and technological advancements, with gender playing a critical role in this process. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments. In such societies, many agents with entrepreneurial human capital and investment opportunities may lack effective property rights protection.

The underlying point of agreement is that gender dynamics significantly influence long-run economic outcomes through their impact on human capital investments and labor market participation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The remaining disagreement centers around the specific mechanisms by which these influences operate. For instance, one view emphasizes the role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), while another highlights the broader impact of gender dynamics on long-term economic performance through the skill premium and human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the transformation of women's employment in the U.S. which shows how changes in labor market participation can drive technological advancements and human capital investments.
