The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question for understanding persistent global inequalities. At stake are the mechanisms through which historical patterns of gender inequality have influenced economic trajectories over centuries. A central disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the direct impact of gender roles on economic growth, and those who argue that other factors, such as institutional changes or labor allocation, play more significant roles. The former group posits that gender dynamics directly influence long-run economic outcomes through human capital investment and workforce participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The latter group contends that broader structural factors, such as colonial institutions or labor market disequilibria, are the primary drivers of economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48)(Temin 2002, p.9).

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is evident in historical patterns of women's employment and education. Women have traditionally been marginalized in sectors that offer little opportunity for skill development or career advancement. For instance, Goldin highlights the transformation of women’s roles from piece workers in manufacturing to service sector jobs such as domestics and laundresses (Goldin 2006, p.3). These jobs often provided minimal on-the-job learning and required little formal education beyond elementary school. This dynamic began to shift as professional opportunities for women expanded, particularly in teaching and clerical roles starting in the 1910s. This shift reflected a broader change in societal attitudes towards women's work, moving from mere economic necessity to a source of identity and worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). Women’s entry into more skilled professions marked a significant departure from traditional gender roles, which had historically limited their economic contributions.

The historical contributions of African women to economic growth further illustrate the significance of gender dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women played pivotal roles in both pre-colonial and colonial economies, contrary to views that marginalize their economic impact (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Their analysis shows that gender discrimination can lower overall productivity and adversely affect long-run growth by disinvesting in human capital for female workers. This perspective is supported by studies linking historical plough use to contemporary gender norms and workforce participation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5), suggesting a deep-seated influence of traditional gender roles on economic development.

Institutional factors also play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, often intersecting with gender dynamics. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, which either protected property rights or facilitated rent extraction, had profound effects on economic development. This disequilibrium in labor markets suggests that structural factors beyond gender roles can significantly influence long-run economic outcomes.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which attributes contemporary economic disparities to colonial institutions, has been challenged for oversimplifying historical causation. Austin argues that this thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, leading to an incomplete understanding of economic development (Temin 2002, p.9). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced approach that considers multiple factors, including gender dynamics and institutional changes.

The interplay between gender roles, development strategies, and long-run economic outcomes is complex. Institutions and labor market structures significantly shape these dynamics, often reinforcing or mitigating the effects of gender inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The persistence of unequal distributions of wealth and human capital in many societies highlights the enduring impact of historical factors on contemporary economic disparities. Understanding this interplay is essential for developing policies that address both immediate needs and long-term structural issues.

Women were heavily involved in labor-intensive activities such as agriculture and manufacturing, where they contributed to the production of goods and services essential for societal sustenance. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that this role was crucial, challenging conventional views that marginalize their economic impact. The centrality of African women to production and reproduction during these periods underscores their pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This pattern suggests that historical factors, such as colonial policies and cultural norms, have significantly influenced contemporary economic disparities by limiting opportunities for women's full participation in the economy.

The disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry is proposed as a key driver of economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age (Temin 2002, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This perspective emphasizes that the rapid economic growth observed during this period was not merely driven by policy interventions or ordinary factors but rather by a fundamental misallocation of resources. Temin highlights that this disequilibrium is distinct from low income levels, which are typically associated with conditional convergence (Temin 2002, p.9). Instead, the developmental deficit arises from an inefficient distribution of labor between sectors. Sokoloff and Engerman offer insight into how factor endowments and government policies can shape economic structures. They note that colonies with abundant mineral resources and low human capital tended to develop extremely unequal wealth distributions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This pattern suggests that the initial allocation of labor, influenced by colonial policies and resource availability, set the stage for long-term economic disparities. This view is qualified by the recognition that while factor endowments are crucial, they do not solely determine economic outcomes. For example, the efficiency of large plantations in certain economies indicates that technological advantages and international markets also played significant roles (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Therefore, while labor allocation is a critical factor, it operates within a broader context of technological and market influences, shaping long-run economic trajectories in complex ways. The interplay between labor allocation and other factors underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). The process of colonization often involved the establishment of extractive institutions that concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite. This concentration of power led to insecure property rights for the majority, hindering investment and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, colonialism disrupted traditional societies by creating ethnic fractionalization and insecurity, further impeding long-term development (Nunn 2008, p.4). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. It compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative that may not fully capture the complexity of economic development during colonial rule (Austin 2008, p.1). This simplification risks overlooking the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes in diverse contexts. For example, the stagnation of the South African economy under apartheid highlights how systematic racial discrimination hindered human capital formation and market flexibility, complicating the straightforward institutional explanation advanced by Acemoglu et al (Austin 2008, p.23). Therefore, while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, their impact was mediated by a range of historical and contextual factors that varied across different regions.

Komlos (1998, p.7) and de Vries (1994, p.17) highlight that the Industrious Revolution, marked by intensified labor input and evolving consumption habits, significantly reshaped economic landscapes. This shift fostered a more capital-intensive production system tailored to full-time male workers. De Vries elaborates that this transformation altered market demand, focusing on family-consumed goods rather than individual items. Komlos extends this view, noting that the benefits of modern economic growth were unevenly distributed, leading to increased disparities in nutritional resources and physical stature across different socio-economic groups. The divergence between average physical stature and income trends underscores the complex interplay between labor allocation and human capital formation during this period.

The Industrious Revolution's impact on household production systems was profound, influencing both market structures and broader social outcomes (de Vries 1994, p.17). De Vries' analysis reveals that the shift towards family-consumed goods minimized home labor inputs. This transformation had significant implications for childhood nourishment and human capital formation, as families prioritized quality over quantity in child-rearing. Komlos provides a complementary perspective by illustrating how economic growth during this era did not uniformly enhance biological well-being; rather, it exacerbated inequalities in nutritional resources and physical stature (de Vries 1994, p.17; Komlos 1998, p.7). This finding suggests that while the Industrious Revolution drove economic progress, its benefits were unevenly distributed. De Vries' observation about minimizing home labor inputs further qualifies this pattern by showing how changes in household production systems influenced broader social outcomes beyond economic growth. In particular, these shifts in household production may have contributed to the stagnation of childhood nourishment and human capital formation observed by Komlos.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Pamuk, explore regional economic performance in Europe and Africa by investigating historical factors such as per capita income levels and structural changes due to events like the Black Death (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.15). These studies demonstrate how exogenous shocks can alter economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous narratives about Africa's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).. The Black Death, according to Pamuk, initiated long-term structural changes in Europe, including a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe. This divergence was driven by severe labor shortages that encouraged human capital formation and influenced urbanization rates (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Broadberry and Gardner's findings on African economies suggest that similar exogenous shocks may have also played a role in shaping regional economic outcomes. Pamuk's analysis of Europe highlights the importance of understanding how these structural changes interacted with existing socio-economic conditions to produce divergent economic trajectories. These studies underscore the need for further research into how such historical events and their interactions with gender dynamics might have shaped long-run economic outcomes in different regions.

van Zanden and Koepke and Baten both find that economic development does not clearly translate to improvements in standards of living (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Van Zanden argues that early modern Europe saw declining real wages despite economic growth. This finding is particularly relevant for understanding the role of gender and development, as it suggests that economic progress does not necessarily lead to widespread improvements in living conditions including those related to gender equality. Koepke and Baten similarly find that European nutritional status did not improve significantly over two millennia. Their analysis reveals that factors like population density and social inequality were significant determinants of nutritional outcomes. This highlights the complex interplay between economic development, social structures, and health outcomes (van Zanden 1999, p.3). Notably, gender inequality is identified as a factor affecting these outcomes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). Koepke and Baten's findings also indicate that while population density had an economically significant impact on nutritional status, it was not statistically significant. This nuance underscores the need for further research into how specific socio-economic factors including gender dynamics interact with economic development to shape long-run outcomes in different regions.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is grounded in historical patterns of inequality that have persisted across different regions and periods. The quiet revolution that transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1) shows how shifts in gender roles can drive significant changes in economic structures, even when these changes are not immediately apparent. This phenomenon is mirrored in African societies where women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The historical contributions of African women underscore that gender dynamics have always been integral to economic development (Temin 2002, p.9).

The persistence of inequality across different regions can be understood through the lens of institutional frameworks. Institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework is evident in the economic structures that evolved in these colonies, where the distribution of wealth and human capital was extremely unequal (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes. This reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which have had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis shows that the kind of institutions established by European colonialists resulted in significant shifts in economic prosperity across different regions (Austin 2008, p.1). The interaction between gender and these institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding how specific socio-economic factors shape long-run outcomes. Historical evidence from Africa shows that women's contributions to economic growth were central to production and reproduction, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of gender dynamics in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This is shown by the argument that racial discrimination hindered both human capital formation and the growth of demand, as well as generally reducing the flexibility of markets (Austin 2008, p.23). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement involves measuring how different colonial policies affected gender roles and economic outcomes in various regions.
