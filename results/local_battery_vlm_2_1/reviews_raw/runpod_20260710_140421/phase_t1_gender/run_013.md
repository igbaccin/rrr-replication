The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake are competing interpretations of the drivers behind modern economic growth and the persistence of inequality across societies. One position posits that gender disparities have historically limited economic growth by constraining the pool of available human capital, while another view argues that institutional frameworks, rather than gender dynamics, are the primary determinants of long-run economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether gender-based inequalities in education, employment, and political participation are endogenous to broader economic processes or exogenous factors that independently shape development trajectories.

This disagreement is evident in the historical analysis of women's roles in economic growth. For example, Goldin highlights a significant shift in women's employment patterns in the U.S., transitioning from piece workers in manufacturing and domestics to more professional roles beginning in the 1910s (Goldin 2006, p.3). This shift indicates that as societies develop, women's economic participation evolves, suggesting a dynamic interplay between gender and development. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) emphasize that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This underscores the importance of considering diverse historical contexts when examining the role of gender in long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between gender dynamics and broader economic processes is complex, with evidence suggesting that institutional changes can either exacerbate or mitigate gender-based inequalities over time.

The significance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a key theme in this literature. For instance, Acemoglu et al (2002) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective suggests that institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on how they address gender-based inequalities. However, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) contend that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This indicates that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, with gender dynamics playing a crucial part in this process. The interplay between gender and development is further complicated by the skill premium, which van Zanden (2009, p.27) argues as a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This suggests that addressing gender-based inequalities in education and employment can enhance human capital formation, thereby promoting economic growth. By focusing on the role of institutions and the skill premium, scholars can gain a more nuanced understanding of how gender dynamics shape long-run economic outcomes.

This institutional framework, however, is not monolithic; it can be qualified by historical contexts. Austin (2008, p.23) challenges the simplification of causal factors in the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by demonstrating that systematic racial discrimination in South Africa hindered human capital formation and market flexibility, contributing to economic stagnation (Austin 2008, p.23). Temin (2002, p.4) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) emphasize that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth. This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that focus on population and savings as principal determinants of economic development, highlighting the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering long-term economic outcomes. North (1989, p.5) elaborates that "economic growth can and has occurred as a consequence of increasing productivity" (North 1989, p.5). This mechanism is driven by technological change or institutional changes that affect the specification and enforcement of property rights, underscoring the pivotal role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) provide an example from seventeenth-century England to illustrate this point. They note that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political commitment to these rules, which enabled secure contracting across time and space, was a necessary condition for creating modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical example underscores how institutional frameworks can facilitate long-run economic outcomes by ensuring the stability and predictability required for economic transactions. Temin (2002, p.4) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that economic growth is not solely determined by institutions but also influenced by resource misallocation, as seen in post-WWII Europe (Temin 2002, p.9). This suggests that while institutional frameworks are essential, their effectiveness can be conditioned by broader economic factors.

De Vries (1994, p.17) and Komlos (1998, p.7), shared finding (de Vries 1994, p.17; Komlos 1998, p.7), argue that the economic and social changes during industrialization significantly impacted long-run outcomes. This shift had profound consequences for the economy, including better nutrition, lower morbidity and mortality rates, and greater domestic comfort. These changes were achieved primarily through the household productive system rather than the formal economy (de Vries 1994, p.17). This suggests that while industrialization brought about significant economic changes, its benefits were not uniformly distributed. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) qualify this pattern by showing that gender inequality also played a role in shaping nutritional status, with social inequality having a more pronounced effect than gender inequality (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This indicates that industrialization's impact on long-run economic outcomes was influenced by both economic changes and social and gender dynamics. These findings underscore how the complex interplay between economic growth, social inequality, and household production patterns during industrialization shaped long-term economic outcomes.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) show that economic growth and inequality dynamics are driven by shifts in the structure of occupations and changes in productivity. This framework focuses on how factor incomes accruing to labor, land, and capital influence long-run economic outcomes. For example, rapid expansion in the derived demand for skills can incentivize investment in human capital. The historical evidence from Britain indicates that these dynamics were pivotal in shaping the Kuznets Curve across the 19th century (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This perspective offers insights into how economic growth and inequality are interconnected, with productivity advances favoring skill expansion and thereby driving income disparities. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that this view assumes any economic growth above subsistence results from external interventions such as colonial rule or infrastructure development. This qualification suggests that while internal dynamics like occupational shifts and productivity changes are crucial, external factors also play a significant role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (Pamuk 2007, p.15) examine how structural changes during early modern Europe shaped long-term economic outcomes. They argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with European economies benefiting from localized production and merchant distribution. This process, known as economies of localization, involved producers gaining advantages from proximity to others in the same industry, facilitating information flows, skilled labor availability, and market access (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This divergence was rooted in earlier demographic shifts and labor market dynamics.

These findings highlight how early modern economic structures, shaped by localized production and demographic shocks, laid the groundwork for long-term economic disparities. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.25) caution against overgeneralizing these findings to Asia, noting that while Europe experienced significant gains in living standards through falling relative prices of manufactured goods, similar claims cannot be made for regions like China or India. This qualification underscores the importance of regional specificity in understanding economic divergence.

Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.25) nuanced view reminds us that while structural changes were pivotal, their impact varied across different regions, influenced by local conditions and historical contexts.

Technological and institutional drivers are crucial for sustained economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that economic growth requires technological advancements and corresponding institutional adjustments to effectively harness these innovations. The skill premium, which reflects the relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of human capital formation, can be a significant predictor of a country's economic trajectory.

This dynamic interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is further illustrated by Mokyr (2005, p.4), who posits that the growth of useful knowledge underpins sustained economic development. By increasing specialization and professionalization, societies can accumulate greater amounts of collective knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This accumulation fosters an environment where innovation thrives and technological advancements are more readily adopted, driving long-term economic growth.

However, while technological progress and institutional efficiency are pivotal for economic development, van Zanden (2009, p.27) qualifies that the relationship between skill premiums and economic performance is not uniform across regions (van Zanden 2009, p.25). The "little divergence" within Europe between 1400 and 1800 highlights how regional differences in the price of human capital can influence economic outcomes. This suggests that while technological and institutional drivers are essential, their impact on long-run economic outcomes must be understood within specific historical and geographical contexts.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is intricately linked to historical patterns of human capital investment and institutional frameworks. Goldin shows that women's employment and education significantly transformed during the early 20th century, shifting from "jobs" to "careers," which involved substantial human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation was not uniform across regions or time periods; regional differences in skill premiums influenced economic outcomes by altering the demand for human capital. Van Zanden highlights how a low skill premium often reflects high levels of human capital formation, indicating that institutions facilitating human capital investment are crucial for long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This dynamic is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack's finding that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that gender discrimination tends to lower average productivity of workers and adversely affects long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Both these perspectives highlight how historical patterns of skill premiums and human capital investment are pivotal in understanding the role of gender in shaping economic outcomes. This is also supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that societies with extractive institutions typically did not or could not obtain education, leading to systematic cross-country differences in human capital correlated with institutional differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Therefore, the historical evidence shows that understanding long-run economic outcomes requires a nuanced view of how gender roles and human capital investment interact within specific historical and geographical contexts.
