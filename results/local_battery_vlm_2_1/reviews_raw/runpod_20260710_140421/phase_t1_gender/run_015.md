The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious topic that intersects with broader debates about economic growth and human capital investment. The central debate revolves around the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic trajectories over extended periods. One school of thought posits that gender equality can drive sustained economic growth by enhancing productivity, fostering innovation, and promoting inclusive development. This perspective argues that societies where women have equal access to education, employment opportunities, and economic resources are likely to experience more robust long-term economic performance. Another view contends that the role of gender in shaping economic outcomes is overstated; instead, structural factors such as institutional quality, technological advancements, and macroeconomic policies play a more decisive role. This perspective suggests that while gender equality may have short-term benefits, its impact on long-run economic growth is secondary to these broader determinants.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) argue that historical plough use correlates with unequal gender roles today, while van Zanden (2009, p.25) shows a link between the skill premium and GDP per capita growth over time. These findings suggest that long-term economic performance is influenced by both gender dynamics and broader structural factors such as human capital formation and institutional efficiency.

This transformation was driven by the interplay of gender roles and broader economic changes, as evidenced by Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3). This transformation was driven by the shift from 'jobs' to 'careers,' where women began to invest more in human capital and seek occupations that defined their identity and societal worth.

Goldin (2006, p.3) details how this revolution played out in the U.S., with women moving from piece work in manufacturing or domestic service to professional roles, particularly in teaching and clerical positions. These changes were marked by increased investment in education and skill acquisition, which in turn drove economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p. 10) observe that this revolution varied across cultures or historical periods. In African contexts, the contributions of women to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This suggests that while the shift from 'jobs' to 'careers' is a key mechanism in understanding long-run economic outcomes, it may be necessary but not sufficient; cultural and historical factors also play a pivotal role.

This implies that while the quiet revolution in women's employment was transformative, its impact on economic outcomes could be constrained by persistent gender inequalities. Therefore, addressing these inequalities is crucial for fully realizing the potential benefits of increased female participation in the workforce.

The persistence of these inequalities is further exacerbated by the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity, as shown by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and van Zanden (2009, p.27). The skill premium is one mechanism through which this institutional context influences economic outcomes. A low skill premium reflects a high level of human capital formation, whereas high skill premiums are often associated with barriers to entry into skilled professions (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This indicates that the efficiency of institutions in forming human capital plays a crucial role in determining long-run economic performance. Acemoglu et al. provide further insight into this dynamic by showing how extractive institutions can hinder widespread education and access to resources, thereby perpetuating inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The result is an institutional framework that shapes long-term economic outcomes by determining who has access to opportunities for growth and development.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Broadberry & Gardner (Temin 2002, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1) examine how labor allocation between agriculture and industry influenced economic growth. The misallocation of resources is the key mechanism behind this phenomenon. Temin identifies a disequilibrium in labor allocation as a crucial factor driving Europe's post-WWII Golden Age, separate from the usual determinants of low income such as physical and human capital levels relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9). This misallocation highlights a developmental deficit that extends beyond typical convergence narratives, suggesting that economic growth can be influenced by factors other than those traditionally considered in standard models. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further illustrate this point by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This finding challenges the notion that economic growth in Africa was solely a product of European colonial interventions, suggesting instead that local factors and resource allocation played significant roles. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) also note that early economic histories of Africa often overlooked the complex dynamics at play, assuming that any growth above subsistence was due to external influences (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This perspective underscores the importance of considering both internal and external factors when examining long-run economic outcomes. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding how labor allocation impacts economic development can provide valuable insights into broader patterns of growth and inequality, particularly in regions where traditional models may fall short.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p. 8); North (1989, p. 5) show that these institutions evolve over time in response to economic pressures and social changes. The structure of pay by skill and the incentives for human capital investment are critical factors in shaping economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize that secure property rights, facilitated by effective enforcement mechanisms, are essential for fostering economic development (North&Weingast 1989). This is because secure contracting across time and space enables specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The robust economy in seventeenth-century England illustrates how political factors can underpin economic growth by ensuring that property rights are enforced. North (1989, p. 5); North (1989, p. 1) point out that neoclassical theories often neglect the role of institutions in economic growth, focusing on population and savings. This perspective underscores the importance of considering both internal and external factors when examining long-run economic outcomes. These findings suggest that understanding how secure property rights influence economic development can provide valuable insights into broader patterns of growth and inequality, particularly in regions where traditional models may fall short.

Technological advancements and institutional frameworks drive economic growth by expanding useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.4) argues that the accumulation of useful knowledge is crucial for economic development, as it enables societies to collectively "know more" than previous generations. These adjustments facilitate the rapid changes in economic structure necessary for sustained growth. De Vries (1994, p. 17) modifies this view by stressing that the Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p. 18; de Vries 1994, p. 17). This suggests that while technological advancements are critical, the broader socio-economic context in which they occur is equally important. The interplay between technological progress, institutional adaptations, and socio-economic changes shapes long-run economic outcomes by creating a dynamic environment conducive to growth.

McCloskey and van Zanden share that the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is not their focus; instead, they argue that ideas and ideologies, as well as the standard of living during this period, were crucial drivers for economic change. McCloskey (2015, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) show that early modern economic growth did not necessarily translate into improved living standards or widespread prosperity, highlighting the complex interplay between economic development and socio-economic conditions. The standard of living in Europe during this period was influenced by a range of factors beyond economic growth alone, including ideological shifts and biological well-being. Komlos (1998, p. 7) modifies this view by noting that while economic growth may have been widespread in late eighteenth-century Europe, it did not uniformly benefit all groups. This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that the impacts of economic development on long-run outcomes were mediated by social and nutritional factors, which varied significantly across different regions and occupations. The implications of these findings extend to understanding how gender roles might interact with socio-economic conditions, potentially shaping long-run economic outcomes in ways that are not directly addressed by a focus on ideas or biological well-being alone.

This interplay between ideological shifts and socio-economic conditions underscores the importance of considering multiple determinants when examining long-run economic outcomes. The broader implications of these findings suggest that while technological advancements and institutional adaptations are critical, they must be understood within the context of prevailing ideologies and socio-economic conditions.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) along with Ogilvie (2007, p.25) demonstrate that economic institutions and structural changes, such as guilds and budget policies, played pivotal roles in shaping long-term economic outcomes. These institutions were not merely efficient; they were also influenced by distributional conflicts. The role of serfdom, for instance, was more about managing power dynamics than optimizing efficiency. This perspective underscores the complexity of how economic institutions functioned historically: they were shaped by socio-economic conditions and power struggles that went beyond simple economic logic. Simson (2019, p. 27) points out an important caveat, revealing that the post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive despite the challenges posed by economic headwinds and political instability. The interplay between these factors illustrates how institutional frameworks, shaped by both efficiency considerations and power dynamics, can have varied impacts on economic development depending on the specific historical and regional contexts.

Pamuk (2007, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that these structural changes were not uniform across regions, leading to varied impacts on wage dynamics and economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.27) jointly establish that the post-Black Death period saw a decline in the skill premium due to severe labor shortages. This shift was not uniform; urbanization rates varied significantly between northwestern and southern Europe, reflecting differing trajectories of economic growth during this era (Pamuk 2007, p.16; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19).

In fact, social inequality and gender inequality played substantial roles in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Pamuk 2007, p.16; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This suggests that while the Black Death initiated structural changes, the broader socio-economic context, including issues of labor allocation, gender dynamics, and urbanization, significantly influenced regional economic trajectories. The interplay between these factors underscores how historical events, along with underlying socio-economic conditions, shaped long-run economic outcomes in Europe.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is significant, as evidenced by historical and contemporary studies. Goldin shows that the transformation of women's employment patterns in the U.S. involved a shift from "jobs" to "careers," indicating a deeper integration of women into the economy (Goldin 2006, p.1). This change involved greater investment in human capital and a redefinition of personal identity through employment (Goldin 2006, p. 3; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p. 8). Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women played crucial roles in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The contribution of African women to economic development is evident in their involvement in production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This underscores the broader socio-economic context in which gender dynamics significantly influence economic trajectories.

The interplay between institutions and human capital formation further highlights the importance of gender in long-run economic outcomes. Van Zanden argues that the skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance, suggesting that societies with higher levels of human capital formation tend to have lower skill premiums (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions often limit access to education and human capital development for the majority of the population, particularly affecting women who are already disadvantaged by gender norms (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This interaction between institutions and human capital underscores how historical events and underlying socio-economic conditions shape long-run economic outcomes. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. shows that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, highlighting the institutional barriers to human capital development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This is consistent with Akyeampong and Fofack's finding that gender discrimination tends to lower average productivity of workers and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10).

The historical evidence shows that the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is profound. All sources agree that institutions play a crucial role: van Zanden shows that skill premiums are related to the efficiency of institutions for the formation of human capital, and Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions limit access to education and human capital (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The disagreement lies in whether the primary driver is institutional change or gender dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that gender discrimination lowers average productivity, while Acemoglu et al. focus on how extractive institutions limit education access for the majority of the population. The historical evidence from African women's contributions to economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods shows that gender dynamics are a primary driver (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).
