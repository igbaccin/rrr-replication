The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue among scholars. The intellectual stakes involve understanding whether the historical participation of women in economies has been a catalyst for growth or merely an incidental byproduct of broader socio-economic changes. One position argues that gender dynamics have significantly influenced economic trajectories, with historical shifts in women's roles driving substantial economic transformations. Another position posits that while gender may play a role, it is not as pivotal as other factors such as institutional quality and factor endowments. The debate hinges on whether the causal arrow runs from gender and development to long-run economic outcomes or if these are merely correlated phenomena.

The historical transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. for instance, has been described as a "quiet revolution" that altered family dynamics and economic structures (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift involved a change from individuals working primarily out of necessity to those seeking careers that define their identity and societal worth. Women transitioned from piecework and domestic labor to professional roles, especially in teaching and clerical positions (Goldin 2006, p.3). The skill premium, a key indicator of economic performance, has been linked to long-term economic outcomes. A low skill premium often reflects high levels of human capital formation, which is crucial for sustained economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.25). This dynamic suggests that the economic contributions of women, particularly through their roles in skilled labor and education, have played a significant part in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Kuznets (1973, p.4); (Mokyr 2005, p.4) present a unified view that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge. Kuznets posits that modern economic growth necessitates rapid changes in societal structures, including occupational shifts and urbanization, to efficiently utilize innovations. This process requires high aggregate growth rates and significant structural adjustments in society. Mokyr builds on this idea by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth; societies with greater consensus on and confidence in this knowledge experience more rapid and sustained economic development. The expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all individual pieces of knowledge, has significantly increased due to specialization and professionalization. This accumulation of knowledge is crucial for technological advancements that drive economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9; 2015, p.8) qualifies this perspective by highlighting the role of ideas and ideologies in shaping economic outcomes. In France, for instance, reactionary parties opposed economic growth if it conflicted with their ideological goals. The cultural struggle, or what the French called the "Franco-French War, " underscores how ideas and ideologies can either foster or hinder economic progress. This dynamic suggests that while technological advancements and useful knowledge are essential drivers of economic growth, the ideological context in which these developments occur is equally important. Economic progress is determined by technological capabilities and prevailing ideas and ideologies within a society.

Economic growth and labor allocation fundamentally shape long-run economic outcomes by influencing how resources are distributed across different sectors and activities (Temin 2002, p.4). Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) both emphasize the importance of labor allocation in driving economic development. This perspective aligns with de Vries' (1994) argument that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, facilitated subsequent industrial developments (de Vries 1994, p.17). The Industrious Revolution transformed household production systems, making them more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers, which in turn drove broader economic changes. This transformation altered market demand and shifted the focus of reproduction goals toward quality over quantity, emphasizing human capital formation in children.

The shift in labor allocation during these periods had profound implications for long-run economic outcomes. Temin (2002, p.4) notes that this disequilibrium is separate from low income levels that generate conditional convergence; instead, it results from a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.9). This perspective underscores how the reallocation of labor can create developmental deficits or surpluses, influencing economic growth trajectories. De Vries (1994, p.17) further elaborates on this by suggesting that the Industrious Revolution's impact was more pronounced through changes in household production systems rather than the formal economy (de Vries 1994, p.17). These changes included lower morbidity and mortality rates, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort, all of which are critical for sustained economic development. De Vries (1994, p.17) also acknowledges that this transformation came with a preference for market goods that minimized home labor inputs and reduced human capital formation in children (de Vries 1994, p.18). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between labor allocation, household dynamics, and long-run economic outcomes, where increased productivity can coexist with diminished investment in future generations.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework enabled economic growth by facilitating secure contracting across time and space, which is essential for specialization and division of labor. The role of transaction costs is pivotal here: Coase's seminal work highlights that neoclassical models assume zero transaction costs, but with positive transaction costs, institutions matter significantly (North 1989, p.1). This perspective aligns with North and Weingast’s emphasis on the importance of secure contracting for economic growth. North (1989, p.5) acknowledges that while institutions are crucial, technological change also plays a significant role in increasing productivity.

Acemoglu et al. along with Austin and Sokoloff and Engerman, argue that colonialism's institutional changes significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The inequality of wealth distribution in New World societies was heavily influenced by the policies implemented under colonial rule. These policies favored elites through property rights protection and restricted competition, which led to persistent inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. highlight that the concentration of political power in a small elite means that those with investment opportunities often lack secure property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This dynamic can discourage entrepreneurial activities and investments, further reinforcing economic disparities. Austin qualifies this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis may oversimplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.23; Austin 2008, p.1). The systemic racial discrimination observed in South Africa during the apartheid regime exemplifies how institutional changes can hinder human capital formation and market flexibility, ultimately affecting economic outcomes. This underscores that while colonial institutions are pivotal, their impact is shaped by various historical contingencies.

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) argue that the transformation of women's roles in employment and education significantly altered long-run economic outcomes. This transformation involved substantial human capital investment, changing both occupational horizons and societal perceptions of women's economic roles. This shift involves individual gains and systemic changes in human capital investment and societal norms. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) point out that women's roles have evolved; historical plough use in some cultures has led to enduring gender inequalities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This suggests that despite the quiet revolution in women's employment, certain cultural legacies can persistently affect economic outcomes.

van Zanden (2009, p.27); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) share an interest in the skill premium and economic divergence but do not directly address how gender and development shaped long-run economic outcomes. This is consistent with Broadberry and Gupta's observation that economies of localization can drive productivity gains in traded goods sectors in Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.25). Broadberry and Gupta also point out that high productivity in these sectors may arise through merchant distribution and finance as much as production. One implication is that while skill premiums and human capital formation are important, the role of financial intermediaries and market structures cannot be overlooked. This nuance highlights that economic divergence is driven by skill premiums and broader institutional and market factors.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with broader development processes. Goldin shows that women's employment patterns transformed dramatically over time, shifting from piece work and domestic labor to careers that offered greater human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation involved a fundamental change in how women perceived their roles in the economy, moving from 'jobs' to 'careers' (Goldin 2006, p.1).

Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution extended to productive labor, challenging views that marginalize their role in historical economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The skill premium, as van Zanden argues, is a predictor of long-term economic performance, highlighting the importance of human capital formation in driving economic growth. Institutions for the formation of human capital and barriers to entry are crucial factors influencing this premium (van Zanden 2009, p.27).

Sokoloff and Engerman illustrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional framework highlights how economic structures can perpetuate disparities. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions shaped the distribution of wealth and human capital, influencing long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Temin shows that unusually rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, underscoring the importance of resource misallocation in economic development (Temin 2002, p.9).

Institutional efficiency is key to understanding both skill premiums and long-run economic outcomes. The role of women as contributors to economic growth is evident across different historical contexts, whether through changes in employment patterns or direct contributions to production. The precise mechanisms by which gender dynamics influence economic development remain a subject of debate. The underlying point of agreement is that human capital formation and skill premiums are critical drivers of long-run economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The remaining disagreement lies in how these factors interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes. This disagreement can be settled by examining the historical evidence on gender roles and their impact on economic growth, as shown by Akyeampong and Fofack's study on African women’s contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The empirical comparison of skill premiums across different regions and time periods provides a robust framework for understanding the interplay between gender dynamics and long-run economic outcomes.
