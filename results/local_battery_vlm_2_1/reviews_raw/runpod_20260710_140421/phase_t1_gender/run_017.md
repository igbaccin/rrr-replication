The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue that intersects with broader debates about institutional change and human capital investment. At stake intellectually are the relative contributions of structural factors like institutions versus individual-level factors such as education and employment patterns to economic growth. One side posits that institutional frameworks, shaped by historical inequalities, fundamentally determine long-term economic trajectories. The opposing view emphasizes the critical role of human capital, particularly the educational and occupational advancements of women, in driving sustained economic development.

The quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. for example, marked a significant shift from "jobs" to "careers, " where occupation and employment began to define one’s fundamental identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation involved substantial human capital investment, which is linked to long-term economic growth. Institutions also play a crucial role in shaping these outcomes. For instance, the persistence of inequality in New World societies was influenced by institutional structures that favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and limiting opportunities for broader segments of society (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on their design and implementation.

The evolution of institutions plays an instrumental role in driving economic growth, shaping long-term outcomes by facilitating secure property rights and transactional productivity (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; North 1989, p.5). North and Weingast argue that the development of markets is contingent on robust institutional frameworks. These institutions govern economic exchange and how rules are enforced and adapted over time. The success of these institutions hinges on a credible commitment to secure contracting across both time and space, which fosters specialisation and division of labour (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). For instance, the political stability in seventeenth-century England facilitated the evolution of property rights through mechanisms like the Magna Carta. This political environment enabled economic growth by ensuring that transactions were secure, thereby reducing transaction costs and enhancing productivity. North (1989, p.1) cautions that economic growth depends on technological change and institutional adaptations that affect property rights (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are crucial for fostering economic growth, their effectiveness is contingent on broader socio-economic conditions.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are pivotal drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.4) underscores that economic growth necessitates technological progress and significant changes in societal structures. Kuznets argues that the dynamic forces behind modern economic growth involve rapid shifts in occupational status groups and urbanization, alongside evolving views on individual roles within society. This process is facilitated by major improvements in transport and communication, enabling the sequential spread of economic development to less advanced regions (Kuznets 1973, p.4). These technological advancements entail material progress and profound social transformations.

Mokyr (2005, p.4) builds on this by emphasizing that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. He posits that societies with greater consensus and confidence in their beliefs about the physical environment experience more rapid economic growth. This collective knowledge, which Mokyr terms "social knowledge, " is the aggregate of all individual pieces of information within a society (Mokyr 2005, p.4). The expansion of this social knowledge base, facilitated by increased specialization and professionalization, results in a vastly larger reservoir of knowledge than ever before. McCloskey (2015, p.9) highlights that ideas and ideologies play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, their impact is significantly influenced by the prevailing ideologies and beliefs within a society.

The Industrious Revolution, which preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution, was marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, but it did not directly address gender's role in shaping long-run economic outcomes (de Vries 1994, p.17; Komlos 1998, p.7). De Vries describes how this revolution led to a more capital-intensive production system oriented towards full-time male workers. This shift had profound consequences for the economy as market demand focused on family-consumed goods, and human capital formation became prioritized over quantity of children. The household productive system played a critical role in achieving major industrial society benefits such as lower morbidity and mortality rates (de Vries 1994, p.17). This suggests that while the Industrious Revolution brought about significant economic changes, it also exacerbated inequalities. Koepke and Baten further qualify this by noting that both gender inequality and social inequality had negative effects on nutritional status, with social inequality corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low extremes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This indicates that while the Industrious Revolution brought about significant economic changes, it also exacerbated inequalities, particularly along gender lines. The downstream implication is that addressing these inequalities could have led to more equitable distribution of benefits from industrialization.

This cluster examines various factors influencing economic growth and labor allocation, such as the impact of historical events like the Black Death and post-WWII labor disequilibria (Temin 2002, p.4; Pamuk 2007, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Temin (2002, p.4) posits that rapid economic growth in Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This perspective contrasts with views attributing growth solely to policy or other ordinary factors, emphasizing instead the role of resource misallocation. Pamuk (2007, p.15) extends this idea by showing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The skill premium began declining under severe labor shortages, encouraged human capital formation due to dropped interest rates across Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This dynamic illustrates how historical events can reshape economic structures over the long run. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) notes that there is no clear relationship between early modern economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.3). This suggests that while labor allocation and structural changes are significant, their impact on long-run economic outcomes can be nuanced and multifaceted.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and Stern (2004, p.8) share a focus on how distributional conflicts shape economic institutions in Europe. Ogilvie's research shows that pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations. These institutions arose to protect the interests of powerful groups and maximize overall economic welfare. Stern adds that this institutional landscape influences trade patterns: under free trade, countries specialize in goods intensive in their abundant factors, labor and natural resources for developing countries, human capital and manufactured capital for developed ones (Stern 2004, p.8). This specialization can lead to environmental degradation in middle-income countries as they industrialize. The downstream implication is that while distributional conflicts are a key driver of institutional formation, the resulting institutions can have complex and sometimes detrimental effects on long-run economic outcomes. This suggests that understanding the role of gender and development requires considering how these dynamics influence economic institutions in both positive and negative ways.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) together with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) share the view that African women have been crucial to economic growth and development. This role is evident in their contributions during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, where they played central roles in production and reproduction. The mechanism linking gender roles to economic outcomes is a pattern of historical plough use, which correlates with unequal gender norms today. This pattern has implications for female labor force participation, influencing the broader economy by shaping gender norms that persist over time (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) note that early economic histories often overlooked these contributions, assuming any growth above subsistence was due to European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This highlights a significant gap in the understanding of African economic development, where the role of women has been underappreciated.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33); (Austin 2008, p.23) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This cluster of literature suggests that these institutions were extractive and inclusive, leading to a concentration of political power in the hands of an elite who could hold up investments and stifle economic growth. The mechanism here is clear: when property rights are insecure or concentrated among elites, entrepreneurial opportunities are squandered as the majority population lacks incentives to invest (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern aligns with Austin (2008, p.1) argument that colonial institutions often led to systematic discrimination, hindering human capital formation and market flexibility, a factor which can also impede economic growth.

The downside of this concentration is more than an abstract theoretical concern: it has practical implications for gender roles as well. For example, the extractive nature of these institutions could have reinforced existing unequal gender norms by limiting women's access to education and economic opportunities (Nunn 2008, p.4). In turn, these limitations impact female labor force participation and broader economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.23) notes that this is not a simple story: the effects of colonialism on African economies are complex and multifaceted, and cannot be reduced to a single narrative. This complexity underscores the need for nuanced analysis when examining the long-run economic outcomes of colonial institutions, particularly in relation to gender roles.

The focus on institutions as drivers of economic development highlights that the legacy of colonial rule is about immediate economic interventions and enduring structures it created.

Economic growth and equality are intertwined outcomes. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Goldin (2006, p.3) show that the relationship between economic growth and inequality is dynamic and influenced by occupational shifts in the economy. They demonstrate that the distribution of income can change over time as productivity advances and skills deepen, altering the structure of occupations and pay gaps. The changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and skill deepening were critical forces driving the British Kuznets Curve across the 19th century (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This dynamic is particularly relevant for understanding gender roles within economic development as they evolve over time.

This shift reflects changes in economic opportunities and broader societal transformations that redefine fundamental identities and societal worth. This transformation was not uniform across all occupations; most women workers remained in low-skill jobs with little opportunity for advancement. The interaction between gender roles and economic development is complex: while economic growth can create new opportunities, it also risks reinforcing existing inequalities without targeted interventions.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is deeply intertwined with broader societal transformations that redefine fundamental identities and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). While the transformation in women's employment opportunities has been significant, it has not been uniform across all occupations. The vast majority of women workers remained in low-skill jobs with little opportunity for advancement (Goldin 2006, p.3). This pattern underscores how occupational shifts can both drive economic growth and perpetuate inequalities.

The interaction between gender roles and economic development is complex: while economic growth can create new opportunities, it also risks reinforcing existing inequalities without targeted interventions. This dynamic is evident in historical contexts where women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). African women's contributions highlight that gender dynamics have long been integral to economic development trajectories.

The persistence of inequality despite economic growth shows the need for policies that actively address disparities. This is particularly relevant in societies where institutions favor elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The interplay between gender roles and economic development is further complicated by the role of human capital. While education can empower women, it does not always translate into equal opportunities in the workforce. This is evident in societies where women from cultures that historically used the plough have less equal gender norms and participate less in the workforce (Temin 2002, p.9). The misallocation of resources due to a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry also plays a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions or human capital are more critical in determining long-term economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis shows that gender discrimination tends to lower the average productivity of workers and may adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Temin 2002, p.9). Acemoglu et al.'s study demonstrates that institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by determining the distribution of power and resources within society (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence settles this disagreement. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize may capture the possible interaction between industrialization and some country characteristics correlated with human capital differences, showing that societies with extractive institutions typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).
