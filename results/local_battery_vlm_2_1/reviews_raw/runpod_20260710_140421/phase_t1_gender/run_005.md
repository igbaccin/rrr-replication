The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical area of inquiry that intersects multiple disciplines, including economics, history, and sociology. Understanding this relationship is crucial for unpacking the complex dynamics that have influenced global economic trajectories over centuries. The key debate revolves around whether gender dynamics have been a primary driver of economic growth or if they are part of a broader set of institutional and structural factors that collectively determine long-run outcomes. One position argues that gender roles and economic participation significantly influence development paths, while another contends that the impact of gender is secondary to other institutional and geopolitical factors.

Goldin (2006, p.3) highlights how women's employment and education in the United States underwent a transformative shift from mere necessity-driven work to career-oriented labor (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This transition altered individual lives and had broader implications for economic growth. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) emphasize the significant contributions of African women to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). Their findings suggest that understanding gender dynamics is essential for a comprehensive analysis of long-run economic outcomes. These studies demonstrate that gender roles and economic participation are integral to the broader narrative of economic development, shaping both individual trajectories and societal and national economic paths.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Goldin (2006, p.3) extend this narrative by examining how women's contributions to economic growth varied across different historical contexts. They demonstrate that changes in occupational structures and human capital investment were critical forces driving economic inequality. Women were often employed as piece workers or laborers, experiencing little on-the-job learning (Goldin 2006, p.3). The historical roles of women in economic development are evident across diverse contexts. The transformation of women's employment from "jobs" to "careers," as Goldin notes, reflects a broader shift in societal values towards recognizing the importance of occupational identity and human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift did not uniformly benefit all women; historical patterns of gender discrimination persisted, affecting long-run economic outcomes by disinvesting in female workers' human capital. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between gender roles and economic development that is crucial for understanding broader economic trajectories.

This interplay is further complicated when considering the legacy of colonialism, which introduced institutional changes that significantly altered long-run economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cluster focuses on how the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes. The concentration of political power in the hands of a small elite under extractive institutions typically meant that the majority of the population could not obtain education, and thus did not develop sufficient human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic had profound implications for economic development, as societies with extractive institutions faced significant barriers to industrialization and technological advancement. However, the reversal of fortune thesis is challenged by Austin's argument that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.23; Austin 2008, p.1). He argues that this compression fails to account for the complex interplay between colonial institutions and other factors such as racial discrimination and human capital formation. Nunn's research on Africa's slave trades further complicates this picture, showing how these trades had an adverse effect on economic development by increasing ethnic fractionalization and reducing long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.4). These findings suggest that the legacy of colonialism is shaped by a range of factors including institutional changes, racial discrimination, and historical legacies such as slave trades.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1), examine economic performance in African and New World societies, focusing on per capita income levels and the role of institutions in shaping inequality. They show that these regions' economic structures were significantly influenced by factor endowments such as mineral resources and labor availability. For example, Broadberry and Gardner document how many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations that attributed any growth to European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This challenges the view expressed in early economic histories of Africa, which assumed that any economic growth above subsistence resulted from European colonial interventions. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that inequalities in wealth and human capital were exacerbated by policies that restricted competition and favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). These institutional frameworks maintained and deepened existing disparities, creating a persistent cycle of inequality. This highlights how the persistence of inequality can be traced back to colonial-era institutions that privileged certain groups over others, a dynamic that continues to influence long-run economic outcomes in these regions.

This divergence is further explored by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) who argue that economies of localization were crucial in driving the economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800. This mechanism is supported by Pamuk's (2007, p.15) finding that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including a decline in the skill premium due to severe labor shortages. These labor market adjustments encouraged human capital formation, which in turn fueled economic growth. This process was not uniform across Europe; Pamuk (2007, p.16) highlights that urbanization rates differed significantly between northwestern and southern Europe, indicating varying levels of productivity and economic development. Ogilvie points to a different but related mechanism: distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.25). These conflicts led to poor contract enforcement and property rights, which choked off economic dynamism. This suggests that while economies of localization and labor market adjustments were crucial for growth, the institutional environment played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are central to economic growth (Kuznets 1973; Mokyr 2005). Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights that modern economic growth demands technological progress and significant changes in societal structures. This includes shifts in occupational status, family formation, urbanization, and individual perceptions of achievement. The dynamic nature of this growth necessitates continuous expansion into less developed areas, facilitated by improvements in transport and communication. Mokyr (2005, p.4) expands on this by identifying useful knowledge as the primary driver behind economic growth. He posits that the collective knowledge within societies has expanded significantly over time, driven by increased specialization and professionalization. This accumulation of knowledge has led to a more capital-intensive production system focused on full-time male workers, altering market demand towards family-consumed goods (de Vries 1994, p.17). The Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, preceded the Industrial Revolution and facilitated it significantly [de Vries 1994]. De Vries (1994, p.18) suggests that these changes involved profound shifts in household production systems as well as technology. These shifts focused on enhancing human capital formation in children, improving nutrition, education, and overall domestic comfort all of which are crucial for long-term economic development.

Ideas and ideologies played a pivotal role in shaping economic change during the early modern period (Stern 2004, p.8; McCloskey 2015, p.9). McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Stern (2004, p.8) argue that the shift towards economic growth was driven by new ideas and ideologies. For example, McCloskey (2015, p.9) shows how the success of bourgeois economic and political models in neighboring countries influenced France's economic trajectory (McCloskey 2015, p.8). The influence of these ideas was profound: even in a country like France that was resistant to change due to its anti-bourgeois elites, ideas from abroad fostered growth. However, these shifts are contingent on the ideological context: McCloskey demonstrates that even with clever advocates of trade-tested betterment, France's progress was stunted by its entrenched anti-libertarian elite.

Temin (2002, p.4) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) build on this by emphasizing that these institutional changes fundamentally drove economic growth through secure property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms. This perspective is echoed by North (1989, p.5), who argues that productivity increases can result from technological advancements or shifts in institutions that enhance the specification and enforcement of property rights. The critical role of institutions in economic growth is underscored by their ability to reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating secure contracting across time and space (Temin 2002, p.9). Temin (2002, p.9) highlights how misallocation of resources can lead to developmental deficits, suggesting that institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in ensuring efficient resource allocation. North (1989, p.5) also notes that while institutions are crucial, they operate within broader economic contexts shaped by factors such as population dynamics and savings rates. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutional changes and other economic variables, suggesting that while institutions are essential for growth, their impact is contingent on a range of interacting factors.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Komlos (1998, p.7) both investigate how economic growth in Europe impacted living standards during key historical periods (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Komlos 1998, p.7). They find that the relationship between economic development and living standards is not straightforward. Van Zanden argues that there was no clear connection between real wages, the standard of living, and economic growth in early modern Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.3), while Komlos shows that industrialization could worsen biological well-being for some groups due to uneven distribution of benefits (Komlos 1998, p.7). This indicates that even if GDP increases, living standards might not improve uniformly across society. One mechanism explaining this is the unequal distribution of economic gains. For example, handloom weavers, who possessed inelastic human or physical capital, were likely made worse off by industrialization (Komlos 1998, p.7) as the demand for their skills declined with mechanisation. This suggests that without inclusive growth policies, economic development can exacerbate inequalities. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) qualify this pattern by noting that even during mortality crises, nutritional quality could improve due to enhanced land availability (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Thus, while economic growth does not guarantee uniform improvement in living standards, specific conditions such as increased agricultural land can mitigate some of the negative effects.

Gender roles have been instrumental in shaping economic development trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). They were central contributors to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their role. This contribution is seen in the context of historical plough use and unequal gender roles today, where cultures that historically used the plough have less equal gender norms and lower female labor force participation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The economic structures that evolved in these societies were influenced by factor endowments, leading to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income.

The role of gender extends beyond economic contributions to shaping long-run economic outcomes through institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions concentrate political and social power in the hands of a small elite, which can hold up investments from the majority population (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This concentration of power limits access to education and human capital development for the masses, perpetuating inequality over generations. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities reflects systematic cross-country differences in human capital correlated with institutional differences, showing that societies with extractive institutions typically do not or cannot obtain education (Goldin 2006, p.3). This institutional framework is crucial in understanding how gender roles influence economic development, as it directly impacts the distribution of resources and opportunities.

The quiet revolution in women's employment and education transformed family dynamics in the U.S. Goldin argues that this transformation was a shift from agents who work because they need the money to those employed because occupation and employment define one's fundamental identity (Goldin 2006, p.1). This change involved a transition from "jobs" to "careers," where the distinction concerns both horizon and human capital investment. Women experienced little or no learning on the job and saw only slight gain from formal human capital beyond common school, with a scant minority being professional workers such as teachers and clerical employees (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This shift in women's roles had profound implications for economic outcomes, as it altered the structure of occupations and the distribution of skills within the economy.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is further underscored by the impact of institutions on resource allocation. Sokoloff and Engerman show that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Temin 2002, p.9). This institutional framework directly influences economic development by determining who has access to resources and opportunities. The disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age highlights how resource misallocation can drive rapid economic growth, showing that low income is produced by low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9). This misallocation of resources is a key factor in understanding how gender roles influence long-run economic outcomes.

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions shape long-term economic outcomes through the distribution of resources and opportunities, with a focus on human capital development (Goldin 2006, p.3; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether this institutional framework is primarily driven by historical plough use or extractive institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack show that cultures that historically used the plough have less equal gender norms today, indicating a long-term impact on economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Acemoglu et al., however, argue that extractive institutions concentrate political and social power in the hands of a small elite, limiting access to education and human capital development for the masses (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of African women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods settles this disagreement. This specific historical evidence shows that gender roles have been instrumental in shaping long-run economic outcomes through both institutional frameworks and direct economic contributions, highlighting the complex interplay between gender, institutions, and economic development.
