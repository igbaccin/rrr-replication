The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question for understanding the dynamics of economic growth and inequality over time. This issue is central to debates about the persistence of economic disparities between regions and nations, as well as within societies. A key disagreement revolves around whether gender dynamics are primarily a cause or an effect of broader economic structures, with some arguing that gender roles have been fundamentally shaped by economic development and others positing that gender dynamics themselves drive long-term economic outcomes. The stakes are high: understanding these relationships can inform policies aimed at reducing inequality and promoting sustainable growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) argue that "little is known about the origin of these cultural differences." This gap in knowledge underscores the need for further investigation into how gender dynamics interact with economic development over time, highlighting the importance of historical context in understanding long-term economic outcomes. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.”

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) (Goldin 2006, p.3) argue that African women played significant roles in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The role of gender dynamics is crucial to understanding long-term economic outcomes because the contributions of women were central to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their importance. These roles highlight how gender dynamics have shaped economic development over time.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition. The factor endowments of these economies contributed to the extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income. This inequality was further exacerbated by the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). Moreover, the lack of effective property rights protection for the majority of the population meant that entrepreneurial opportunities were often monopolized by this privileged group. In societies where these elites held secure property rights, agents with significant human capital might have been excluded from investment opportunities due to potential hold-ups by powerful elites (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic could limit economic growth and development as the broader population is unable to realize its full economic potential. Austin (2008, p.23) notes that systematic racial discrimination can hinder both human capital formation and market flexibility, which are crucial for sustained economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) argue that the institutional frameworks governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The evolution of these institutions was crucial for creating an environment where economic agents could engage in secure contracting across time and space, a necessary condition for modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional framework ensured that the regime or sovereign was committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange, thereby reducing uncertainties and enhancing market stability. The transaction costs associated with these institutional changes are significant, as they influence the costs of production and overall economic efficiency (North 1989, p.1). The robustness of English property rights and common law, which evolved from historical precedents like the Magna Carta, played a critical role in circumscribing royal behavior and fostering responsible governance. This institutional stability was essential for sustaining long-term economic development despite political uncertainties. These findings have profound implications: secure property rights and effective governance institutions are necessary for sustained economic growth.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) both focus on economic growth and labor allocation, discussing phenomena such as the Industrious Revolution and post-WWII Golden Age of European growth. These papers highlight that periods of rapid economic growth can be driven by shifts in labor allocation alone. Temin (2002, p.4) shows how differences in education between countries eliminated the prediction of unconditional convergence, leaving room for conditional convergence for groups of similar countries (Temin 2002, p.4). This suggests that human capital development is a critical factor in economic growth and resource allocation. The Industrious Revolution facilitated the Industrial Revolution through technological advancements and significant shifts in household productive systems. This implies that economic growth is deeply intertwined with changes in labor allocation and human capital development. However, de Vries (1994, p.17) acknowledges that this process is complex and involves a systematic preference for market goods that minimize home labor inputs (de Vries 1994, p.18), suggesting that the relationship between labor allocation and economic growth is multifaceted and influenced by various factors including shifts in household productivity patterns.

The skill premium and technological progress are key determinants of long-term economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Van Zanden (2009, p.27) argues that the skill premium reflects both factor endowments and institutional efficiency. This suggests that countries with higher levels of human capital formation tend to have lower skill premiums, which in turn can drive economic growth through increased productivity. Kuznets (1973, p.4) extends this idea by highlighting that technological advancements necessitate significant adjustments in institutions and ideologies for effective use, underscoring the importance of adaptive institutional frameworks in harnessing the benefits of technological progress. Mokyr's (2005, p.4) emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge adds a nuanced layer to this relationship. He posits that the accumulation of social knowledge, defined as the collective understanding of a society, is crucial for sustained economic growth. This implies that while the skill premium and technological advancements are pivotal, they must be complemented by a robust framework of shared knowledge and adaptive institutions to fully realize their potential in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) examine economic performance in Africa and Asia, focusing on historical economic growth and divergence. Their work underscores that many African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous narratives that attributed any such growth to European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.25) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This perspective is significant because it highlights the role of endogenous factors in driving economic development, attributing growth not solely to exogenous shocks such as colonial rule or technological transfers from Europe. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that early economic histories of Africa often overlooked the region's pre-colonial economic achievements, assuming that any growth was due to European interventions. This oversight is problematic because it overlooks the complex dynamics at play in African economies. This perspective challenges the notion that technological progress or colonial rule were the primary drivers of economic development in Africa and Asia, suggesting instead that local economic conditions and endogenous growth mechanisms played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is significant, as shown by the contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This contribution was substantial despite the discrimination they faced in accessing formal employment and productive assets. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that this discrimination tended to lower the average productivity of workers and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The persistence of inequality is also evident in New World societies with greater inequality, where institutions advantaged elites through various government policies restricting competition. This shows that the role of gender and development intersects with broader institutional frameworks.

The skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance, as shown by van Zanden’s argument (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This skill premium reflects relative abundance of capital and efficiency of institutions for human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.25; Goldin 2006, p.1). Goldin shows that women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. transformed during a quiet revolution, indicating a shift from "jobs" to "careers, " where occupation and employment defined one's fundamental identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation underscores how gender roles can evolve over time and influence economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Temin 2002, p.4). This suggests that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes, aligning with Temin’s argument about the disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age. This disequilibrium is separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence, highlighting how resource misallocation can impact economic growth (Temin 2002, p.9). The underlying point of agreement among these studies is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether technological progress or colonial rule were the primary drivers of economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack show that local factors such as merchant distribution and finance drove economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).
