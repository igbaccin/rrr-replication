The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical area of inquiry that intersects with broader debates about institutions, human capital, and economic growth. The live disagreement concerns the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic performance over the long term. Some scholars argue that gender-based constraints on education and employment opportunities limit human capital formation, thereby stunting economic growth. Others point to institutional factors, such as property rights and political power structures, as more decisive in shaping economic trajectories. The interplay between these factors is complex: institutions may both reflect and reinforce gender inequalities, creating a feedback loop that affects long-run economic performance.

The skill premium provides insights into this dynamic by measuring relative earnings of skilled versus unskilled workers. A high skill premium can indicate low investment in human capital or barriers to entry for skilled labor (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This is relevant because gendered access to education and employment opportunities can influence both the supply of skilled labor and the overall level of human capital in an economy. For example, historically, women's work often involved little on-the-job learning or formal education beyond elementary school (Goldin 2006, p.3), which limited their contribution to human capital accumulation. This historical context is crucial for understanding how gender dynamics have shaped long-run economic outcomes.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) along with Goldin (2006, p.3), argue that changes in women's employment and education have been pivotal to long-run economic outcomes. The shift from traditional gender roles towards more equitable opportunities for women has influenced both the supply of skilled labor and societal perceptions of work itself. This dynamic is mirrored in Goldin's account of the "quiet revolution" in women's employment, where the transition from 'jobs' to 'careers' signified a deeper change in societal attitudes towards work and its role in defining personal identity and worth (Goldin 2006, p.1).

One specific mechanism through which gender dynamics influence economic outcomes is the expansion of education opportunities for women. This limited their contribution to human capital accumulation, a critical factor in long-run economic growth. As educational barriers fell and more women gained access to higher education, the economy benefited from an expanded pool of skilled workers. However, this process was not uniform across all sectors; Goldin notes that while some professions like teaching saw significant gains, others remained largely untouched by these changes (Goldin 2006, p.3). This uneven expansion underscores the complexity of gender dynamics in economic development and suggests that targeted policies may be necessary to fully realize the potential benefits of increased female participation. The impact of women's employment on long-run economic outcomes is thus multifaceted, involving both quantitative increases in skilled labor and qualitative shifts in societal attitudes towards work and education.

Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women highlights their pivotal role in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Their contributions were multifaceted, encompassing both production and reproduction, which were central to the growth and development of societies. The agricultural sector, for instance, saw significant participation from women, who played crucial roles in cultivation, processing, and marketing of crops. This engagement was not merely supplementary; it was foundational to the economic fabric of many communities. Koepke and Baten's findings on nutritional status further illuminate this dynamic. While European nutritional progress was limited over two millennia, the availability of agricultural land post-mortality crises improved nutritional quality (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This mechanism likely mirrors African contexts where women's roles in agriculture were vital for sustaining communities through periods of stress. The implications of these contributions extend beyond immediate economic gains; they shaped societal structures and norms that influenced long-term development trajectories. The persistence of gender discrimination over historical time underscores the complex interplay between economic growth and social dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This points to a critical need for policies that recognize women's roles in economic development and amplify them, ensuring their contributions are fully realized and sustained over time.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in these economies was reinforced by institutional frameworks that secured property rights for a privileged few, while marginalizing the majority. This concentration of political and social power in the hands of an elite class created a system where many agents with entrepreneurial potential lacked effective property rights protection (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implications of this institutional setup were profound, as it hindered broader societal development by limiting opportunities for investment and innovation among the general population.

The mechanisms through which these institutions operated varied significantly across different colonial contexts. For instance, in regions dominated by large plantations and slave labor, the economic structures were heavily influenced by factor endowments such as mineral resources and abundant but low-skilled labor (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These conditions led to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income, further solidifying the power of the elite. This pattern highlights how institutional changes introduced by colonialism shaped immediate economic outcomes and had long-term effects on societal structures and norms.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the complexity of these historical processes (Austin 2008, p.1). The thesis, which posits that institutions established by European colonialists determined current economic disparities, oversimplifies the causal pathways by compressing different historical periods. Austin argues that systematic racial discrimination and policies hindering human capital formation played crucial roles in economic stagnation during the apartheid regime in South Africa (Austin 2008, p.23). This qualification suggests that while institutional frameworks were pivotal, their impact was mediated by specific historical and social contexts, including racial dynamics and policies affecting human capital development.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are pivotal for economic growth as they enable societies to adapt to technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets posits that economic development requires technological progress as well as institutional and ideological changes that facilitate the efficient use of innovations. This perspective is echoed by Mokyr, who highlights that the growth of useful knowledge underpins economic growth, suggesting that societal consensus on this knowledge drives economic expansion. The dynamic interplay between technology and institutions is evident in Kuznets's description of how modern economic growth necessitates rapid structural changes in society. Yet North and Weingast show that credible commitment to property rights is crucial for fostering secure contracting across time and space (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This implies that while technological advancements are essential, the institutional framework governing public choice must ensure secure property rights for sustained economic growth. However, North and Weingast acknowledge that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, but they also recognise that there is no definitive theory of economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Ogilvie argues that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, and North's work on the role of institutions in economic growth is consistent with this view (Ogilvie 2007, p.25; North 1989, p.5). Ogilvie contends that poor contract enforcement and property rights limited investments in physical and human capital, while North highlights how changes in political or economic institutions can increase productivity by affecting property rights. These perspectives suggest that the evolution of economic institutions is crucial for fostering secure property rights and sustained growth; however, McCloskey's work on ideologies complicates this picture (Ogilvie 2007, p.25; McCloskey 2015, p.9). For instance, McCloskey shows that ideas and ideologies were primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This indicates that while institutions are important, the ideological context in which they operate can significantly influence their impact on economic growth. Therefore, the interplay between economic institutions and prevailing ideologies is essential for understanding long-run economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) examine various aspects of economic growth and labor dynamics during different historical periods. Temin argues that the post-WWII Golden Age's rapid growth resulted from a disequilibrium caused by resource misallocation rather than just low income or insufficient physical and human capital (Temin 2002, p.9). Similarly, de Vries (1994, p.17) shows that increased labor input and new consumption patterns characterized the Industrious Revolution. This revolution preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution, emphasizing changes in household production systems (de Vries 1994, p.17).

These shifts had profound consequences for the entire economy. The production system became more capital-intensive and oriented to full-time male workers. Additionally, market demand shifted towards family-consumed goods, focusing on quality over quantity of human capital formation (de Vries 1994, p.18). Komlos (1998, p.7) notes that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7), suggesting that not all groups benefited equally from these changes. This uneven distribution highlights how economic growth can exacerbate inequalities in nutritional resources and physical stature across different social groups. These findings underscore the complex interplay between labor dynamics, economic growth, and the distribution of benefits during historical periods of rapid change.

Pamuk (2007, p.15) and van Zanden (Pamuk 2007, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.1) examine how historical events and economic structures influenced long-term development trajectories. Pamuk highlights that the Black Death catalyzed structural changes in Europe, leading to a wage gap between the northwest and other regions by 1450, driven by labour shortages and declining skill premiums. This shift was part of broader structural changes that reshaped economic dynamics across Europe. Urbanization rates further underscore these divergences, with northwestern Europe experiencing higher urbanization, indicative of greater productivity and economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Van Zanden's analysis complements this by showing that early modern economic growth did not uniformly translate into improved living standards; real wages often declined despite overall economic expansion. This disconnect suggests that the benefits of economic growth were unevenly distributed, with some regions and social groups gaining more than others (van Zanden 1999, p.3). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) build on this by arguing that economies of localization, such as industrial districts, played a crucial role in driving productivity gains. These localized benefits could thrive in proto-industrial settings, where rural peasants supplemented agricultural incomes with industrial production without requiring large-scale urbanization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The formation of these industrial districts highlights how economic development can be driven by specialized information flows and skilled labor supply, even in regions that do not undergo significant urbanization. This pattern suggests that the impact of economic growth on long-term development is mediated by local conditions and institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder productivity gains. These insights underscore the complex interplay between historical events, structural changes, and economic outcomes across different regions.

Stern and Nunn both examine how environmental and budget policies influence economic development (Stern 2004, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.27). The Hecksher-Ohlin trade theory suggests that developing countries specialize in labor- and resource-intensive production under free trade, while developed nations focus on human capital. This specialization could explain the reduction of environmental degradation in wealthy countries. Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades fostered ethnic fragmentation which can impede economic development (Simson 2019, p.27). Simson notes that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were shaped by global economic trends and military instability rather than exceptional clientelism or rent-seeking (Stern 2004, p.1; Simson 2019, p.6) , highlighting the complex interplay of historical, political, and economic factors. These dynamics underscore how environmental and budgetary decisions are intertwined with broader socio-economic conditions, shaping long-run economic outcomes in ways that go beyond simplistic narratives about growth and development.

The skill premium is a key determinant of long-term economic performance and is closely tied to human capital formation and institutional efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). Van Zanden (2009, p.27) demonstrates that the skill premium reflects both the demand for skilled labor and the efficiency of institutions responsible for human capital development. This relationship underscores how effective educational and training systems can drive economic growth by enhancing the productivity of the workforce. Broadberry and Gardner's (2022) research on African economies provides a nuanced perspective, suggesting that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence before colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding challenges conventional narratives about Africa's economic history and highlights the importance of indigenous factors in shaping long-term economic outcomes. This limitation underscores the need for further research into the historical determinants of economic growth in Africa, particularly as they relate to human capital and institutional development.

Gender dynamics significantly shape long-run economic outcomes through their influence on human capital and institutional development. The role of women in economic growth is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. Goldin demonstrates that the transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. was a quiet revolution (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift from "jobs" to "careers" involved substantial human capital investment, altering societal perceptions of women's roles. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Their contributions were central to production and reproduction, underscoring the importance of gender dynamics in historical economic development.

Institutional factors also play a crucial role in mediating the impact of gender on long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions can hinder human capital investment by concentrating political power in the hands of elites (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in New World societies, where institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These institutional barriers can stifle economic growth by limiting opportunities for human capital development. Inclusive institutions that protect property rights and foster competition can enhance long-run economic performance.

The skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance, reflecting the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.25). van Zanden shows that a low skill premium often indicates high levels of human capital formation. This suggests that policies promoting gender equality and inclusive institutions can enhance economic growth by fostering human capital investment. Lindert and Williamson argue that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). Their analysis shows that shifts in the structure of occupations and skills deepening are critical forces driving economic inequality, highlighting the importance of inclusive policies.

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that gender dynamics and institutional factors shape long-run economic outcomes through their influence on human capital development. Acemoglu et al show that extractive institutions can hinder human capital investment by concentrating political power in the hands of elites (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement centres on whether this dynamic applies universally or varies across different historical contexts and institutional settings. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This historical evidence shows how institutions shaped by gender dynamics can influence long-term economic performance.
