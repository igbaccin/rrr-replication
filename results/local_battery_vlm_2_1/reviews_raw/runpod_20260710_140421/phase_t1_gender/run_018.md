The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. At stake is the understanding of how historical patterns of gender roles and development trajectories have influenced contemporary economic disparities. One live disagreement centers around whether gender dynamics are primarily shaped by institutional frameworks or by technological and economic factors. The debate hinges on whether institutions that promote equality or inequality in gender roles drive long-term economic performance, or if technological advancements and economic structures independently influence both gender dynamics and economic growth. This question is crucial for understanding the interplay between societal norms, economic policies, and historical development paths.

The causal mechanisms linking gender roles to long-run economic outcomes are complex. The skill premium, a key indicator of economic performance, is influenced by various factors including factor endowments and institutional efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Institutions that facilitate human capital formation tend to lower the skill premium, which in turn can drive economic growth. For example, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the centrality of women to production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of gender roles in shaping economic outcomes. The role of institutions in mediating these contributions remains unclear. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) suggest that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This implies that institutional frameworks can either amplify or mitigate the economic contributions of different gender roles. The interplay between gender dynamics and institutional efficiency is a critical area for further investigation, as it directly influences long-run economic performance.

The impact of technological advancements on gender roles and economic growth is another key factor in this debate. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) argue that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This perspective suggests that technological progress can drive both economic growth and reduce inequality, potentially altering gender dynamics in the process. The relationship between technology and gender roles is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This indicates that technological advancements alone may not be sufficient to drive long-term economic outcomes; instead, the interaction between technology and institutions is crucial. The role of human capital investment is particularly important in this context. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating human capital formation and economic growth.

The role of gender dynamics in shaping long-run economic outcomes is further complicated by the interplay between technological advancements and societal norms. Goldin (2006, p.3) argues that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S., highlighting the shift from "jobs" to "careers" (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation was driven by changes in occupational identity and human capital investment, suggesting that technological advancements can alter gender roles and economic outcomes. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) emphasizes that the skill premium predicts long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.25). The link between the price of human capital and economic growth suggests that societal norms and institutions play a crucial role in mediating the impact of technological advancements on gender dynamics and economic outcomes. This interplay highlights the complex nature of the relationship between gender, development, and long-run economic performance.

This perspective challenges the marginalization of women's contributions to economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how African women were integral to production and reproduction, countering views that overlook their significance. Goldin provides a comparative lens through her analysis of U.S. women in the labor market, illustrating the transformative shift from "jobs" to "careers." This evolution underscores the broader implications of gender dynamics on economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.1). However, this transformation is not universal; Akyeampong and Fofack note that historical plough use correlates with contemporary unequal gender roles, suggesting a persistent legacy of gender discrimination. This finding qualifies the idea of a straightforward evolution in women's economic roles by highlighting enduring structural barriers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The historical contributions of African women to economic development remain significant, though their impact is shaped by complex interactions between cultural norms and institutional frameworks.

Valuable mineral resources and abundant low-human-capital labor are examples of factor endowments that contribute to the persistence of inequality. Austin qualifies this by noting that systematic racial discrimination can hinder human capital formation and market flexibility, further exacerbating inequality (Austin 2008, p.23).

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (North 1989, p.5), together with McCloskey (2015, p.9), reveal that the evolution of political institutions and ideologies were crucial drivers in shaping economic growth. They show how secure property rights and a commitment to enforce rules fostered market development in seventeenth-century England. This process involved establishing rules and ensuring their enforcement, which underpinned the ability to engage in secure contracting across time and space (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Ideas supporting economic growth were pivotal; McCloskey's analysis shows that France's lack of economic development until the Revolution was due to its elite's anti-bourgeois ideology. This ideological factor inhibited the adoption of pro-growth policies and institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.8). The implication is that without supportive institutions and ideologies, long-run economic outcomes remain stagnant. However, North cautions that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex; while institutions can drive growth, they also depend on the broader historical context (North 1989, p.1).

Kuznets and Mokyr share a view of economic growth as driven by technological advancements and institutional adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets posits that the dynamic drives of modern economic growth necessitate rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn demand rapid shifts in society's other aspects. These include family formation, urbanization, and societal views on roles and achievements. Mokyr builds upon this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This useful knowledge encompasses beliefs about the physical environment and natural phenomena, driving societies to collectively expand their social knowledge. This collective expansion results from greater specialization and professionalization, not merely an aggregation of individual knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.3). The implication here is that economic growth is deeply intertwined with broader societal changes, including shifts in how work and consumption are organized.

This perspective on economic growth is further nuanced by de Vries's concept of the Industrious Revolution. De Vries argues that increased labor input and new consumption patterns preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.17). This shift in household production patterns had profound consequences for the economy, making it more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers. The structure of market demand also evolved to focus on family-consumed goods rather than individually consumed ones (de Vries 1994, p.18). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they are not sufficient on their own; institutional adjustments and changes in societal behavior are equally important for sustained economic growth. Kuznets's emphasis on the rapid changes in society highlights a potential tension: institutional adjustments are necessary but may also create social disruptions that complicate long-term economic stability.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Lindert and Williamson (Temin 2002, p.4; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8) examine post-WWII economic growth and labor allocation. They highlight that the rapid economic growth during Europe's Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, which led to significant shifts in productivity and wage structures. This disequilibrium is critical because it created incentives for investment in human capital, as the derived demand for skills increased rapidly. These dynamics are linked to changes in occupational structures, where shifts in the distribution of jobs across sectors played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes. Temin argues that this misallocation of resources was distinct from low income generated by insufficient physical and human capital relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9). This perspective is further supported by Lindert and Williamson's (1985) analysis, which shows that unbalanced productivity advances favored skills, driving earnings inequality and incentivizing human capital investment. Lindert and Williamson note the inelastic supply response in skills per worker, suggesting that while the demand for skilled labor increased, the supply did not keep pace (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This imbalance implies that the rapid economic growth during this period was deeply rooted in structural changes within the economy, not solely due to policy interventions.

van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) demonstrate that structural changes within European economies in the early modern period influenced long-run economic outcomes, even though their work does not directly address gender dynamics. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) highlights the lack of a clear relationship between real wages and economic growth during this era, suggesting that factors beyond wage levels shaped Europe's economic trajectory. This insight is complemented by Pamuk's (2007, p.16) examination of urbanization rates as key indicators of productivity and development, indicating that structural shifts in labor markets and human capital formation were crucial drivers of long-term economic performance.

The drop in interest rates across Europe post-Black Death also encouraged human capital formation, further influencing the trajectory of economic development. Pamuk's findings on urbanization rates provide additional context, indicating that structural changes and productivity gains were closely tied to economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This evidence collectively underscores how institutional factors and demographic shifts shaped long-run economic outcomes in Europe, even though gender dynamics are not explicitly addressed.

The Great Divergence within Europe highlights the role of human capital formation and institutional efficiency in driving economic growth. These findings suggest that while gender may not have been the primary driver of economic outcomes in early modern Europe, it is likely that gender roles interacted with these broader structural changes in complex ways.

The literature on biological well-being and nutritional status (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19) does not directly address how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes. It provides valuable insights into the relationship between economic growth and physiological health, which can be linked to broader developmental patterns affecting both men and women. This decline in physical stature indicates that not all segments of society benefited equally from economic growth, suggesting a mismatch between aggregate economic indicators and individual well-being. This finding underscores the importance of considering factors beyond economic growth when assessing long-term development outcomes, as nutritional status is influenced by a range of social and environmental conditions. The implication is that gender-specific impacts on health and nutrition could be significant but are not directly addressed within these studies. For instance, Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) note that both gender inequality and social inequality have negative effects on height, with the effect of gender inequality being about half as strong (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This suggests that while economic growth may not uniformly improve biological well-being, addressing inequalities in health and nutrition could lead to more equitable long-term developmental outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) (Nunn 2008, p.27) jointly assert that African economies were more developed than commonly believed before colonial interventions. They argue that the economic performance of Anglophone Africa has been misunderstood by previous characterizations, which often attributed slow growth to a lack of European intervention. Nunn further illustrates this point by showing how Africa's slave trades, which predated colonialism, adversely affected economic development due to ethnic fractionalization (Nunn 2008, p.4). This suggests that pre-colonial factors, such as internal conflicts and social fragmentation, played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Nunn also notes that the environment of uncertainty and insecurity during this period required individuals to defend themselves with weapons like iron knives and firearms (Nunn 2008, p.4), which could have had downstream implications for technological development and resource allocation. This underscores the complex interplay between social instability, defense needs, and economic growth in pre-colonial Africa.

Gender dynamics significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes by shaping labor markets and human capital development. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions often concentrated political power in the hands of elites, which could disadvantage the majority population (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of African women's contributions to economic growth, where gender discrimination has affected both short-term productivity and long-run human capital investment (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women played a central role in production and reproduction during pre-colonial and colonial periods, indicating their significant economic contributions despite facing barriers to formal employment and productive assets (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3).

The role of gender in long-run economic outcomes is further illuminated by the skill premium's impact on economic performance. Van Zanden argues that a low skill premium reflects high levels of human capital formation, which is crucial for economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This finding aligns with historical evidence from African societies, where women's contributions to economic activities were substantial but often underrecognized. The skill premium and its determinants help explain the 'little divergence' within Europe between 1400 and 1800, suggesting that similar dynamics could have influenced long-run economic outcomes in other regions (van Zanden 2009, p.25). Lindert and Williamson demonstrate that changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening are critical forces driving the Kuznets Curve across the 19th century (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This underscores the importance of human capital investment in shaping long-run economic outcomes, a factor closely linked to gender dynamics.
