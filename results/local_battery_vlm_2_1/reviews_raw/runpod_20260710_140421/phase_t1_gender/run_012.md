The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is pivotal for understanding historical patterns of growth and inequality. At stake intellectually is the question of whether gender dynamics are merely a byproduct of economic processes or fundamental drivers that influence institutional structures and technological advancements over time. This debate centers on two primary positions: one emphasizes the transformative power of women's labor and education in fostering long-term economic development, while the other underscores the role of institutions and technology in shaping gender roles and economic outcomes. The interplay between these factors reveals a complex relationship where gender dynamics can both influence and be influenced by broader economic developments.

The first stream addresses the direct impact of women's participation in the labor force and education on long-term economic growth. Goldin highlights that women transitioned from being piece workers or domestics to professionals, marking a significant shift in their economic roles (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation represented a quiet revolution that altered both employment patterns and societal perceptions of women's work (Author Year p.N). Concurrently, Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women played crucial roles in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical perspective underscores the importance of recognizing women's labor as a fundamental driver of economic development, viewing it as secondary to male-dominated sectors is not accurate. The evolution of gender roles in the workforce is thus intricately linked to broader economic changes, influencing long-term growth trajectories.

Economic growth is driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.4) describes how economic growth necessitates rapid changes in societal structures. These transformations are not limited to mere shifts in labor force distribution or urbanization; they also involve a fundamental rethinking of man's role and achievements within society. This expansion involves a collective accumulation of societal knowledge. The total amount of knowledge in modern societies has vastly increased due to greater specialization and professionalization. This collective knowledge is a critical driver of economic development. McCloskey (2015, p.9) highlights how the cultural and ideological environment can either foster or hinder growth, showing that ideas and ideologies are crucial drivers of economic change. For instance, in France, the lack of bourgeois support for economic growth was a significant barrier to development. This underscores that while technological advancements and useful knowledge are essential, the societal context in which these operate also plays a vital role. The interplay between technological progress, accumulated knowledge, and ideological frameworks shapes long-term economic outcomes. This interplay suggests that gender roles, as part of the broader societal structure, can influence how technological advancements and knowledge accumulation translate into economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes in former European colonies. These institutions, often designed to extract resources and consolidate power, created enduring economic disparities. For instance, the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in societies with abundant natural resources and low human capital was exacerbated by colonial policies that restricted competition and favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework, which secured property rights for a privileged few while marginalizing the majority, stifled broader economic development. The concentration of political power in the hands of a small elite meant that investments were often at risk of being expropriated by those in control, discouraging widespread entrepreneurial activity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic was particularly pronounced in regions with valuable mineral resources and abundant labor, where colonial policies reinforced existing inequalities. Austin (2008, p.23) highlights the complexity of these institutional impacts, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For example, systematic racial discrimination under apartheid in South Africa hindered human capital formation and market flexibility, contributing to economic stagnation (Austin 2008, p.1). This underscores how colonial institutions shaped immediate economic outcomes and had long-lasting effects on development trajectories.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.5; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the regime's commitment to rules underpinned economic development, highlighting how credible commitments fostered secure contracting across time and space. This institutional framework was vital for enabling specialization and division of labor, which in turn drove economic growth. North (1989, p.5) emphasizes that transaction costs play a pivotal role in shaping productivity and economic outcomes. The ability to enforce property rights effectively reduced these transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic development. This institutional evolution also underscored the importance of political factors, such as the commitment to rules by the sovereign, which ensured that economic exchanges were secure and predictable. This security was essential for fostering long-term economic growth and development in England.

The literature on biological well-being and economic growth does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Both Komlos (1998, p.7) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) underscore how economic growth can have differential effects on various population segments, notably those with limited access to resources. Komlos's analysis of physical stature during the Industrial Revolution reveals that not all groups benefited equally from economic expansion; instead, disparities in nutritional resources and income distribution led to uneven outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7). This pattern suggests that while aggregate measures like GDP may indicate growth, underlying inequalities can obscure significant declines in biological well-being for specific subgroups. Their findings indicate that these inequalities have substantial economic significance, with social inequality corresponding to notable differences in physical stature between high and low socio-economic groups. Gender inequality also affects biological well-being; however, its impact is less pronounced than that of broader social disparities (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This nuanced understanding of how different forms of inequality influence health outcomes underscores the complexity of economic development processes; it reveals that even when overall growth occurs, specific populations may experience stagnation or decline in key indicators like nutritional status.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) share a focus on the historical economic growth and divergence in Africa and Asia. They show that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1), and that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This perspective challenges previous assumptions about economic growth being solely driven by European colonial interventions. In particular, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) demonstrate that early economic histories of Africa often overlooked local developments in favor of attributing growth to external factors like railways and malaria prophylaxis (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that African economies were more robust than previously thought, with local conditions playing a significant role in economic performance. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) add nuance by highlighting the differences between proto-industrialization and specialized industrial production, which can be misleading when assessing economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.25). This distinction underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms driving economic development in different regions. These insights into historical economic performance in Africa and Asia provide a broader context for examining how gender and development might have shaped long-run economic outcomes, particularly by revealing that local conditions and industrial development played crucial roles in economic growth.

The precise role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a point of agreement across literature on development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The persistence of inequality in societies where women's roles were historically marginalized underscores the broader impact of gender dynamics. There remains disagreement over the specific mechanisms through which these historical factors influence contemporary economic performance.

The debate hinges on whether colonial institutions or pre-colonial conditions better explain current disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). Sokoloff and Engerman show that New World societies with greater inequality developed economic structures that advantaged elites. These unequal distributions of wealth and income were perpetuated by policies that restricted competition, thus preserving the status quo for those in power.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They contend that the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments.

The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from research on African women's contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The origins, dynamics, and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time are crucial in understanding the contemporary economic landscape. This evidence shows that local conditions and industrial development played crucial roles in economic growth, highlighting how gender and development have shaped long-run economic outcomes.
