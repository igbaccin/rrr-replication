The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue that engages scholars across various disciplines. At stake is the understanding of how historical patterns of gender roles and economic development interact to influence contemporary economic performance. The debate hinges on whether gender dynamics are a critical driver of economic growth or merely a secondary factor influenced by broader developmental processes. Some scholars argue that gender roles, shaped by historical institutions and cultural norms, significantly impact long-run economic outcomes. Some scholars propose that economic development itself propels changes in gender roles, indicating that institutional and technological factors are primary drivers of economic growth.

The debate is further complicated by the interplay between gender and other socio-economic factors. For instance, some scholars suggest that the skill premium, a key determinant of long-term economic performance, is influenced by both factor endowments and human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how gender roles interact with broader economic structures to shape development trajectories. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This suggests that the impact of gender roles on economic outcomes may be mediated through institutional frameworks that are themselves shaped by historical and colonial legacies.

Goldin argues that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). She finds that this shift was marked by a transition from women working out of necessity to those pursuing careers driven by identity and societal worth. This evolution involved significant changes in human capital investment, where occupations evolved from "jobs" into "careers," with the latter involving greater long-term commitment and skill development. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women played a crucial role in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, often overlooked by traditional narratives of history. This historical contribution suggests that gender roles have been pivotal in shaping economic outcomes across different contexts. The mechanisms behind these transformations are intricate. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack note that historical plough use is correlated with contemporary unequal gender roles, indicating that technological and cultural factors can perpetuate gender inequalities over time (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This finding underscores the need to consider both historical legacies and contemporary policies in understanding how gender roles influence economic development.

North and Weingast argue that institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.25). The development of institutions governing public choice created an environment where secure contracting across time and space became possible. This institutional framework was crucial for the evolution of markets, as it ensured that rules governing economic exchange were both enforced and adaptable. Secure property rights facilitated long-term investment and impersonal exchange, which are foundational to modern economies (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The historical evidence from seventeenth-century England underscores the importance of institutional stability in driving economic growth. McCloskey questions the primacy of institutions alone by highlighting the role of ideas and ideologies in shaping economic change. For instance, in eighteenth-century France, anti-bourgeois sentiments within the elite hindered economic growth despite the presence of clever advocates for trade (McCloskey 2015, p.8). The implication is that while institutional frameworks are necessary, they may not be sufficient without supportive ideological contexts. This suggests that the interplay between institutions and ideologies is crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes in different historical contexts.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Austin (2008, p.23) argue that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic outcomes by determining the distribution of wealth and human capital in former colonies. Colonialism's institutional legacy, particularly the security of property rights for elites, influenced investment patterns and entrepreneurship. When colonial policies concentrated power among a small elite, they created incentives for extracting rents rather than fostering broad-based economic development. This concentration of political and social power left the majority vulnerable to exploitation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The result was an unequal distribution of wealth and human capital, where elites secured their interests at the expense of broader societal growth. The persistence of these institutional frameworks has had lasting effects on economic outcomes in former colonies.

One mechanism through which colonial institutions affected long-run economic outcomes is by shaping labor markets. For instance, in societies with extractive institutions, large-scale plantations dominated economies and relied heavily on slave labor (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This concentration of wealth among a few elites restricted opportunities for the broader population to accumulate human capital or engage in entrepreneurial activities. Austin (2008, p.23) qualifies this mechanism by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causation. He argues that colonial institutions interacted with local conditions in complex ways and were not solely responsible for economic outcomes, thereby highlighting the need to consider multiple factors influencing long-run economic development.

The literature on Africa's slave trades highlights how these historical events shaped long-term economic outcomes through mechanisms such as ethnic fractionalization (Simson 2019, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades contributed to high levels of ethnic fractionalization in Africa, which in turn hindered economic development by creating barriers to cooperation and trust among different ethnic groups. This process was exacerbated by the insecurity and uncertainty that characterized the period, as individuals were compelled to rely on their own ethnic networks for protection (Nunn 2008, p.4). Pamuk (2007, p.15) illustrates how similar historical shocks, such as the Black Death in Europe, led to structural changes that had long-term economic implications. This implies that while historical shocks like slave trades and pandemics have profound impacts on economic development, their effects can be tempered by strategic interventions aimed at fostering growth and stability.

Technological and institutional drivers are pivotal in economic growth, as Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Mokyr (2005, p.4) jointly claim that the rise of useful knowledge and technological advancements underpin modern economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). The dynamic interplay between technology and institutions fosters labor force adaptation. As societies adopt new technologies, they must make institutional adjustments to efficiently harness these innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.4) elaborates on this by noting that the integration of advanced technology into the economy necessitates rapid changes in occupational structures and relations among status groups (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This dynamic is further supported by Mokyr's emphasis on useful knowledge, which he defines as a collective understanding of the physical environment and natural phenomena. The interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for fostering long-term economic development.

Technological advancements impact labor patterns beyond simple productivity gains. De Vries (1994, p.17) argues that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.17). This revolution in household production had profound consequences for the entire economy, shifting market demand towards goods that required less home labor. The shift also focused on enhancing human capital formation in children, which led to improvements in morbidity and mortality rates, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort. These changes were achieved more through the household productive system than through the larger formal economy (de Vries 1994, p.17). De Vries (1994, p.17) contends that the Industrious Revolution was a distinct phenomenon preceding and facilitating the Industrial Revolution, emphasizing the importance of labor patterns in economic development (de Vries 1994, p.18).

This implies that while technological advancements drive growth, they also necessitate institutional changes to fully realize their potential.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that long-term economic development is driven by economies of localization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) as well as institutions that affect the specification and enforcement of property rights (North 1989, p.5). These factors are key in understanding the great divergence between Europe and Asia, which was already underway before 1800. This divergence can be attributed to various historical and economic factors, including the role of merchant distribution and finance in increasing productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.25). These factors do not directly address how gender and development influenced long-run economic outcomes.

Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) highlight that social inequality and gender inequality have significant impacts on nutritional status, which is a critical indicator of overall well-being. This suggests that while economic growth can occur through increasing productivity, the distribution of this growth and its impact on different segments of society are influenced by underlying social structures. The implications for long-run economic outcomes are significant, as persistent inequalities can hinder overall development and well-being.

Broadberry and Gupta’s (2006, p.9) focus on economies of localization, along with North's (1989, p.5) emphasis on institutions affecting property rights, underscores the importance of local economic conditions and institutional frameworks in driving long-term growth. These factors create a conducive environment for economic development but do not specifically address how gender dynamics within these structures influence outcomes.

Van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Broadberry and Gardner (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1) examine various aspects of economic performance and living standards. This insight is echoed by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) finding that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. These findings underscore the importance of human capital and economic institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes, suggesting that investments in education and skill development can drive sustained growth.

Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this view by arguing that despite overall economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined for certain groups due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7). This implies that while economic indicators such as GDP may show growth, the distribution of benefits within a population can significantly affect living standards. When assessing long-term economic outcomes, it is crucial to consider both aggregate measures and the distributional impacts of economic growth on different segments of society.

Temin argues that rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age resulted from disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.4; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This perspective emphasizes the role of structural changes in labor markets rather than focusing on gender dynamics or development policies. The mechanism at play involves a shift in the derived demand for skills, which incentivizes investment in human capital. Lindert and Williamson highlight that this dynamic is driven by unbalanced productivity advances favoring skill expansion, creating significant incentives for human capital investment. This process is further elaborated by Kuznets' observations on shifts in occupational structures (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Temin's work underscores how misallocation of resources leads to a developmental deficit separate from low income levels generated by insufficient physical and human capital relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9), which can be seen as a structural driver rather than a gender-specific one. This suggests that while gender roles may influence labor allocation, the primary driver of post-WWII economic growth was the broader reallocation of labor between sectors.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is integral to understanding broader development trajectories. The transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. illustrates how shifts in occupational structures can drive economic growth (Goldin 2006, p.3; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Goldin highlights that women moved from piecework in manufacturing to more professional roles, marking a shift towards careers rather than mere jobs, which involved significant human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This trend aligns with Lindert and Williamson's observations on occupational shifts as drivers of economic growth. The quiet revolution in women’s employment indicates that gender dynamics are closely linked to the broader reallocation of labor between sectors.

The skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance, reflecting both human capital formation and institutional efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). Van Zanden shows how a low skill premium generally reflects high levels of human capital formation, which is crucial for sustained economic growth. This perspective complements Lindert and Williamson's argument that changes in the rate of skills deepening are critical forces driving long-term economic outcomes. The historical evidence from both papers underscores that human capital investment, driven by shifts in occupational structures and gender roles, is a fundamental driver of economic development.

In African societies, women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women were central to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their role. This contribution is evident in the unequal distributions of wealth and income in New World societies, where institutional structures advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The persistence of inequality in these societies highlights how gender roles intersect with broader economic structures to shape long-run outcomes.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions led to disinvestment in human capital, particularly for female workers, which adversely affected long-run growth. This perspective aligns with Lindert and Williamson's findings on the role of occupational shifts in driving economic growth. The historical evidence from both papers underscores that institutional changes and gender roles are crucial factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that human capital investment, driven by shifts in occupational structures and gender roles, is a fundamental driver of economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which these factors operate. Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions in disinvestment in human capital, particularly for female workers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), while van Zanden highlights the importance of institutional efficiency and human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The historical evidence from both papers underscores that the specific historical context and empirical comparison of skill premiums and occupational shifts settles this disagreement.
