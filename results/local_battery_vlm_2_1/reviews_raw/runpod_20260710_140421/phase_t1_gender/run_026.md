The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question that intersects with debates about the origins of inequality and the pathways to economic growth. At stake intellectually is understanding whether gender dynamics are a primary driver of economic trajectories or merely a secondary effect of other factors. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which gender roles influence economic development versus being shaped by it. Some scholars emphasize the causal role of gender in economic outcomes, while others view gender as a consequence of broader economic and institutional forces. The debate also touches on whether historical gender inequalities can be reversed through policy interventions or if they are deeply entrenched due to path dependencies. A related question is whether economic development inherently leads to more egalitarian gender roles, or if specific policies and institutions are necessary to achieve this outcome. This literature review will explore these tensions by examining the role of gender in long-run economic outcomes and the institutional frameworks that shape both.

Gender has been a significant factor in shaping labor market dynamics and economic growth across different historical periods. Goldin (2006, p.1) argues that women's employment, education, and family dynamics underwent a transformative shift in the U.S. transitioning from 'jobs' to 'careers.' This transition involved an increase in human capital investment as well as a change in societal perceptions of women's roles. Akyeampong and Fofack similarly highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. This contribution was crucial for production and reproduction, underscoring the importance of gender dynamics in historical economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The skill premium, which reflects the relative abundance of capital and efficiency of institutions for human capital formation, is another key factor in long-term economic performance. Van Zanden notes that a low skill premium generally indicates high levels of human capital formation, which is essential for economic growth. This suggests that gender roles, by influencing human capital investment, can indirectly affect economic outcomes through the skill premium (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The interplay between gender dynamics and the skill premium highlights how historical changes in women's roles have contributed to long-run economic outcomes.

The institutional frameworks that shape both gender roles and economic development are complex and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions play a crucial role in determining long-run economic outcomes, particularly through their impact on property rights and the protection of entrepreneurial investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Sokoloff and Engerman also emphasize the importance of institutions in shaping economic structures, noting how policies favoring elites can restrict competition and perpetuate inequality. This institutional perspective suggests that gender roles are shaped by economic development and also by the broader institutional context. For instance, Acemoglu et al. note that extractive institutions often limit access to education for the masses, which in turn affects human capital formation and long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This interplay between institutions, gender roles, and economic development underscores the need for a holistic approach that considers both historical and contemporary factors.

The role of human capital investment is central to understanding how gender dynamics influence long-term economic performance. Lindert and Williamson argue that shifts in the structure of occupations and investments in human capital are critical forces driving economic growth and distribution (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This perspective aligns with van Zanden's observation that a low skill premium reflects high levels of human capital formation, which is essential for economic growth. This indicates that gender dynamics drive economic development through their impact on human capital investment and the skill premium.

Goldin argues that women's employment in the U.S. underwent a quiet revolution (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This transformation involved a shift from piecework and domestic labor to professional careers, marking a significant change in women's economic roles. The vast majority of women workers were initially confined to low-skill jobs with minimal on-the-job learning and little formal education beyond elementary school. Beginning in the 1910s, there was an enormous expansion in professional roles for women, particularly in teaching and clerical positions (Goldin 2006, p.3). This shift indicates that women's employment involved a significant transformation in their economic participation. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the central role of women in production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. They note a strong correlation between historical plough use and unequal gender roles today, suggesting deep-rooted cultural influences on women's economic participation. This historical perspective underscores the importance of considering long-term cultural factors when examining women's roles in economic development.

They argue that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers prevented widespread education and human capital formation, hindering economic development. The concentration of political power in the hands of a small elite under extractive institutions implies that the majority of the population faced risks in undertaking investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is compounded by the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades, which exacerbated ethnic fractionalization and hindered long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). The link between ethnic fractionalization and economic development underscores the multifaceted impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.1) challenges this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods into a single narrative. He contends that systemic racial discrimination during apartheid in South Africa hindered human capital formation and market flexibility, illustrating how specific historical contexts can shape economic trajectories beyond colonial institutions. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role, other factors such as post-colonial policies also influence long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.25) share a claim that institutional frameworks are critical for economic growth because they foster secure property rights and effective governance. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. This is supported by North who states that "economic growth can and has occurred as a consequence of increasing productivity" (North 1989, p.5). The implication is that while secure property rights are necessary for economic growth, they must be accompanied by effective institutions that enforce these rights. This suggests that the relationship between institutional frameworks and economic growth is complex and multifaceted; it requires both the establishment of secure property rights and the presence of effective governance structures to drive market development.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are critical drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets's work highlights that economic growth necessitates rapid changes in the structure of society, including shifts in occupational status groups and family formation. This dynamic drive of modern economic growth is facilitated by major changes in transport and communication, which enable the spread of technological innovations to less developed areas. Mokyr extends this perspective by emphasizing that the accumulation of useful knowledge underpins economic growth; societies with mechanisms for consensus-shaping around beliefs about the physical environment experience greater economic development. This shared finding suggests that technological progress and institutional frameworks that promote the diffusion of useful knowledge are essential for sustained economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) qualifies the primacy of technology by arguing that ideas and ideologies play a pivotal role in driving economic change. He contends that the cultural struggle over ideas, such as those shaping views on religion and governance, can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. For instance, France's resistance to bourgeois economic successes until the Revolution illustrates how ideological barriers can throttle modern economic growth. This implies that while technological advancements are necessary for economic development, they must be accompanied by a supportive ideological framework that encourages innovation and change.

The relationship between economic development and living standards is intricate, shaped by multiple factors including skill premiums, labor input, and biological well-being during industrialization (van Zanden 2009, p.27; de Vries 1994, p.17). Van Zanden (2009, p.27) illustrates how the skill premium, influenced by capital abundance and institutional efficiency, serves as a predictor of long-term economic performance. This premium reflects demand for skilled labor and also the efficacy of human capital formation institutions. This period saw a shift towards capital-intensive production systems oriented to full-time male workers, with market demand focusing on family-consumed goods (de Vries 1994, p.17). The implications of these changes were profound, leading to improvements in nutrition, education, and domestic comfort, benefits that could not be readily purchased but were achieved through the household productive system. However, Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this by noting that despite overall economic growth during industrialization, there were groups who experienced a decline in biological well-being due to uneven distribution of income and nutritional resources (Komlos 1998, p.7). This suggests that while economic development can drive improvements in living standards, these benefits are not uniformly distributed.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) assert that economic development does not necessarily translate into improvements in living standards. They suggest that the relationship between economic growth and standard of living is complex and not always direct. This indicates that while economies grew, the benefits did not uniformly reach all segments of society. They highlight that factors such as land availability and urbanization influenced nutritional outcomes more than economic growth alone. They also point out that gender inequality played a role in shaping these outcomes. The impact of social inequality on height differences was significant, suggesting that the distribution of resources within societies mattered greatly. This implies that while economic development is crucial, its benefits are contingent upon how resources are allocated and who has access to them.

Regional economic performance varied considerably over time, and these differences were influenced by multiple factors such as urbanization and industrial development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) challenge the notion that African economies only began to grow after European colonial interventions. Their analysis shows that early economic growth in Anglophone Africa was driven by local conditions rather than external factors, highlighting a more nuanced picture of pre-colonial economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This perspective is echoed in Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.9) work on the great divergence between Europe and Asia. They argue that the differences in economic performance were already pronounced before 1800, emphasizing the role of industrial districts and urbanization in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.25). Pamuk (2007, p.15) supports these findings by showing how long-term structural changes initiated by events like the Black Death led to significant wage gaps across Europe. This indicates that regional disparities were influenced by both immediate shocks and longer-term economic conditions, which shaped urbanization rates and productivity levels (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) also note that high productivity in traded goods sectors resulted from merchant distribution and finance as well as production, suggesting that economic growth was driven by a complex interplay of factors beyond industrial output alone (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.25). This complexity underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions when analyzing long-run economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Stern (2004, p.8) both examine economic growth through the lens of resource allocation and trade theories (Temin 2002, p.4; Stern 2004, p.8). Temin argues that post-WWII Europe's rapid growth was driven by a labor disequilibrium between agriculture and industry, not just policy shifts. This perspective underscores how misallocated resources can hinder long-run economic outcomes. Stern’s analysis of the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) reveals that economic development can lead to specialization in production based on factor endowments. Thus, developing countries may initially experience increased environmental degradation as they industrialize, but this process can ultimately drive improvements in environmental standards over time (Stern 2004, p.1). This pattern suggests that while immediate shocks and resource misallocation can impede growth, long-term economic development can lead to sustainable improvements. However, Simson (2019, p.27) qualifies this by showing that budget policies in East Africa post-independence were not as unproductive or clientelist as commonly believed (Simson 2019, p.6). This qualification highlights the role of public expenditures and state growth in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6), share the posture that economic policies and institutional structures are pivotal in shaping long-term economic outcomes. They focus on how various government policies can influence inequality. This dynamic highlights how policy-induced shifts in the structure of occupations can lead to long-run economic growth and distribution outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman illustrate a parallel mechanism: institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to favor elites through restrictive policies, which impede competition and perpetuate inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These findings suggest that public expenditures and state growth significantly influence the distributional consequences of development (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28).

These insights must be qualified by considering the role of gender in shaping economic outcomes. Lindert and Williamson's focus on factor incomes and human capital investment does not explicitly address how gender dynamics might alter these processes. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman's emphasis on elite advantage through government policies overlooks potential gender-specific impacts of these policies. This omission suggests that while inequality and economic policy are intertwined, the specific role of gender in this relationship remains underexplored.

Gender dynamics significantly influence economic outcomes by shaping human capital investment and labor market participation. Van Zanden shows that the skill premium is closely linked to both factor endowments and institutional efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.27), while Goldin highlights how changes in women's employment transformed their identity and societal worth, shifting from "jobs" to "careers" with increased human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift indicates that gender-specific factors are integral to understanding long-run economic performance. The role of African women in economic growth further illustrates this point. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This contribution was through labor and also through the reproduction of human capital, which is crucial for long-term economic development.

The specific mechanisms by which gender dynamics influence economic outcomes remain under-explored. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions can limit human capital investment, particularly for those without effective property rights protection (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This limitation suggests that gender-specific barriers to property rights and political power may exacerbate inequality and hinder long-term growth. Sokoloff and Engerman show that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This dynamic is likely influenced by gender, as women often face additional barriers to political and economic power.

The disagreement lies in whether gender dynamics are a primary driver of long-run economic outcomes or merely a secondary factor influenced by broader institutional and policy frameworks. Van Zanden shows that the skill premium reflects both relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Akyeampong and Fofack show that historical plough use is strongly correlated with unequal gender roles today, indicating a deep-rooted influence of gender dynamics on economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The specific historical evidence of African women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods settles this disagreement by demonstrating the direct impact of gender dynamics on long-run economic performance.
