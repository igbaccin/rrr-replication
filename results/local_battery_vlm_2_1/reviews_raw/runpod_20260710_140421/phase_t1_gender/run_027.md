The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question that intersects multiple scholarly debates. This inquiry is pivotal for understanding the dynamics of economic growth and inequality over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether the historical roles and contributions of women to economic activities have been adequately recognized and how these roles influence contemporary economic structures. One position emphasizes the transformative shifts in women's employment and education, highlighting a quiet revolution that has reshaped societal norms and economic participation. Another perspective emphasizes the lasting impacts of institutional changes from colonialism on gender roles and economic development. The historical record and current policies aimed at fostering inclusive economic growth are both at stake.

The centrality of women in pre-colonial and colonial African economies has been documented, highlighting their significant contributions to production and reproduction (Austin 2008, p.23; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The roles and contributions of African women have been systematically overlooked by scholars who focus on other determinants of economic growth. This marginalization is evident in the literature that emphasizes formal employment and productive assets as primary drivers of economic development, often at the expense of recognizing informal but critical economic activities performed by women (Temin 2002, p.9; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The historical plough use is strongly correlated with unequal gender roles today, indicating a persistent legacy of gender-based labor division. This suggests that historical practices continue to influence contemporary gender norms and economic participation.

The impact of human capital investment on long-run economic outcomes is well-documented, but the specific role of women in this process remains under-examined (Temin 2002, p.9; Goldin 2006, p.1). The transition from "jobs" to "careers" for women reflects a broader shift in societal values and economic participation. This transformation includes increased employment opportunities and a redefinition of occupational identity, where work becomes integral to one's sense of worth and social status (Temin 2002, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.1). This transition is not uniform across all sectors or regions; the gains from human capital investment vary significantly. The slow supply response in skills per worker indicates that while there is an incentive for human capital investment, the actual realization of these investments lags behind.

Despite the recognition of women's contributions to economic development, the institutional frameworks that govern their participation remain a critical factor in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.1; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite often results in the marginalization of the majority population. This includes women, who may have entrepreneurial human capital but lack effective property rights protection (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). The interplay between institutions and economic opportunities is complex, with historical plough use still influencing gender norms today.

The long-run effects of these institutional frameworks are evident in the persistent disparities in economic outcomes. The misallocation of resources due to institutional barriers highlights a developmental deficit that goes beyond low income or inadequate physical capital (Temin 2002, p.9). This deficit highlights the need for policies that address immediate economic needs and the structural barriers that hinder long-term growth and development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) argue that African women played a pivotal role in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods; this is shared with the findings of Goldin (2006, p.3). The contributions of African women were multifaceted, encompassing significant roles in production and reproduction. Their involvement was foundational to economic activities. Women often worked in manufacturing as piece workers or labored in service sectors such as domestic work and laundering; these roles, although demanding, did not always offer substantial learning opportunities or formal education benefits (Goldin 2006, p.3). The persistence of gender norms influenced by historical practices, such as plough use, continues to shape current economic outcomes. For instance, cultures with a history of plough use exhibit less egalitarian gender norms and lower female workforce participation today. This historical legacy underscores the enduring impact of past gender roles on contemporary economic dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The long-term effects of these historical gender roles highlight the need for policies that address both immediate economic needs and the structural barriers rooted in historical practices.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33); (Nunn 2008, p.27) share that colonialism shaped long-run economic outcomes by introducing institutional changes that reversed relative incomes among former European colonies. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite under colonial rule often resulted in extractive institutions, where the majority of the population faced risks of being held up by the powerful elite after making investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic hindered long-term economic development as it discouraged investment and entrepreneurship among the broader populace. This institutional perspective does not fully capture the complexity of historical changes. Austin (2008, p.23) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This critique highlights the need to consider multiple factors beyond just institutions when examining long-run economic outcomes, including social and political dynamics that can be shaped by colonial legacies. Furthermore, the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development underscore the importance of considering pre-colonial factors as well. Nunn shows that the slave trades contributed to ethnic fractionalization, which has been linked to long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). This suggests that colonialism's impact on long-run economic outcomes is mediated by a complex interplay of historical and institutional factors, including pre-colonial legacies.

The papers in this cluster examine how economic growth and living standards in Europe during specific historical periods were shaped by factors such as income gains and nutritional resources (Komlos 1998, p.7; van Zanden 1999, p.1). These studies highlight that while there was significant economic growth, especially during the Industrial Revolution, it did not uniformly translate into improved biological well-being. Van Zanden supports this by noting that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. This suggests that while economic growth occurred, it did not necessarily lead to improved living standards for all segments of society (van Zanden 1999, p.3). The uneven distribution of resources is further explored by Koepke and Baten, who found that social inequality had a significant impact on nutritional status, corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low socio-economic groups, whereas gender inequality was about half as strong (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This indicates that while economic growth may have benefited some, it did not universally improve the living standards of all Europeans.

Temin argues that Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by labor allocation disequilibrium between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.4; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This view complements Lindert and Williamson's analysis of occupational shifts in the 19th century, which highlights how changes in productivity and skill demands influenced earnings inequality. This disequilibrium is distinct from low income generated by insufficient physical and human capital relative to saving rates; instead, it arises from a misallocation of resources. The rapid economic growth during this period can be attributed to the reallocation of labor from agriculture to industry, which was not merely driven by policy or other ordinary factors but rather by structural shifts in the economy. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how resource allocation and occupational changes shape long-run economic outcomes, even if they do not directly address gender roles.

The implications of this labor reallocation are significant for understanding broader economic dynamics. This perspective suggests that addressing resource allocation inefficiencies could potentially enhance long-term economic growth, independent of traditional factors such as physical and human capital accumulation. Therefore, while this stream does not directly address gender roles in shaping economic outcomes, it provides a lens through which to examine how structural changes in labor allocation can influence broader economic trajectories.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are pivotal drivers of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.4) posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge is instrumental in fostering economic development. This perspective emphasizes that societies with robust mechanisms for consensus-building around scientific and technological advancements tend to experience more rapid and sustained economic growth. This interplay necessitates a continuous expansion into less developed areas, facilitating the spread of modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.4) also highlights that this process involves rapid changes in economic structure, which can lead to shifts in family formation and urbanization patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These structural adjustments are essential for integrating a labor force into the modern economic system. The implication is that while technological progress and institutional adaptations drive economic growth, they also precipitate profound social transformations. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between economic development and societal changes, setting the stage for examining how gender roles might influence these processes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) illustrate this complexity by showing that colonial policies in Ghana had a lasting impact on gender roles through education reforms that limited women's access to secondary schools. This historical context underscores how structural changes in labor allocation, driven by technological progress and institutional adjustments, can have far-reaching implications for gender dynamics. The interaction between economic growth and gender roles is multifaceted, involving immediate policy considerations and long-term societal transformations that shape economic trajectories.

Broadberry and Gardner's work on economic performance in Africa highlights that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). While Broadberry and Gupta focus on Asia, both argue that precolonial growth was not solely dependent on European interventions. This perspective challenges previous narratives that attribute African economic development primarily to colonial influences. These localized economies did not necessarily imply large-scale urbanization. This suggests that while local economic activities were robust, they might have faced constraints in scaling up without broader infrastructural support or integration into larger markets. Broadberry and Gardner's findings align with the idea that precolonial African economies had intrinsic growth potential, which was later affected by colonial policies.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is deeply intertwined with broader development trajectories. African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This contribution was as agents who shaped production and reproduction dynamics, which are essential for sustainable economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) argue that the role of African women in economic development has been historically marginalized. This marginalization has roots in historical dynamics that have persisted over time. Goldin (2006, p.3) shows how women's employment, education, and family dynamics transformed in the U.S., highlighting that these changes involved a shift from 'jobs' to 'careers,' where occupation and employment defined one's fundamental identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation underscores the broader implications of gender roles in economic development. The quiet revolution in women's employment Goldin describes was driven by shifts in human capital investment, which are critical for long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Historical evidence shows that gender discrimination tends to lower the average productivity of workers and can adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This finding aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly impacted economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes, which often marginalized certain groups, including women (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Temin (2002, p.4) shows how rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium highlights the importance of resource allocation and human capital investment in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Temin 2002, p.9). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which is relevant to understanding the role of gender in development. The stagnation of South African economy during apartheid owed much to racial discrimination hindering both human capital formation and growth of demand (Austin 2008, p.23). The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutional frameworks and resource allocation significantly impact long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise role of gender in this dynamic remains a subject of debate. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) argue that African women's contributions have been historically marginalized, while Acemoglu et al. focus on institutional changes as the primary driver of economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The historical evidence of women's employment and education transformations in the U.S., as described by Goldin (2006, p.3), provides a clear measurement type that settles this disagreement. The shift from "jobs" to "careers" for women, which involved significant human capital investment, shows how gender roles can directly influence long-run economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.3).
