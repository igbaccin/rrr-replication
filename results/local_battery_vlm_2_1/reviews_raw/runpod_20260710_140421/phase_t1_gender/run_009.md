The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious area of study, as scholars debate whether gender dynamics are primary drivers or secondary effects of broader economic processes. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societal structures influence economic growth and inequality over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether gender roles and development trajectories are intertwined in causal relationships that reinforce each other, or if they operate independently with only superficial correlations. Another key debate involves the extent to which historical gender dynamics can explain contemporary economic disparities, or if these disparities are better attributed to institutional factors. The interplay between gender and development is complex, as both variables can influence long-term economic outcomes through various mechanisms such as human capital investment, labor market participation, and policy formation. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) highlight that African women played a pivotal role in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.3). The authors emphasize that women's contributions were central to production and reproduction, challenging the marginalization of their roles. Akyeampong and Fofack show that gender discrimination can lower average worker productivity and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). They also note that historical plough use correlates with unequal gender roles today, indicating a persistent impact on contemporary economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p. 10) acknowledge the complexity of this relationship by pointing out that there is a well-established link between gender norms and female labor force participation; however, the origins of these cultural differences remain less understood. Goldin's study on U.S. women workers provides a comparative perspective, noting that many women were piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector with limited opportunities for skill development (Goldin 2006, p.3). This historical context suggests that economic conditions and societal structures shape gender roles, influencing long-term economic outcomes. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for appreciating the multifaceted role of gender in economic development across different regions and time periods.

Kuznets and Mokyr share a focus on technological advancements as a primary driver of economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets posits that modern economic growth hinges on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments it demands. This process involves high aggregate growth rates as well as rapid changes in economic structure, family formation, urbanization, and societal views on individual roles and achievements. These shifts are facilitated by major advancements in transport and communication, which allow for the continuous expansion of modern economic systems into less developed areas (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr complements this perspective by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is a key factor in driving economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This useful knowledge encompasses beliefs about the physical environment and natural phenomena, which societies harness to foster economic growth. The expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all pieces of individual knowledge within a society, has significantly contributed to the increased wealth of Western societies over the past two centuries. Van Zanden builds on this by noting that skill premiums are closely linked to factor endowments and institutional efficiency, reflecting both the abundance of capital and the effectiveness of capital markets. This institutional framework is crucial for determining the demand for skilled labor and the formation of human capital, which in turn influences long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Van Zanden also highlights that low skill premiums often indicate high levels of human capital formation. This observation suggests a complex interplay between technological advancements, institutional efficiency, and human capital development in shaping economic outcomes. The role of gender in this dynamic remains under-explored, particularly how societal structures and economic conditions influence the participation of women in the labor force and their opportunities for skill development.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes, particularly in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. and Sokoloff and Engerman share a focus on the role of inequality in shaping economic institutions. They argue that societies with greater initial inequality tended to develop institutions that concentrated power and wealth, restricting competition and fostering extractive practices. This institutional framework limited opportunities for broader segments of the population, perpetuating inequality over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis shows how colonial policies in New World societies with abundant mineral resources and low human capital led to highly unequal distributions of wealth and income. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, as Acemoglu et al.'s work illustrates how societies with such institutions struggle to industrialize and achieve sustained growth. Austin's critique highlights the complexity of historical trajectories, emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the diverse paths taken by different societies. He notes that systematic racial discrimination and policies hindering human capital formation have also played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Austin 2008, p.23).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) examine economic performance in Africa and Asia, focusing on historical economic growth and divergence. They present an alternative narrative that challenges the conventional view of African economies as perpetually stagnant prior to colonial intervention. Broadberry and Gupta's analysis underscores how pre-colonial economic activities were more robust than previously acknowledged, with trade networks and agricultural productivity playing pivotal roles in shaping regional economies (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.25). This perspective highlights the importance of endogenous factors, such as local institutions and technological adaptations, in driving economic growth. These findings align with Broadberry and Gardner's assertion that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the narrative that European colonial interventions were solely responsible for economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This view is nuanced by the recognition that local economies exhibited resilience; however, broader structural changes and external influences also played significant roles in shaping long-term economic trajectories. This interplay between endogenous development and exogenous shocks underscores the complexity of African economic history and its implications for understanding contemporary economic challenges.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) together show that institutions governing public choice and economic exchange are instrumental in fostering secure property rights. The political commitment to enforcing these rules underpins market development by enabling secure contracting across time and space, which is essential for impersonal exchange and specialization. This institutional framework facilitates long-term economic growth, as it allows for the efficient allocation of resources and encourages investment. McCloskey (2015, p.9) illustrates how ideas and ideologies can both support and hinder this process: even in the presence of strong institutions, cultural attitudes can either foster or impede economic development. The French example demonstrates that reactionary ideologies can stifle growth by prioritizing non-economic goals over market-oriented policies. This ideological influence highlights a crucial condition for sustained economic growth: the alignment of political and cultural factors with institutional frameworks that support market development. North (1989, p. 1) cautions that these institutions are not static; they evolve in response to changes in transaction costs and economic conditions. The dynamic nature of institutions means that their impact on long-run economic outcomes is contingent on continuous adaptation and enforcement.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) share the posture that economic growth during certain historical periods was driven by significant shifts in labor dynamics (Temin 2002, p.4). Temin's analysis of Europe's post-WWII Golden Age highlights a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, which drove rapid economic growth. This misallocation of resources, separate from low income levels that generate conditional convergence, underscores the importance of efficient resource distribution in fostering economic development (Temin 2002, p.9). De Vries argues that household production systems became more capital-intensive, focusing on full-time male workers and shifting market demand towards family-consumed goods (de Vries 1994, p.17). This shift in labor dynamics altered economic structures as well as had profound social implications, such as improved nutrition and higher educational levels. However, Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this optimistic view by noting that despite overall economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined for certain segments of the population due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7). This suggests that while labor dynamics can drive economic growth, the benefits may not be evenly distributed. The uneven distribution of resources and the resulting disparities in health outcomes highlight a critical condition for sustained economic development: equitable access to the fruits of growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) jointly establish that economic policies designed to address distributional conflicts can shape long-run outcomes by influencing investment in human capital. Lindert and Williamson show that the British Kuznets Curve was driven by shifts in occupational structures, with unbalanced productivity advances favoring skills development (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This dynamic is significant because it highlights how changes in economic policies that prioritize certain sectors or skill sets can lead to long-term economic growth. She argues that the core institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were shaped by these conflicts, which in turn affected long-term economic performance. Simson's analysis of post-independence budget policies in East Africa shows that even in contexts marked by significant distributional conflicts, proactive policy agendas can still drive economic outcomes (Simson 2019, p.6). This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a critical factor, the specific policies implemented to address them can mitigate their negative effects on long-run economic performance.

The literature on economic development and standard of living does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This scholarship instead focuses on the relationship between economic growth and changes in living standards. Similarly, Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) found no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia. This suggests that even when economic development occurs, it does not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards. These findings are significant because they challenge optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth. One specific mechanism through which this relationship is mediated is urbanization rates; Pamuk (2007, p.15) highlights how the Black Death led to long-term structural changes that increased urbanisation and altered productivity levels across Europe by 1450. This indicates that the relationship between economic growth and living standards is complex and influenced by various factors beyond just economic performance.

Nunn (2008, p.27) and Stern (2004, p.8); (Nunn 2008, p.27) both argue that historical and environmental factors have significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. Nunn identifies how Africa's slave trades, which spanned from the 16th to the 19th century, had detrimental effects on economic development by creating an environment of insecurity and ethnic fractionalization. This insecure environment constrained people within ethnic boundaries, limiting interaction and cooperation necessary for economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). Stern complements this view by suggesting that specialization in natural resource-intensive activities has been a driver of environmental degradation in middle-income countries. This pattern implies that the long-term effects of historical events like slave trades can be compounded by contemporary economic policies. These adverse outcomes are not inevitable; they depend on how societies adapt to and mitigate these historical legacies (Stern 2004, p.8).

Gender roles have shaped economic outcomes throughout history by influencing human capital investment and labor market participation. The skill premium is a critical factor in long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Akyeampong and Fofack show that women's contributions to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Goldin argues that the transformation of women’s employment and education in the U.S. was a quiet revolution, leading to fundamental changes in societal perceptions of work and identity (Goldin 2006, p.1).

The historical legacy of gender roles persists today through its impact on human capital formation. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack show that cultures with a history of plough use exhibit more unequal gender roles and lower female labor force participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This dynamic is compounded by the fact that societies with extractive institutions often fail to invest in the education of the masses. Acemoglu et al. argue that this lack of investment in human capital for the majority population is a direct result of institutional frameworks that concentrate power and resources in the hands of a small elite (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The resulting inequality in wealth distribution further entrenches historical gender roles, as these elites maintain control over economic opportunities.

The interplay between institutions and human capital development highlights the enduring effects of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework perpetuated inequality as well as shaped gender roles by limiting access to education and economic opportunities for women.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes is further underscored by the work of Lindert and Williamson. They argue that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality, highlighting how changes in the structure of pay by skill can drive both (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This perspective aligns with van Zanden’s findings on the link between the skill premium and institutional efficiency, showing that a low skill premium generally reflects a high level of human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.25).

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is grounded in the historical legacy of institutions and human capital investment. The shared finding is that unequal access to education and economic opportunities has perpetuated historical gender roles, leading to persistent inequality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism; geographic factors are not the cause (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from a measurement of human capital investment and its distribution across societies. This empirical comparison shows that societies with extractive institutions fail to invest in the education of the majority population, thereby perpetuating inequality and entrenching historical gender roles.
