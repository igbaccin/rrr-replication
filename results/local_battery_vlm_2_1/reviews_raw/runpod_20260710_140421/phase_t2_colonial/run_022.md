The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding whether and how the legacy of colonial rule continues to influence contemporary economic development in Africa. This debate centers around two main positions. One position argues that colonial institutions were extractive and detrimental to long-term economic growth, while the other suggests that the impact of these institutions has been overstated or mitigated by other factors such as cultural attitudes toward trust. The question is not whether colonialism had an effect on Africa but rather what specific mechanisms, be they extractive institutions, cultural legacies, or other factors, have exerted the most significant influence on Africa's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories through the establishment of extractive systems. Extractive institutions were more profitable in densely populated areas where there was ample labor to exploit, such as in Meso-America and Africa. In regions with high population density, Europeans found it profitable to implement forced labor systems and slave trade networks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The profitability of these extractive institutions led to their widespread adoption, which in turn hindered economic development by preventing the accumulation of human capital. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further explain that extractive institutions delayed industrialization by limiting access to education for the masses, thereby perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment. This dynamic underscores how colonial institutions extracted resources and systematically impeded the development of local human capital, a crucial factor in long-term economic growth. In this way, the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence contemporary African economies by constraining their ability to invest in education and industrialization.

The persistence of these extractive policies long after colonial rule ended further solidified their impact on economic trajectories. For example, Latin America's post-independence era saw the continuation of monopolies and regulations established by Spain, which hindered economic progress for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

This institutional framework, characterized by extractive practices, stifled immediate economic growth and laid the groundwork for enduring structural inequalities.

The economic history of Africa and institutional reforms offers insights into development challenges that are relevant to understanding African economic growth trajectories, but it does not directly address the role of colonial institutions in shaping these trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This is because it provides a nuanced view of how colonial rule interacted with pre-existing African institutions, revealing that the impact varied over time. For example, Hopkins explains that during the early phase of mercantilist imperialism, productive institutions were scarce even in Europe; therefore, they could not have been established overseas (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This aligns with Olsson's argument that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle unproductive institutions already in place. The extractive nature of early colonial institutions was a consequence of historical constraints. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive options were available (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This temporal shift is crucial because it highlights that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories was not uniform throughout the colonial period. The extractive policies implemented during this later phase had enduring effects on economic development, as they entrenched structural inequalities that persisted long after independence. These impacts were mediated by pre-colonial conditions and patterns of settlement (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

Economic performance under colonial rule in Africa was influenced by land scarcity and infrastructure development (Austin 2007, p.16; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Austin (2007, p.16) shows that Ghana's shift from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 led to institutional changes in factor markets, as evidenced by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transformation was driven by increasing population density and the intensification of cocoa cultivation, which created pressure on available land resources. As a result, localised scarcity prompted the development of new institutions that facilitated more efficient use of land and labour. Broadberry and Gardner support this finding by highlighting the significant improvements in infrastructure during this period, including the tripling of motorable roads and the mechanisation of goods transport. This infrastructural development was crucial for enhancing economic performance by reducing transportation costs and improving market access (Austin 2007, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema's (2012) work on British rule in sub-Saharan Africa indicates that infrastructure development was important while the colonial education system also played a pivotal role. The British colonial administration welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their indirect rule system, which led to higher primary-school enrolment rates compared to French colonies (Frankema 2012, p.4). This suggests that educational systems under colonial rule had significant downstream implications for economic growth trajectories, as they shaped human capital development and influenced the long-term productivity of African economies. By fostering both infrastructure development and education, British colonial institutions laid the groundwork for sustained economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Bryceson (2002, p.11) show that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that the systematic codification of patriarchy under colonial rule, often struck through alliances between colonial administrations and local male leaders, led to significant disadvantages for women across Africa. This patriarchal codification is evident in the "patchwork-quilt of patriarchies" described by Belinda Bozzoli (1983) in segregationist South Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Bryceson's analysis of rural labor markets and food security further illustrates this dynamic, noting that as land availability contracts, nonagricultural activities become crucial for poverty alleviation (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Bryceson 2002, p.5). This shift highlights the broader socio-economic changes driven by economic restructuring. The implications extend to contemporary African economies where women's roles are increasingly vital in non-agricultural sectors, reflecting a shift from traditional gender dynamics under colonial rule to more diversified economic participation today.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, as a key driver of reduced transaction costs and enhanced security of property rights over time. This institutional development was instrumental in facilitating long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.3) further underscores that despite potential opportunism and cheating, complex institutions evolve to manage intricate interdependence, suggesting a non-linear but inevitable progression towards more sophisticated institutional frameworks as societies develop. The transition from basic to advanced institutions is not automatic; it requires overcoming significant hurdles, including the need for third-party enforcement mechanisms. This nuanced view on institutional development in Europe underscores that while financial and political institutions were critical for economic growth, their evolution was contingent on addressing specific challenges posed by interdependence and opportunism.

The slave trade had profound and lasting impacts on African economic development through its influence on institutional and cultural legacies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and significantly affects growth trajectories. This mistrust has tangible economic consequences: it impedes social cooperation essential for collective action and economic development. This trust deficit operates through multiple channels, both internal, such as reduced community cohesion, and external, like diminished international trade partnerships (Nunn 2008, p.28). The internal channel, in particular, is at least twice as impactful as the external one, underscoring the deep-seated nature of this institutional legacy (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.19).

Nunn (2008, p.28) further elaborates on how the slave trade's adverse effects are rooted in the disruption of precolonial institutions. This institutional underdevelopment had long-term repercussions, as it hindered the capacity of African societies to adapt and grow in response to external economic pressures. The legacy of mistrust and weakened institutions thus created a vicious cycle where limited trust further stymied institutional development, perpetuating economic underperformance.

Herbst (2000) also highlights that Africa's poor economic performance is deeply linked to the underdevelopment and instability of precolonial polities (Nunn 2008, p.28). The slave trade exacerbated this by destroying existing social structures and preventing the emergence of strong, centralized states capable of providing public goods and infrastructure. This institutional fragmentation continues to affect contemporary African economies, as societies struggle to overcome the legacy of mistrust and weak governance.

The impact of the slave trade is particularly important because it has left lasting effects on trust within African societies. The effects of colonial institutions on economic growth are further compounded by the persistence of extractive policies long after independence. Young emphasized that the control structures set up in nonsettler colonies during the colonial era persisted, and these extractive institutions formed the basis of current-day governance in many African countries (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of these extractive institutions has had a significant impact on economic growth by limiting the ability of societies to provide public goods and infrastructure. This institutional continuity shows that the legacy of colonial rule is still felt today, as societies struggle to overcome the challenges posed by weak governance and mistrust.

The underlying point of agreement among scholars is that colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). There remains disagreement over whether colonial rulers had enough time to dismantle unproductive institutions already in place. Hopkins argues that colonial rulers did not have sufficient time to dismantle these unproductive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.13), while Acemoglu et al. contend that extractive regimes were entrenched during the colonial period and persisted afterwards (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This disagreement is settled by historical evidence showing that extractive policies remained intact after independence, as seen in Latin America where monopolies and regulations created by Spain persisted for most of the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).
