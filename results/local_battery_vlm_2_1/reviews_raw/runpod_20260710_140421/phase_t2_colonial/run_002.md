The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. The question is intellectually significant because it addresses the persistence of poverty and underdevelopment on the continent. Scholars disagree over whether colonial institutions have had a lasting negative impact on Africa's economic performance, or if other factors play a more decisive role. Some argue that extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers stifled economic growth and development in African colonies. Others contend that these institutional legacies are not as significant as other factors such as geography, culture, or contemporary policy choices. The debate hinges on the relative importance of colonial institutions versus alternative explanations for Africa's economic trajectory. These arguments have profound implications for understanding the long-term effects of colonialism and informing policies aimed at fostering sustainable development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were more profitable in densely settled areas where there was more to be extracted by European colonists. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who highlights that causal relationships are complex and path-dependent, suggesting that colonial legacies have shaped Africa's economic trajectory through a variety of mechanisms. Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) extends this view by noting the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, indicating that the type of colonial institutions varied over time and had different impacts on African economies. This historical context is crucial for understanding how extractive institutions were established and maintained in various regions.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.8) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8) argument is also supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This suggests that while colonial institutions may have been extractive, they did not necessarily prevent economic growth entirely. However, Acemoglu et al.'s argument is challenged by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3; 2011, p.4), who contend that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This perspective indicates that cultural factors, along with institutional legacies, significantly influence Africa's economic trajectory.

These institutions were established in response to economic profitability and the availability of labor. This persistence shows how colonial institutions have both shaped and limited economic growth trajectories by preserving exploitative systems. Acemoglu et al. also observe that while extractive institutions hindered industrialization, they did not completely halt economic development. In fact, the persistence of these institutions into the independence era is evident in the enduring policies and regulations inherited from colonial powers, which continue to influence economic growth trajectories today.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Bryceson (2002, p.11) both show that colonial rule in Africa facilitated significant educational development and social changes, with British rule promoting formal education through coordinated missionary efforts (Frankema 2012, p.15; Bryceson 2002, p.11). British policies welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule. This approach differed markedly from the French model, where restrictions on Christian missions hindered educational growth, resulting in lower primary-school enrolment rates in French colonies compared to British ones by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). The divergence in educational trajectories between French and British colonies underscores how colonial policies shaped access to education. These educational developments were intertwined with broader social changes, including depeasantization and the rise of non-agricultural activities in rural areas. Bryceson (2002, p.11) highlights that as rural populations expanded and land availability contracted, nonagricultural activities became increasingly important for rural poverty alleviation (Bryceson 2002, p.11). This shift indicates that colonial economic restructuring altered agricultural landscapes and sparked profound social changes. These changes were not always beneficial; recurrent food shortages and increased reliance on food aid in regions like Ethiopia and Zimbabwe reveal the complex and often harmful effects of colonial policies on rural livelihoods (Bryceson 2002, p.5).

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This critique is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's findings on levels of per capita income above subsistence in many Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century. The notion that colonial institutions are solely detrimental to economic growth overlooks the nuanced historical dynamics at play. For instance, the introduction of infrastructure such as railroads and ports facilitated trade and commerce, which had positive effects on local economies. These developments were unevenly distributed and not universally beneficial; regions with extractive industries often experienced concentrated wealth accumulation without broader economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner maintain that enhancing long-term performance in Africa involves reducing the rate of decline as well as focusing on growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This insight underscores a critical point: the impact of colonial institutions on African economies is complex and multifaceted, involving both growth-enhancing and stagnating factors.

The adverse effect of Africa's slave trades is proposed as an explanation for African underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn argue that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. This mistrust hinders economic development by reducing social capital and impeding cooperative behavior essential for growth (Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn further argues that the slave trades had adverse effects on Africa's economic development. The historical dependence on fishing and other activities correlated with distance from the coast are significant (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3) and show the importance of considering how institutions and practices were shaped by these historical events. The presence of centralized precolonial state institutions in some African countries today provides more public goods, such as education, health, and infrastructure (Nunn 2008, p.28). This suggests that the legacy of slave trades is not uniform across Africa but varies based on pre-existing institutional structures.

Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) echo this view by noting that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments; however, these impediments are not necessarily the product of colonial institutions. The presence of extractive regimes under colonial rule, as argued by Acemoglu et al., did not always result in entrenched non-productive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.13). Instead, Hopkins suggests that colonial rulers often lacked sufficient time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions rather than introducing new ones (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This implies that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development may have been less direct and more contingent on pre-colonial conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that varying degrees of income inequality, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans, played a significant role in shaping long-run trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The differences in wage income growth across regions indicate that the path-dependence nature of African economic development is more complex than often assumed. Future research should focus on explaining these varied long-term growth trajectories to understand better how colonial institutions influenced Africa's economic landscape.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive institutions that persisted long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.13). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these extractive institutions were designed to benefit colonial powers at the expense of local populations, leading to significant income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of such institutions is evident in the enduring economic inequalities observed today.

The legacy of colonialism extends beyond immediate economic exploitation. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies that continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact complements the direct extractive policies, creating a multifaceted barrier to economic development. The combination of institutional frameworks and cultural legacies underscores the deep-rooted nature of colonial influences on Africa's economic trajectory.

Colonial institutions also shaped African economies through their impact on human capital development. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions hindered education and skill acquisition, which are crucial for industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This lack of investment in human capital further exacerbated the economic disparities and slowed long-term growth. Broadberry and Gardner provide a nuanced view by showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, indicating that while colonial institutions were extractive, they did not entirely stifle economic activity (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17).

The differing paths of economic development in Africa highlight the complexity of colonial institutional legacies. Austin argues for a more differentiated view of causal relationships, suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the historical processes at play (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who points out the disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, each with distinct institutional outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.12).

There is broad agreement that colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions were more profitable for Europeans in high-density population areas (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Austin disagrees with the simplification of causal relationships, arguing instead that these processes are more differentiated and complex than often assumed (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence of varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans settles this disagreement. This evidence shows that the path-dependence nature of African economic development is influenced by a multitude of factors beyond institutional legacies alone.
