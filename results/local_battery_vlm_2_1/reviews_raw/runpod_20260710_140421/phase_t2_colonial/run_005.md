The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that engages scholars across multiple disciplines. At stake are the fundamental mechanisms driving long-term economic development and the legacy of historical policies on contemporary outcomes. One live disagreement centers around the persistence and impact of extractive institutions established during colonial rule. Some argue that these institutions have had enduring negative effects on economic growth, while others contend that the causal relationships are more complex and multifaceted. The debate also involves the interplay between geographic factors, human capital, and institutional frameworks in determining economic trajectories.

For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high-mortality areas more likely to develop extractive institutions that persist to this day. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which continues to affect economic development. These perspectives underscore the enduring influence of colonial-era policies on contemporary African economies.

The debate over the causal mechanisms linking colonial institutions to economic growth is far from settled, as scholars continue to refine their theories and methodologies.

Acemoglu et al argue that these causal mechanisms have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The profitability of extractive institutions, which Europeans often established in high population density areas, played a crucial role. In densely settled regions, the presence of abundant labor made it profitable for European colonists to implement forced labor systems and other exploitative practices (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional framework, which prioritized resource extraction over local economic development, had long-lasting effects on African economies.

The persistence of these extractive institutions beyond the colonial era is a key factor in understanding contemporary African economic conditions. The continuity of control structures set up during colonialism ensured that extractive policies remained intact even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). For instance, Latin American countries inherited and maintained monopolies and regulations created by Spain long after gaining independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional persistence highlights how colonial-era policies continue to influence economic outcomes in Africa. The impact of these institutions varies across regions. Acemoglu et al note that the correlation between extractive institutions and human capital levels suggests that education was often restricted under such regimes, which further delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This underscores how colonial institutions have shaped African economic trajectories by limiting both immediate resource development and long-term human capital investment. The enduring legacy of these institutions is evident in the persistent challenges faced by many African economies today.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) both critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They argue that examining growth theories against long-term history reveals relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence, but also suggests more differentiated causal relationships than previously recognized (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.16). This perspective highlights the importance of considering localised scarcity as a key driver in institutional changes. Austin (2007, p.16) demonstrates this through the case of Ghana during 1874-1939, where land abundance shifted to localised scarcity, leading to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.16). This shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The mechanization of goods transport further underscored this transition, as the length of motorable roads tripled from 1874-1939, facilitating more efficient cocoa export transportation (Austin 2007, p.8). Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies that while these changes were significant, they did not uniformly benefit all regions or social groups. This nuanced view suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform and varies depending on local conditions and historical paths.

The slave trade had a profound impact on African economic development, creating lasting institutional and cultural legacies that continue to affect growth trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This suggests that the institutional legacies of the slave trade are deeply intertwined with Africa’s economic trajectories.

This mistrust and institutional underdevelopment have significant implications for contemporary African economies. For instance, trust is crucial for economic development (Nunn 2008, p.28), as it facilitates cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and enhances market efficiency (Nunn 2008, p.28). The legacy of mistrust created by the slave trade continues to hinder these processes, making it difficult for African countries to fully leverage their economic potential. This underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on Africa's growth trajectories.

Hopkins and others (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) argue that economic history in Africa can inform institutional reforms but do not directly address how colonial institutions explain African growth trajectories. Both the historical context of colonialism and the timing of its implementation were pivotal factors influencing the trajectory of African economies. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions coexisted in Europe. This period marked a shift where medical advances enabled European settlement in Africa and altered the dynamics of colonial policy (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This does not imply that the transition to productive institutions was immediate or straightforward. Olsson's argument suggests that colonial rulers did not have sufficient time to dismantle existing unproductive institutions, which complicates the direct linkage between colonial rule and institutional change in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

Hopkins (2019, p.11) notes that poverty in Africa is a historical creation rather than an original condition. This perspective aligns with the broader understanding that European capitalism played a significant role in creating underdevelopment through networks of dependence and unequal exchange (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This historical context does not negate the potential for institutional reforms to address contemporary economic challenges. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape African economies today, but recognizing this history also opens avenues for targeted interventions that can mitigate its enduring effects.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) both find that British colonial rule supported the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa. The British approach to governance through indirect rule allowed for the integration of pre-colonial institutions into the colonial administration. This integration facilitated the development of infrastructure and social services such as education and healthcare.

The educational policies of colonial powers had profound implications for economic development, with British colonies experiencing higher enrollment rates in primary school programs compared to French colonies (Frankema 2012, p.4). The differing educational trajectories can be attributed to the varying degrees of support provided by these colonial powers. For instance, the difference in government expenditure per student was significant, highlighting the differential investment in education across these regions. This disparity in educational development has lasting effects on economic growth, as higher enrollment rates and greater investment in education during the colonial period correlate with better economic outcomes in post-colonial Africa.

These frameworks influenced both immediate educational outcomes and long-term economic development. The legacy of these policies persists, influencing contemporary African economies by determining the availability of skilled labor and the capacity for innovation.

This perspective provides a lens through which to examine the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories, as it highlights the importance of cultural factors beyond mere institutional frameworks. The ideological underpinnings of colonial powers significantly influenced their approaches to governance and development in Africa. For instance, McCloskey (2015, p.9) "Franco-French War" illustrates how deeply entrenched ideologies can hinder economic growth by prioritizing cultural and religious agendas over material progress. This dynamic is not confined to Europe; similar ideological struggles played out in colonial Africa, where the imposition of foreign values and institutions often clashed with local cultures. Bryant (2006, p.12) further underscores this point by noting that European political institutions, which were seen as advantageous for modernization, did not necessarily translate into comparable benefits when imposed on non-European societies. The ideological underpinnings of colonial rule in Africa thus created a complex interplay between imported institutions and local conditions, shaping economic trajectories in ways that were both direct and indirect. This interplay highlights the need to consider the multifaceted nature of colonial legacies, where institutional frameworks are just one piece of a larger puzzle.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, primarily through their extractive nature, which was established to maximize European profits (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). Acemoglu et al. argue that the high population density and abundant labor supply in Africa made it profitable for Europeans to implement forced labor systems and other extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This extractive framework persisted long after colonial rule ended, as seen in Latin America where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact well into the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of these institutions highlights their enduring impact on economic development. Moreover, Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development due to the mistrust they fostered within African societies (Nunn 2008, p.28; Nunn 2008, p.19).

The continuity of colonial legacies is further emphasized by Hopkins, who points out that the distinction between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism is crucial in understanding the institutional frameworks imposed during different phases of colonial rule. This differentiation underscores how early colonial periods lacked productive institutions, while later phases saw both productive and extractive options (Hopkins 2009, p.12). Austin also acknowledges the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur due to path dependence. The causal mechanisms involved in Africa's economic trajectory are more complex than previously recognized, involving differentiated historical paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced view highlights the multifaceted nature of colonial legacies and their impact on African economies.
