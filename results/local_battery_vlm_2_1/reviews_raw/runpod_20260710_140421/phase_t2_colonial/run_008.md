The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because this question bears on our understanding of Africa's economic development and the long-term impacts of colonial rule. One position posits that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in creating extractive structures that persist today, hindering economic growth. Another perspective argues that the causal relationships involved are more complex and differentiated than previously recognized, suggesting that other factors besides colonial institutions may have significantly influenced Africa's economic trajectory. This debate revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions can be held responsible for Africa’s current economic challenges or if a broader set of historical and contextual factors should also be considered.

The first stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of extractive institutions established by European colonists, arguing that these institutions were deliberately designed to exploit resources and labor. Acemoglu et al. contend that in areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions to maximize short-term gains at the expense of long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These extractive institutions persisted even after independence, creating a legacy of underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.13). This perspective is supported by evidence that shows how densely populated areas in Africa were particularly targeted for exploitative practices due to the availability of labor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America, for instance, facilitated the establishment of forced labor systems. Similarly, high population density in Africa created profitable opportunities for slave traders supplying labor to American plantations. The correlation between institutions and human capital reflects that extractive institutions typically prevented masses from obtaining education, thereby delaying industrialization (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The profitability of extractive institutions was a key determinant in their establishment; high population density areas were particularly targeted for exploitation due to the availability of labor. Acemoglu et al. found that "the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America, for instance, facilitated the establishment of forced labor systems" (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Similarly, Africa's relatively high population density created a profit opportunity for slave traders supplying labor to American plantations. This dynamic highlights how colonial powers strategically implemented extractive institutions where they could maximize profits by exploiting local resources and labor.

The enduring nature of these institutions means that many African countries continue to grapple with the legacy of colonial exploitation, which has hindered industrialization and economic growth. The persistence of extractive institutions in Africa has also been a primary mechanism through which human capital development was delayed (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Extractive institutions typically prevented masses from obtaining education, thereby delaying industrialization. This delay in human capital development has had profound implications for economic growth in African countries, as educated populations are crucial for driving innovation and productivity gains.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). The differing educational trajectories between French and British colonies are clearly illustrated by the primary-school enrolment rates, which were roughly half as high in French West Africa compared to British West Africa in 1938. This disparity can be attributed to the fact that the British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, while the French restricted the activities of Christian missions (Frankema 2012, p.4). However, the implications of these educational disparities are qualified by Frankema's (2012, p.15) observation that education alone does not ensure economic growth. The Kenyan case demonstrates that differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth were influenced by varying degrees of income inequality and differing types of economic dynamics (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

The institutional revolution in factor markets, such as land use rights and hired labour, significantly altered Ghana's economic landscape during 1874-1939 (Austin 2007, p.16; Austin 2008, p.1). This transformation was driven by the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which necessitated new institutions to manage resources efficiently. The emergence of markets in land use rights and hired labour facilitated greater economic activity and productivity. For instance, the rapid expansion of motorable roads and the increased use of lorries for goods transport exemplify how infrastructure development complemented institutional changes (Austin 2007, p.8; Austin 2008, p.2). These developments were crucial as they enhanced connectivity and reduced transportation costs, thereby integrating local markets more effectively into the broader economy.

Hopkins and Austin's work (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) shows how colonial institutions were shaped by the evolving nature of imperialism in Africa. They argue that early phases of colonial rule did not focus on establishing productive institutions, as these had scarcely developed even in Europe at the time; disease constraints also influenced policy outcomes. Hopkins points out that by the late 19th century colonial rulers had both productive and extractive institutional options available to them. This period saw medical advances that reduced disease constraints on European settlement, allowing for more deliberate institutional choices to be made. The implication is that later colonial policies could have been more transformative if they had aimed at fostering productive institutions rather than merely extracting resources. However, Hopkins (2019, p.11) cautions against over-reliance on historical determinism, noting the risks of compressing complex histories into simplistic narratives. This warning underscores the need for nuanced analysis that recognizes the multifaceted influences shaping African economic trajectories.

The economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has catalyzed significant social changes and depeasantization (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.17). This transformation is evident in the shift from traditional agrarian societies to more diversified economies where nonagricultural activities play a crucial role in rural poverty alleviation. For instance, local casual wage labor markets have emerged, reducing reliance on family labor and creating new economic opportunities (Bryceson 2002, p.11). These changes are not merely economic but also reflect deeper social transformations that affect governance and democratization. The extent to which these shifts contribute to sustained economic growth remains debated. The revisionist view, which dismisses Eurocentric perspectives, suggests that major Eurasian societies were progressing along comparable paths to Europe. Yet, Bryant (2006, p.17) argues that this perspective overlooks the critical role of patrimonial systems of domination and the institutional constraints they impose (Bryant 2006, p.12). This implies that while economic restructuring can drive social changes, the effectiveness of these transformations in fostering long-term growth depends on the quality of governance and the institutional environment.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) show that this mistrust is a direct result of the historical impact of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment where trust was difficult to establish. This institutional legacy has profound implications for economic development, as it creates an environment where cooperation and collective action are hindered. Nunn (2008, p.28) further elaborates on this by emphasizing that the slave trades had adverse effects on Africa's economic development, highlighting how precolonial institutions were undermined by these historical events. This institutional decay is not limited to economic structures but also affects social and political frameworks, making it difficult for contemporary societies to rebuild trust and foster sustainable growth. The impact of mistrust extends beyond economic interactions; it permeates social relationships and political institutions, creating a pervasive environment that inhibits progress. Consequently, the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape African economies by limiting the ability of societies to leverage collective action and cooperation effectively.

Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by introducing and maintaining extractive structures that persist to this day. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions were more profitable for European colonizers in densely populated areas, leading to the establishment of systems like forced labor and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This profitability drove the Europeans to implement these institutions, which in turn hindered long-term economic development by limiting human capital accumulation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Nunn and Wantchekon further illustrate how the slave trade, a direct outcome of these extractive policies, fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic interactions within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The persistence of these institutions is evident in their continuity from colonial times into post-independence eras. Acemoglu et al. highlight that the extractive structures set up by colonizers remained intact long after independence, forming the basis of current-day governance and economic policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional persistence is a key factor in understanding why many African countries continue to struggle with economic development. Nunn's work on the adverse effects of slave trades complements this perspective by showing that precolonial state institutions, which were often disrupted by colonial rule, played a crucial role in facilitating trade and economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). The disruption of these institutions further exacerbated the negative impact of extractive policies.

Moreover, the historical context of colonialism reveals that different phases of imperialism had varying impacts on institutional development. Hopkins points out that early mercantilist forms of colonialism did not offer productive institutions as an option, but by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This differentiation is important because it shows that the type of colonial rule and its timing significantly influenced the institutional frameworks established in Africa. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the complexity of these historical processes, arguing that causal relationships are more differentiated than often recognized (Austin 2008, p.2).

The slave trade's role in shaping African economies is particularly noteworthy. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the internal effects of the slave trade, such as the development of a culture of mistrust, are at least twice as large as the external effects (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust permeates social relationships and political institutions, creating an environment that inhibits progress and collective action. The legacy of the slave trade is thus deeply intertwined with the institutional decay observed in many African societies today.

The precise historical evidence that settles these disagreements lies in the measurement of trust levels within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This empirical finding underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic growth trajectories, highlighting how historical events continue to shape current economic realities.
