The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that engages scholars from multiple disciplines. At stake intellectually are the underlying causes of Africa's economic stagnation and underdevelopment, which have persisted despite independence from colonial rule. The live disagreement centers on whether colonial institutions were the primary drivers of Africa’s current economic state or if other factors such as geography, culture, and post-colonial policies played a more significant role. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive in nature, created enduring barriers to economic development by restricting opportunities for local populations to accumulate human capital and engage in productive activities. Other researchers posit that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, suggesting instead that Africa's economic trajectory was influenced by a complex interplay of factors including pre-colonial conditions, post-independence policies, and external shocks.

The extractive nature of colonial institutions is central to understanding their enduring impact on African economies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the profitability of extractive institutions influenced European colonizers' choices in establishing governance structures. In regions with high population density, such as Meso-America and Africa, Europeans found it profitable to implement forced labor systems and slave trades respectively. This institutional framework persisted even after independence, perpetuating economic underdevelopment by limiting access to education and productive opportunities for the local populations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elucidate this dynamic, highlighting that institutions in societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, thereby hindering economic growth.

The historical persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in post-colonial African states. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) provide examples where monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers remained intact long after independence. This institutional continuity suggests that the legacy of colonial rule extends beyond immediate economic impacts to shape long-term development trajectories. The persistence of extractive policies into the independence era underscores the deep-rooted nature of these institutions, which continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2).

They assert that extractive institutions such as monopolies and forced labor systems were particularly profitable in areas with high population density. These policies were implemented where Europeans could exploit abundant local labor for agriculture or mining, further reinforcing the economic viability of such regimes. For example, in Meso-America, the presence of numerous Amerindian workers facilitated the establishment of forced labor systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Similarly, in Africa, dense populations provided profit opportunities for slave traders supplying labor to American plantations.

The persistence of these extractive institutions beyond the colonial era has had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. note that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not obtain education, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This lack of human capital development is a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions have hindered growth. The continuation of these policies post-independence underscores their enduring impact on contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) jointly demonstrate that historical institutional changes in Africa are marked by differentiated causal relationships that are best understood through long-term history. These authors show that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced approach to understanding economic growth trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.16). For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transformation was driven by population growth and economic development, which created new demands for land and labour. The emergence of these markets facilitated agricultural intensification and commercialization, as seen in the expansion of cocoa production. However, this process also revealed tensions between traditional institutions and emerging market forces, with localised scarcity prompting policy shifts such as bans on new farms. The length of motorable roads tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles during 1930-32, reflecting the mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana (Austin 2007, p.8). These changes underscore how historical institutional shifts can have profound implications for economic development, even when they do not directly address colonial institutions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.9), along with North (1989, p.9), highlight the pivotal role of secure property rights and financial innovations in driving long-term economic success. This institutional shift fostered a robust capital market, which was essential for sustained economic growth. The implication is that these secure institutions were necessary for fostering both political freedom and economic prosperity. North (1989, p.9) also notes that the development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is not automatic (North 1989, p.3). This observation suggests that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, the process is contingent on specific historical and political contexts. The evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights and financial innovations can catalyze long-term economic success.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Bryant 2006, p.17) share a focus on how inequality and institutional frameworks influence economic development. They argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality often favor elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The implications of this institutional framework are significant: when elites control key resources and opportunities, they can maintain their status over time, but at the expense of broader economic growth. This pattern suggests that institutional design plays a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Bryant (2006, p.12) extends this perspective by highlighting how patrimonial systems of domination typically held sway in the East. These systems, characterized by authoritarian and consultative arrangements, often limited the development of mercantile orders (Bryant 2006, p.17). The institutional structures that emerged under these conditions were not conducive to modern economic growth, as they favored elite control over resources and restricted broader economic participation. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in determining economic outcomes, even if the specific mechanisms differ across regions.

The role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories is further complicated by the historical context in which they develop. For instance, the persistence of patrimonial systems in some societies can be traced back to historical patterns of elite dominance and the lack of competitive pressures that might have driven institutional change (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This historical contingency means that understanding the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth requires a nuanced analysis of how these institutions emerged and evolved within specific contexts.

Hopkins and others have shown that the economic history of Africa offers insights into poverty reduction (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights how the early colonial period lacked productive institutions both in Europe and overseas, making the choice between extractive and productive regimes a later development. This temporal distinction is crucial for understanding the varied institutional legacies of colonialism across different regions and periods. The implication here is that the timing and nature of colonial institutions significantly influenced their impact on African economic growth trajectories.

The nuanced analysis of colonial institutions’ role in shaping African economic development underscores the importance of historical context. Hopkins (2019, p.11) argues that the use of instrumental variables in studying path dependence can oversimplify complex historical processes, leading to deterministic views that overlook intervening influences. This critique aligns with the broader theme of institutional change and its impact on economic trajectories. For instance, the idea that poverty was a historical creation rather than an original condition (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This perspective suggests that understanding the historical roots of underdevelopment can inform current policy recommendations aimed at reducing poverty.

The detrimental impact of the slave trade on African economic development was independent of colonial institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) found that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa. This culture persists to this day, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The internal channel through which this mistrust operates is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The adverse effects of the slave trades include broader economic and institutional structures that hinder development in addition to a decline in trust. Nunn (2008, p.28) argues that the selection processes during the slave trades were highly selective, favoring societies with more developed institutions that could facilitate trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.28; Nunn 2008, p.19). This selection process did not uniformly benefit all regions; it often led to the exploitation and underdevelopment of those areas most affected by the slave trade. This historical legacy of mistrust and institutional underdevelopment continues to shape Africa's economic trajectory, highlighting the enduring impact of the slave trade on contemporary development challenges.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that persist to this day. The extractive nature of many colonial institutions has been particularly detrimental. High population density and abundant labor supply made extractive institutions more profitable for European colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This profit-driven approach led to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trades, which not only exploited African societies but also hindered their economic development by preventing the accumulation of human capital and other productive investments. The persistence of these extractive institutions well into the post-colonial era has been well-documented (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional legacy has significantly affected current income per capita in many African countries, highlighting how colonial policies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The historical context of colonialism reveals a complex interplay between different forms of imperialism and their impact on institutional development. The transition from mercantilist to modern forms of imperialism introduced new dynamics that shaped the institutions established during colonial rule (Hopkins 2009, p.12). For example, by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were available options for European colonizers, influencing the type of governance structures they implemented in Africa. This period also saw medical advances that removed some constraints on European settlement, further altering the institutional landscape (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

The role of colonial institutions involves both their immediate economic impact and their long-term effects on societal trust and cohesion. The slave trade, for instance, fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This deep-seated mistrust has broader implications for economic development, as it affects social capital and the ability of communities to collaborate effectively. The historical dependence on fishing and other factors correlated with distance from the coast further illustrates how colonial policies shaped economic activities and institutional frameworks in specific regions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3).

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that colonial institutions have had a lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories, particularly through the establishment of extractive systems that persist to this day (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether colonial rulers actively entrenched extractive regimes or simply did not have enough time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions. This debate is settled by the historical evidence showing that colonial rulers often had sufficient time and resources to implement new governance structures, particularly in areas with high population density and abundant labor supply (Hopkins 2009, p.12). For example, the persistence of extractive policies in Latin America, such as monopolies and regulations created by Spain, remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8), illustrating how colonial institutions were actively maintained and reinforced.
