The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake intellectually is the extent to which historical institutional legacies constrain or enable contemporary economic performance. Colonial institutions are seen as either enduring features that perpetuate inequality and underdevelopment, or as adaptable frameworks that can be reformed for growth. The disagreement centers on whether colonial institutions primarily acted as extractive mechanisms that hindered development, or if they were more complex and context-dependent, with some elements potentially fostering growth under certain conditions. Colonial institutions are argued to have persisted long after independence, shaping current economic outcomes through their impact on human capital and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Austin 2008, p.2). The persistence of these institutions raises questions about the mechanisms through which they continue to influence economic development in Africa today.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The profitability of extractive institutions was influenced by population density and labor availability; high-density areas offered more to be extracted, making such institutions profitable for European colonists. This created a vicious cycle where extractive institutions hindered human capital development, as masses under these systems typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This low level of human capital delayed industrialization in Africa, as it was a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions impeded economic growth.

Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg jointly suggest that the economic history of Africa is marked by significant periods of institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Hopkins discusses the evolution of colonial institutions in Africa. He argues that during the early phases of colonialism, there was little choice between productive and extractive institutions because neither existed at a meaningful level. By the late nineteenth century, both options were available, enabling more deliberate institutional choices (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is important as it shows how African economic development can be understood as a series of institutional changes over time, influenced by colonial institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg add to this perspective by noting that the impact of colonialism has been relatively small due to the short period of effective European occupation compared to precolonial slave trading (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, their influence was not as persistent or uniform as often assumed. Frankema and van Waijenburg further elaborate on this by highlighting the need for future research to focus more on differences in long-term growth trajectories within Africa. This suggests that economic development in Africa is shaped by diverse factors over time, including colonial institutions.

The examination of historical economic changes and institutional shifts in Africa reveals that these transformations are complex processes shaped by diverse factors over time (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin (2008, p.1) argues for the need to understand growth theories within long-term histories. This approach highlights recurring relationships due to similar situations or path dependence, emphasizing that causal relationships are more differentiated than previously recognized. This shift was driven by the increasing demand for cocoa exports, which necessitated more efficient transportation infrastructure. The length of motorable roads tripled from 1921 to 1930, facilitating the mechanization of goods transport and integrating local markets more closely with global trade networks (Austin 2007, p.8). This institutional revolution in factor markets underscores how economic changes can drive structural shifts even in the absence of direct colonial intervention. Austin (2007, p.16) also notes that these developments varied across regions; for example, while Ashanti faced land scarcity by the 1930s, it was still dependent on cocoa income and struggled to implement effective policies (Austin 2007, p.16). This variability suggests that economic changes in Africa are highly contextual, shaped by local conditions and historical trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Pamuk (2007, p.16) argue that historical inequalities shaped by institutional development have long-term economic consequences. The differentials in paths of development are influenced by institutions that determine growth-relevant conditions such as property rights security, corruption prevalence, financial sector structures, infrastructure investment, social capital, and entrepreneurial inclination. This perspective is echoed by Pamuk (2007, p.21), who highlights how the Black Death triggered long-term structural changes that led to significant economic divergence in Europe. These structural changes included shifts in urbanization rates, which are key indicators of productivity and economic development. The institutional framework that emerged from these historical events played a crucial role in maintaining or altering economic trajectories. This institutional path dependency varies; Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12) note that where extreme inequality existed, institutions favored elites and restricted access to economic opportunities for the broader population. This dynamic allowed elites to maintain their status over time but at the cost of limiting overall economic growth potential. Thus, historical inequalities and institutional frameworks significantly influence long-term economic outcomes, shaping the trajectories of regions through mechanisms such as property rights security and urbanization rates.

The literature on institutions and economic growth in Europe highlights how secure property rights and financial innovations fostered long-term economic success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for establishing secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The security of property rights over time enabled a robust capital market to flourish. North (1989, p.9) further argues that institutions matter more than neoclassical theories would suggest, which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants of economic growth. This perspective underscores the critical role of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes, aligning with the idea that secure property rights are essential for sustained growth (North 1989, p.3). North (1989, p.9) also notes that the development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is contingent on various factors. This nuance suggests that while institutional security is crucial, the path to economic growth through institutional reforms can be fraught with challenges and uncertainties.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) contend that Africa's economic development has been stunted by the legacy of the slave trade. This historical trauma is argued to have instilled a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and continues to hinder economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.28) elaborates on this mechanism, highlighting how the slave trades disrupted precolonial institutional structures and undermined societal cohesion. The process was not just about the extraction of labor but also involved the destruction of social capital and trust networks that were crucial for economic activity. This disruption created a vicious cycle where weakened institutions struggled to support economic initiatives, further entrenching underdevelopment. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) emphasize that this mistrust presents a challenge that can be addressed through targeted interventions aimed at rebuilding social capital and fostering trust within communities.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive systems that prioritized European interests over local development. In densely populated areas, Europeans found it profitable to implement forced labor and slave trading systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These extractive institutions not only exploited local resources but also hindered the accumulation of human capital, as masses did not obtain education under these regimes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), this mechanism is a primary way that extractive institutions delayed industrialization. Moreover, the persistence of these extractive institutions long after colonial rule ended further entrenched economic underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The legacy of colonialism in Africa is thus marked by a deep-seated mistrust within societies, which has persisted and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The impact of colonial institutions extends beyond economic exploitation to include the destruction of social capital and trust networks crucial for economic activity (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This disruption created a vicious cycle where weakened institutions struggled to support economic initiatives, further entrenching underdevelopment. The reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), suggesting that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This is because colonialism presided over non-productive institutions, and colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle the unproductive institutions that were already in place (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era further highlights how these policies created long-lasting barriers to economic development. In Latin America, for example, monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

Institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). This path of institutional development may have affected growth, where extreme inequality limited access to economic opportunities for much of the population. In African societies, high population density made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35), these systems not only exploited local resources but also hindered the accumulation of human capital.

The underlying point of agreement is that colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories. This is shown by the persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era, which created long-lasting barriers to economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized or not. Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), while Acemoglu et al. argue that the correlation between institutions and human capital most likely reflects the fact that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era, which created long-lasting barriers to economic development. This is shown by the persistence of the most prominent extractive policies in Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12).
