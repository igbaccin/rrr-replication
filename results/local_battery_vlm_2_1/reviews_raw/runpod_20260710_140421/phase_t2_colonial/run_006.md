The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is how to understand the persistence of poverty and underdevelopment in Africa. The disagreement hinges on whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive versus productive. Some scholars argue that colonialism introduced institutions that hindered economic development, while others contend that these institutions were not necessarily detrimental but rather were products of specific historical contexts and constraints. Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) both underscore the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history and path dependence. This focus on long-term historical perspectives helps to reveal relationships that recur because situations are similar, as well as those influenced by path dependence. There is an ongoing debate over whether colonial institutions were inherently extractive or if their effects varied based on contextual factors such as population density (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The key question remains: how did colonial institutions shape African economic growth trajectories, and what mechanisms underpin these relationships?

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions have been instrumental in shaping African economic growth trajectories through the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions were more likely to be imposed in areas with high population density, where they could be more profitable for European colonizers. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) note that the presence of abundant labor in densely settled regions made extractive institutions particularly lucrative. This profitability incentivized Europeans to implement systems that exploited local populations, such as forced labor and slave trading. The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence has had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) observe that the control structures set up during colonialism often endured long after independence, forming the basis of current governance systems in many African countries. These enduring institutions have hindered economic growth by limiting access to education and other forms of human capital development, which are crucial for industrialization. The correlation between extractive institutions and low levels of human capital suggests that these institutions have been a primary mechanism through which colonial legacies continue to affect economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This highlights the need to consider the long-term impacts of colonial policies on contemporary African economies.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin's critique emphasises that the causal relationships involved in economic growth are more differentiated than recognised in earlier formulations. This differentiation is evident in the varied institutional responses to changes in factor markets, such as land scarcity. For instance, Ghana experienced a significant shift from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 (Austin 2007, p.8), leading to an institutional revolution in factor markets marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional change was driven by the need to adapt to new economic realities, highlighting that colonial institutions were not static but evolved in response to local conditions. Austin (2007, p.16) notes that these adaptations were often limited by existing power structures and colonial policies. The shift towards mechanised transport infrastructure in Ghana illustrates this point; the expansion of motorable roads and increased imports of cars and lorries (Austin 2007, p.8) show how infrastructural changes could facilitate economic growth but were also constrained by colonial administrative decisions. Thus, while institutional change was a dynamic process, it was also shaped by broader colonial frameworks that influenced the trajectory of African economies.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight that British rule's educational impact was not universally positive across all African economies. This educational development was facilitated by the British policy of indirect rule, which allowed for the integration of missionary schools into the colonial administration. British colonial authorities welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their administrative efforts (Frankema 2012, p.4). These schools were often better funded and more accessible than those in French-controlled territories; primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa were notably higher than in French West Africa by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). This educational disparity had significant implications for economic growth, as improved access to education contributed to the development of a skilled workforce. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that improving long-term performance in Africa will require not just an increase in growing but also a reduction in the rate and frequency of shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This suggests that while British colonial policies promoted education, which is crucial for economic growth, the broader institutional framework under colonial rule may have constrained other aspects of economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) share the view that African economic history is marked by significant changes over time. They jointly argue for a nuanced understanding of Africa's development trajectory, one that acknowledges the dynamic nature of colonial rule and its evolving impact on institutional frameworks. Hopkins describes how European settlement patterns shifted due to medical advances which mitigated disease constraints (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This evolution in health conditions enabled colonial powers to implement different strategies over time, suggesting a more complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes than a static view might suggest.

The dynamic nature of colonial rule is further illuminated by the varying degrees of income inequality observed across different regions. Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight that differences in long-term growth trajectories are not merely due to commercialization but also stem from disparities in income distribution among European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This pattern underscores the multifaceted influence of colonial institutions on economic development, as these inequalities can shape the broader institutional landscape. Yet, this dynamic view is qualified by Hopkins's observation that colonial rulers did not always have the time or capacity to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions before independence (Hopkins 2009, p.13). This suggests that while colonial rule could introduce new institutional frameworks, its impact on long-term economic growth was often constrained by historical and temporal factors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) examine how distinct institutional frameworks shaped economic outcomes in pre-industrial Europe. They argue that institutions are deeply interdependent with other elements of society, including cultural beliefs and economic conditions. Ogilvie notes that European family systems, welfare structures, and labour markets were interconnected in ways that fostered growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency highlights a crucial mechanism: institutions do not operate in isolation but are embedded within complex social structures. The downstream implication of this interdependence is that changes to one part of the system can have cascading effects on others; however, this dynamic is qualified by North and Weingast's observation that secure property rights, governed by robust public choice institutions, were crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This suggests that while institutional frameworks are interconnected, certain key institutions play a pivotal role in driving economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3); (Nunn 2008, p.28) findings indicate that the legacy of colonial institutions extends beyond economic structures to deeply ingrained social attitudes. This mistrust is rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade. The internal channel of this mistrust is particularly significant, as it involves the erosion of social cohesion within African societies themselves. This lack of trust has profound implications for economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collective action, both of which are crucial for sustainable growth. Nunn (2008, p.28) further elaborates that the early trade between Africans and Europeans was predominantly in commodities other than slaves, indicating that societies with developed institutions were better equipped to facilitate this trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the historical legacy of mistrust, coupled with underdeveloped institutional frameworks, has created a complex barrier to economic progress in Africa.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is profoundly influenced by the persistence of extractive institutions established during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) highlight that high population density made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading. This institutional framework facilitated exploitation and hindered the development of productive economic activities. The persistence of these extractive institutions into the post-colonial era is evident in the continuation of monopolies and regulations that were initially created by colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical legacy has had a lasting impact on African economies, as the institutions set up during colonialism have formed the basis for current-day governance structures. These structures often perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth by limiting opportunities for investment and development.

The historical context of colonial rule in Africa further complicates the understanding of its economic trajectories. Colonial powers introduced different types of extractive institutions depending on the local conditions, such as population density and the availability of labor (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Hopkins (2009, p.13) shows that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutional options were present, but colonial rulers often opted for the latter due to their immediate profitability. This choice had long-term consequences, as these institutions became entrenched and persisted even after independence. The persistence of extractive institutions is a key factor in understanding why some African countries have struggled to achieve sustained economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized, suggesting that a nuanced examination of historical paths is necessary.

The legacy of colonial institutions also includes a culture of mistrust that persists within African societies today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop, which accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust hinders cooperation and collective action, both of which are crucial for sustainable growth. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework has been recognized by some studies (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. This interlinked institutional system suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources shows that colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories through the establishment of extractive frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement lies in whether the causal relationships are more differentiated than previously recognized or if they follow a straightforward path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2). This disagreement is settled by examining historical paths and measuring institutional persistence, as shown in the continuity of monopolies and regulations from colonial times to the present day.
