The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that has sparked significant debate among scholars. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how historical institutional frameworks have influenced contemporary economic outcomes and whether these effects are persistent over time. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions, particularly those characterized as extractive, have hindered or facilitated economic growth in Africa. On one hand, some scholars argue that the persistence of extractive institutions introduced by colonial powers has been a primary mechanism through which African economies have been stunted. Some scholars suggest that causal relationships are more complex and differentiated, encompassing various historical periods and paths. The debate hinges on whether the legacy of colonialism is primarily one of institutional stagnation or if there are additional factors at play that contribute to Africa's economic trajectory. Colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories by creating enduring institutional frameworks that either fostered or hindered development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial rule was influenced by the profitability of extractive versus inclusive institutions, and that these institutions persisted post-independence. In densely populated areas where labor could be easily exploited, Europeans were more likely to implement extractive institutions such as forced labor or slave trading. Acemoglu et al. further note that these extractive institutions often persisted after independence, continuing to impede economic development by maintaining systems that extracted wealth from the local population while not fostering inclusive growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on African economies, as they have hindered the accumulation of human capital and infrastructure necessary for sustained economic growth. This institutional legacy underscores the profound impact of colonial rule on Africa's economic trajectory, highlighting the need to address these deep-seated institutional issues in order to promote sustainable development.

The slave trade's role in shaping African economic development is proposed as an alternative explanation to colonial institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which has persisted and significantly impedes contemporary economic growth. This mistrust acts as a barrier to cooperation and collective action, essential for economic development. The legacy of this mistrust is evident in reduced social capital, hindering the formation of institutions necessary for sustainable growth. Nunn supports this perspective by highlighting the adverse effect of Africa's slave trades on economic development, demonstrating that societies with more developed precolonial institutions were better equipped to facilitate trade with Europeans. These findings suggest a deep-seated institutional legacy that persists beyond colonial rule, impacting African economies through reduced trust and hindered collective action. The historical evidence indicates that the slave trades' impact on African economic development cannot be overlooked when considering the continent's trajectory.

The economic history of Africa and institutional reforms offers an important perspective on the continent's development challenges by examining the historical context and the impact of colonial institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins argues that colonial institutions in Africa reflected both extractive practices and the limited time colonial rulers had to implement changes. This view aligns with Olsson's contention that the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism is crucial; while early colonial periods lacked productive institutions, later phases saw both productive and extractive options becoming available due to medical advances and reduced disease constraints (Hopkins 2009, p.12). The historical context matters: African regions' economic development before 1500 were similar to other prosperous areas. This perspective underscores that the trajectory of African economies was influenced by a complex interplay of precolonial institutions, colonial policies, and post-colonial developments.

Colonial rulers did not have sufficient time to dismantle existing unproductive institutions, which implies that the legacy of colonialism is more about continuity than radical change (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.13). This perspective challenges simplistic notions of institutional change and highlights the need for a more detailed examination of how precolonial economic structures interacted with colonial policies. The historical creation of poverty through European capitalism further complicates this narrative, as it underscores the role of unequal exchange in perpetuating underdevelopment (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This view aligns with Amin's contention that foreign capitalism created underdevelopment by drawing other societies into networks of dependence and exploiting them through unequal exchange. The implication is that understanding African economic growth requires considering both internal institutional dynamics and external economic forces.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that British rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers. The British promoted missionary schools to support their system of indirect rule, leading to higher primary-school enrolment rates compared to French colonies, which restricted Christian missions (Frankema 2012, p.4). This educational investment was facilitated by the British administrative strategy that integrated existing indigenous state structures for administration and taxation; this approach allowed colonial powers to capitalize on preexisting institutions for education and infrastructure development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; Frankema 2012, p.15). The long-term implications of these educational investments are significant. The differences in educational trajectories between British and French colonies have persisted, influencing the broader economic dynamics of African countries. This is evident in the varying degrees of income inequality observed across different regions, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) caution that attributing all long-term growth trajectories solely to colonial educational policies oversimplifies the complex interplay of factors influencing African economic development. This nuanced perspective underscores the need for future research to delve deeper into the multifaceted dynamics driving African economic history.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.16) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This view posits that examining growth theories against long-term history reveals relationships that recur due to similar situations, as well as path dependence. It also suggests that causal relationships are more differentiated than previously recognised in Acemoglu et al.'s formulations. The institutional revolution in factor markets observed in Ghana during 1874-1939 provides a concrete example of this differentiation (Austin 2007, p.16). This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity. This transformation was marked by significant infrastructural developments, such as the tripling of motorable roads and the importation of thousands of cars and lorries (Austin 2007, p.8). These changes facilitated the mechanisation of goods transport, except for the final leg from farm to local market. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not account for these nuanced historical dynamics, which were shaped by complex interactions between economic, social, and environmental factors.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.9) argue. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs. This reduction reflected an increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of fiscal crises, illustrating how institutional changes can emerge from specific historical contexts (North 1989, p.9). This perspective highlights that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. North also posits that the progression from primitive societies to modern Western societies is not automatic or unilinear; instead, it involves complex interactions between institutions and interdependence (North 1989, p.3). The complexity of these interactions suggests that the development of institutions capable of handling more intricate social and economic arrangements is a non-trivial process.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by influencing the establishment and persistence of extractive institutions. The profitability of these institutions was contingent on local factors such as population density and labor availability (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Moreover, Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions often persisted long after colonial regimes ended, significantly impacting current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.13).

The persistence of these institutions is further underscored by the fact that they were not merely imposed but also adapted to local conditions. This adaptation process ensured their longevity and deep integration into societal structures. The extractive nature of these institutions, which often involved forced labor and resource exploitation, created a cycle where low levels of human capital reinforced the persistence of these systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-term economic trajectories by influencing property rights, transaction costs, and overall economic growth.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economies varies. Austin argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than recognized in previous formulations. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional interactions and their long-term effects on economic development.

The precise mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced African economies remain a subject of ongoing debate, but the evidence shows that they were central to shaping the continent's economic trajectory. The persistence of extractive institutions, as well as the cultural impacts such as mistrust within societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), are key factors in understanding Africa's current economic challenges. This historical context is crucial for informing institutional reforms and addressing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12).
