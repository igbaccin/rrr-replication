The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because understanding the causal mechanisms at play can inform policy and development strategies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth on the continent. One key disagreement centers around whether extractive colonial institutions are primarily responsible for Africa's current underdevelopment, or if other factors such as precolonial state structures, cultural shifts, or more recent events bear significant responsibility. There is broad agreement that colonial institutions played a critical role in shaping African economies during and after the colonial era, but scholars diverge on the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated and their enduring impact. The question of whether colonial institutions are the primary cause of Africa's economic trajectory, or if they are just one factor among many, remains unresolved.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the direct causal link between extractive colonial institutions and contemporary African economic underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions persisted after independence, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The profitability of extractive institutions was influenced by factors such as population density and labor supply (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35), which made them more viable in certain regions. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who highlight that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of these institutions over time has had lasting effects on economic development and social structures.

Another stream of scholarship challenges the notion that extractive colonial institutions are the sole or primary cause of African underdevelopment. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This view is supported by Hopkins, who notes that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12). These scholars suggest that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized. This perspective underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.13).

This nuanced understanding of African economic development highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and other factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. They posit that the type of institutions Europeans established in their colonies depended on the expected profitability of extractive versus inclusive institutions. This institutional framework has had persistent effects on economic development: extractive institutions created by colonial powers have endured, shaping current economic outcomes. As a result, societies with extractive institutions typically exhibit lower levels of human capital and delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The persistence of these institutions varies among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) note that in some regions, such as Latin America, the extractive policies established by colonial powers remained intact for extended periods after independence. This suggests that the longevity of colonial institutions can vary based on post-colonial governance structures and regional dynamics. The persistence of these extractive institutions has profound implications for economic growth: they create barriers to human capital development and industrialization, which are crucial for sustained economic progress. Thus, understanding the role of colonial institutions in Africa requires a nuanced examination of how these institutions have evolved and interacted with local contexts over time; this includes how their persistence has been shaped by post-colonial governance structures.

The institutional landscape of colonial Africa underwent significant transformations driven by shifts in factor markets, notably from land abundance to scarcity (Austin 2007, p.16; Austin 2008, p.1). Austin (2007, p.16; 2008, p.1) argue that these changes led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, fundamentally altering economic growth trajectories. The scarcity of land in areas like Ghana's Ashanti region by the late 1930s illustrates this dynamic: it prompted local authorities to implement policies aimed at managing land use more efficiently (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional response to resource constraints highlights how colonial institutions adapted to changing economic conditions, moving beyond static extractive practices. The development of infrastructure, such as the tripling of motorable roads in Ghana between 1930 and 1932, further supported this transition by enhancing transport networks and facilitating market integration (Austin 2007, p.8). Austin (2008, p.1) observes that institutional changes across Africa were varied. The causal relationships driving economic growth are more nuanced than simplified models suggest. This complexity underscores the importance of examining long-term historical contexts to understand how colonial institutions shaped African economies over time.

The slave trade's impact on African economies was multifaceted and cannot be attributed solely to colonial institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust persists to this day and is a significant factor in Africa's reduced economic growth. Nunn reinforces the idea that the adverse effects of the slave trades on African economies were substantial and long-lasting, suggesting that societies with more developed institutions were better able to engage in trade with Europeans. The historical evidence shows that early trade between Africans and Europeans was primarily in commodities other than slaves, indicating that institutional development played a crucial role in facilitating this trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). Ultimately, the slave trade undermined these institutions, leading to economic stagnation and underdevelopment. This institutional decay had profound implications for Africa's economic trajectory, as it hindered the ability of African societies to develop robust economic systems capable of sustaining growth. The legacy of mistrust and weakened institutions continues to impede economic development in many parts of Africa today.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) show that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other regimes. This is because the British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, whereas French colonies restricted missionary activities and shut down schools. Consequently, primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa were almost double those in French West Africa by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). The differential impact of colonial powers on educational development had profound implications for African societies' ability to develop robust economic systems capable of sustaining growth. The legacy of colonial education policies is complex. For instance, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4; 2012, p.28) argue that while British rule facilitated formal education, it also led to significant income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans in countries like Kenya. This pattern highlights the need for future research to concentrate on charting differences in long-term growth trajectories within African economic history.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) along with Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2019, p.11) note that the economic history of Africa has largely focused on institutional reforms without directly addressing how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.12) argues that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions already in place. This perspective challenges the notion that colonial rulers had sufficient time to dismantle existing unproductive institutions, suggesting a more nuanced view of colonial impact. This argument does not directly address how these institutional legacies translated into specific growth trajectories post-independence. Hopkins (2019, p.11) highlights the difficulties in using instrumental variables and the pitfalls of path dependence, which can oversimplify complex historical processes (Hopkins 2019, p.11). This critique underscores the need for a more granular analysis that considers the diverse experiences within African countries and avoids broad generalizations about colonial institutions. While this approach acknowledges the challenges in linking distant pasts to present outcomes, it also opens avenues for future research to explore how specific institutional reforms during and after colonial rule might have influenced economic growth differently across regions. The complexity of these historical processes necessitates a deeper understanding of local contexts and the varied impacts of colonial policies on African economies over time.

Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive systems that persisted long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show the persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era, where they continued to hinder economic development. This enduring impact is further highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who demonstrate how colonial-era policies fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic outcomes today.

The relationship between extractive institutions and economic stagnation is evident in the data, which shows that densely populated areas were more likely to have extractive institutions imposed upon them. The presence of abundant labor and resources made such systems particularly profitable for European colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This profitability drove the establishment of forced labor systems in regions like Meso-America and slave trading networks in Africa, which had profound long-term effects on economic development.

The historical evidence shows that the persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era significantly affected income per capita. For example, Latin America retained many of the monopolies and regulations established by Spanish colonizers for much of the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This continuity underscores how colonial policies did not end with decolonization but instead formed the basis of post-colonial economic structures.

There is disagreement on whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis adequately captures these complex historical processes. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that this framework oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative. This critique suggests that a more nuanced understanding of African economic growth requires examining the varied impacts of colonial policies over time.

The specific historical evidence of African slave trades settles these disagreements, as it provides measurable data on how extractive institutions directly affected economic development. This historical dependence on fishing (Nunn 2008, p.19), for example, shows that societies with developed precolonial state institutions were better equipped to facilitate trade and resist the imposition of extractive systems.
