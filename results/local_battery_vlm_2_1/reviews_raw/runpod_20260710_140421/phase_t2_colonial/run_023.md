The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is pivotal for understanding contemporary development challenges and opportunities across the continent. At stake intellectually are competing explanations about the persistence of underdevelopment in Africa. One position argues that extractive colonial institutions were the primary cause of enduring economic stagnation by preventing the accumulation of human capital, fostering mistrust within societies, and perpetuating a culture of dependency on external actors. Another perspective posits that the impact of colonialism has been overstated as a determinant of current conditions in Africa; instead, it suggests that the causal relationships are more complex and nuanced than typically acknowledged. These positions converge on the critical role of institutions but diverge significantly regarding the mechanisms through which they operate. The debate is not merely academic: resolving this question can inform institutional reforms aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth across African societies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions are pivotal in shaping African economic growth trajectories. Their research shows that European settlers established extractive institutions where mortality rates were high and profitable extractive opportunities abounded. Dense populations, for example, offered a labor supply conducive to forced labor systems or slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional setup was not merely an initial condition but persisted long after colonial rule ended, shaping the economic landscape of these regions well into independence.

The persistence of extractive institutions has significant implications for human capital accumulation. Acemoglu et al.'s argument is that in societies burdened by extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This lack of investment in human capital further delayed industrialization and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) provide an example from Latin America where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence, illustrating how extractive policies can endure and hinder growth. The persistence of these institutions underscores their enduring impact on African economic trajectories; the legacy of colonial extractive institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

Austin argues that institutional change in African economies was driven by shifts in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). During 1874-1939 in Ghana, the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity catalyzed an institutional revolution. This period saw widespread emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour. Austin (2008, p.1) notes that this transformation was propelled by a complex interplay of factors including population density and resource availability (Austin 2007, p.16). The emergence of new institutions reflected broader economic changes, such as increased mechanization in transport, which facilitated the integration of local markets into regional and global economies. Austin (2007, p.16) notes that the length of motorable roads tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles during 1930-32 (Austin 2007, p.8). This infrastructural development further stimulated economic activity by reducing transport costs and enhancing connectivity. These institutional changes varied across regions; for example, Ashanti had a lower population density than the Gold Coast Colony yet land scarcity still emerged by the end of the 1930s (Austin 2007, p.16). This regional variation highlights the nuanced impact of colonial institutions on economic trajectories.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10); (Frankema 2012, p.15) show that the British colonial administration fostered formal education more extensively than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by Britain's approach to indirect rule, which encouraged missionary activity and allowed for broader educational access in its colonies. The British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, leading to higher primary-school enrolment rates compared to French West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.4). This policy had significant implications for economic growth trajectories across sub-Saharan Africa by enhancing the development of human capital and fostering a more educated workforce.

The British approach had its complexities. The educational system under British rule, while more expansive than in other colonial regimes, was often tailored to serve colonial administrative needs rather than local economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This focus on training clerks and administrators meant that while education levels were higher, the specific skills acquired did not always align with the needs of a growing industrial sector. Thus, while British rule stimulated formal education, its impact on economic growth was nuanced, influenced by both the quantity and quality of educational outcomes.

Hopkins and others (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) highlight that African economic history is shaped by diverse factors beyond colonial institutions, including ecological conditions and precolonial trade patterns. Hopkins points out that the nature of colonialism varied significantly over time, from mercantilist to modern forms of imperialism, each with distinct impacts on institutional development in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This temporal variation in colonial practices suggests that the extractive nature of institutions was not uniform across Africa and changed over time. For instance, the Kenyan case illustrates how different types of commercialization and income disparities among European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans influenced long-term growth trajectories. This nuanced view challenges simplistic narratives about colonial institutions' persistent negative effects on African economic development. Hopkins also notes that by the late nineteenth century, medical advances had removed some constraints on European settlement, altering colonial policy towards Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). The variability in colonial practices and their impacts underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when examining African economic history.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast and North underscore that the financial revolution played a pivotal role in England's long-term success, highlighting how secure property rights and financial innovations were essential for sustained economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework reflects increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North further elaborates that even with third-party enforcement, institutions must evolve to handle increasing complexity as societies develop (North 1989, p.3). The progression from primitive to modern societies requires the development of complex institutions to manage interdependence effectively; this pattern can be seen in how England's fiscal crises led to institutional changes that fostered economic growth. This suggests that the path to economic growth through institutional development is fraught with obstacles that must be navigated carefully.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that this mistrust is deeply ingrained, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The historical evidence shows that early trade between Africans and Europeans was primarily in commodities other than slaves; only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate this trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests a complex interplay between institutional development and economic activities, where pre-existing institutions influenced both trade dynamics and the subsequent impact of the slave trade. The legacy of mistrust has enduring implications for contemporary African societies. This mistrust, fostered by the slave trade, has a profound impact on various aspects of socio-economic development in Africa.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by fostering mistrust and extractive practices that persist today. A shared finding across studies is the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The precise mechanisms through which these institutions exert their influence remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Austin posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The causal relationships involved are more differentiated than is recognised in Acemoglu et al.'s formulations (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This disagreement can be settled through a measurement type or case that the cited sources have already used: the persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era. For example, the full panoply of monopolies and regulations, which had been created by Spain, remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.13).
