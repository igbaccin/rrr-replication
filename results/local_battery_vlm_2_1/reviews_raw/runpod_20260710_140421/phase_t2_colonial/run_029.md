The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake are the mechanisms by which historical legacies influence contemporary economic outcomes. One live disagreement centers on whether colonial institutions established extractive systems that persist to this day, hindering economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions introduced during colonialism have had lasting effects on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Their argument hinges on the persistence of these institutions into post-colonial eras, which they illustrate with examples from Latin America where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact long after independence. Another perspective posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This view suggests that colonial institutions are just one factor among many that have shaped Africa's economic trajectory, and that the relationship between these factors is more complex than often acknowledged. The debate thus revolves around whether colonial institutions are a primary cause of stagnation or merely one piece in a larger puzzle.

Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Extractive institutions established during the colonial period were particularly detrimental to economic development in Africa. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these extractive systems, which concentrated on resource extraction and focused on broad-based economic development, were more profitable for European colonizers in densely populated areas. This profitability incentivized Europeans to implement and maintain these institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions well beyond the colonial era is evident; they continued to hinder growth by restricting human capital accumulation and industrialization. In fact, Acemoglu et al. posit that these institutions "delayed industrialization" in part through the mechanism of "low levels of human capital" (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era is exemplified by the continuation of monopolies and regulations created by Spain long after independence, with similar patterns observed across Latin America. This institutional legacy has had lasting effects on economic performance in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35).

The slave trade significantly impeded economic development in Africa by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) show that the slave trade's impact on African economies is mediated through two channels: internal conflict and external trade. Internal conflict, which arises from the mistrust engendered by the slave trade, has a more substantial adverse effect than the disruption of external trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust persists today, affecting economic development by limiting cooperation and social capital (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). Nunn argues that precolonial institutions in Africa were crucial for facilitating early trade with Europeans. The selection process during the slave trades meant that only societies with developed institutions could effectively engage in commerce (Nunn 2008, p.19). The same institutions that facilitated trade also became conduits for the slave trade, creating a complex interplay between economic development and institutional decay. This dynamic illustrates how the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape African economies through direct economic disruptions as well as enduring social and institutional factors. The impact on trust is one mechanism by which the slave trade has had lasting effects on Africa's economic trajectory.

British colonial policies played a pivotal role in shaping African education systems (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) demonstrate that British rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, particularly the French. This divergence is evident in primary-school enrolment rates, which were roughly double in British West Africa compared to French West Africa by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). The British approach welcomed missionaries of all denominations, integrating them into their system of indirect rule. This policy facilitated the establishment and growth of educational institutions. The impact of these policies extended beyond education, influencing long-term economic trajectories. For instance, the Kenyan case illustrates how varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans shaped different growth dynamics in East Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) advise that while British policies were more conducive to education development, this did not necessarily result in persistent economic benefits. The complex interplay of colonial institutions, educational development, and long-term economic outcomes highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of Africa's growth trajectories.

Austin (2007, p.16) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2007, p.16; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) examine institutional changes and economic performance in specific regions of Africa during the colonial period. Their research shows that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by influencing factor markets and infrastructure development, which are key drivers for long-term growth. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) ) documents how Ghana's shift from land abundance to scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transformation was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, highlighting the dynamic nature of colonial institutions in response to local pressures. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate this point by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding suggests that colonial institutions were capable of facilitating economic growth under certain conditions, even if they did not always lead to sustained development. The implication is that understanding these dynamics requires a nuanced examination of how specific institutional changes interacted with local economic conditions and broader regional trends.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) advise that improving long-term performance in Africa will require a reduction in the rate and frequency of economic shrinking. This insight emphasizes the importance of focusing on institutional factors that contribute to economic stability and resilience as well as growth. Austin (2007, p.8) ) detailed account of infrastructure development in Ghana during 1874-1939 supports this perspective, showing how improved transport networks significantly enhanced economic activity by reducing transportation costs and increasing market access. This indicates that colonial institutions could foster economic growth through targeted investments in infrastructure, which in turn supported broader economic development.

Bryceson (2002, p.5) and Peters (2004, p.14), in their examination of social changes in sub-Saharan Africa, argue that economic restructuring has led to significant depeasantization. This process involves deep-rooted social transformations in addition to shifts in economic activities. This shift is particularly pronounced in regions experiencing recurrent food shortages and climate-induced harvest failures, where rural populations increasingly rely on external support such as food aid. These changes are intertwined with broader social dynamics, not isolated economic phenomena. For example, the commodification of land and labor relations has fragmented family structures and exacerbated conflicts within kin-based groups (Peters 2004, p.14). This pattern underscores how economic restructuring in Africa is intricately linked to social changes that extend beyond mere economic shifts, impacting familial and communal dynamics significantly. The processes of exclusion and class formation are evident as private commercial transactions gain prominence over state institutions (Peters 2004, p.19), highlighting the multifaceted nature of depeasantization.

The evolving understanding of Africa's economic past reveals that colonialism did not uniformly impose extractive institutions; rather, it interacted with pre-existing conditions and varied over time (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.6). Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) both highlight that the nature of colonial institutions changed significantly between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. Early colonial periods lacked productive institutions, even in Europe, making it unlikely that such institutions were transplanted overseas (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is crucial because it underscores that the impact of colonial rule on African economic development was contingent on specific temporal and geographical factors, not monolithic.

This perspective does not negate the broader argument that colonialism generally entrenched non-productive institutions. The historical creation of poverty in Africa was a complex process involving unequal exchange and dependence on European capitalism (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This dynamic suggests that while the specifics of colonial institutions varied, their overall effect was to perpetuate economic underdevelopment. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further complicates this narrative by noting the challenges in using instrumental variables and path dependence to link distant historical events to contemporary outcomes. The reliance on such methods can lead to oversimplified deterministic views that ignore intervening influences (Hopkins 2019, p.11). This cautionary note underscores the need for nuanced approaches that consider the multifaceted nature of colonialism's impact on African economic development.

Colonial institutions have been shown to play a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that persist beyond the colonial era and significantly influence current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Austin 2008, p.1). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions, implemented for profit maximization during colonial rule, have sustained their impact on development through long-term persistence of these structures after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This is further substantiated by Nunn and Wantchekon, who illustrate how the slave trade, a key component of colonial exploitation, has cultivated a culture of mistrust that continues to impede economic progress in Africa today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The continuity of extractive institutions from the colonial period into post-independence eras highlights their enduring impact on African economies. Acemoglu et al. emphasize how high population densities and abundant labor resources made extractive institutions profitable for European colonists, leading to their establishment in densely populated areas like Africa (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This economic rationale underpins the persistence of these institutions, which continue to shape contemporary economic conditions by perpetuating low levels of human capital development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). The historical evidence shows that even after independence, many African countries retained the extractive policies and regulations imposed during colonial rule, further entrenching their negative effects on economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic trajectories is also evident in the institutional reforms they necessitate. Hopkins argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform effective institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people (Peters 2004, p.14). These reforms are crucial for addressing the long-term impacts of colonial extractive institutions, which have left a lasting imprint on African economies by creating enduring structures that hinder development (Austin 2008, p.2). The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the persistence of extractive policies and regulations in post-independence Africa. This continuity shows how colonial institutions have shaped economic growth trajectories by perpetuating systems that limit human capital development and foster mistrust, ultimately impeding progress towards poverty reduction.
