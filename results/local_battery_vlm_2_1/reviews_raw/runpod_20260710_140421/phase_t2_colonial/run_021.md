The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that pivots around institutional determinism versus alternative explanations such as geography and culture. At stake is the extent to which historical legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The debate centers on whether colonial institutions, often characterized by their extractive nature, have left an enduring imprint on Africa's economic trajectory or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural attributes, play a more significant role. This disagreement underscores the broader question of how historical processes shape long-term economic development.

The extractive nature of colonial institutions has been a central focus in this discourse. Extractive institutions were designed to maximize resource extraction from colonies for the benefit of the colonizers and often involved forced labor systems and monopolies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). These institutions were more profitable in densely populated areas where there was more to be extracted by European colonists. For example, high population density in Africa created opportunities for slave traders to supply labor to American plantations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions after colonial rule ended has been a significant factor in shaping post-colonial economic trajectories.

The legacy of colonial institutions extends beyond immediate economic impacts to include cultural and social dimensions. One notable aspect is the development of a culture of mistrust within Africa due to the slave trade, which persists today and affects contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust is significant because it has been shown to hinder economic development. The persistence of this cultural legacy underscores the deep-rooted impact of colonial institutions on African societies.

One key area of focus in the literature is how colonial institutions influenced human capital accumulation and industrialization. Extractive institutions often resulted in limited access to education for the masses, which in turn delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This highlights how institutional frameworks can shape long-term economic development by influencing critical factors such as education and technological advancement. The persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era further illustrates this point.

Colonial institutions shaped economic trajectories both directly and indirectly through institutional persistence. The control structures set up during the colonial era often persisted long after independence, forming the basis of current-day institutions in many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional persistence has been a critical factor in determining post-colonial economic outcomes. The enduring influence of these institutions underscores their significance in shaping long-term economic development trajectories.

The debate on the role of colonial institutions in Africa's economic trajectory highlights the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes. While extractive institutions imposed during colonial rule have had profound effects, the persistence of these institutions post-independence further exacerbates the challenges faced by African economies today. The enduring impact of colonial institutions underscores their significance in shaping long-term economic development trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories. These institutions varied based on extractive systems profitability which was more likely where high population density provided abundant labor.

Understanding the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic growth requires examining their post-independence persistence. The continuation of such institutions in Latin America, for instance, demonstrates how the structures set up by colonial powers can outlast the colonial regime itself and significantly influence contemporary economic outcomes. This institutional persistence underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economies today.

Austin (2007, p.16) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) share an emphasis on how institutional changes in factor markets influenced economic performance in Africa (Austin 2007, p.16; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). They argue that the transformation of land use rights and the emergence of labour markets were pivotal in shaping African economies. The shift from abundant to scarce land, as documented by Austin (2007, p.16), led to significant institutional changes in Ghana during 1874-1939. This period saw widespread markets for land use rights and hired labor emerge, marking a crucial transition that altered the economic landscape of Ghana (Austin 2007, p.16). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) build on this by noting that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century. This is a critical qualification because it shifts focus from growth to sustainability, suggesting that institutional changes must address both expansion and resilience (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17).

Austin argues that simplistic causal models oversimplify the intricate dynamics of African economic development (Austin 2008, p.1; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). He critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting the complexity of historical periods and paths in African economic development. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who emphasize that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Austin 2008, p.2). The causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized, revealing relationships that recur because situations are similar or due to path dependence. This insight underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, thereby revealing complex patterns and dynamics. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism has profound implications for contemporary economic outcomes.

Nunn (2008, p.28) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) both emphasize that the historical factors influencing African development are more complex than mere structural impediments. The early trade between Africans and Europeans was not solely focused on slaves; it included various commodities, indicating that societies with developed institutions facilitated this trade. This suggests that precolonial institutional development played a crucial role in enabling African societies to engage in commerce with Europeans and other external actors. These historical factors had a significant impact on the economic dynamics of African societies (Nunn 2008, p.19). The Kenyan case illustrates how different types of economic dynamics, such as commercialization, and varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans, shaped long-term growth trajectories. This points to the need for future research to focus more on charting and explaining differences in these long-term growth trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Hopkins (2019, p.11); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) share the view that African economic history is deeply entwined with broader themes of development and gender dynamics. The historical creation of poverty through European capitalism has been a significant factor in shaping African economies, as Hopkins argues that European capitalism drew other societies into networks of dependence and exploited them through unequal exchange (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This perspective aligns with Akyeampong and Fofack's observation that the systematic codification of patriarchy across African societies during colonial times further disadvantaged women. The historical layering of key institutional developments in kinship, commerce, and nascent capitalism highlights how colonial institutions shaped economic trajectories and entrenched social inequalities that persist today. For example, the "patchwork-quilt of patriarchies" described by Bozzoli in segregationist South Africa illustrates how these dynamics played out in different contexts across the continent (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Moreover, the focus on pre-colonial and colonial periods underscores the centrality of women to production and reproduction, suggesting that a more inclusive economic history could reveal additional pathways for growth. This points to the need for future research to further explore these dynamics, particularly how gender discrimination influenced long-term economic development in Africa.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) share a posture that institutions governing public choice foster secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They argue that the evolution of these institutions was critical for ensuring the security of property rights over time, which in turn facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This reduction was pivotal because it reflected an increased security in the temporal dimension of property rights, a factor essential for both capital market stability and overall economic growth. The implication is that without these institutional developments, economic growth would have been slower and less decisive.

This argument underscores how credible commitments and secure property rights are fundamental to economic development. North (1989, p.3) qualifies this by noting the progression from primitive societies to modern ones is neither automatic nor unilinear. The development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence requires overcoming significant challenges related to opportunism and cheating. This suggests that while secure property rights are necessary for economic growth, the path to achieving them is fraught with institutional hurdles that must be navigated carefully.

These insights into the role of institutions in fostering economic growth in England provide a valuable framework for understanding similar dynamics in colonial Africa. The absence of secure property rights and credible commitments can hinder long-term economic development, as seen in the challenges faced by African economies post-colonialism.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is deeply intertwined with the legacy of extractive practices introduced during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that extractive institutions, which prioritized resource extraction over long-term development, were particularly profitable in densely populated areas, leading to their widespread adoption by European colonizers. This institutional framework hindered human capital accumulation and delayed industrialization by limiting educational opportunities for the masses. The persistence of these extractive institutions well into post-colonial periods is evident in the continuity of policies that stifled economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate how monopolies and regulations introduced by colonial powers remained intact long after independence, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment. This institutional inertia is a critical factor in understanding the current economic landscape of many African nations.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8), is further complicated by historical and cultural factors. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of long-term history. This perspective highlights how path dependence and similar situations across different periods have shaped economic trajectories in Africa. The slave trade, for example, played a significant role in fostering a culture of mistrust within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), which continues to affect economic development today.

Colonial institutions' impact on African economies is further illustrated by the enduring effects of extractive policies and cultural shifts. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) show that both internal and external channels of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, are significant factors in Africa's reduced trust levels. This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it affects various aspects such as international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The historical dependence on extractive institutions, coupled with the cultural legacy of mistrust, creates a complex web that continues to influence African economies.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that colonial institutions have had a profound and lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). There remains a disagreement over the precise mechanisms through which these institutions operate.
