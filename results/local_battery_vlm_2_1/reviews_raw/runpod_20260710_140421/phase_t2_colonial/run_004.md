The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a pivotal question that engages with broader debates about the persistence of historical legacies and the determinants of long-term economic development. The study examines Africa's economic past and explores mechanisms through which institutional frameworks influence growth over extended periods (Author Year p.N). The live disagreement centers around whether colonial institutions are primarily extractive, stifling growth, or if other factors such as geographic endowments and cultural legacies play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers have had enduring negative effects on economic development. This perspective is countered by those who contend that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that other historical and contemporary factors are more critical in determining Africa's economic trajectory.

The debate often revolves around the extent to which European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that high European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted after independence and significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective is supported by Hopkins' assertion that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions, but Olsson suggests that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle the unproductive institutions already in place (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The persistence of these extractive institutions is a key mechanism through which colonial legacies continue to shape African economies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories. The profitability of extractive institutions, particularly in regions with high population density, played a pivotal role in this process. For instance, European colonists found it more profitable to establish forced labor systems and other exploitative practices where labor was abundant (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This dynamic is evident in both the Americas and Africa, where dense populations provided a ready supply of labor for extraction by Europeans. The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence has been a significant barrier to economic development; they created a cycle where low levels of human capital reinforced the extractive nature of governance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varies across regions. While extractive institutions have significantly hindered growth in many African countries, the persistence of these institutions varies based on historical and geographical factors. For example, the extractive policies implemented by Spain in Latin America remained largely intact well into the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial legacies and contemporary economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) share a view of African economic growth that rejects oversimplified causal frameworks, instead highlighting the intricate interplay of historical factors. They argue that examining long-term history is crucial for understanding the complex relationships between different periods and paths in Africa's economic development (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin (2008, p.1) emphasizes that path dependence, where past events influence future outcomes, plays a significant role in shaping African economies. The causal relationships involved are more differentiated than what is recognised by Acemoglu et al., whose formulations compress different historical periods and paths into singular narratives. For instance, the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by attributing economic trajectories solely to colonial institutions.

Austin (2007, p.16) illustrates this complexity through Ghana's shift from land abundance to localized scarcity during 1874-1939. This transition led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.8). These changes had nuanced implications; they suggested more efficient resource allocation but also revealed underlying tensions and constraints. For example, despite increasing motorable roads and mechanized transport during 1921-30 in Ghana, dependence on head loading for farm-to-market transport persisted (Austin 2007, p.8). This pattern suggests that while institutional changes were occurring, they did not uniformly transform economic structures across all sectors.

Moreover, the persistence of traditional practices amidst modern developments underscores the enduring influence of historical legacies. Austin (2007, p.16) notes that even in areas with lower population density, such as Ashanti, indications of land scarcity emerged by the end of the 1930s (Austin 2007, p.16). This highlights how localised factors, such as demographic changes and resource availability, interacted with broader economic trends to shape institutional responses. Despite the adoption of policies like banning new farms in 1939, these measures often proved excessive or ineffective, indicating the challenges of implementing sweeping reforms without considering context-specific conditions.

The historical periods and paths in African economic growth reveal a complex interplay of factors that go beyond colonial institutions alone.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) and Olsson (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) acknowledge that the literature on African economic history offers rich insights into the continent's development. These scholars acknowledge that this body of work does not directly address how colonial institutions shaped Africa’s economic growth trajectories. The central mechanism by which colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes in Africa is through the establishment and perpetuation of extractive regimes. These regimes were characterized by policies and practices designed to exploit resources rather than foster sustainable development. This, in turn, led to the entrenchment of non-productive institutions. The extent and nature of these extractive regimes varied across different colonial periods; early mercantilist forms differed significantly from later modern imperialism. The historical creation of poverty in Africa can be traced back to the unequal exchange mechanisms imposed by European capitalism (Hopkins 2019, p.6). This underscores how the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape contemporary economic challenges. Instrumental variables and path dependence in historical analysis can oversimplify these complex dynamics, potentially leading to deterministic conclusions that overlook intervening influences (Hopkins 2019, p.11).

Frankema and colleagues (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) find that British colonial policies were more favorable for developing formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This educational development did not necessarily translate into persistent economic growth. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the differential treatment of Christian missions by French and British colonial authorities. The French restricted missionary activities, particularly non-Catholic ones, while the British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their indirect rule system (Frankema 2012, p.4). This policy divergence resulted in significantly higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa by 1938. Despite these educational advances under British rule, the legacy of colonial institutions did not uniformly translate into economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). They highlight how the Kenyan case illustrates varying degrees of income inequality between different population groups, suggesting that the path-dependence nature of African economic development is more complex than often assumed. This complexity calls for a nuanced understanding of long-term growth trajectories in Africa, emphasizing the need to chart and explain differences in these trajectories rather than attributing them solely to colonial institutional legacies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Akyeampong and Fofack contend that women were central to economic growth in Africa during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Peters 2004, p.19). Their analysis reveals how patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and African chiefs systematically disadvantaged women by codifying patriarchy across societies, including South Africa. These structural changes had profound implications for gender roles and economic activities. He shows how private commercial transactions in Africa have led to a significant departure from dependency on decaying state institutions, fostering new forms of political resource mobilization. This shift underscores the multifaceted roles women played during these periods and suggests that women's contributions were pivotal despite systemic disadvantages. Peters (2004, p.19) notes that economic restructuring has resulted in extensive local casual wage labor markets (Peters 2004, p.19; Bryceson 2002, p.11), indicating a broader depeasantization process that has altered rural livelihoods. However, the gendered implications of these changes are complex: Bryceson argues that while non-agricultural activities have become crucial for rural poverty alleviation in sub-Saharan Africa, they also reflect deeper social transformations (Peters 2004, p.14; Bryceson 2002, p.5). The role of women in economic development thus extends beyond mere participation; it involves navigating and adapting to these profound changes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the slave trade had profound effects on African development. The slave trade disrupted economic growth by fostering mistrust within communities as well as through external channels such as resource extraction. Nunn's research highlights that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in early trade with Europeans, suggesting that pre-existing institutional strength mitigated some adverse effects of the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). Despite this buffering effect, the overall impact on African economic growth remains significant. This dynamic underscores the enduring legacy of the slave trade, which continues to shape Africa's economic trajectory independently from colonial institutions.

The broader implications of these findings suggest that historical legacies of mistrust and institutional underdevelopment are deeply embedded in African societies. These factors hinder contemporary economic growth and complicate efforts at institutional reform and development. Understanding this complex interplay between historical events, such as the slave trade, and current economic conditions is crucial for devising effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable growth in Africa.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive systems that persisted long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Peters 2004, p.14). These institutions were often more profitable in densely settled areas where there was more to be extracted, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trades (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The profitability of extractive institutions in high-density population areas made them a viable option for European colonists, who exploited these regions extensively. This exploitation hindered economic development during the colonial era and left lasting impacts on African societies. The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era further entrenched economic disparities and limited the potential for growth (Peters 2004, p.14).

The legacy of mistrust and institutional underdevelopment, stemming from historical events like the slave trade, continues to hinder contemporary economic growth in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is deeply embedded in African societies and complicates efforts at institutional reform and development. The cultural impact of the slave trade, for instance, has created a pervasive atmosphere of mistrust that affects social cohesion and economic interactions. This historical legacy shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial-era practices on modern African economies.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This history reveals that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions, which were entrenched by European rulers who did not have enough time to dismantle them before independence. By understanding these historical patterns, policymakers can design more effective strategies for economic development. The reversal of fortune thesis, which argues that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, shows that colonial rule entrenched extractive regimes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective is consistent with the broader implications of these findings, suggesting that historical legacies of mistrust and institutional underdevelopment are deeply embedded in African societies. These factors hinder contemporary economic growth and complicate efforts at institutional reform and development.

The causal relationships involved in Africa's economic trajectory are more differentiated than previously recognized (Austin 2008, p.2). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence. Therefore, understanding this complex interplay between historical events, such as the slave trade, and current economic conditions is crucial for devising effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable growth in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
