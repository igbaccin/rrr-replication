The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic historiography and development studies. At stake is the extent to which contemporary economic outcomes can be attributed to institutional legacies dating back to colonial rule. The live disagreement revolves around whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive or productive, and how these institutions influenced long-term economic development. On one hand, some scholars argue that colonial powers established extractive institutions designed to exploit resources and labor with little regard for local development. This perspective posits that such institutions persist today, hindering economic growth. On the other hand, there are those who contend that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, suggesting that other factors, such as geographical conditions or cultural traits, play a more significant role in shaping economic trajectories.

The debate is further complicated by the persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended and their interaction with other historical forces. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) highlight how extractive institutions set up during colonialism remained intact post-independence, while Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) argue that improving long-run performance in Africa requires understanding the link between institutions and economic shrinking. This interplay of historical forces underscores the complexity of attributing contemporary economic outcomes solely to colonial institutional legacies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories by introducing extractive systems that persisted long after independence. These extractive institutions were often more profitable for European colonizers in areas with high population density, where forced labor and resource extraction were readily exploitable. For instance, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America facilitated forced labor systems, while Africa's relatively high population density created opportunities for slave trading to supply American plantations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions into the post-independence era is evident in the continuity of monopolies and regulations. For example, in Latin America, Spanish-imposed policies remained intact for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. further note that societies under extractive regimes typically denied education to the masses, thereby stunting economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional legacy continues to influence African economies today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic outcomes.

A detailed examination of specific periods and institutional changes in Africa reveals that economic growth trajectories are shaped by complex historical dynamics (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin (2008, p.1) argues for the importance of long-term historical analysis, asserting that causal relationships in African economic development are more nuanced than previously recognized. This perspective is supported by Austin's (2007, p.16) study on Ghana during 1874-1939, which highlights how shifts from land abundance to scarcity led to significant institutional changes in factor markets. The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor in Ghana exemplifies how localised scarcity can drive institutional innovation. The expansion of infrastructure, notably the tripling of motorable roads and increased mechanisation of transport, further illustrates how institutional changes can reshape economic landscapes. These historical examples underscore the importance of considering path dependence and local contexts when examining African economic development trajectories.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) and Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2019, p.11), argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. Both authors propose that colonialism in Africa evolved significantly over time, with different phases marked by distinct institutional frameworks (Author Year p.N). This evolution is marked by a shift from mercantilist to modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12). In the early phase, productive institutions were scarce even in Europe, making their establishment overseas unlikely. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions became viable options. Medical advances during this period also mitigated some constraints on European settlement, altering colonial policies towards Africa.

This temporal differentiation is crucial for understanding the varied economic outcomes across different regions and time periods in Africa. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.12) notes that by the late nineteenth century, colonial rulers had more options available to them, which could have led to different institutional trajectories depending on local contexts and policy choices (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This period also saw significant medical advances reducing disease-related constraints on European settlement in Africa. These changes did not uniformly result in productive institutions across the continent. This underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how different phases of colonialism influenced institutional development.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). The British approach to indirect rule facilitated educational development by welcoming missionaries of all denominations. This support for missionary activities resulted in higher primary-school enrolment rates compared to French colonies, which restricted non-Catholic missions. For instance, primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa were roughly double those in French West Africa by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). The varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants and native Africans also played a significant role in shaping long-run trajectories of wage income growth. This pattern highlights the importance of considering the complex interplay between colonial policies, educational development, and economic disparities when examining African economic growth trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development both during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4)(Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Colonial institutions often marginalized these contributions through patriarchal alliances with local chiefs. Women's roles in production and reproduction were central to the economy. The specific mechanisms through which these patriarchal alliances impacted women's economic contributions varied across regions, but all served to codify and systematize patriarchy. This systematic marginalization of women during colonial periods had long-lasting effects on African economies, as it limited the full potential of human capital development by excluding a large portion of the population from meaningful economic participation.

The slave trade had a direct adverse effect on African economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa. This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The internal channel, which involves the loss of human capital due to the forced removal of individuals from their communities, is particularly significant (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.28) argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. This is supported by evidence showing that societies with institutions sufficiently developed were able to facilitate trade with Europeans before the slave trade became dominant. The historical dependence on fishing and other factors correlated with distance from the coast are potential confounding variables, but instrumental variable regressions produce estimates qualitatively identical to ordinary least squares estimates (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This suggests that the impact of the slave trade is robust to different methodological approaches. The precise mechanisms by which the slave trade affected economic development are complex and multifaceted.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show how the extractive nature of colonial institutions, particularly those imposed on densely populated areas, was driven by profitability considerations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These institutions were designed to maximize resource extraction and labor exploitation, often at the expense of local development. Similarly, Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), highlighting the economic structures already in place during colonial rule. These findings underscore the persistent impact of historical institutional legacies on current economic conditions.

The persistence of these colonial institutions into post-independence eras has been a significant factor in Africa's economic development. Acemoglu et al. note that extractive state institutions established during colonial times continued to shape policies long after independence, such as the enduring monopolies and regulations in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional continuity suggests that the economic structures imposed by colonial powers have had lasting effects on African economies. Austin argues for a more nuanced understanding of these relationships, emphasizing the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to reveal recurring patterns due to similar situations and path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of trust in economic development is another critical aspect highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This cultural impact complements the institutional legacy, illustrating how historical events continue to influence contemporary social and economic dynamics. The persistence of extractive institutions and the erosion of trust both contribute to the challenges faced by African economies in achieving sustained growth.

Broadberry and Gardner suggest that improving long-run performance in Africa requires reducing the rate and frequency of economic shrinking rather than focusing solely on growing (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on the persistence of extractive institutions, which have historically hindered economic growth. Hopkins highlights the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, noting that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutional options were available (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is crucial for understanding the varying impacts of colonial institutions on different regions.

Both Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on the persistence of extractive institutions and Austin's call for a differentiated view of causal relationships point to a shared finding: colonial institutions have had a profound impact on African economic development. The key disagreement lies in whether this impact is primarily due to institutional persistence or to more complex, path-dependent historical processes. Nunn and Wantchekon provide the specific evidence that settles this dispute by showing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This cultural legacy, combined with the enduring extractive institutions, offers a comprehensive explanation for the economic challenges faced by African countries.
