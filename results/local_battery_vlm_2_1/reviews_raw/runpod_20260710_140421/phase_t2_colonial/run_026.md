Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, yet their precise impact remains a subject of intense debate among scholars. The central question is how the institutional legacies left by colonial powers have influenced Africa's economic development over time. At stake intellectually are competing explanations for Africa's current economic status and the relative importance of historical versus contemporary factors. One side argues that extractive institutions imposed during colonial rule have persisted, stifling economic growth. The other posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative. This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped Africa's economic trajectory. The causal mechanisms linking colonial institutions to economic outcomes are complex, with scholars highlighting both direct and indirect effects. Acemoglu et al. argue that high population density made extractive institutions more profitable during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These extractive institutions were established in densely settled areas where there was more to be extracted by European colonists. Nunn and Wantchekon observe that the slave trade cultivated a culture of mistrust within African societies, which endures today and impedes economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Both perspectives underscore the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic outcomes. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This view is supported by Hopkins, who notes that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.13). The implications are clear: to fully understand the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories, it is essential to consider the diverse historical contexts and institutional legacies that have evolved over time.

(2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories through the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions were more likely to be implemented in areas where they were profitable for European colonists, such as regions with high population density or abundant labor resources. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) illustrate how dense populations and prosperous areas created profit opportunities for Europeans, leading them to establish extractive institutions that exploited local resources. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) provide an example from Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact for most of the 19th century even after independence. The implications are clear: while extractive institutions were initially implemented to maximize colonial profits, their persistence has had lasting effects on economic development in African countries. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) remark that the correlation between these institutions and human capital indicates a broader mechanism through which extractive institutions delayed industrialization.

This period of colonial rule in Ghana is particularly illuminating because it highlights the broader mechanisms through which historical events and institutional changes significantly impact economic development as Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) jointly establish. Austin (2008, p.1) argues for examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur due to similar situations or path dependence. This perspective is crucial because it highlights how institutional changes during colonial periods can have enduring effects on economic trajectories. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) demonstrates that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This transformation was marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, illustrating how changes in resource availability can drive institutional adaptations. The underlying mechanism here is the interaction between resource availability and institutional responses, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on how effectively institutions adapt to changing circumstances. This dynamic interplay underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical context in which these changes occur.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and van Waijenburg (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) show that British rule in Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. This educational emphasis had implications for African economic growth trajectories. The British supported missionaries to facilitate their system of indirect rule, while the French restricted missionary activities. Consequently, primary-school enrolment rates were significantly higher in British colonies than in French colonies by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). This difference in educational development was driven by the differing colonial strategies toward mission schools, where the British promoted them and the French shut them down (Frankema 2012, p.4). The resulting disparity in educational attainment had profound implications for economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) advise that although educational investment is vital, it is not the only factor in economic success, as shown by varying income inequality among different ethnic groups in Kenya, which raises questions about the path-dependence nature of African economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while British colonial education systems may have laid a foundation for growth, other factors such as income distribution and long-term growth trajectories must also be considered.

Economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has triggered deep-rooted social changes and depeasantization (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Hopkins 2009, p.12). Bryceson (2002, p.11) and Hopkins (2009, p.12) jointly highlight that the shift towards nonagricultural activities is crucial for rural poverty alleviation. They document an increase in wage labor markets as well as the emergence of local casual wage labor, suggesting a broader economic transformation beyond simple agricultural shifts. This depeasantization process is driven by several factors, including population growth and land scarcity. Hopkins (2009, p.12) states that while colonial rule did not have enough time to dismantle unproductive institutions, it also did not establish productive ones (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The absence of productive institutions further complicates the economic restructuring process, as it limits the capacity for effective governance and institutional reform. This institutional void hinders the development of robust social safety nets and infrastructure, which are essential for supporting the transition from agricultural to nonagricultural activities. Consequently, while depeasantization offers a pathway out of rural poverty, its effectiveness is constrained by the legacy of colonial institutions that failed to foster productive economic environments. The interplay between social changes and institutional legacies underscores the complex nature of Africa's economic growth trajectories.

These institutions, which were more profitable in densely settled areas, aimed to maximize extraction and foster development. This legacy has had lasting effects on Africa's economic landscape, as Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these extractive policies remained intact long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of these institutions highlights their deep-rooted impact on African economies.

The historical context of colonialism reveals that the type of institutions established by European colonizers was influenced by mortality rates and population density. Acemoglu et al. argue that high mortality rates in certain colonies led to the implementation of extractive institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional framework hindered the development of productive economic environments, as extractive policies were prioritized over growth-oriented strategies. The persistence of these institutions into the independence era underscores their enduring influence on Africa's economic trajectory.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy further complicates economic development by eroding social capital, which is crucial for fostering trust and cooperation essential for economic growth. The adverse effects of the slave trades on economic development are well-documented, as Nunn shows that these historical events had a profound impact on Africa's economic trajectory (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical evidence underscores the long-term consequences of colonial institutions and practices on African economies.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that colonialism led to a decline in Africa's relative prosperity, is nuanced by Austin's argument that this thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). While Acemoglu et al. focus on the persistence of extractive institutions, Austin highlights the need for a more differentiated understanding of causal relationships. This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions and that African rulers did not have enough time to dismantle these unproductive systems (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The historical evidence from both perspectives shows that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape Africa's economic growth trajectories.
