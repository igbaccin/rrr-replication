The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a pivotal question in understanding the continent's historical development and contemporary challenges. At stake is the explanatory power of institutional theories versus geographic determinism in accounting for Africa's post-colonial economic performance. One position contends that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers have persistently hindered economic development. This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of these institutions on economic outcomes, suggesting that they have created barriers to growth that continue to affect African economies today. Another view posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, arguing for a more nuanced understanding of historical processes and their complex interactions with institutional frameworks. This debate is central to comprehending Africa's economic trajectory and the potential paths forward for sustainable development.

The extractive nature of colonial institutions has been extensively documented. Acemoglu et al. argue that high population density in African regions made extractive institutions more profitable for European colonists, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These institutions were designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development, thereby creating a legacy of underdevelopment that persists today. The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in the continuation of policies such as monopolies and regulations long after independence was achieved (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This enduring impact illustrates how colonial institutions have molded African economies by encouraging reliance on external resources and hindering self-sustaining growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that high-income regions in 1500 became low-income today due to colonial exploitation, has been challenged for oversimplifying the complex historical processes involved. Austin argues that this thesis compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the need for a more differentiated understanding of how colonial institutions interacted with local contexts over time. For instance, Hopkins notes that Olsson's argument about the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism sheds light on the varying impacts of colonial rule across different periods (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This nuanced perspective is crucial for accurately assessing the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic trajectories.

The legacy of colonial institutions extends beyond economic structures to cultural dimensions. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight how the slave trade, a significant aspect of colonial exploitation, has left a lasting impact on African societies by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This cultural dimension is integral to understanding the broader effects of colonial institutions, as it influences social cohesion and economic interactions. The persistence of this mistrust demonstrates how colonial policies have influenced economic structures and deeply embedded societal attitudes that continue to affect development.

The profitability of extractive institutions in regions with high population density and abundant labor resources drove Europeans to implement such systems. The persistence of these institutions has had profound implications for human capital development and industrialization. For instance, societies with extractive institutions typically did not invest in mass education, leading to low levels of human capital which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is further complicated by the enduring effects of these policies on social cohesion and trust. This cultural dimension, intertwined with institutional frameworks, creates a complex web that influences contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) argue that colonial institutions' impact on African economic growth is more nuanced than the "reversal of fortune" thesis suggests. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which significantly altered economic interactions and growth trajectories (Austin 2007, p.8; Austin 2008, p.2). The causal relationships involved are more differentiated than Acemoglu et al.'s formulations recognise, as they involve complex interplay between historical periods and local conditions. This differentiation is evident in Ghana's experience with land scarcity, where the Ashanti Confederacy Council of Chiefs banned the creation of new farms in 1939 due to indications of land scarcity (Austin 2007, p.16). The policy was abandoned in 1946 as it proved excessive. The process of mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana also played a significant role, with the length of motorable roads tripling from 2,241 miles (3,606 km) to 6,738 miles (10,841 km) in 1930-32 (Austin 2007, p.8). This mechanisation completed the process of goods transport in Ghana. Austin's work shows that colonial institutions shaped African economic growth through specific mechanisms tied to local conditions and historical periods, offering a more granular understanding of this complex relationship and its implications for contemporary economic outcomes.

The slave trade had an adverse effect on African economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the legacy of slavery has persisted in Africa through reduced levels of trust among its people. This mistrust is more than a cultural artifact; it has tangible economic consequences, as it obstructs cooperation and collective action essential for economic development. The slave trade's impact on African economies was multifaceted, involving both internal and external channels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn notes that the adverse effects of the slave trades are evident in the underdevelopment of precolonial polities, which hindered Africa's ability to facilitate trade and establish robust institutions even before colonial rule. This historical context is crucial for understanding Africa's economic trajectory, as it highlights how the legacy of slavery continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes by limiting the development of trust and institutional capacity. The adverse effects of the slave trades on African economies are evident in the underdevelopment of precolonial polities (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This is evident in the higher primary-school enrollment rates recorded in British West Africa compared to French West Africa, where missionary schools were shut down by the French colonial government (Frankema 2012, p.4). The British approach of welcoming missionaries from all denominations aligned with their system of indirect rule, which facilitated a more robust educational infrastructure than in other colonies. This pattern is qualified by the varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers and native Africans that the Kenyan case study illuminates; this disparity highlights how economic dynamics differ across regions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The educational legacy of British colonial rule thus had downstream implications for postcolonial African economies, as higher levels of education correlate with improved economic outcomes.

Hopkins and others (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) argue that economic history research in Africa has contributed to understanding institutional reforms but does not directly address how colonial institutions have shaped African economic growth trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.12) explains that the period of colonialism spanned significant shifts, from mercantilist forms where productive institutions were scarce even in Europe. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions coexisted, influenced by medical advances that facilitated European settlement. This shift underscores how different phases of colonial rule could have varying impacts on institutional development, with later periods potentially offering more opportunities for productive institutions to take root. Hopkins (2019, p.11) builds on this by highlighting the methodological challenges in using instrumental variables and path dependence to link historical events to current outcomes. These approaches can oversimplify complex historical processes, leading to a deterministic view that overlooks intervening influences. This perspective is echoed by Austin (2008, p.1), who notes how reliance on such methods may obscure the nuanced ways in which colonial institutions interacted with local contexts over time, potentially missing key factors that influenced economic trajectories.

Bryceson (2002, p.11) and Peters (2004, p.19) (Bryceson 2002, p.11) highlight the deep-rooted social changes and depeasantization that accompanied economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa. This restructuring did not solely involve economic shifts but also profound social transformations, including democratization and governance reforms. These authors emphasize a shift towards private commercial transactions, which has led to a significant departure from state institutions' patrimonial dependence for political resources. This process is marked by the emergence of negotiable land systems that, despite reports of flexibility, actually conceal processes of exclusion and class formation. Economic restructuring in Africa has altered production patterns and reshaped social structures, leading to new forms of inequality and marginalization. These changes have resulted in a complex interplay between economic and noneconomic spheres, where democratization and good governance become crucial for sustainable development. Peters (2004, p.19) further underscores how these shifts are often misinterpreted as mere survival strategies under pressure rather than structural transformations that fragment family and kin-based groups (Peters 2004, p.14). This nuanced understanding of economic restructuring in Africa suggests that the trajectory of African economic growth is intrinsically linked to social changes, which include democratization and governance reforms.

Colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive systems that persist to this day. Acemoglu et al. show that high population density made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This exploitation was more than a historical anomaly; it was a persistent feature that delayed industrialization by limiting human capital development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies these dynamics, highlighting that causal relationships are more complex and differentiated than often recognized. These extractive institutions were imposed and maintained due to path dependence and similar recurring situations over time (Austin 2008, p.1; Peters 2004, p.14).

The persistence of these colonial institutions into the post-independence era has been a critical factor in Africa's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive policies, such as monopolies and regulations, remained intact long after independence, forming the basis for current economic structures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Hopkins notes that while colonialism often entrenched non-productive institutions, the extent to which these were dismantled varied significantly. This variation is crucial in understanding why some African countries have struggled more than others with economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

The cultural impact of colonial institutions further complicates Africa's growth trajectory. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is rooted in historical experiences of exploitation and has profound implications for contemporary economic development. The internal channel of mistrust, stemming from intra-African conflicts and exploitation, is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the deep-seated nature of this issue (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3).
