The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates revolving around whether these institutions have been a persistent hindrance to development or if other factors are more significant. At the heart of this disagreement lies the question of how extractive institutions established during colonial rule have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. On one hand, some scholars argue that the persistence of extractive institutions, those designed to benefit colonizers at the expense of local populations, has been a primary barrier to African economic growth. These scholars contend that these institutions have stifled investment in human capital and infrastructure, thereby delaying industrialization and perpetuating poverty (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). On the other hand, critics challenge this perspective by arguing for more nuanced causal relationships between colonial institutions and economic outcomes and suggest that other factors might be at play. This ongoing debate underscores the complexity of African economic development and the need to understand the multifaceted impacts of colonial legacies on contemporary economic trajectories. The role of extractive institutions in hindering human capital investment is a pivotal point of contention, with some scholars emphasizing their enduring negative effects while others propose alternative explanations for Africa's economic stagnation.

This disagreement over the impact of extractive institutions on African economies is also tied to broader debates about the persistence of these institutions and how they have shaped contemporary outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions introduced during colonial rule have persisted into the post-colonial era, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). They suggest that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective aligns with North and Weingast's argument that credible commitment underpins growth. Institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.5). Other scholars suggest that the causal relationships involved are more complex than this view acknowledges. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests a more complex interplay between colonial institutions, economic development, and other factors such as disease and settlement patterns.

One key area of focus in this debate is the impact of extractive institutions on human capital investment. This perspective emphasizes the long-term effects of colonial policies on economic development and suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions has hindered Africa's ability to invest in education and infrastructure. This perspective is contested by scholars who advocate for more nuanced causal relationships between colonial institutions and economic outcomes. Austin contends that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than recognized (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests that while extractive institutions may have played a role in shaping African economies, other factors such as disease and settlement patterns also need to be considered.

The impact of extractive institutions on Africa's economic development is further complicated by the role of external factors. Acemoglu et al. note that high population density made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This perspective highlights how geopolitical and demographic factors influenced colonial policies and their subsequent effects on African economies. Additionally, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This argument suggests that external factors such as the slave trade have had enduring impacts on African economies beyond the direct effects of extractive institutions. The interplay between internal and external factors in shaping Africa's economic trajectory underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of colonial legacies and their multifaceted impacts on contemporary development.

For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.16). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings on the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28) illustrate how colonial legacies can create lasting institutional and cultural impacts that complicate the relationship between colonial institutions and economic development. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa by disrupting social structures and eroding community cohesion. The internal channel, where mistrust emerges from within communities due to historical trauma, is particularly significant and at least twice as large as the external channel, which involves interactions with outsiders. This finding underscores the deep-seated nature of the institutional changes wrought by the slave trade, which are not easily reversed through policy interventions alone.

The adverse effects of the slave trade on African economic development are further exacerbated by the historical evidence on selection during the slave trades. Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that societies with more developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with Europeans before the onset of large-scale slavery, suggesting that pre-existing institutional strengths could mitigate some of the negative impacts of the slave trade. This historical context highlights how the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape African economic development through institutional and cultural mechanisms that persist over generations.

British rule fostered more robust educational systems in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15). British indirect rule facilitated the development of formal education by welcoming missionaries and promoting missionary schools. This approach resulted in higher primary-school enrolment rates compared to French colonies, which restricted missionary activities (Frankema 2012, p.4). The differential impact of British colonial rule on educational systems is evident in the higher enrollment rates and lower government expenses per student in British West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.4). This educational advantage likely contributed to better long-term economic performance, as education is a key factor in human capital development. However, Broadberry and Gardner highlight that improving long-run performance in Africa requires not just growth but also reducing the frequency of economic shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17), suggesting that while education is crucial, it is not the sole determinant of economic success.

Hopkins' and Frankema and van Waijenburg's emphasis on the multifaceted nature of African economic history (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) suggests that while colonial institutions are significant, they are just one piece of a much larger puzzle. They suggest that the development trajectories of African economies cannot be solely attributed to the extractive nature of colonial rule, but rather are influenced by an array of historical and environmental factors. These scholars argue that during mercantilist imperialism, productive institutions were scarcely present in Europe itself, let alone overseas (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This implies that the initial colonial period did not offer a choice between productive and extractive institutions, challenging the notion that colonial rule inherently entrenched extractive regimes. The historical context of European settlement and medical advances further complicates this narrative: by the late 19th century, both productive and extractive institutional options were available (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This period also saw a reduction in disease-related constraints on European settlement, altering colonial policy towards Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg assert that African poverty has been influenced by varying degrees of income inequality between different groups (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This perspective suggests that the path-dependence nature of African economic development may have been overstated.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that secure property rights and financial innovations were pivotal in driving long-term economic growth in Europe. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly lowered transaction costs and bolstered the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework was instrumental in fostering a robust capital market and sustained economic expansion (North 1989, p.9). North also posits that these institutional developments emerged from specific historical contexts, such as the fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.3). The precise conditions under which such institutions emerge remain complex. The development of more sophisticated institutions to manage increasingly intricate interdependencies is far from automatic and requires a nuanced understanding of political and economic dynamics (North 1989, p.3). This complexity underscores how institutional change in Europe was driven by both endogenous factors within societies and exogenous shocks, suggesting that the path to robust property rights and financial systems can be highly contingent on historical circumstances.
