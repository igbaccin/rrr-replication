Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping Africa's economic growth trajectories, yet disagreement persists over their precise impact and mechanisms of influence. One live disagreement centres on the causal efficacy of colonial institutions versus other factors such as geography or mortality rates; another concerns whether colonial institutions are best understood as extractive regimes that persist after independence or as institutions that were already unproductive before they were implemented by European colonizers. The debate over these issues is vital for understanding historical economic development and for informing contemporary policy to address Africa's persistent poverty.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita. These extractive institutions persisted long after colonial rule ended, influencing economic development trajectories and perpetuating poverty in Africa. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) cite Young's work emphasizing that the control structures set up during the colonial era persisted into the independence period. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic growth, suggesting that the legacy of extractive institutions continues to hinder development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2002, p.48) also assert that the profitability of alternative policies influenced the establishment of extractive institutions. High population density made these institutions more profitable for Europeans, as it provided a supply of labor that could be forced into agriculture or mining. This interpretation underscores the economic rationale behind the implementation of extractive institutions and their role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. The persistence of these institutions has been a primary mechanism through which they delayed industrialization and economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.1; 2008, p.2) offers an alternative view, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective suggests that causal relationships are more differentiated than recognized in previous formulations. Austin's argument highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history, one that considers long-term historical contexts and path dependence.

Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) discusses how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins argues that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions, but also suggests that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle the unproductive institutions that were already in place. This view complements Acemoglu et al.'s perspective by acknowledging the enduring impact of colonial institutions while also considering the pre-existing conditions that shaped Africa's economic trajectory.

This discussion is crucial for understanding how colonial institutions have influenced Africa's economic growth and for developing policies to address persistent poverty.

Colonial institutions have shaped African economic growth trajectories by introducing extractive structures that persistently hindered development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. posit that the profitability of extractive institutions influenced European colonizers' choices in Africa and other regions, where high population density made forced labor systems and exploitation profitable.

This institutional persistence is evident in Latin America, where Spanish-imposed monopolies and regulations endured well into the 19th century, illustrating how colonial institutions can have long-lasting effects on economic trajectories.

The variation in these investments had profound implications for post-colonial economic development. Frankema (2012, p.15) and van Waijenburg (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) show that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial regimes. The British welcomed missionaries from all denominations, which facilitated the expansion of schools and educational infrastructure under their system of indirect rule. This approach contrasts with French policies in West Africa where missionary activities were restricted, leading to lower primary-school enrolment rates (Frankema 2012, p.4). Colonial education systems had a profound impact on African economic growth trajectories by influencing human capital development; this was particularly pronounced in British colonies, where educational investments laid the groundwork for future economic progress. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) note that varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans also influenced long-term growth trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while colonial education systems were instrumental in shaping human capital, the broader socio-economic dynamics within colonies also played a significant role. The differential educational investments made during colonial rule continue to resonate in contemporary African economic landscapes.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) investigate how specific historical events and institutional changes shaped economic development in Africa during the colonial period, particularly focusing on Ghana from 1874 to 1939. They argue for a more nuanced understanding of growth theories by examining them against long-term history (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This approach reveals that causal relationships are more differentiated than previously recognized, highlighting the importance of path dependence in economic development. For example, Austin (2007, p.16) shows how the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.8). This transformation was driven by the increasing mechanisation of goods transport, exemplified by the tripling of motorable roads and the significant rise in car and lorry imports. The emergence of localised scarcity in Ghana underscores how institutional changes, such as those resulting from increased population density, can drive economic development through the creation of new markets and labour dynamics. However, Austin (2007, p.16) also notes that these policies were sometimes excessive or ineffective, suggesting that while institutional changes are crucial, their implementation must be carefully managed to ensure sustainable growth. This nuanced view of historical events and institutional changes provides a deeper understanding of how economic development in Africa was shaped during the colonial period.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade generated a culture of mistrust within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This mistrust has profound implications for economic development. The mechanism by which this mistrust operates is through its impact on social capital, which is crucial for fostering cooperation and collective action in society. Nunn (2008, p.28) shows that the slave trade disrupted traditional African societies by removing large portions of the population, thereby weakening social networks and institutions (Nunn 2008, p.28). This disruption resulted in a diminished ability to form strong cooperative relationships necessary for effective economic activity. The impact is not merely historical; it persists today as a legacy that continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.28) further underscores the enduring nature of this mistrust by highlighting how precolonial state institutions, which were often undermined by the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.28), continue to influence current public goods provision and overall economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). In West Africa, the role of women in kinship, commerce, nascent capitalism, and colonialism was pivotal. This institutional framework diminished women's economic contributions by marginalizing their roles, which had previously been central to production and reproduction. The implication is that these historical developments have long-lasting effects on contemporary gender discrimination and economic outcomes. This finding underscores the significant role of women in economic activities during this period. The historical contributions of African women to economic development highlight the need for a more inclusive approach to understanding Africa's growth trajectories.

Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins's work suggests that understanding the evolution of institutions over time is crucial for grasping their impact on current economic conditions. This perspective is supported by Olsson, who points out that colonialism evolved significantly from mercantilist to modern forms, with different implications for institutional development. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, marking a shift from earlier periods when such choices were not available (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This evolution highlights the importance of temporal specificity in analyzing colonial institutions' effects on African economic growth. The implications are significant: if poverty is indeed a historical creation rather than an original condition, as Hopkins argues in another work (Hopkins 2019, p.6), then understanding and addressing the legacy of colonial institutions becomes paramount for economic development.

The impact of colonialism on African economies extends beyond extractive policies to include cultural dimensions. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy further complicates efforts to address economic disparities. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10; 2022, p.17) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. The shared finding is that colonial institutions significantly impacted Africa’s economic trajectory (Hopkins 2009, p.12). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms and extent to which these institutions affected growth.
