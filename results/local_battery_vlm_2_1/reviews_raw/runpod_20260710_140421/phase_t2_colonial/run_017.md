The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars, who debate whether these institutions were the primary drivers of underdevelopment or merely one factor among many. The central disagreement pivots around two main positions: those who argue that extractive colonial institutions persistently hindered economic development and those who contend that the impact of colonialism was more nuanced and varied over time and space; in addition, some scholars have proposed alternative explanations for Africa's economic trajectory.

The first stream of scholarship emphasizes the enduring effects of extractive colonial institutions on African economies. Acemoglu et al.'s seminal work argues that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies where mortality rates were high, which persisted after independence and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These extractive institutions, characterized by monopolies and regulations, were designed to exploit local resources for the benefit of colonial powers rather than foster long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. further note that the profitability of these institutions was influenced by factors such as population density and labor availability (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective underscores the lasting impact of colonial policies on Africa's economic underdevelopment, suggesting that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes. Hopkins' work complements this view by highlighting the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, noting that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present (Hopkins 2009, p.12). Hopkins argues that colonial rulers often lacked the time needed to dismantle existing unproductive institutions before decolonization. This stream of research collectively suggests that the institutional frameworks imposed during colonial rule have had profound and enduring effects on Africa's economic trajectory.

The next stream of scholarship offers a more differentiated view of the causal relationships between colonialism and African economic development, challenging the notion that extractive institutions were universally detrimental. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to reveal relationships that recur due to similar situations or path dependence. Austin's work suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economies was more complex and varied, influenced by a range of factors including local conditions and historical contingencies. Nunn and Wantchekon contribute to this stream by arguing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural dimension adds another layer of complexity to the understanding of how colonial institutions shaped African economic trajectories, highlighting the importance of social and psychological factors in addition to purely economic ones. These scholars collectively suggest that the impact of colonialism on Africa's economic development was multifaceted and influenced by a variety of factors beyond institutional legacies.

The final stream of research proposes alternative explanations for Africa's economic trajectory, focusing on factors other than colonial institutions. Broadberry and Gardner challenge previous characterizations of African economies, arguing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This perspective suggests that pre-colonial economic conditions were more robust than often portrayed, implying that colonial institutions may not have been the sole or primary factor in Africa's economic underdevelopment. These scholars collectively propose that alternative factors, such as pre-colonial economic conditions and post-independence policies, played significant roles in shaping African economic growth trajectories.

The extractive policies introduced by colonial institutions were often tailored to local conditions, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48; 2002, p.35) argue. These institutions were designed to maximize resource extraction from colonies and were particularly profitable in densely populated areas where labor could be coerced into agriculture or mining. This profit-driven approach led to the establishment of systems such as forced labor and slave trading, which had lasting impacts on economic development. For example, in Meso-America, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor facilitated forced labor systems, while high population density in Africa created opportunities for slave traders supplying labor to American plantations. This institutional framework not only shaped immediate economic outcomes but also influenced long-term trajectories by affecting human capital accumulation and industrialization. This persistence is evident in Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact for much of the nineteenth century. The persistence of extractive institutions does not uniformly determine current economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Nunn (Austin 2008, p.1; Nunn 2008, p.28) argue that colonialism's impact on African economic development is complex. The causal relationships involved are more nuanced than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests, which simplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths. For example, Austin highlights how economic growth theories should be examined against long-term history to reveal recurring relationships due to similar situations or path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the need for a more differentiated understanding of Africa's economic development. Nunn (2008, p.28) supports this by showing that precolonial institutions, such as centralized state structures, played a crucial role in facilitating trade and economic interactions with Europeans during the early slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). These institutions were essential for societies to engage in commerce, suggesting that Africa's economic trajectory was influenced by its institutional foundations well before colonial rule. However, Nunn also notes that the adverse effects of slave trades on African economies persisted beyond the immediate colonial period and continue to impact public goods provision today (Nunn 2008, p.28). This implies that while precolonial institutions were important, they did not fully mitigate the long-term economic damage caused by the slave trades. The persistence of these negative impacts underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional legacies on Africa's economic development trajectories.

Austin and other scholars (Austin 2007, p.16; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) focus on various aspects of economic performance in colonial Africa, including institutional changes and infrastructure development. Their work shows that the economic landscape of colonial Africa was shaped by significant shifts in land use and transportation networks. Austin highlights that during 1874-1939, Ghana experienced a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.16). This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, indicating that economic changes were not merely reactive but also proactive. This finding underscores a nuanced view of colonial Africa's economic performance: while institutional changes and infrastructure development were pivotal, the region's economic trajectory was also influenced by broader factors such as shifts in land use and transportation networks. This implies that while colonial institutions played a role, the broader economic context, including institutional changes and infrastructure development, was crucial to understanding Africa's economic trajectory.

The broader economic context, including poverty and development, was influenced by these historical factors. Hopkins (2009, p.12) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) examine the evolution of colonial institutions over time, noting that early mercantilist imperialism did not offer productive institutions as an option, whereas later phases saw both productive and extractive institutions present. This temporal shift in institutional frameworks suggests that the impact of colonial rule on African economies varied significantly depending on when and how colonies were established. The period of European settlement was pivotal: medical advances allowed for greater European presence in Africa, altering colonial policies and potentially influencing the types of institutions implemented (Hopkins 2009, p.12). However, Hopkins notes that colonial rulers did not always have sufficient time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, which complicates the direct linkage between colonial rule and extractive regimes (Hopkins 2009, p.13). This dynamic is further complicated by Frankema and van Waijenburg's observation that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They emphasize that the East-West gap in economic development is influenced by varying degrees of income inequality, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, the broader socio-economic dynamics within colonies were equally crucial in shaping long-term growth trajectories. Therefore, understanding Africa's economic history requires considering both the institutional legacies of colonial rule and the complex social and economic interactions that occurred during and after colonization.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This enduring mistrust is rooted in historical experiences where communities were often betrayed by their own members during the slave raids, creating an environment where trust was difficult to establish or maintain. The paper shows that the internal channel of mistrust, stemming from within African societies, is at least twice as large as external factors. This internal mistrust has had a profound impact on economic growth trajectories in Africa, as it affects social cohesion and cooperation, which are crucial for economic development. This codification further exacerbated mistrust within communities, as traditional power structures were manipulated to maintain control. The systematic marginalization of women in economic activities, coupled with the entrenched culture of mistrust, has created a complex social dynamic that continues to hinder economic growth. The implications of these findings are significant, as they suggest that addressing the legacy of mistrust and gender discrimination is essential for fostering sustainable economic growth in Africa.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that persist to this day. Acemoglu et al. argue that the extractive institutions imposed during colonial rule were designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that colonial policies often entrenched pre-existing unproductive institutions rather than implementing productive ones (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, has significantly hindered Africa's economic growth. Acemoglu et al. provide historical evidence showing that extractive state institutions established during colonial rule remained intact long after the end of colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional continuity has created a cycle where low levels of human capital and limited industrialization are perpetuated, directly impacting current income per capita.

The legacy of colonial institutions is further complicated by the entrenched culture of mistrust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade, facilitated by colonial powers, fostered a pervasive mistrust among Africans (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This mistrust has had lasting effects on economic development, as it undermines social capital and hinders cooperative economic activities. The implications of this mistrust are profound, as it not only affects intra-African relations but also influences broader economic policies and governance structures. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies these complex historical dynamics by compressing different periods into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This simplification can obscure the nuanced ways in which colonial institutions have shaped African economies over time.

The economic impact of colonial institutions is also evident in the varying levels of per capita income across Anglophone Africa. Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This finding challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance during this period, highlighting the need for a more differentiated understanding of colonial legacies. The persistence of extractive institutions, coupled with the culture of mistrust and gender discrimination, has created a complex social dynamic that continues to hinder economic growth in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This multifaceted legacy underscores the importance of addressing both institutional reforms and cultural factors to foster sustainable economic development.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that colonial institutions have had a profound impact on African economic trajectories. Extractive institutions, entrenched during colonial rule, persist today, shaping current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The precise remaining disagreement centres around the extent to which these institutional legacies are deterministic versus malleable. Austin argues that the causal relationships involved in Africa's economic trajectory are more differentiated than commonly recognized, suggesting a need for nuanced historical analysis (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate can be settled by examining specific historical evidence such as the persistence of extractive policies post-independence, which shows that these institutions have remained largely unchanged despite shifts in political regimes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. provide examples where monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers remained intact for decades after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical continuity underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic growth trajectories.
