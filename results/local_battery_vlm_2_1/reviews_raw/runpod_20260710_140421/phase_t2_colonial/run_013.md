The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that engages scholars across disciplines. At stake is the understanding of why some regions developed rapidly while others stagnated, and how historical legacies continue to influence contemporary economic performance. This debate centers on the enduring effects of colonial institutions, with one position asserting that extractive institutions imposed by colonizers have persisted and hindered growth. Another perspective advocates for a more nuanced view of causality, highlighting differentiated causal relationships over time.

Institutional persistence is a key theme in this debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that extractive institutions set up during colonial rule endured long after independence, shaping current economic outcomes. This is evident as the full panoply of monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after Latin American independence for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.12) notes that Olsson's argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s in recognizing non-productive institutions under colonialism. Olsson suggests that colonial rulers lacked sufficient time to dismantle existing unproductive institutions, indicating an alternative dynamic at play.

The profitability of extractive institutions further underscores their persistence. High population density made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This economic logic drove the establishment and maintenance of these institutions, as they were seen as profitable ventures by colonizers. The correlation between institutions and human capital reflects that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not obtain education, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The relationship between colonial institutions and contemporary economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) emphasize the persistence of these institutions into the independence era, while Austin (2008, p.2) argues that causal relationships are more differentiated than recognized in existing formulations. This complexity suggests that a single narrative of institutional persistence may oversimplify the historical and economic dynamics at play.

The debate extends to the cultural impacts of colonial institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3)(Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly affects contemporary development. This cultural legacy is intertwined with the economic legacies of colonial institutions, highlighting how historical experiences shape both social and economic outcomes.

The role of inequality in shaping institutional development is another critical aspect. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic illustrates how colonial institutions persisted and evolved to maintain elite advantages, influencing long-term economic trajectories.

Institutional reforms informed by the new economic history of Africa can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

These extractive institutions were established in colonies where they were more profitable for Europeans to implement them, such as in high population density areas. In these regions, the profitability of extractive institutions was enhanced by a readily available labor supply, making it easier to exploit local resources and populations. This dynamic highlights how colonial powers strategically implemented extractive policies based on economic considerations, which in turn shaped the long-term economic development of African colonies. The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence results from their enduring structural influence on governance and economic policy.

The persistence of extractive institutions is further illustrated by their enduring impact on governance structures. This continuity underscores how deeply entrenched these institutional frameworks were, making it challenging for newly independent states to implement meaningful reforms that could foster economic growth. Colonial institutions' extractive nature shaped immediate economic outcomes and laid the groundwork for long-term economic stagnation by inhibiting the development of inclusive and productive institutions.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies African economic history by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This perspective is crucial for understanding African economic development because it highlights the importance of examining growth theories against long-term historical contexts. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions led to Africa's underdevelopment by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few, often overlooks the nuanced local changes and specific periods that shaped African economies. Austin underscores this point by focusing on the shift from land abundance to scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, which triggered an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.16). This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The localised changes in Ghana illustrate how specific historical contexts can lead to significant economic shifts, even within a broader colonial framework. Austin also notes that these institutional changes varied across Africa based on local conditions (Austin 2008, p.2). This variability underscores the complexity of African economic development and the need for more granular analyses that consider localised factors. For instance, the ban on new cocoa farms in Ashanti in 1939 highlights how land scarcity could lead to policy changes that aimed to manage resources more effectively (Austin 2007, p.16). The subsequent abandonment of this policy in 1946 further illustrates the adaptive nature of these institutions, demonstrating that economic policies evolved in response to changing conditions. This adaptability is crucial for understanding how African economies have responded to various challenges over time and highlights the importance of considering localised changes when examining economic development trajectories.

The evolution of secure property rights and financial institutions underpins long-term economic success. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the development of these institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering sustained economic growth and political freedom. The creation of the Bank of England, for example, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets which are crucial for economic growth. However, the development of such institutions is not automatic but rather depends on complex interactions and bargaining processes within society. North notes that even with third-party enforcement, issues like opportunism and cheating can complicate the evolution of sophisticated institutions (North 1989, p.3). This complexity means that while secure property rights and financial institutions are necessary for economic growth, their development requires specific historical and political conditions to overcome these challenges. The implication is that African economies, which often lack these institutional foundations, may face significant barriers to achieving sustained growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.17) show that institutions shaped by inequality can restrict economic opportunities for the broader population, which is adjacent to how colonial institutions may have influenced African economic growth trajectories. In Latin America, extreme inequality allowed elites to preserve their elite status over time by limiting access to economic opportunities for the broader population. This institutional framework, which favored elites, resulted in a path of institutional development that may have affected growth trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12).

Bryant (2006, p.12) highlights how Eurocentric views often oversimplify historical differences and ignore the nuances of political institutions across different societies. The revisionist view is empirically suspect because it does not consider the political context in which institutions operate, which is crucial for understanding economic development. This suggests that the nature of political institutions and the balance of class forces play a critical role in shaping development trajectories (Bryant 2006, p.17). The implication is that African economies, which may have faced similar challenges with patrimonial systems during colonial rule, would require substantial institutional reforms to overcome these barriers.

This cluster of literature discusses progress and challenges in African economic history but does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins (2009, p.12) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective is shared by those who believe that understanding the past can guide future policy decisions, particularly in addressing the historical creation of poverty through foreign capitalism (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.6). Hopkins (2009, p.12) points out that colonial rule did not have enough time to dismantle existing unproductive institutions and that this period was marked by a significant reversal of fortune for Africa. Hopkins (2019, p.11) highlights the challenges in using instrumental variables to understand these historical influences, noting that large leaps of faith across centuries can produce a deterministic view that overlooks intervening factors. This suggests that while colonial institutions may have played a role, their impact is not straightforward and requires careful consideration of multiple historical influences (Hopkins 2019, p.11). The complexity of African economic history underscores the need for nuanced approaches to institutional reforms, recognizing both the legacy of colonial rule and the dynamic nature of economic development.

The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists in Africa today and significantly impacts contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) estimate this effect to be large enough to account for at least half the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Historical evidence also shows that societies with well-developed institutions were more likely to engage in early commodity trades with Europeans before the onset of large-scale slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). These findings suggest that pre-existing institutional strengths may have mitigated some of the adverse effects of the slave trade, as societies with developed trading institutions had more resilient economic foundations. This interaction between pre-existing institutional strengths and the legacy of the slave trade highlights the complex interplay between historical factors and current economic outcomes in Africa.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by perpetuating extractive systems that hindered development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that the extractive institutions established during colonial rule persisted long after independence, entrenching policies that limited economic opportunities for the majority. This institutional persistence is evident in the enduring structures and practices inherited by post-colonial states from their colonial predecessors. Austin (2008, p.2) agrees with this perspective, highlighting the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to reveal recurring relationships due to path dependence. Acemoglu et al.'s perspective on institutional persistence faces critique. Hopkins (2009, p.13) notes that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, suggesting a more nuanced view of institutional change during the colonial period.

The legacy of colonial institutions in Africa has also manifested through cultural and social dimensions that continue to impact economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and significantly affects economic development. This cultural dimension complements the institutional perspective by illustrating how historical events have shaped contemporary social norms and attitudes toward trust, which are crucial for economic growth. Austin's critique (Austin 2008, p.2) underscores that while the reversal of fortune thesis is valuable, it oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths.

The enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic trajectories is further supported by evidence from other regions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12) demonstrate that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This finding parallels Acemoglu et al.'s argument (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8) about the extractive nature of colonial institutions in Africa, suggesting a broader pattern where institutional frameworks designed to benefit a privileged few hinder overall economic development.

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that colonial institutions have had a profound and lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that long-term historical analysis reveals recurring relationships due to path dependence, which supports the idea that colonial institutions have shaped current economic outcomes. A precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which colonial rulers actively entrenched extractive regimes or inherited and perpetuated pre-existing unproductive institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.13) argues that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, suggesting a more nuanced view of institutional change during the colonial period. This disagreement is settled by examining the specific historical evidence of institutional persistence in post-colonial states, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which demonstrates that extractive policies remained intact long after independence, providing a clear measurement of the enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic trajectories.
