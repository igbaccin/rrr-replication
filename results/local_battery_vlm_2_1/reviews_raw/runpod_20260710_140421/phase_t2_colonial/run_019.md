The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories has been a subject of intense debate among scholars, with significant implications for understanding the continent's development path. The central question is whether and how colonial institutions have influenced Africa's economic trajectory, either by creating enduring institutional frameworks that persist to this day or by setting in motion processes that have had lasting effects on economic outcomes. One position argues that extractive institutions imposed during colonial rule have left a legacy of poor governance and economic underdevelopment. Another suggests that the impact of colonial institutions is more nuanced, with different historical periods and paths playing crucial roles. A third view posits that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly affects economic development. These perspectives highlight the complexity of understanding how colonial legacies continue to shape contemporary African economies.

The legacy of extractive institutions established during colonial rule has been extensively studied for its impact on current economic outcomes in Africa. Acemoglu et al. assert that European colonizers introduced extractive institutions where high mortality rates made settlement difficult (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions, designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development, have persisted beyond the colonial era and continue to hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.13). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely populated areas, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these institutions has resulted in low levels of human capital, which in turn delays industrialization and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin agrees with the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, but argues that causal relationships are more differentiated than previously recognized (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.1).

The role of colonial institutions is further complicated by the impact of the slave trade on African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade has had a profound effect on contemporary levels of trust within Africa, which in turn affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). Their findings suggest that the internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, is at least twice as large as the external channel. This highlights the enduring impact of historical events on current social and economic conditions. The persistence of extractive institutions has also been linked to the maintenance of elite status over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman note that where there was extreme inequality, institutions advantaged elites and limited access to economic opportunities for much of the population. This institutional framework has contributed to the persistence of inequality and hindered broader economic development.

The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how understanding colonial legacies can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). Hopkins argues that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the considerable disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. This perspective underscores the importance of differentiating between various historical periods and paths when examining the impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The debate surrounding the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of historical legacies and their enduring effects on contemporary economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. They note that extractive institutions were often more profitable for European colonizers in densely populated areas, leading to the establishment of systems designed to exploit local labor and resources. These extractive institutions persisted long after colonial rule ended, forming the basis of post-colonial governance structures. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis suggests that these extractive institutions delayed industrialization and created a feedback loop where low levels of human capital reinforced the persistence of extractive governance (Author Year p.N).

The slave trades of Africa had an adverse effect on economic development, challenging those who attribute underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3)(Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) propose that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists today, significantly impacting economic development. The mechanism behind this is multifaceted: both internal and external channels are important, but the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that the internal dynamics within African societies, shaped by historical experiences of exploitation and betrayal, have had a profound impact on contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.28) further elaborates that precolonial state institutions played a crucial role in facilitating trade with Europeans, highlighting how the selection process during the slave trades affected which societies were able to engage in trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). However, the legacy of these historical events is complex: while some societies developed sufficiently to engage in trade, the overall impact on economic development was deeply negative. This underscores the enduring effects of historical institutions and practices on Africa's contemporary economic trajectory.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more than other colonial regimes (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This educational development was driven by the British approach to indirect rule, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations. The British strategy contrasts with French policies that restricted missionary activities and shut down schools (Frankema 2012, p.4).

This difference in educational policy had tangible effects on primary-school enrollment rates. In 1938, British West Africa had a higher enrollment rate (7.1 percent) compared to French West Africa (3.6 percent). The varying impacts of colonial educational policies highlight how different colonial strategies shaped African societies and their economic trajectories, with British rule contributing to higher enrollment rates.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that the Kenyan case illustrates how differences in income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans influenced long-term growth trajectories. This suggests that while formal education was promoted under British rule, its benefits were not uniformly distributed (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The implications of these educational disparities are profound: they underscore the complex interplay between colonial policies, educational development, and economic outcomes in Africa.

Bryceson (2002, p.11) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) both demonstrate that African women's economic contributions have been critical to growth trajectories during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in sub-Saharan Africa. Bryceson's work highlights the deep-rooted social changes brought about by economic restructuring, including depeasantization and shifting labor dynamics. In particular, the emergence of local casual wage labor markets has become increasingly important for rural poverty alleviation as land availability contracts. These alliances resulted in the systematization and codification of patriarchy across African societies, even in segregationist South Africa. The implications are profound for understanding economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack show that this marginalization was reinforced through colonial policies, which further disadvantaged women by limiting their access to resources and opportunities. This systematic marginalization had downstream effects on the economic contributions of African women. Bryceson's analysis shows that rural poverty alleviation strategies must account for these historical dynamics if they are to be effective in addressing contemporary challenges.

Austin’s analysis of Ghana’s economic trajectory illustrates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis needs to account for different historical paths and periods. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This shift was driven by increasing population density and the intensification of cocoa cultivation, which created localised scarcity even in regions like Ashanti. The development of motorable roads and the importation of vehicles further mechanized goods transport, completing the process of mechanisation except for farm-to-local-market transit (Austin 2007, p.8). However, the effectiveness of these institutional changes was not uniform; policies such as the 1939 ban on new farms in Ashanti proved excessive and largely ineffective, highlighting the complexities of implementing land use regulations. This nuanced approach underscores the importance of considering historical contexts and institutional dynamics in understanding economic growth trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.9) examine how institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They argue that the financial revolution was pivotal to long-term success and that institutions played a necessary role in enabling economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated both a robust capital market and sustained economic growth. However, North also acknowledges that developing complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is not automatic; it requires addressing issues like opportunism and cheating (North 1989, p.3), which complicates the straightforward path from primitive societies to modern Western economies.

Pamuk (2007, p.16) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Pamuk 2007, p.16) examine how historical events and institutional differences shaped long-term economic disparities within Europe. They show that the Black Death in 14th-century Europe initiated significant structural changes, altering urbanization rates and wage gaps between regions. This divergence was not solely due to later developments but was deeply rooted in the institutional responses to the plague. The Black Death led to increased wages and a shift in labor dynamics, which in turn influenced economic development trajectories. These historical events highlight how exogenous shocks can catalyze institutional changes that persist over centuries.

Pamuk (2007, p.16) emphasizes that the Black Death triggered a decline in interest rates, which had long-term effects on economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.21). This institutional change was not an isolated event but part of a broader shift in economic conditions. The persistence of these institutions suggests that historical events can set in motion institutional frameworks that perpetuate economic disparities over time.

The implications for Africa are clear: exogenous shocks like the Black Death or colonial interventions can reshape institutions. These changes can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on how they alter property rights and access to economic opportunities. The long-term effects of such shocks underscore the importance of understanding historical context in assessing current economic conditions.

Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2019, p.11) argue that significant progress has been made in understanding Africa's economic history through institutional reforms and the new economic history of Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.12) notes that the colonial period was marked by distinct phases, with the late nineteenth century seeing both productive and extractive institutions as viable options for European settlers. This period also witnessed medical advances that facilitated European settlement. These developments highlight how colonial rule shaped African institutions, which in turn influenced economic growth trajectories.

One key mechanism through which colonial institutions affected Africa is via their impact on property rights and economic opportunities. Hopkins (2019, p.11) highlights the challenges of relying solely on instrumental variables and path dependence to understand historical influences on current outcomes. These approaches often overlook intervening factors that could have significantly altered economic trajectories.

This perspective aligns with the broader understanding that historical events, such as colonial interventions, can set in motion institutional frameworks that either facilitate or hinder economic growth. By examining these dynamics, researchers can better understand how Africa's current economic conditions are rooted in its historical context. The nuances of this history underscore the need for comprehensive analyses that consider both long-term and immediate factors influencing African economic development.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is deeply intertwined with the persistence of extractive systems introduced during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that these control structures set up by European colonists persisted long after independence, forming the basis for current-day institutional frameworks. The continuity of extractive institutions is evident in the maintenance of monopolies and regulations that were created by colonial powers and remained intact post-independence, as seen in Latin America and other regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional persistence underscores how historical events set in motion long-term economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight similar dynamics, noting that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. The security of property rights, prevalence of corruption, structures of the financial sector, investment in public infrastructure and social capital, and entrepreneurial inclinations are all factors influenced by these extractive institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). In this context, African economic development is fundamentally shaped by institutional legacies that hinder growth.

However, the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonialism led to a decline in African prosperity relative to other regions, has been challenged. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognised. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.13), who notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The historical dependence on slave trades, as well as the differential impact of mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, reveal a more nuanced picture of how colonial institutions influenced African economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This nuance is further highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3), who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa. This persistent mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, illustrating how historical events continue to shape contemporary economic conditions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
