Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, but there is a live disagreement over whether these institutions are primarily extractive and detrimental to long-term development, or if their impact has been more nuanced. The debate centres on the extent to which colonial legacies, particularly institutional frameworks, have either stifled or facilitated economic progress. Scholars argue that colonial powers established extractive institutions designed to exploit resources and promote local development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These institutions persist today and continue to hinder growth by perpetuating economic inefficiencies and social inequalities. Some researchers contend that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic outcomes is more complex, influenced by a variety of historical factors and not solely determined by institutional frameworks (Hopkins 2009, p.13; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This complexity suggests that while colonial institutions have had significant impacts, their effects are mediated by other variables such as cultural and geographical factors. Understanding this debate is crucial for grasping the underlying mechanisms that drive economic development in Africa, as it highlights the enduring influence of historical legacies on contemporary economic performance.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by introducing institutional changes that persist long after independence. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, for instance, created enduring structures that hindered economic development. These extractive institutions were particularly profitable in densely populated areas where there was more to exploit, leading to the implementation of forced labor systems and other exploitative practices (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional framework extracted resources and stifled local human capital development as masses under these regimes typically did not have access to education. The lack of human capital further delayed industrialization, creating a vicious cycle where economic growth was systematically impeded by the very institutions meant to govern it (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by historical evidence showing that extractive state institutions persisted well into the independence era, such as the enduring monopolies and regulations in Latin America that were initially created by Spain and remained intact for most of the nineteenth century.

The importance of institutions in driving economic development is further underscored by Europe's growth trajectories (North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.30)). North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and promoting economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated a robust capital market and sustained economic growth. The development of these institutions was complex; it required addressing interdependencies and mitigating opportunism (North 1989, p.3). North's work underscores that while third-party enforcement can help manage these complexities, it is not sufficient on its own to ensure institutional progress. The implication for African economic growth trajectories is that the legacy of colonial institutions, which often lacked robust property rights and secure transaction frameworks, could have significantly impeded long-term development.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) suggest that examining economic growth theories through the lens of long-term history reveals recurring relationships due to situational similarities and path dependence. The colonial period in Africa was marked by significant institutional changes driven by the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity (Austin 2008, p.2). This shift led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, notably the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, population density and land scarcity influenced policy decisions, such as the Ashanti Confederacy Council's ban on new farms in 1939. This institutional change highlights how localised scarcity can drive market developments that support economic growth. The effectiveness of these policies varied, as seen in the subsequent abandonment of the farm creation ban in 1946 (Austin 2007, p.16). The implications for African economic growth trajectories are clear: while colonial institutions could foster certain market developments, their impact was contingent on local conditions and policy effectiveness.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms provides valuable insights into the historical context of Africa's development, but it does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins notes that the nature of colonialism evolved over time, with early mercantilist phases lacking productive institutions and later periods seeing more structured imperial control. This temporal variation in colonial rule suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was not uniform across Africa. Overlapping this view, Hopkins highlights that reliance on path dependence can obscure the complex interplay of factors influencing African development. A key implication is that historical analyses must account for the dynamic nature of institutional changes over time and their contingent effects on economic outcomes. Hopkins also notes that poverty in Africa was a historical creation shaped by European capitalism's exploitative mechanisms. This perspective underscores the need to consider how colonial institutions failed to foster growth and actively created conditions that hindered it. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for appreciating the multifaceted role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic trajectories.

British colonial rule stands out for its significant impact on education development in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Frankema (2012, p.15) and van Waijenburg (2012) assert that British indirect rule facilitated missionary activities, leading to higher primary-school enrollment rates compared to French colonies. This educational investment supported the colonial system (Frankema 2012, p.4). The divergence in educational trajectories between British and French West Africa illustrates how colonial policies shaped long-term economic outcomes. Indigenous state institutions played a crucial role in administering public goods like education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the success of colonial education initiatives depended on pre-existing administrative structures. The impact of colonial education policies extended beyond immediate enrollment rates; they influenced broader economic dynamics by shaping income inequality patterns and long-term growth trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that differences in wage income growth between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans highlight the complex interplay of commercialization and inequality. This interplay underscores the need for future research to focus on the nuanced effects of colonial education policies on African economic development.

Nunn (2008, p.28) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) highlight that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust, which had direct adverse effects on economic development. They show that this mistrust persists to this day and contributes significantly to contemporary underdevelopment; it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mechanism operates through both internal and external channels, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn 2008, p.28; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). The internal effects are driven by the disruption of social capital within African societies, while the external effects stem from the exploitation and extraction of resources. Nunn argues that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, contra those who might attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive systems that persisted long after independence. Acemoglu et al. show that high population density and abundant labor made extractive institutions more profitable for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This led to the establishment of forced labor systems and slavery, which significantly impacted economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in the enduring structures and practices inherited by post-colonial states (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). For example, the monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence in Latin America for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The legacy of colonial institutions extends beyond economic structures to cultural factors, such as mistrust. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact is intertwined with institutional underdevelopment, creating a complex web of factors that continue to influence economic outcomes. The historical dependence on fishing and distance from the coast are potentially correlated with distance from the coast, underscoring how the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3).

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into institutional reforms that could help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins highlights that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, showing that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is crucial for understanding the different paths African economies have taken. Austin argues that examining growth theories against long-term history reveals relationships that recur because situations are similar as well as due to path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is evident in both the extractive systems they established and the cultural legacies they left behind. The persistence of these institutions, coupled with the enduring effects of the slave trade, has significantly impacted Africa's economic development. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights and economic growth are fostered by the evolution of institutions governing public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.5). The historical evidence shows that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. The implication is that colonial institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in comparing the persistence of extractive institutions across different colonial regimes and their long-term effects on economic development, drawing on measurements such as GDP per capita and institutional quality indices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3).
