The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that hinges on the extent to which historical institutional legacies influence contemporary economic performance. At stake intellectually is whether colonial institutions have left an enduring imprint on Africa's development path, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or post-colonial policies have been more determinative. One position holds that colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive in nature, have persisted and significantly impeded economic growth. Another perspective posits that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, arguing instead for a more nuanced understanding of historical causality that considers multiple, interdependent factors. The debate revolves around whether institutional legacies from colonial rule can be understood as a primary driver of Africa's current economic challenges. This is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.8) argument that extractive institutions have persisted long after the end of colonial regimes; meanwhile, Austin (2008, p.2) argues for more differentiated causal relationships in growth theories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) report, “The economic profitability of alternative policies.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) research demonstrates that colonial institutions have significantly influenced African economic growth trajectories by introducing extractive systems. These institutions were implemented when high population density made them economically viable for European colonizers. This pattern is evident in regions like Meso-America, where abundant labor facilitated forced labor systems, and Africa, where dense populations created opportunities for slave trading. This persistence is a key factor in understanding why many African countries still grapple with economic challenges today. These institutions influence human capital development as well as economic structures. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) suggest that extractive institutions hindered education access for the masses, thereby delaying industrialization. This delay in industrialization is a critical downstream implication of colonial institutional legacies, as it directly affects a country's capacity to achieve sustained economic growth.

The importance of secure property rights in fostering long-term economic success is further emphasized by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30), who show how England's economic development was shaped by this institutional framework. The English financial revolution of the 17th century exemplified this dynamic; the establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. This institutional framework secured property rights and fostered a robust capital market essential for sustained economic development.

The evolution of these institutions was driven by political dynamics that responded to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). These crises necessitated trade-offs between the monarch and Parliament, leading to the creation of new institutions that ensured long-term stability. This process underscores the importance of institutional adaptation in response to economic challenges, a mechanism that contrasts with neoclassical theories focusing on population and savings as primary determinants (North 1989, p.3).

North also emphasizes that complex institution development requires overcoming issues like opportunism and cheating (North 1989, p.3). This insight suggests that while institutional evolution can drive economic growth, it is contingent on effective enforcement mechanisms. The complexity of these interactions implies that economic growth is influenced by intricate political and institutional dynamics.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This critique underscores the need to consider long-term history when examining growth theories. By doing so, one can reveal relationships that recur due to similar situations or path dependence. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) highlights how shifts in land abundance and scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.8). This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The shift from abundant to scarce land resources necessitated new institutional arrangements, illustrating how changes in resource availability can drive institutional evolution. This differentiation suggests that while resource scarcity can prompt institutional change, the specific nature of these changes is context-dependent and influenced by a variety of factors beyond simple economic determinants. Therefore, understanding the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories requires a nuanced approach that acknowledges the complexity and diversity of historical experiences and their interaction with long-term structural conditions.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10); (Frankema 2012, p.15) establish that the impact of colonial rule on African education varied significantly among different colonial powers. British colonial policies were more conducive to educational development in sub-Saharan Africa than French or Belgian regimes, which often restricted missionary activities. For example, the post-World War division of German Togoland resulted in markedly divergent educational trajectories: primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa (7.1 percent) were nearly double those in French West Africa (3.6 percent) by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). This disparity is attributed to the British policy of indirect rule, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support colonial administration, whereas the French maintained stricter control over missionary activities and education spending was higher for fewer students (Frankema 2012, p.4). The different educational policies influenced long-term economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner show that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, indicating a more robust economic foundation than previously characterized (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that British colonial education policies may have contributed to better economic performance in these regions. Broadberry and Gardner also note that improving long-term economic performance in Africa will require reducing the frequency of economic shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). These findings underscore the complex interplay between colonial educational policies and economic development trajectories in sub-Saharan Africa; they suggest that while British rule fostered better educational outcomes and economic growth above subsistence, these gains were not always sustained.

The literature on African economic history and poverty highlights diverse factors influencing Africa's development trajectory, including ecological conditions and commercialization dynamics (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who contend that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, thus challenging recent literature emphasizing such impediments. They posit that hostile ecological conditions have hampered the development of advanced agricultural civilizations in Africa suggesting these factors played a significant role in shaping economic trajectories along with colonial institutions. Hopkins further elaborates on this by noting that "good" institutions scarcely existed even in Europe during the early phases of colonialism, implying that the impact of colonial institutions on African development was not as straightforward as often assumed. This view is nuanced by Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) analysis of Kenya, which reveals that long-term growth trajectories in Africa are influenced by varying degrees of income inequality between different ethnic groups and settlers. This finding underscores the complex interplay between ecological conditions, commercialization dynamics, and colonial institutions in shaping African economic development.

The slave trade played a significant role in shaping African economic development trajectories through its enduring impact on social capital and trust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the legacy of the slave trade created a culture of mistrust within Africa, which has persisted over time. This lack of trust is a persistent feature with measurable economic effects on development. This institutional and human capital depletion occurred because only societies with sufficiently developed institutions could initially facilitate trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). The resulting mistrust undermined African economic growth by reducing social cohesion and impeding cooperation. Gennaioli and Rainer (2006) note that this mistrust was exacerbated by the underdevelopment and instability of precolonial polities, further hindering Africa's economic success (Nunn 2008, p.28).

Colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by introducing extractive policies that persisted long after independence, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8). These policies were more profitable for Europeans in densely populated areas, where forced labor systems and slave trading became prevalent (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these institutions into the post-independence era further hindered Africa's economic development by preventing the establishment of productive institutions that could foster growth. This institutional legacy is evident in the continued presence of monopolies and regulations that were originally created by colonial powers, such as those seen in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The extractive nature of these institutions delayed industrialization and stifled human capital development, as masses typically did not or could not obtain education under such regimes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is further complicated by the historical context and path dependence. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to understand the differentiated causal relationships involved. Hopkins echoes this view, noting that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some constraints on European settlement (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is crucial for understanding how colonial institutions shaped African economic trajectories.

The cultural impact of colonial institutions cannot be overlooked. The slave trade, facilitated by these institutions, caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day. Nunn and Wantchekon show that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy further hindered economic development by reducing social cohesion and impeding cooperation. The persistence of extractive institutions and the resulting mistrust highlight the profound and lasting effects of colonial policies on African economic growth trajectories.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth is grounded in their enduring impact on institutional frameworks and human capital development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence that settles this debate lies in the persistence of extractive policies, such as monopolies and regulations, which were originally created by colonial powers and remained intact after independence. These policies delayed industrialization and stifled human capital development, as masses typically did not or could not obtain education under such regimes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).
