The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a pivotal question in understanding historical legacies' influence on contemporary development. The stakes are high as identifying the causal factors can illuminate pathways for effective policy interventions. At the core of this debate lies a disagreement over whether extractive colonial institutions have entrenched systems that hinder Africa's economic trajectory, or if other factors such as geography and internal governance structures play more significant roles. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective is supported by Hopkins' assertion that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.13). Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that causal relationships are more complex than these formulations acknowledge. The debate hinges on the extent to which colonial institutions have persisted and continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, with some scholars emphasizing the entrenched nature of extractive systems established during colonial rule.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions are key factors in shaping African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). They assert that when extractive institutions were more profitable for Europeans, these systems were established to exploit local labor and resources, particularly in densely populated areas. This approach aligns with the idea that high population density made forced labor systems economically viable, as seen in Meso-America and Africa during colonial times (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The economic viability of extractive institutions was further supported by the presence of abundant labor, which created profit opportunities for European colonizers. Acemoglu et al. note that this institutional framework persisted even after independence, continuing to influence economic outcomes in post-colonial societies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This persistence is evident in Latin America, where extractive policies established during colonial rule remained largely unchanged well into the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The enduring nature of these institutions highlights their profound impact on contemporary economic development.

Historical institutional changes in factor markets played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories. Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16; 2008, p.1); (Austin 2007, p.16) highlight that these shifts were marked by fundamental transformations in land use rights and labor dynamics. These changes occurred as localised scarcity emerged due to population density, necessitating the development of robust markets for both land and labor. This shift can be seen in Ghana during 1874-1939, where an institutional revolution occurred, characterized by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). The emergence of these new markets responded to immediate economic pressures and reflected broader historical forces shaping long-term growth patterns. Austin (2008, p.1) underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because situations are similar or due to path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2). Causal relationships involved were more intricate than previously acknowledged suggesting a nuanced interplay between institutional changes and economic outcomes. Moreover, Austin (2007, p.16) notes the mechanization of goods transport in Ghana, which completed the process of mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana. This transformation highlights how technological advancements complemented institutional changes, driving economic growth through improved infrastructure and market efficiency (Austin 2007, p.8). This interplay between institutional reforms and technological progress underscores the multifaceted nature of African economic development during colonial rule.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. The British colonial administration actively promoted missionary schools, which significantly enhanced educational opportunities for Africans. This policy diverged from other colonial powers such as France, which restricted missionary activities, leading to lower primary-school enrollment rates (Frankema 2012, p.4). Consequently, by 1938, British West Africa had higher primary school enrolment compared to French West Africa, demonstrating the impact of British policies on educational development. This educational infrastructure laid a foundation for skilled labor and administrative capabilities crucial for economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner note that improving long-run performance in Africa requires reducing economic shrinking, focusing on growth incentives (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17).

The broader context of African economic history and institutional reforms offers valuable insights into factors influencing Africa's development, even when these do not directly address the impact of colonial institutions on growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Hopkins argues that understanding the evolution of colonialism is crucial for comprehending its economic impacts. He notes that early mercantilist imperialism differed significantly from modern forms, with the latter characterized by medical advances allowing European settlement and altering colonial policies towards Africa. This shift in colonial dynamics suggests a temporal dimension to institutional changes, where earlier extractive institutions were not necessarily a product of deliberate policy but rather a result of constraints such as disease. Frankema and van Waijenburg build on this temporal aspect highlighting that the persistence of African poverty is influenced by varying degrees of income inequality and different economic dynamics, such as commercialization in regions like Kenya. This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of long-term growth trajectories in Africa, which must account for both historical institutional changes and contemporary economic factors.

The Kenyan case study demonstrates how differences in wage income growth can be attributed to varying degrees of income inequality among European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This highlights the importance of considering multiple factors beyond colonial institutions when examining African economic development. The shared finding is that the path-dependence nature of African economic development requires a more comprehensive analysis that includes both historical institutional changes and contemporary socioeconomic dynamics.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) demonstrate that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering economic growth in Europe through secure property rights. They argue the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly lowered transaction costs due to enhanced security in long-term property rights. This institutional development was a product of fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The secure property rights enabled a robust capital market, which is crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.3) qualifies this by noting that developing complex institutions to handle interdependence is not automatic; it requires overcoming issues like opportunism and cheating. He argues that the progress from primitive societies to modern Western societies is not unilinear or inevitable. This implies that while secure property rights are essential, their development and maintenance depend on overcoming significant institutional challenges, which may vary across different historical and geographical contexts. Therefore, understanding African economic growth trajectories requires considering both the role of colonial institutions in shaping property rights and the broader socioeconomic dynamics that influence institutional development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that is still evident today. This distrust has significant implications for economic development, as it affects everything from interpersonal transactions to broader social institutions. The historical legacy of slavery has created persistent barriers to trust, which in turn impedes economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.19) explains the slave trade's role in shaping African economies by highlighting that only societies with well-developed institutions could engage effectively in early trade with Europeans. This institutional development was crucial for facilitating commerce and establishing the foundations for later economic activities. The adverse effects of slavery extended beyond immediate economic disruptions; they also undermined the social fabric necessary for sustained growth. Therefore, understanding Africa's economic growth requires recognizing how past institutional frameworks, shaped by events like the slave trade, continue to affect present-day socio-economic conditions.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring frameworks that continue to influence contemporary development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that these colonial legacies persist through mechanisms such as extractive institutions and cultural shifts, respectively. The persistence of these institutions is evident in the continuation of policies and practices from the colonial era into post-independence periods. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show this continuity in Latin America where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact for nearly a century after independence. This institutional legacy has profound implications for economic development, as shown by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue that secure property rights fostered by effective institutions are crucial for long-term economic growth.

The impact of colonial institutions on African economies is further complicated by the differential effects of various colonial policies over time. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits a direct link between colonial extractive institutions and current economic underdevelopment, oversimplifies the historical context. Hopkins (2009, p.12) supports this view by highlighting the distinction between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, each with different institutional outcomes. This nuanced perspective is crucial for understanding how specific historical periods and types of colonial rule have shaped Africa's economic trajectory.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economies is thus multifaceted, involving both direct economic impacts and deeper cultural and institutional legacies. The persistence of extractive institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), and the enduring effects of historical events like the slave trade, as demonstrated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), underscore the long-term influence of colonial rule. The precise nature of these impacts varies across different regions and historical periods, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.12) show through their analyses of differentiated causal relationships and varied institutional outcomes. Empirical comparisons reveal that extractive institutions are more prevalent in areas with high population density, where forced labor systems were more profitable for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that the profitability of extractive institutions directly influenced their establishment and persistence, thereby shaping African economic growth trajectories.
