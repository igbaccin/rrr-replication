Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic trajectories, a question of significant historical and theoretical importance. The debate centers around the causal mechanisms through which colonial legacies influence contemporary economic outcomes. One prominent position argues that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers have persisted and hindered growth; this perspective is contested by those who emphasize the complexity and diversity of colonial experiences, suggesting that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic development is more nuanced than often portrayed. Another position highlights the enduring effects of inequality in shaping institutional frameworks that favor elites, thus perpetuating economic disparities. The discussion around this topic is crucial for understanding how historical factors continue to influence present-day economic conditions in Africa. The debate over how colonial institutions have shaped African economic trajectories underscores the need for a deeper examination of the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate and their long-term effects on growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions have significantly influenced economic growth trajectories in Africa by establishing extractive systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Extractive institutions were often more profitable for European colonizers, particularly in areas with high population density where forced labor could be efficiently exploited. This profitability incentivized the establishment of extractive systems in densely populated regions, such as those found in Africa and Meso-America. For example, abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America facilitated the creation of forced labor systems, while Africa's high population density provided a profit opportunity for slave traders supplying labor to American plantations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These extractive institutions persisted beyond the colonial era, shaping post-independence economic trajectories and inhibiting industrialization. The persistence of these institutions is evident in the enduring extractive policies seen in Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact well into the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional legacy has had profound implications for human capital development, as extractive institutions typically prevented the masses from obtaining education. Consequently, low levels of human capital became a primary mechanism through which these institutions delayed industrialization and economic growth in African countries (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Austin challenges the simplistic causal narratives of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by asserting that it conflates distinct historical periods and trajectories (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin's critique reveals that colonial institutions in Africa were not static; instead they evolved over time, shaped by local conditions. For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to scarcity triggered significant institutional changes in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.8; Austin 2007, p.16). This transition led to widespread markets for land use rights and hired labor, illustrating how local economic conditions influenced institutional development. Austin also observes that these institutional changes varied across Africa (Author Year, p.N). The differentiated causal relationships highlight the complexity of African economic growth trajectories, where path dependence and similar situations recur but do not determine outcomes in a straightforward manner (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced view highlights that colonial institutions influenced African economies differently based on local historical contexts.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade directly impeded African economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This dynamic is evident in how the slave trade influenced intra-African trust dynamics. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and created enduring mistrust among communities, which persists today and significantly impacts economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings complement recent studies documenting the importance of trust for various aspects of development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). For example, Nunn provides historical evidence that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to facilitate trade with Europeans during the early stages of contact. This suggests that precolonial institutional strength played a crucial role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, even before the onset of colonial rule. The adverse effects of the slave trade on trust and social cohesion further complicated this dynamic.

Nunn (2008, p.28) notes that centralized precolonial state institutions were instrumental in providing public goods such as education, health, and infrastructure (Nunn 2008, p.28). This points to a broader pattern where historical institutional development, both positive and negative, has had lasting effects on Africa's economic growth trajectories. The enduring mistrust fostered by the slave trade continues to influence contemporary African economies, highlighting the complex interplay between historical events and current economic conditions.

The broader context of African economic history and institutional reforms provides essential insights into development factors beyond colonial institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Hopkins shows that the evolution of colonialism in Africa transitioned from mercantilist to modern imperialism, with significant implications for institutional development. This shift coincides with medical advances that facilitated European settlement, altering the dynamics of colonial policy toward Africa.

Frankema and van Waijenburg challenge the notion that African poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They argue against the view that hostile ecological conditions have consistently hampered Africa's development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This perspective aligns with Hopkins' assertion that the nature of colonial institutions evolved over time, suggesting that the impact of these institutions on economic growth may not be as monolithic as often portrayed. The variability in long-term growth trajectories across African regions underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional change and its effects on development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This nuance is crucial for interpreting the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic conditions, highlighting that the legacy of colonial institutions is just one piece of a much larger puzzle.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) show that institutions shaping public choice were pivotal to economic growth in seventeenth-century England, with secure property rights and financial innovations playing key roles. This institutional framework enabled the evolution of a robust capital market, which in turn facilitated long-term investments crucial for sustained economic development. This institutional stability was instrumental in fostering an environment conducive to economic growth. North (1989, p.3) points out that even with third-party enforcement, institutions face challenges from opportunism and cheating. These insights underscore the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic trajectories, suggesting that while secure property rights and financial innovations are essential, their implementation and maintenance require overcoming significant obstacles.

Historical events and institutional differences played pivotal roles in shaping long-term economic disparities within Europe. Pamuk (2007, p.16) argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap between northwest and southern Europe by 1450, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These institutional frameworks limited economic opportunities for large segments of the population, thereby maintaining elite status over time. The persistence of such inequalities was reinforced by institutional structures that favored elites, restricting competition and hindering broader economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). This dynamic contrasts with the role of colonial institutions in Africa, where the legacy of extractive institutions has been similarly detrimental to economic development. Pamuk's findings on the Black Death underscore how exogenous shocks can catalyze long-term institutional changes, suggesting that similar shocks in African colonies might have altered their economic trajectories if different institutional responses had emerged (Pamuk 2007, p.21). The persistence of inequality in Europe shows that institutional change is a product of deeply entrenched power structures as well as reactions to external events. This interplay between historical events and institutional frameworks provides insights into how colonial institutions shaped African economic growth trajectories by perpetuating extractive practices that stifled development.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive practices that persisted long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that colonial powers set up control structures tailored to their interests, which often involved exploiting local resources and labor without fostering sustainable development. These institutions were deeply entrenched power structures that perpetuated inequality and hindered growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional framework was particularly detrimental in regions with high population density, where extractive institutions were more profitable for European colonizers due to the availability of labor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35).

Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view by emphasizing the need to examine growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar. This perspective underscores the importance of path dependence in understanding how colonial institutions shaped African economic trajectories. Austin's argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8), highlighting that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized. Hopkins (2009, p.13) further elaborates on this by noting that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle the unproductive institutions already in place, which were entrenched during the colonial era and persisted into independence. This continuity of extractive institutions is a key factor in understanding Africa's economic stagnation.

The role of trust in economic development is another critical aspect highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4). They argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy of mistrust, combined with the extractive institutions established during colonial rule, has significantly impeded economic growth in Africa. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12) add that where there was extreme inequality, and institutions advantaged elites and limited access to economic opportunities for much of the population, members of elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time. This institutional framework contributed to the persistence of inequality and hindered economic development.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive practices (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement lies in whether colonial rulers had the capacity or intent to dismantle existing unproductive institutions, or if they were merely constrained by time and other factors (Hopkins 2009, p.13). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of institutional persistence. Specifically, Acemoglu et al.'s analysis (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8) shows that control structures set up during colonialism continued to shape economic outcomes long after independence, providing a clear measurement of how these institutions persisted and affected current income per capita.
