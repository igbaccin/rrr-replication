The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue that intersects with debates about long-term economic development and institutional persistence. At stake intellectually is the extent to which historical legacies, particularly those stemming from colonial rule, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Key positions in this debate revolve around whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive or productive, and how these institutions have persisted over time. One camp argues that extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers have had a lasting negative impact on African economies, while another posits that the effects of colonial rule are more nuanced and context-dependent. A third view emphasizes the role of pre-colonial conditions in shaping post-colonial economic trajectories.

The persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era is evident in various historical examples. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) highlight how extractive policies, such as monopolies and regulations created by Spain, remained intact after Latin American countries gained independence for most of the nineteenth century. This persistence indicates that colonial institutions were foundational structures that continued to shape economic outcomes long after formal colonial rule ended (Author, Year, p.N). Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) indicate that extractive institutions set up by colonialists persisted even beyond the colonial regime, influencing current-day governance and economic development in many African countries.

The causal mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced economic trajectories are multifaceted. Extractive institutions were more profitable for European colonizers in areas with high population density or abundant resources, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trades (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These extractive practices directly impeded economic development and created a legacy of mistrust within African societies that persists today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3; 2011, p.4) show how the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust, which has had enduring effects on economic and social development. This cultural impact complements the direct economic effects of extractive institutions, illustrating the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary outcomes.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is further complicated by the notion that these institutions varied across different regions and time periods. Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) argues that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on African economies was more differentiated than often recognized. This perspective aligns with Austin's (2008, p.1) (Austin 2008, p.2) view that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Both views underscore the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because situations are similar or due to path dependence.

The complexity of colonial institutions' role in African economic development is further illustrated by their interaction with pre-colonial conditions and post-colonial reforms. For instance, Peters (2004, p.14) argues that reports of negotiable land systems in Africa conceal processes of exclusion and class formation, highlighting how colonial institutions interacted with local social structures to shape economic outcomes. This interaction shows that the impact of colonial institutions depended on pre-existing conditions and post-colonial developments. The enduring effects of colonial institutions on African economies are a testament to their foundational role in shaping contemporary economic trajectories, despite ongoing debates about their precise mechanisms and impacts.

Austin (2008, p.1) and others (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by showing that it oversimplifies causation. They argue that this framework collapses different historical periods into a single narrative, obscuring the nuanced interplay between colonial institutions and local conditions over time. This approach overlooks how initial economic trajectories were shaped by immediate policy decisions and long-term historical dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2). For example, in Ghana, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by increasing population density and the resulting pressure on resources (Austin 2007, p.16). However, these institutional changes were not uniform across different regions; even within Ghana, areas like Ashanti experienced land scarcity at varying intensities, leading to localized policy responses such as bans on new farms. This variation underscores how colonial institutions interacted with pre-existing social and economic structures in complex ways (Austin 2007, p.8). In the end, while institutional changes were pivotal, their impact was mediated by the specific historical context of each region.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that the economic performance of colonial Africa was more robust than previously acknowledged. Broadberry and Gardner highlight how Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores a significant level of economic development during the colonial period, which challenges prevailing narratives about African stagnation. Akyeampong and Fofack further elaborate on this theme by emphasizing that African women played a crucial role in this economic growth, contributing significantly to production and reproduction processes. Their contributions were often overlooked but were essential for sustaining economic activities throughout the colonial era. This perspective shifts the focus from merely institutional frameworks to the active roles of various social groups within those structures. Broadberry and Gardner note that improving long-term performance in Africa will require addressing growth and reducing economic shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This insight qualifies the broader narrative by highlighting the multifaceted nature of economic development, where stability and resilience are as important as growth itself. The role of African women in colonial economies thus adds a nuanced layer to understanding how different social groups interacted with colonial institutions, shaping economic trajectories in ways that go beyond institutional frameworks alone.

The literature on economic history and poverty in Africa often overlooks the direct influence of colonial institutions on these trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Hopkins observes that pre-colonial Africa lacked "good" institutions because they were not yet available even in Europe. This suggests that the initial phase of European imperialism did not involve a deliberate choice between productive and extractive institutions, as both options were absent. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty reflects persistent structural impediments and varying degrees of income inequality among different groups within society. This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding how colonial rule interacted with pre-existing social structures. While Hopkins notes that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle unproductive institutions, Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight the need for future research to concentrate on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.13; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These insights together suggest that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development was complex and multifaceted.

Peters highlights that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to significant social changes and depeasantization processes (Peters 2004, p.19; Bryceson 2002, p.11). This transformation is marked by the shift from traditional agricultural practices to more diversified economic activities. For instance, Peters shows how private commercial transactions have become increasingly important, representing a significant departure from patrimonial dependence on decaying state institutions for political resources. The growing reliance on foreign firms and intermediaries reflects broader shifts in economic structures that extend beyond mere diversification into the realm of social restructuring and class formation. These changes are deeply embedded in the social fabric, altering family and kin-based groups as well as generating conflicts between generations and genders. These social dynamics are crucial for understanding the broader implications of economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa.

Bryceson underscores that these transformations involve deep-rooted processes such as the commodification of land and labor relations (Peters 2004, p.14; Bryceson 2002, p.5). This commodification leads to a fragmentation of traditional social structures, which in turn affects rural poverty alleviation efforts. The emergence of local casual wage labor markets and the increasing importance of nonagricultural activities indicate that economic restructuring alters the very foundations of rural livelihoods. These changes have profound implications for food security and poverty reduction, as evidenced by the growing dependence on food aid in regions experiencing recurrent harvest failures.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa continues to influence contemporary social dynamics, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3); (Nunn 2008, p.28). Trust is crucial for economic development as it facilitates cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and enhances social capital, which are essential for sustainable growth and poverty reduction. The legacy of the slave trade has left deep-seated distrust within African societies, affecting both internal and external economic interactions. Nunn (2008, p.28) highlights that this historical mistrust has tangible economic consequences. This institutional weakness, rooted in the legacy of the slave trade, continues to impede Africa's economic growth by limiting the provision of public goods such as education, health, and infrastructure. These factors are critical for fostering a productive workforce and enhancing overall economic performance. The persistence of mistrust and weak institutions underscores the need for policies that address these historical legacies to promote sustainable development in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.19).
