The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the causal relationship between colonial institutions and long-term economic performance, which has implications for understanding the persistence of poverty and underdevelopment on the continent. The live disagreement centers around two main positions. One position argues that extractive colonial institutions have significantly hindered African economic growth by creating persistent barriers to investment and innovation. The other position suggests a more nuanced view, where the impact of colonial institutions is mediated by historical context and specific institutional arrangements, leading to varied outcomes across different regions and periods. This debate is crucial for understanding how historical legacies influence contemporary economic development in Africa.

The first stream addresses the enduring impact of extractive colonial institutions on African economies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has had lasting effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions, characterized by forced labor and exploitative policies, were more profitable for Europeans in densely populated areas, leading to their widespread implementation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions into the post-colonial era has been well-documented. In Latin America, for instance, the monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8), highlighting the durability of colonial institutional legacies.

The second stream addresses the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth trajectories in Africa through a broader lens, incorporating historical context and specific institutional arrangements. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because situations are similar or due to path dependence. Similarly, Hopkins points out that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the considerable disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12). By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some constraints on European settlement. This historical context is crucial for understanding how colonial institutions shaped economic trajectories in Africa.

The third stream addresses the role of trust and social capital in mediating the effects of colonial institutions on African economies. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the enduring impact of historical legacies on social capital. Broadberry and Gardner provide a counterpoint by arguing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that colonial institutions may have had varied effects on economic performance, depending on specific historical and institutional contexts.

The fourth stream addresses the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic growth trajectories in Africa through the lens of institutional change and adaptation. Broadberry and Gardner argue that improving long-run performance in Africa will require reducing the rate and frequency of shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how institutions can be adapted to promote economic growth and development, even in the face of historical legacies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by introducing institutional changes that persistently affected development outcomes. A key mechanism through which this occurred was the establishment of extractive institutions, which were more profitable for European colonizers in areas with high population density. The persistence of these extractive institutions into the post-colonial era further solidified their impact on economic development. This institutional persistence suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic growth trajectories in Africa today.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers. This difference is attributed to the British policy of welcoming missionaries from all denominations, which supported their indirect rule system. The French, by contrast, restricted missionary activities, particularly non-Catholic missions, leading to lower primary-school enrolment rates and higher government expenses per student in French West Africa compared to British West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.4). This divergence in educational development trajectories highlights how colonial policies shaped the provision of public goods, such as education. Frankema (2012, p.15) explains that the British approach allowed for the coordination of peaceful or violent resistance against colonial regimes, further illustrating the impact of colonial institutions on African societies. For example, they note that in Kenya, differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth were influenced by varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that the legacy of colonial education systems is complex and multifaceted, with implications for understanding African economic growth trajectories.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation through compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This critique is anchored in the idea that African economic growth trajectories are shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including institutional legacies from colonial rule. Austin’s work underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.8). By focusing on Ghana during 1874-1939, Austin illustrates how shifts from land abundance to localised scarcity led to institutional changes in factor markets. Specifically, the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour was a direct response to these economic pressures (Austin 2007, p.16; Austin 2007, p.8). This institutional revolution highlights that colonial institutions evolved dynamically in response to changing economic conditions. This dynamic evolution does not necessarily imply uniform or predictable outcomes across different regions. For instance, while Ghana experienced significant institutional changes, the specific paths and outcomes varied based on local contexts and historical contingencies (Austin 2007, p.16; Austin 2008, p.2).

Hopkins and Olsson's analysis (Hopkins 2009, p.12) of the evolution of colonial institutions in Africa reveals the dynamic nature of these institutions across different phases of imperialism. This evolution was driven by changing economic conditions and medical advances. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutional options were available to colonial rulers, marking a shift from earlier periods when such choices were not viable due to constraints like disease (Hopkins 2009, p.13; Hopkins 2019, p.6).

The dynamic nature of colonial institutions is further underscored by Hopkins's observation that colonial rulers did not always have the time or capacity to dismantle preexisting unproductive institutions. Moreover, the impact of colonialism on African economic development was complex and multifaceted, with significant regional variations. This complexity is evident in the reversal of fortune experienced by Africa, where precolonial levels of economic and social development were similar to other prosperous regions but declined due to external factors like slave trades (Hopkins 2009, p.13; Hopkins 2019, p.6). Hopkins cautions against oversimplifying this narrative through deterministic notions like path dependence, which can obscure the diverse influences shaping African economic trajectories. This nuanced view highlights that while colonial institutions played a role in Africa's economic history, their impact was mediated by various local and historical factors.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) emphasize that African women's contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods offer a critical perspective on the region's development history. Their work underscores the often-marginalized contributions of women, who were central to production and reproduction processes across various African societies. This perspective is bolstered by Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) analysis, which reveals that Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic performance was significantly influenced by the labor and entrepreneurial activities of women, who were integral to both agricultural and commercial sectors. The systematic codification of patriarchy during colonial times, as documented by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), further underscores the institutional barriers that hindered women's full economic participation. This historical layering of gender discrimination has had lasting implications for African economic development, shaping contemporary challenges in achieving sustainable growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) find that the slave trade in Africa instilled a culture of mistrust that persists today. The external channel is one mechanism by which this mistrust manifests: it is exacerbated by the presence of outsiders who may be perceived as potential enslavers, even if they are not. This channel operates through the perception of risk and uncertainty associated with interactions with foreigners. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that societies with more developed institutions were better able to facilitate trade with Europeans during the early phases of contact. These same institutions could have also fostered a heightened sense of vigilance against potential threats from outsiders. The internal channel is another mechanism by which mistrust manifests: it operates through the social dynamics within African communities themselves. This channel can be understood as a response to the internal conflicts and power struggles that arose during the slave trade era, as different groups sought to protect their own interests at the expense of others. When combined with the external channel, this internal mistrust can create a vicious cycle where distrust both within and between communities reinforces itself over time. This persistent mistrust can have profound implications for economic development, as it can hinder cooperation, investment, and innovation, all critical factors in achieving sustainable growth.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that persist long after independence. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how extractive institutions, established during colonial rule, continue to affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions were more profitable for European colonists in densely settled and prosperous areas where there was much to be extracted (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era is evident in the continued presence of monopolies and regulations, which were created by colonial powers and remained intact for decades after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is further highlighted by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon. They show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, hinders cooperation, investment, and innovation, critical factors for achieving sustainable growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The enduring legacy of colonial institutions is also evident in the economic performance of Anglophone African economies. Broadberry and Gardner show that many of these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more complex economic landscape than previously characterized (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This finding underscores the need to consider the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic growth.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a shared finding across multiple studies. They all agree that colonial institutions have had a profound and lasting impact on Africa's economic development, with extractive institutions being particularly detrimental (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The precise mechanisms through which these institutions affect growth remain a subject of debate (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of trust levels and their impact on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust levels can be attributed to the internal channel of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This finding provides a clear empirical basis for understanding how colonial institutions have shaped African economic growth trajectories, highlighting the importance of addressing the legacy of mistrust in fostering sustainable development.
