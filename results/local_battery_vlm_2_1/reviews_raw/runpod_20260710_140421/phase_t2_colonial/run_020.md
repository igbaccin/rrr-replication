The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue within economic history and development studies. At stake is the interpretation of persistent economic disparities between formerly colonized regions and their former colonizers. The central disagreement revolves around whether colonial institutions were the primary drivers of these disparities or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural attributes, played more significant roles. This debate extends beyond academic circles, influencing policy discussions on development strategies for African economies.

One camp argues that extractive colonial institutions stifled economic growth by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few, while another posits that pre-colonial conditions or post-colonial policies were more decisive. The disagreement hinges on whether the legacy of colonialism is one of entrenched inequality due to institutional design or if other factors, such as historical path dependence or exogenous shocks, are more critical.

The role of extractive institutions in African economic development has been extensively studied by Acemoglu et al.. They argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies where mortality rates were high and the local population was deemed less capable of resistance. These institutions persisted long after independence, shaping current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Extractive institutions were more profitable in densely populated areas, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective highlights that the persistence of extractive institutions is a key factor in understanding Africa's economic trajectory.

This mechanism highlights how colonial institutions' legacy continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

This shift towards market-oriented systems, as highlighted by Austin (2008, p.1), was pivotal in altering the dynamics of African economies from traditional to more modern frameworks. Austin (2007, p.16) shows this transformation was driven by localised scarcity of land and the emergence of new economic opportunities. This shift is evident in Ghana during 1874-1939, where the transition from land abundance to scarcity led to widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The institutional changes in factor markets were driven by both external pressures and internal dynamics. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that examining growth theories against long-term history reveals relationships that recur due to similar situations, as well as path dependence. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding historical context in assessing institutional changes. The emergence of new economic opportunities, such as the cocoa industry in Ghana, further drove these institutional shifts and transformed traditional practices into market-oriented systems. Austin (2007, p.8) notes that the length of motorable roads tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles between 1930-32. This infrastructure development facilitated transport mechanisation, except for farm-to-local market journeys where head loading remained common. The institutional revolution in factor markets thus had profound implications for economic growth and structural transformation in African economies.

The comparative effectiveness of British colonial rule in developing formal education systems, as noted by Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), underscores the varied impacts of different colonial powers on African economic development. This difference is attributable to the British policy of indirect rule, which encouraged missionary activity as a support mechanism. British authorities welcomed missionaries from various denominations to assist in governance and administration. This approach stands in stark contrast to French policies, which restricted non-Catholic missionary activities, thereby curtailing educational opportunities (Frankema 2012, p.4). The divergence in educational development is evident in the primary-school enrolment rates: by 1938, British West Africa had a rate of 7.1 percent, nearly double that of French West Africa at 3.6 percent (Frankema 2012, p.4). The higher enrolment rates in British colonies were achieved despite lower government expenditure per student compared to the French colonies (Frankema 2012, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This educational disparity had profound implications for economic growth, as a more educated populace is better equipped to engage with market-oriented systems and capitalise on new opportunities.

The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has significantly shaped African economic development trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This mistrust is deeply ingrained and persists today. Nunn shows that the slave trades had adverse effects on economic development, which can be traced back to the institutions that facilitated trade with Europeans. The early trade between Africans and Europeans was primarily in commodities other than slaves; only societies with sufficiently developed institutions could engage in this commerce (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context underscores how the legacy of mistrust is rooted in the very institutions that were instrumental during the slave trades. Nunn and Wantchekon further highlight that trust, or lack thereof, plays a crucial role in various aspects of economic development, including international trade and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The importance of trust is well-documented across multiple studies, reinforcing the idea that the mistrust engendered by the slave trade has had lasting effects on Africa's economic trajectory.

The historical context of imperialism and ecological conditions has significantly influenced African economic development, shaping trajectories that have evolved over different periods. Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that the nature of colonial institutions varied widely across time, with early mercantilist imperialism differing markedly from later forms of modern imperialism. By the late nineteenth century, colonial rulers had access to both productive and extractive institutional options. This shift is crucial as it suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development depended on the specific historical context and the duration of European occupation. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further illustrate this point by highlighting that the effects of colonialism have been relatively small compared to nearly five centuries of precolonial slave trading, which had a profound negative impact on current levels of GDP per capita in African countries. This perspective underscores the importance of considering both historical and ecological factors when examining African economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p. 28) also note that the Kenyan case demonstrates varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans, suggesting a complex and multifaceted nature to African economic development's path-dependence.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with their enduring impact rooted in both historical and ecological factors. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions introduced by colonial powers were more profitable in densely populated areas, leading to the establishment of systems like forced labor and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This perspective is reinforced by Austin, who argues for a nuanced understanding of economic growth theories against long-term history, highlighting path dependence as a critical factor (Austin 2008, p.2). The persistence of these extractive institutions well into the post-colonial era further underscores their significance. Acemoglu et al. provide historical evidence showing that control structures set up during colonial times often persisted after independence, forming the basis for current-day institutions in many African countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic development is also evident in the cultural and social dimensions. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which continues to affect economic outcomes today. This mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Hopkins aligns with this view, noting that colonial rule often entrenched extractive regimes and unproductive institutions, which were already in place before European settlement (Hopkins 2009, p.13).

Colonial institutions also influenced economic growth by affecting human capital development. Acemoglu et al. argue that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not obtain education, leading to low levels of human capital, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Broadberry and Gardner provide a contrasting perspective, showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This suggests that while colonial institutions may have been extractive, they did not entirely stifle economic growth in all regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17).

The precise role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories remains a subject of ongoing debate. The underlying point of agreement is that these institutions had a profound and lasting impact on Africa's economic development paths. The disagreement lies in the extent to which extractive institutions were the primary driver of economic stagnation versus other factors such as human capital development and cultural influences (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Broadberry and Gardner settle this by showing that many African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence despite the presence of extractive institutions, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial legacies and local economic conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17).
