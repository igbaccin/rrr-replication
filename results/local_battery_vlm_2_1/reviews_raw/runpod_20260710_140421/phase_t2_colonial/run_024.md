The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue among scholars, as it lies at the heart of debates about the persistence of poverty and underdevelopment. At stake is whether contemporary economic outcomes are best explained by historical legacies or other factors such as geography, culture, or global economics. One live disagreement centres on whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive or productive in nature, and how this shaped long-term economic development. Another debate concerns the extent to which current economic conditions can be attributed directly to colonial-era policies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies where mortality rates were high, which persisted after independence and significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35), who highlight the economic profitability of extractive institutions in densely populated areas, making them more likely to be established by European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuity of monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers, which remained intact long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This suggests that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic trajectories.

However, Hopkins (2009, p.12) offers a nuanced view on this topic, arguing that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the considerable disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This perspective aligns with Austin's (2008) critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which argues that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized, suggesting a complex interplay between colonial institutions and other factors over time. Hopkins (2009, p.12) also notes that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, indicating a more varied impact of colonialism on African economies (Hopkins 2009, p.12).

Acemoglu et al. and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) establish that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories. This dynamic is further illustrated by the persistence of extractive state institutions even after independence. However, the impact of these institutions on human capital development was profound; in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which delayed industrialization and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional legacy continues to influence current income per capita levels.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) build on this complexity by showing that historical institutional changes in Africa cannot be reduced to simple causal relationships but are shaped by complex interactions of factors over time. Austin (2008, p.1) argues for the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because situations are similar as well as due to path dependence (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This approach highlights how institutional changes in Africa have been influenced by various factors such as population dynamics, resource availability, and technological advancements. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) demonstrates that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes in factor markets, including widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.8). This institutional revolution was driven by economic pressures and technological advancements, such as the introduction of motorable roads and lorries, which transformed goods transport and reduced railway dependence. However, Austin (2008, p.1) notes that causal relationships are more differentiated than commonly recognised, suggesting that while these historical changes were influential, their impact on economic growth was not uniform or straightforward (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced perspective underscores the complexity of institutional change in Africa and its varied effects on economic trajectories.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) and Olsson (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) argue that while the new economic history of Africa has made significant strides in understanding historical institutional changes, it does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.12) argues that the introduction of European capitalism created underdevelopment by drawing other societies into networks of dependence and exploiting them through unequal exchange (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This perspective suggests that the colonial period was marked by a significant shift in institutional structures, where extractive institutions were imposed on African societies. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further qualifies this mechanism by noting that poverty was a historical creation resulting from these exploitative practices (Hopkins 2009, p.13). This historical creation of underdevelopment through colonial exploitation highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic trajectories.

The nuanced argument presented by Hopkins (2019, p.11) extends to how poverty was not an inherent condition. The paper argues that European capitalism created underdevelopment by integrating other societies into networks of dependence and exploiting them via unequal exchange (Hopkins 2019, p.11). This perspective suggests that the colonial period was marked by a significant shift in institutional structures, where extractive institutions were imposed on African societies. The implication of this perspective is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape Africa's economic development, highlighting the need for institutional reforms that address the historical roots of underdevelopment.

The British colonial administration fostered more favorable conditions for educational development in sub-Saharan Africa than other European powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This is evident in the differential treatment of Christian missions by the French and British. The French restricted missionary activities to maintain a separation between state and church, whereas the British welcomed missionaries to bolster their indirect rule system (Frankema 2012, p.4). The contrasting policies resulted in diverging educational trajectories: primary-school enrollment rates were markedly higher in British West Africa compared to French West Africa by 1938. The implications of these educational disparities are profound, as they suggest that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping the human capital development of African societies. Instead, the historical roots of underdevelopment in Africa are multifaceted and require a nuanced understanding of both colonial and precolonial factors.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The Kenyan case exemplifies this complexity, highlighting varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This underscores the need for future research to concentrate on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories within Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight that African economies during the colonial period were more economically robust than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging earlier views that painted a bleaker picture. This economic performance was driven significantly by the contributions of women, who played crucial roles in production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack underscore that African women's contributions were pivotal in both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This suggests that economic growth during this era was not solely dependent on external factors but also on internal dynamics, including the significant economic activities of women. Broadberry and Gardner note that improving long-term performance will require reducing the frequency of economic shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This indicates that while African economies showed resilience, they were also vulnerable to periods of decline.

The slave trade had a profound impact on African economic development, creating lasting institutional and cultural legacies that continue to affect growth trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon document that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, countering those who attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. The slave trade disrupted African societies by removing large portions of the population and creating a culture of mistrust. This disruption had long-lasting effects on institutions and economic development in Africa. Nunn notes that during early trade between Africans and Europeans, only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade. This suggests that the slave trade may have selected for societies with weaker institutions, as those with stronger institutions were better equipped to resist or manage the trade. The legacy of mistrust from the slave trade may also have hindered economic development by making it more difficult for individuals and communities to cooperate and engage in productive activities. Nunn's work highlights that the impact of the slave trade on African economic development varied across the continent. While some regions were severely affected, others managed to maintain or even develop their economies despite the disruption caused by the slave trade.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive systems that persisted long after independence. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers introduced extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which then delayed industrialization and stunted economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions were designed to extract resources efficiently, often at the expense of local populations' well-being and long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in the continued presence of monopolies and regulations that hindered economic progress even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The legacy of colonial rule also encompasses the development of a culture of mistrust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade, facilitated by European powers, created deep-seated mistrust among Africans, which continues to affect economic cooperation and development today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is a significant barrier to productive activities and economic growth, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and investment. The impact of this mistrust on African economies is substantial, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3).

Furthermore, the economic performance of African countries during the colonial period was more nuanced than previously characterized. Broadberry and Gardner reveal that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a level of economic development that was not uniformly stagnant (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding challenges the notion that African economies were uniformly poor during colonial rule and highlights the variability in economic outcomes across different regions. The persistence of extractive institutions and the legacy of mistrust from the slave trade continue to shape African economic trajectories today.

The underlying point of agreement is that colonial institutions significantly influenced African economic growth trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). There remains a disagreement about whether these institutions were uniformly extractive or varied across different regions.
