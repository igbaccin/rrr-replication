The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses how societies organize themselves to foster prosperity over extended periods. Institutions, defined broadly as the rules and norms governing behavior, can either facilitate or hinder economic progress. A key disagreement centers around whether institutions are endogenous to economic conditions or exogenous determinants of economic outcomes. One camp argues that institutions are shaped by underlying economic factors, such as geography or initial endowments. Another posits that institutions themselves are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth, influencing how resources are allocated and how economic activities are conducted. The debate hinges on whether institutions are a product of economic development or its cause.

Ogilvie (2007, p.3) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions play a crucial role in determining the economic trajectory of nations. Their work emphasizes that different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes. For example, colonies where Europeans settled and established strong property rights and rule of law, such as the United States and Australia, experienced significant economic growth. Extractive institutions in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast hindered development by prioritizing resource extraction over local investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's view that institutions are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) further illustrate this point by examining the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England. They demonstrate that secure property rights and credible commitments underpin long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering a more dynamic economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This historical evidence supports the idea that well-designed institutions can create an environment conducive to investment and innovation, driving sustained economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) extends this line of reasoning by highlighting how institutional reforms in Africa can inform policy to eliminate poverty. He emphasizes that "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks to design effective policies for economic growth.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions also merits attention. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these conflicts often drive institutional changes more than efficiency considerations. Serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were maintained because they served the interests of powerful groups. This perspective suggests that political dynamics shape institutions as much as economic factors do.

The live disagreement is whether institutions are endogenous to economic conditions or exogenous determinants of economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North & Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argued that the development of specific institutions such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments were pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of these institutions significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth. North (1989, p.9) noted that the Bank of England's establishment was a response to fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, leading to increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects a broader pattern where political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and their effective enforcement drive economic growth by lowering transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) also highlighted that as economies grow, the transactions sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic is crucial because it underscores how institutional changes can fundamentally alter economic structures. This qualification suggests that while specific institutional developments are critical, their impact can be context-dependent. The implication is that understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires a nuanced analysis of both the institutions themselves and the broader political and economic contexts in which they operate.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress and knowledge accumulation are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr highlights how institutions facilitate this process by reducing access costs to useful knowledge, thereby fostering a more efficient diffusion of innovations. This institutional role is not merely passive; it actively supports technological progress through policies that rectify coordination failures and promote sustainable economic improvement. Kuznets elaborates on the necessity for institutional adjustments in response to technological changes, emphasizing that modern technology demands significant shifts in societal structures and ideologies. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts without undermining political stability. The implication is that while institutions play a secondary role in facilitating long-run economic growth, their effectiveness hinges on their ability to adapt to technological advancements and manage the societal changes they induce. Kuznets further asserts that only when such institutional adaptations are achieved with minimal cost can modern economic growth be sustained (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The institutional framework imposed during the colonial period determined whether these regions would develop inclusive, growth-promoting institutions or extractive ones that hindered development. The downstream implications of this institutional framework are substantial: regions with extractive institutions faced persistent economic underdevelopment, while those with inclusive institutions experienced sustained growth. This pattern is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) caution that this relationship is complex and multifaceted, involving various measures of institutions such as constraints on executive power, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. Understanding the specific mechanisms by which colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes requires a nuanced analysis of these institutional dimensions.

The literature on African economic development and colonial legacies explores how institutional changes influenced public goods provision but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), highlighting a gap in the understanding of these dynamics. This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) argument that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments; they emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of economic development determinants across colonial Africa. This underscores the complexity of evaluating the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the availability of comprehensive data remains a critical factor in understanding the mechanisms at play.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), along with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced by the early modern period. The key mechanism underlying this divergence lies in merchant distribution and finance, which facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector of north-western Europe. This suggests that the service sector played a pivotal role in fostering economic growth during this era, mirroring its importance in more recent periods. The implications of these findings are significant: they indicate that the benefits of early modern development were not uniformly distributed across society. Instead, skilled laborers likely reaped greater advantages than their unskilled counterparts due to higher surpluses over basic food needs. This pattern of uneven distribution aligns with historical national accounting data for Sub-Saharan Africa, which reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The availability of extensive economic data from this period challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance and underscores the importance of considering regional variations in economic development. This evidence highlights the complexity of understanding economic growth, as it was influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) find that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were shaped to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. They explain that these inequalities persisted over time by influencing the evolution of institutions, which in turn affected economic development. A key mechanism identified by Sokoloff and Engerman is the way in which unequal societies developed institutions that protected the interests of the elite, such as laws and regulations that limited competition and restricted access to resources. For example, they note that in Latin America, institutions were designed to maintain the power of the landed elite, while in North America, institutions were more inclusive and promoted broader economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) supports this perspective by emphasizing the importance of understanding how institutions can be shaped by distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes. She argues that institutions are deeply influenced by the power dynamics within society. This perspective is crucial for understanding how institutional design can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), for example, highlights the importance of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms in reducing transaction costs, which are crucial for economic growth. The development of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby fostering a more dynamic economy.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the design of institutions can either promote or hinder long-term economic growth. They show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, along with geographic factors. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's assertion that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts within society (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These conflicts influence how institutions are designed and implemented, affecting economic outcomes.

The historical evidence from North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provides a clear example of this dynamic. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, which were critical for economic growth. This institutional framework ensured that the regime was committed to and bound by rules, creating an environment conducive to investment and development.

North's (1989, p.9) (North 1989, p.6) work on the English financial revolution illustrates how specific institutions can reduce transaction costs and promote economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) analysis of extractive institutions in colonies shows that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. This highlights the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes.
