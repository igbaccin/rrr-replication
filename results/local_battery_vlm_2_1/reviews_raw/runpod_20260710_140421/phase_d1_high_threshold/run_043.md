The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centered on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. The disagreement hinges on how institutions interact with other variables such as geography, culture, and colonial policies. Some researchers argue that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce the rule of law are crucial for sustained economic growth, while others posit that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs significantly influence institutional development and economic outcomes. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. The stakes are high because understanding the causal mechanisms behind long-run economic growth can inform effective policies to reduce poverty and promote prosperity.

Austin (2008, p.2) and North (1989, p.9) agree that institutions are pivotal in shaping economic outcomes, but they diverge on how these institutions emerge and evolve. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while North contends that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs. North's emphasis on secure property rights as a key to long-term economic growth is shared by Austin, who notes that the institutionalist approach already highlights the political delivery of secure private property rights. Moreover, both researchers acknowledge the complexity of testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively and explaining why institutions favorable for economic growth are adopted in some countries but not others.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) present a compelling argument that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were not merely about geographic factors. They argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies and more inclusive institutions in low-mortality settlements. This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on property rights and transaction costs, as Acemoglu et al. highlight how institutional changes can significantly impact economic development over the long run.

Ogilvie (2007, p.3) offers a contrasting view by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. She suggests that institutions are shaped by shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, which can block the development of property rights and contract enforcement. This perspective challenges the notion that efficient institutions naturally emerge to maximize economic growth, instead emphasizing the role of power dynamics and cultural beliefs in shaping institutional outcomes.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)  work on equilibrium institutions complements Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts by highlighting how politically powerful groups can shape institutions to maximize their rents, rather than promoting long-run growth. This dynamic is evident in the context of former colonies, where equilibrium institutions were designed to benefit European colonists at the expense of local populations. The interplay between power dynamics and institutional design underscores the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth.

The debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on how institutions emerge, evolve, and impact economic outcomes. While some researchers emphasize the importance of property rights and transaction costs, others highlight the role of distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs in shaping institutional development. This ongoing debate underscores the need for further research to better understand the causal mechanisms behind long-run economic growth and inform effective policy-making.

Their work shows that the nature of European settlement in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, was largely determined by mortality rates among Europeans; high mortality led to extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment, while low mortality facilitated the establishment of more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) identify a key mechanism in this process: the political power dynamics between colonizers and indigenous populations. Where European settlers were numerous and healthy, they established institutions that protected their interests and fostered growth; where Europeans were few and susceptible to disease, they implemented extractive institutions to quickly exploit resources before returning home. This dynamic is further nuanced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) observation that population density and prosperity in colonies also influenced European policies. High-density, prosperous areas attracted more sustained European investment and institutional development, whereas less dense or poorer regions were often subject to more exploitative practices. This underscores the complex interplay between colonial policies, institutional development, and economic outcomes in former colonies.

The development of financial institutions and instruments was pivotal in driving long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, along with new financial instruments, significantly decreased transaction costs. This financial revolution was a direct response to fiscal crises and reflected increased security in property rights over time. These changes were both economic and political, involving the trade of certain rights to Parliament for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms played a crucial role in facilitating market exchanges, which in turn led to falling costs per transaction and an increasing proportion of the transactions sector within GNP (North 1989, p.6).

They argue that the success of England's financial institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital market data. This success hinged on the regime's commitment to rules governing public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also note that recent research has upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that institutional differences between England and France were not as revolutionary as previously thought.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are more fundamental than efficiency in shaping key economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the traditional view that institutions primarily emerge to maximize economic productivity. Ogilvie contends that the core economic institutions, serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by power struggles among different social groups rather than by a quest for efficiency. The institutional framework of pre-industrial Europe was driven by conflicts over resources and political influence. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this perspective to New World societies, where institutions in highly unequal environments favored elites through restrictive government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional design impeded competition and reinforced inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can perpetuate economic disparities. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of initial conditions on long-term economic outcomes. This perspective has its nuances. Ogilvie acknowledges that while efficiency considerations are not irrelevant, they often play a secondary role to the immediate interests of powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that even when institutional changes might enhance overall productivity, they may be resisted or delayed due to the perceived threats they pose to existing power structures.

McCloskey (2015, p.3) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) share that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe between 1600 and 1848 (Pamuk 2007, p.23; McCloskey 2015, p.3). They argue that the ideological shifts allowed for greater experimentation and open discourse, which facilitated the adoption of new techniques and institutions. This ideological environment was crucial for the uptake of innovations such as the electric motor and the modern research university, both of which required a society permissive to talk openly about experiments (McCloskey 2015, p.3). Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that institutional flexibility played a significant role in enabling regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that while technological progress is essential, it also requires institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use. These adjustments are necessary for resolving conflicts arising from economic growth and maintaining the political stability of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Thus, the interplay between ideology and institutions was critical in shaping long-run economic growth during this period.

Nunn and Wantchekon's work shows that Africa’s slave trades had an adverse effect on its economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This effect is partially due to the lasting cultural impact of mistrust, which developed as a result of the slave trade. This internal norm of mistrust is at least twice as large as external factors like the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn's research suggests that the duration and intensity of these slave trades were significant, lasting nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. This is notably longer than the period of official colonial rule, which spanned approximately 75 years from 1885 to about 1960 (Nunn 2008, p.2). Furthermore, Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had a profound impact on economic development beyond mere colonialism or more recent events. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape contemporary African economies in ways that go beyond institutional frameworks established during and after colonial rule.

This cluster examines how historical institutional paths shaped economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). These scholars argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further posit that high productivity in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as production, highlighting the role of service sectors in early modern development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the institutional frameworks fostering growth were not merely a result of colonial policies but were deeply intertwined with pre-colonial economic activities. Austin (2008, p.2) also points out that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, adjusting their institutional prescriptions accordingly (Austin 2008, p.15), this indicates that the legacy of institutions is more complex than a simple imposition by colonizers. Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.9) analysis underscores that high productivity in agriculture was influenced by investment patterns and geographic factors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This means that institutional frameworks varied based on local conditions, influencing economic growth trajectories differently across regions.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial rule influenced institutional change in Africa through a complex interplay between African initiatives and exogenous factors. This interaction shaped the development of formal education systems across sub-Saharan Africa. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby affecting its uptake and impact on economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) extend this argument by highlighting that African poverty is not solely a result of persistent historical impediments but rather a product of specific historical and geographical conditions. This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding how colonial institutions interacted with local contexts to shape long-term economic outcomes, suggesting that institutional legacies are more dynamic than often assumed.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) along with Broadberry and Gardner (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) highlight that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women were instrumental in food farming, often relegated to marginal lands, yet their contributions propelled significant agricultural revolutions. For instance, the migration of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in search of farm land for cocoa production made the Gold Coast a leading producer by 1911 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This economic activity contributed to broader market dynamics. In colonial contexts, women's roles were often codified into customary law, which reinforced their subordinate status and limited access to resources like land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Broadberry and Gardner underscore that this economic activity was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, further emphasizing the interconnectedness of local agricultural production and global economic systems (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors including transport costs and political institutions. This variability suggests that while women's contributions were substantial, the institutional framework, including colonial policies and customary law, significantly influenced their economic impact.

Van Zanden's work on Europe's economic performance highlights the complex interplay between human capital and economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Van Zanden's examination of the skill premium in Western Europe underscores how formal institutions, such as guilds, facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills. This institutional framework was crucial for ensuring fair execution of apprentice contracts, an important mechanism for fostering human capital development (van Zanden 2009, p.19). While this aspect of guilds may have been beneficial, their restrictive practices could also limit entry into trades and inflate wages above market-clearing levels. This dual role of institutions in both promoting and inhibiting economic growth is evident in the historical context of Europe's economic performance.

Bolt and van Zanden's collaborative research on measuring long-term economic development further illustrates this dynamic (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). They argue that Maddison’s pioneering work, despite its margins of error, sparked a wave of scholarly engagement that refined our understanding of historical economic growth. Van Zanden's earlier work on European living standards complicates this picture by showing that economic development did not always translate into improved living standards. For example, the shift in economic focus to North Sea countries like England and Holland during the 17th century did not uniformly lead to higher wages or better living conditions across Europe (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 1999, p.9). This variability suggests that while institutions play a critical role in shaping long-term economic growth, their impact is mediated by broader socio-economic factors.

Simson's work on budget policies in post-independence East Africa and Peters' analysis of land systems in colonial Africa both highlight how specific institutional dynamics, while not directly addressing long-run economic growth, shape the socio-economic landscape (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Budgetary decisions, such as subsidies and military spending, were heavily influenced by regional instability and domestic dissent. This pattern is evident in Kenya's high subsidies and interest payments, which peaked at 30 percent of spending in the early 1990s (Simson 2019, p.11). Similarly, the shift from colonial to post-colonial governance led to increased military expenditures as defence became a national rather than imperial responsibility (Simson 2019, p.11). These budgetary shifts reflect broader institutional changes that redirected resources away from public goods and services toward consumption and security. Peters' work reveals how land systems in Africa were not merely flexible but also concealed processes of exclusion and class formation, with agricultural officers expressing dissatisfaction over perceived 'communal' landholding practices (Peters 2004, p.5). This dissatisfaction was fueled by the push for agricultural development, highlighting how institutional perceptions can drive policy changes. However, the extent to which these budgetary and land system dynamics directly influence long-run economic growth remains an open question, as both Simson's and Peters' analyses focus more on immediate socio-political impacts than on sustained economic outcomes. The broader implications of these institutional changes for long-term economic development in Africa warrant further investigation.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are central to the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. These changes involved complex interactions between population density, prosperity, and European policies. Austin (2008, p.15) adds nuance by highlighting how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, underscoring that institutions evolved based on local dynamics rather than being uniformly imposed. This interplay between external forces and local adaptations is crucial for understanding the long-term impacts of colonial institutions on economic development.

North (1989, p.9) explains how the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs and secured property rights, which are critical for economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) complements this by arguing that shared beliefs within a society also constitute part of its institutional structure. These beliefs can either facilitate or hinder economic development by influencing the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. The historical evidence from Europe shows that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights led to falling transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This historical experience underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty. Hopkins (2009, p.18) demonstrates that ethnic homogeneity does not guarantee good governance, and ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This finding is crucial because it shows that institutional quality is more important than ethnic composition in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate how extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote long-run growth, have persistent negative effects on economic development. The shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) is that the quality of institutions matters more than ethnic or geographic factors in determining long-term economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing transaction costs, property rights, and market exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies is tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were not merely about resource extraction but involved complex interactions between population density, prosperity, and European policies. The remaining disagreement is whether the quality of institutions matters more than ethnic or geographic factors in determining long-term economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the experience of ethnically heterogeneous societies like some African countries, which have developed progressive institutions despite their diversity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shows that institutional quality, not ethnic composition, is crucial for economic development.
