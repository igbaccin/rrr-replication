The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has spurred extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake is the very understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centres around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures are crucial for fostering investment and economic development. Others posit that institutional frameworks alone cannot account for economic growth without considering the broader historical and cultural contexts in which they operate.

For example, Acemoglu et al. highlight how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions in colonies, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring influence of institutional frameworks set during colonial periods. Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; his work suggests that a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary to fully grasp long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). This tension between simplistic and complex views of institutional impact sets the stage for a deeper exploration into how institutions shape economic outcomes over time.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework for market exchanges and property rights enforcement. The English financial revolution, which led to the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, exemplifies this dynamic: it lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development facilitated a growing economy characterized by increasing specialization and division of labor, thereby expanding the volume of market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this mechanism by highlighting that institutions can also be extractive in nature: they may prioritize rents for politically powerful groups rather than fostering long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the quality and purpose of institutions are critical factors in determining their impact on economic development. The implications suggest that colonialism's legacy, including its institutional frameworks, can significantly influence a country’s economic trajectory over centuries.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of these institutions ensured that rules governing economic exchange were enforced consistently, providing a stable framework for investment and innovation. This institutional stability was particularly evident in capital markets, where secure property rights encouraged long-term investments. Austin (2008, p.2) elaborates on the importance of testing this institutionalist approach quantitatively, highlighting that it is difficult to explain why some countries adopted growth-fostering institutions while others did not (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the adoption and effectiveness of these institutions are contingent upon specific historical and political contexts. The implication here is that while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, their implementation depends on a complex interplay of factors including political stability and institutional design.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping economic institutions during pre-industrial Europe, influencing the formation of serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie argues that these institutions were the outcome of power struggles among social groups. The specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped these institutions was via the control exerted by elites over political processes, allowing them to enforce rules and regulations that favoured their interests. This perspective underscores how institutional design in pre-industrial Europe was driven more by power dynamics than by efficiency considerations. Sokoloff and Engerman's work on New World societies further illustrates this dynamic, showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites through various government policies restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This pattern highlights how the institutional landscape can be shaped by underlying social inequalities, which in turn influence economic development. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding long-run growth patterns, as they suggest that institutional design is deeply rooted in historical power structures and distributional conflicts, which may persist over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

The specific nature of colonial governance played a significant role in shaping educational development in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This was due to the unique institutional framework established by the British, which influenced both macro-level political decisions and micro-level family calculations about the benefits of education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education in British colonies was driven by a complex set of social and political institutions that altered the perceived advantages of formal education. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely between countries due to factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). While British governance facilitated some level of economic development through education, the variability in growth patterns underscores that colonial policies alone do not determine long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between colonial governance and local conditions shaped educational outcomes and economic performance in complex ways, highlighting the multifaceted role of institutions in economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. The accumulation of useful knowledge propels technological advancements, which in turn stimulate economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights how technological innovation creates new opportunities for productivity and wealth creation, while Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) underscores the need for institutional adjustments to efficiently use these innovations.

Kuznets (1973, p.6) notes that modern economic growth requires resolving conflicts arising from technological changes without weakening societal cohesion. This highlights a condition under which technological progress can be sustained, namely when it aligns with the political fabric of society. These adjustments must address coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) points out, to ensure that economic improvements are sustainable.

Kuznets' analysis introduces a qualification: the institutional adjustments necessary for technological progress to fuel economic growth may not always be straightforward or immediate. The ability of institutions to adapt to new technologies can vary widely, influencing how effectively these innovations translate into economic gains. This variability underscores the complexity of institutional responses to technological change and their impact on long-run economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels, which are crucial for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This enduring mistrust creates an environment where social cooperation is hindered, making it difficult to establish the necessary institutions for sustained economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) point out that this mistrust affects both internal norms and external environments, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This deep-seated mistrust impedes the development of strong social networks and effective governance structures, which are essential for fostering economic progress. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further reinforced patriarchal alliances that marginalized women's access to land and resources, exacerbating economic disparities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

This mistrust is not an isolated factor; it intersects with broader social dynamics shaped by historical events. This systemic marginalization limited women's economic contributions and perpetuated a cycle of mistrust and inequality. The interplay between historical trauma from the slave trade and colonial policies has created a multifaceted barrier to economic development in Africa. While the legacy of the slave trade remains a significant factor, understanding its intersection with gender dynamics and legal systems is crucial for addressing contemporary challenges in African economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) highlight institutional reforms as pivotal for reducing poverty in Africa. They underscore that the new economic history of Africa can provide valuable insights into designing effective institutional reforms, which are essential for fostering development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the quality of public goods and governance significantly influences economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18), while Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize the importance of understanding long-term economic trends to identify structural impediments to growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that by addressing institutional weaknesses, African countries can create a more conducive environment for investment and development. Hopkins cautions that institutional reforms must be tailored to local contexts, as ethnically homogeneous societies may still struggle with dysfunctional institutions while heterogeneous ones can uphold the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This implies that successful poverty reduction strategies require a nuanced approach that considers both historical legacies and contemporary challenges.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic development are multifaceted. North (1989, p.9) argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling costs per transaction, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is critical for long-term economic growth, as it provides incentives for investment. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
