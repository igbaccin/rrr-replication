The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and political science. Institutions are the rules of the game that structure interactions among individuals and organizations. They encompass formal rules such as laws and regulations, as well as informal norms and conventions. The stakes are high because understanding how institutions influence economic outcomes can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable growth. Two key positions exist in this debate: one emphasizes institutions' foundational role in shaping long-term economic performance, while the other focuses on the complex interplay between historical paths and institutional development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This debate is crucial because it shapes how economists and policymakers view the potential for institutional reforms to drive economic growth.

The foundational role of institutions in shaping long-term economic performance is evident in several studies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes, with some colonies developing extractive states while others established rule-of-law-based systems. These institutional differences significantly affected current income per capita. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth by fostering secure property rights and economic development. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlights the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which are crucial for economic growth.

The evolution of institutions over time is a complex process influenced by historical paths and political dynamics. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that economists view patterns and paths of institutional evolution as critical to understanding long-term economic performance. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) suggests that institutional reforms can inform development policies aimed at eliminating poverty. These studies underscore the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic outcomes over time.

Institutional quality is a critical factor in determining long-term economic performance. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which persists today and affects trust levels within Africa, highlighting how historical events can shape institutional outcomes. The quality of institutions, as measured by factors such as the rule of law and property rights, is crucial for fostering an environment conducive to economic growth.

The role of credible commitment in institutional development is another key factor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to rules governing public choice. This perspective aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011, p.27) finding that trust levels within Africa are influenced by historical events, such as the slave trade, highlighting how credible commitment can shape institutional outcomes over time.

Acemoglu et al. extend this by showing that institutions can be extractive or growth-enhancing depending on how they are designed. Extractive institutions can persist when powerful groups prioritize short-term rents over long-run growth, as seen in colonial contexts where European settlers established institutions to maximize their own benefits rather than fostering local economic development (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores that the impact of institutions on growth depends on both their design and the incentives and power dynamics of those who shape them. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this by showing how initial conditions, such as population density and prosperity in colonies, influenced the types of institutions Europeans implemented (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutional frameworks then shaped long-term economic trajectories by determining whether colonies could industrialize or were relegated to producing raw materials. This illustrates that while institutions are crucial for growth, their effectiveness is mediated by historical and contextual factors.

This institutional framework, which ensured credible commitments from rulers to property rights, facilitated long-term investment and economic growth. The mechanism by which this occurred is through the establishment of stable institutions that could enforce contracts and protect investments; these institutions were central in enabling sustained economic progress (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this idea by highlighting how the quality of public goods provision, which is closely tied to institutional effectiveness, can significantly impact economic outcomes. This suggests that even if a society has strong public choice institutions, it must also have effective governance structures in place to ensure that investments are made and resources are efficiently allocated. The downstream implication of these findings is that while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, the broader institutional context, including governance and public goods provision, plays a crucial role in determining long-term economic trajectories.

Mokyr's emphasis on "useful knowledge" as the mainspring of economic growth aligns with Kuznets' observation that epochal innovations drive sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). While institutions are crucial for resolving conflicts and ensuring economic growth, they must also facilitate the diffusion of technological advancements. Mokyr highlights how institutional progress complements technological progress, suggesting that without such complementarity, rent-seeking behavior could undermine economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that modern technology requires significant ideological adjustments for efficient use and integration into society (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and political implications of technological change. For example, Kuznets points out that sovereign states play a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that economic progress is sustainable without weakening societal cohesion. If institutions fail to adapt or coordinate effectively with technological advancements, the potential for long-term economic growth could be severely limited (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had direct adverse effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2), establishing an alternative explanation to institutional factors by showing the enduring impact of historical events. The role of historical institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is particularly relevant here: if historical events such as slave trades fostered mistrust and weakened governance structures, these institutional deficiencies could persistently hinder economic development regardless of other institutional factors. The interplay between historical events such as slave trades and institutional development underscores the complexity of factors influencing long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner argue that Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This challenges previous narratives that uniformly characterized colonial rule as detrimental to economic development. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era has enabled recent work in African economic history to demonstrate this, highlighting that export production varied significantly due to local endowments, transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15). This variation underscores how institutional factors shaped economic outcomes during colonial rule. This suggests that the type of colonial rule and the accompanying institutional frameworks played a crucial role in determining educational outcomes. This variability indicates that while British rule may have been more conducive to formal education development, other institutional factors also significantly impacted economic performance.

Colonial legacies have played a significant role in shaping African development trajectories, offering insights that complement broader discussions on long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Austin (2008, p.2) posits that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Colonial administrations' institutional prescriptions were often responsive to indigenous economic and political action, rather than uniformly extractive or exploitative. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production in the absence of European settlers competing for African labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how local conditions and adaptive governance influenced economic outcomes, suggesting that institutional frameworks under colonial rule were not monolithic but varied based on contextual factors. The debate over the reversal of fortune thesis underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key factors in explaining the relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective contrasts with geographic explanations, emphasizing that it was the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century that played a central role in long-run development.
