The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and why some prosper while others stagnate. The central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and rule of law are crucial for sustained economic development. Others contend that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, influenced by historical contexts and political dynamics. A third perspective suggests that the efficiency of institutions is less important than their role in managing distributional conflicts within societies. These differing views highlight the need to examine how various institutional frameworks have evolved and impacted economic outcomes over time. The interaction between institutions and other factors such as population density and prosperity also shapes long-term development trajectories, especially in former colonies. This interplay underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions operate.

Institutions that enforce property rights and rule of law are central to long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, with extractive states emerging in high-mortality colonies. This highlights how initial conditions can shape the types of institutions that develop and their impact on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North (1989, p.9) argues that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for long-term economic development  (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and innovation.

The role of institutions in managing distributional conflicts within societies highlights another dimension of their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective posits that institutions serve as mechanisms for managing power dynamics within societies and tools for economic efficiency (Author Year p.N). Austin (2008, p.2) further complicates this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). Colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political action, adapting their institutional prescriptions in ways that reflect broader historical contexts. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve within specific historical and political frameworks.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights how institutions can shape economic outcomes over extended periods, even when initial conditions are similar. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is influenced by both ethno-linguistic fragmentation and the effectiveness of institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of understanding how institutions can be reformed to promote economic development and reduce poverty.

Where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly exploit resources. This was driven by a short-term perspective where long-run growth was not prioritized (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Where European settlers had lower mortality rates, they created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping long-run development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights how initial colonial policies can have enduring effects on economic growth, even after centuries of independence.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that seventeenth-century England's evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. The institutional changes in England were so significant that they led to a shift in traditional interpretations of economic history. However, this view is qualified by the fact that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary enough to foster secure property rights and economic growth, as evidenced by the remarkable success of English capital markets during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This highlights how the institutions governing public choice can have a profound impact on long-term economic outcomes.

Frankema contends that British colonial rule fostered formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa through the introduction of new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). The impact of these institutions on African education was not uniform across all colonies; rather, it depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African leaders at both macro- and micro-levels. This dynamic highlights how colonial powers influenced educational development in sub-Saharan Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that British rule was instrumental in developing export markets, which were tightly linked to economic growth patterns across many Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that while colonial institutions may have fostered certain aspects of development, they also created structural impediments that affected long-term welfare development across British Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Therefore, the legacy of colonial institutions on African economic growth is complex and multifaceted: it both facilitated and constrained economic progress.

These changes were not uniform across Africa, leading to varied economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Hopkins 2009, p.18) share the view that African women played significant roles in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) finds that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty. Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system with European courts based on common law and civil law alongside native courts, which codified customary laws that often marginalized women's access to land for cash crops, reinforcing patriarchal alliances (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dynamic is an example of how colonial institutions interacted with pre-existing social structures. The gendered impact of institutional changes during colonial rule highlights the complexities of economic development in Africa. This focus on institutions suggests that understanding their role in African economic development requires a nuanced approach that considers both colonial legacies and local contexts.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) challenge the simplistic compression of historical periods in the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Austin argues that the thesis overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution, investment patterns, and policy experiences unique to each country. This critique highlights a key mechanism: the divergent paths of long-run economic growth are attributable to both colonial institutions and pre-existing local conditions and endogenous responses to colonial policies. Nunn (2008, p.2) adds that Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, significantly impacted subsequent economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). Austin further illustrates this point by noting that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local responses complicates the narrative of African underdevelopment. However, Austin acknowledges that this complexity does not negate the role of institutions entirely but rather suggests a more nuanced understanding of how they interact with other factors over time.

Distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) jointly argue that the core institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, instead highlighting that institutions are often a product of underlying social struggles.

These institutions were not necessarily efficient but served to preserve existing power structures. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This pattern suggests that institutional design is heavily influenced by the distribution of power and economic considerations.

The downstream implications of these distributional conflicts are significant. The interplay between distributional conflicts and the resulting institutions underscores the complex relationship between social dynamics and economic outcomes. This dynamic interplay suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of how power struggles shape institutional development, highlighting the importance of considering both economic and social factors in historical analysis.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to establish and enforce property rights, which are crucial for fostering investment and economic development (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), while North highlights how the creation of financial instruments and secure property rights led to a decline in transaction costs, thereby promoting growth (North 1989, p.6). This shared finding about the importance of secure property rights shows that the institutional framework set up by colonizers had lasting impacts on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time is further supported by Ogilvie's observation that certain beliefs about economic systems can reinforce institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), illustrating how deeply ingrained social dynamics can shape long-term economic trajectories.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions affect growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. contend that extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development underscores the complex relationship between social dynamics and economic outcomes. This dynamic interplay suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of how power struggles shape institutional development, highlighting the importance of considering both economic and social factors in historical analysis.

The empirical evidence from colonial histories shows that institutions designed to extract resources rather than foster growth had lasting negative effects on economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). This is further supported by Hopkins' argument that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, emphasizing the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence from former European colonies demonstrates that extractive institutions persist over time. This persistence is evident in the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
