The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake intellectually is understanding the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty across nations. One live disagreement concerns whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are crucial for fostering investment and innovation, thereby driving long-term economic development. On the other hand, rival explanations propose that geographical factors, such as climate and natural resources, or cultural beliefs about work ethic and individual rights, fundamentally shape a society's economic trajectory. The debate hinges on whether institutions can be seen as the independent variable that shapes growth or if they are themselves shaped by underlying geographic or cultural forces.

This review synthesises scholarship that explores the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth. It then turns to rival explanations that propose different drivers of growth, arguing that these alternative factors operate independently of institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) assert that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They argue that differences in mortality rates led Europeans to establish either extractive or inclusive institutions. This pattern is evident in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, as seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast. The persistence of these colonial institutions has had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) further substantiate this by showing that the quality of institutions, whether measured by constraints on the executive or property rights, significantly correlates with current income per capita. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies on economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century; this suggests that while colonial institutions set initial conditions, subsequent historical processes have also shaped long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis highlights how political changes during this period led to financial innovations such as the creation of the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development is crucial because it reflects an increased security of property rights over time, a dimension critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political model described by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) shows that the trade-off between certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises was instrumental in this process.

The implications of these institutional changes are further elucidated by examining the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges, which led to falling costs per transaction despite an increasing volume of exchanges due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic is significant because it demonstrates how political stability, underpinned by secure property rights, can foster a robust financial system that supports long-term economic growth. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between political stability, property rights, and economic growth, indicating that while English institutions played a significant role, they were part of a broader historical context that also influenced economic outcomes. The evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England thus set a foundation for secure property rights and economic growth, but their impact must be understood within the wider dynamics of European economic history.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both critique a narrow view of institutional causation in economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique addresses periodization and the mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting a dynamic interplay between local incentives and external policies (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this by suggesting that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that institutions are not merely efficient responses to economic needs but are also shaped by power dynamics and social structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

This dynamic view of institutions complicates the idea that secure property rights alone drive long-run growth, suggesting instead a more complex interplay between institutional forms, local incentives, and broader historical contexts. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) warns against reducing institutions to mere fashion accessories in economic historiography (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The implication is that while institutions are crucial, their role must be understood within the broader context of social and political dynamics. This nuanced view challenges simplistic notions of institutional causation and underscores the need for a more holistic approach to understanding economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) jointly establish that colonial institutions shaped educational development in sub-Saharan Africa through mechanisms of demand for missionary education. The implementation of new social and political institutions by British rulers altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing both African political leaders' macro-level calculations and families' micro-level decisions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was rooted in broader economic inequalities that advantaged elites through government policies, restricting competition and contributing to enduring disparities. Sokoloff and Engerman point out that these institutions evolved from the specific factor endowments of colonial societies, which varied significantly across regions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implication is that while British rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, this outcome was contingent on the initial economic conditions and institutional frameworks established during colonization.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) focus on historical economic performance in Africa and Europe, showing that per capita income levels and trade patterns varied significantly across regions. Their work demonstrates the availability of extensive economic data for colonial-era Africa, contradicting previous notions of uniform economic stagnation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight the tight linkage between export production growth and foreign trade in many African economies. This pattern suggests that external market forces played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories. The variability in the timing and scale of this growth indicates that local factors, such as endowments and transport costs, also influenced these outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner's analysis underscores the importance of considering both global trade dynamics and regional specifics when examining long-run economic performance. This nuanced view is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's (2006, p.15) examination of early modern Europe, which shows that institutional changes drove agricultural investment and productivity gains in northwestern Europe. Their findings suggest that while external factors like trade were important, internal institutional developments also significantly impacted economic growth. The implications for understanding long-run economic growth are clear: both global market forces and local institutional frameworks must be considered to fully grasp the complexities of economic development.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) jointly find that economic development and living standards in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not follow a straightforward trajectory. This is further complicated by van Zanden's (2009, p.19) examination of the skill premium, which highlights how institutional factors like guilds influenced both the supply and demand for skilled labor. However, van Zanden (2009, p.19) also notes that these institutions could act as local cartels, restricting entry into trades and keeping wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This dual nature of guilds, both facilitating skill transfer and potentially hindering competition, illustrates the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) also notes how interest rates influenced the net present value of increased income from skilled labor, highlighting another institutional factor that could affect long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This suggests that while institutions like guilds had a role in shaping economic development, their impact was not uniformly positive. The shift in economic focus to countries bordering the North Sea during this period underscores how regional dynamics and institutional frameworks shaped the trajectory of European economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.9).

Peters (2004, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (Peters 2004, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) examine the nuances of land systems, class formation, and economic measurement in Africa and Europe, highlighting how these factors influence political-economic processes. They reveal that land systems embody social relations and power structures that shape economic outcomes. This perspective is crucial because it underscores how land tenure systems can either facilitate or hinder economic development by influencing who has access to resources. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) extend this idea by noting the complexities in estimating past national accounts, which are often based on partial data and assumptions about economic activities (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). They emphasize that these estimates, while subject to margins of error, provide valuable insights into long-term economic growth. Peters (2004, p.19) further qualifies this implication by showing how perceptions of 'custom' can be manipulated to maintain control over resources, illustrating the political dimensions of land systems (Peters 2004, p.5). This highlights that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of how land systems and class formation interact with broader political-economic processes.

Nunn and Wantchekon establish that the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trade affected economic development through immediate extraction of labor and by fostering internal norms of mistrust. These norms, in turn, have persisted and continue to hinder economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is not merely due to the external environment such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others but also through individuals' internal norms of mistrust, with this internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one. This finding is bolstered by Nunn's broader argument that Africa's underdevelopment cannot be attributed solely to colonialism or more recent events, but must also consider the long-term effects of the slave trades. Economic development in Africa has been stunted by immediate resource extraction and by the enduring cultural and social legacies of the slave trade. This suggests that addressing Africa's current underdevelopment requires addressing these deep-seated issues.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) argue that specific institutional changes can drive long-run economic growth in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). They propose that targeted reforms to governance structures and budget policies can create incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The key mechanism here is the ability of societies to develop progressive institutions regardless of their ethnic composition. Simson (2019, p.2) demonstrates how post-independence budget policies in East Africa, often seen as clientelist and unproductive, were not inherently detrimental to economic growth (Simson 2019, p.2), instead, these policies reflected strategic responses to regional instability and growing domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This nuanced understanding of budgetary shifts suggests that institutional reforms can be tailored to address specific historical and political contexts. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this view by showing that African poverty is not a persistent, structurally-impeded phenomenon but rather a dynamic issue influenced by historical wage trends (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The implication of these findings is that institutional reforms can be designed to mitigate the effects of historical economic disparities and promote sustainable growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that economic growth is driven primarily by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr highlights how the advancement of technology and innovation creates a positive feedback loop, where new knowledge leads to further technological developments. This process drives economic growth and requires institutional adjustments to fully harness these innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) explains that these adjustments are essential for managing conflicts arising from technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). For instance, the industrialization process in early developed countries necessitated significant institutional reforms to address social and political tensions caused by rapid economic shifts.

The sovereign state plays a crucial role in this context by providing a stable framework that resolves growth-induced conflicts peacefully. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements may not sustain long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the effective use of new technologies and knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that these adjustments must be made efficiently to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. Thus, while technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, institutional developments are indispensable for sustaining this growth over the long term.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.3; Pamuk 2007, p.23). He posits that the shift towards a more open intellectual environment allowed for the diffusion of new techniques and institutions. This ideological shift enabled experimentation and open discourse, which were crucial for economic innovation. For instance, McCloskey highlights that the Dutch Republic after 1600 and England after 1688 fostered societies where unconventional ideas could be proposed without fear of retribution (McCloskey 2015, p.4). This intellectual freedom facilitated the development of new technologies and institutions, such as the electric motor and modern research universities. Pamuk supports this view by showing that institutional flexibility was key in enabling regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities after the Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.23). However, Pamuk also notes that while institutional flexibility was important, it was not the sole driver of economic change, urbanisation rates and productivity levels varied significantly between northwestern and southern Europe. This variation suggests that while institutions played a role, ideologies and ideas were pivotal in shaping long-term economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.16).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of property rights and market exchanges. North argues that institutions matter because they define the rules of the game in society, which affect the efficiency of markets (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by Austin, who notes that the evolution of institutions over time has been critical to understanding patterns of long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). Both scholars emphasize that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering a conducive environment for investment and economic development. The establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in 1694, which led to the English financial revolution, is an example of how institutional changes can significantly reduce transaction costs and promote growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings highlight that the creation of efficient property rights and their enforcement are essential for economic development.

The role of institutions extends beyond mere efficiency. Distributional conflicts also shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can persist and significantly impact long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings underscore the importance of understanding how different groups within society influence institutional design. The persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. illustrates how initial conditions and power dynamics shape long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Colonialism played a significant role in shaping institutional frameworks in former colonies. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, arguing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are central to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings show that colonial policies, which often prioritized resource extraction over long-term growth, had lasting effects on economic development. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century highlights how historical institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a shared finding across these sources (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). All agree that institutions matter for economic growth, but they disagree on the precise mechanisms by which this occurs. North emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6), while Ogilvie highlights distributional conflicts as a driving force behind institutional design (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This evidence provides a clear measurement of the impact of institutions on long-run growth, showing how initial conditions and power dynamics shape institutional frameworks and subsequent economic outcomes.
