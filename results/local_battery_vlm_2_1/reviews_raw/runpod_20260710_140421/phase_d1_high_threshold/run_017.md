The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it challenges our understanding of the drivers behind sustained prosperity. At its core, this debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinants of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Two prominent positions emerge: one emphasizes the causal power of institutions in driving growth, while another argues that institutional effects are mediated by other variables and cannot be studied in isolation. The tension between these views highlights the need to understand how institutions interact with various economic and historical factors to influence long-term development.

One stream of scholarship underscores the critical role of institutions in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional frameworks, which significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is complemented by North's assertion that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). These arguments highlight how institutions shape economic trajectories by influencing investment decisions and resource allocation. The role of secure property rights in fostering a stable environment for economic activity is crucial, as it encourages investment and innovation.

The interplay between institutions and historical contexts further enriches our understanding of their impact on growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates has had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical context in which institutions are established is therefore pivotal, as it shapes their effectiveness and longevity.

Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their research demonstrates that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement. This divergence in institutional design had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as extractive institutions impeded development by discouraging investment and innovation. The persistence of these institutions over time further entrenched economic disparities between colonies with different institutional legacies.

Acemoglu et al.'s analysis reveals that the initial conditions under which colonial institutions were established were crucial in determining their long-term effects (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). In regions where Europeans could settle and establish effective governance, the institutions fostered economic growth. In areas marked by high mortality rates and extractive institutions, economic stagnation was prevalent. This pattern underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between political power and institutional design, suggesting that even within a colonial context, the motivations and interests of the ruling elite significantly shape economic trajectories.

North and Weingast assert that financial institutions like the Bank of England were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of these institutions facilitated market exchanges by lowering the cost per transaction, which became a growing proportion of GNP as specialization increased.

The development of financial instruments during this period resulted from both economic factors and political negotiations. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, reflecting a broader political model that underpinned institutional change (North 1989, p.9). This interplay between politics and economics highlights how institutions can be shaped by historical events and power dynamics, further reinforcing their role in economic growth. Debate exists regarding how much these institutions truly transformed economic performance. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on growth, indicating that while financial institutions were crucial, their effects must be understood within a broader historical context. The evolution of political institutions that govern public choice and enforce property rights remains central to understanding economic development over time.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts gave rise to institutions that reflected the interests of those with political power, thus influencing long-term economic outcomes. Ogilvie highlights how distributional struggles influenced institutions like serfdom and guilds, which were products of power dynamics. She argues that these institutions served to maintain the status quo by ensuring that the powerful could retain their advantages (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Institutions reflect the interests of dominant groups. This perspective challenges conventional views by highlighting the role of power dynamics in institutional formation. For example, Ogilvie critiques exogenous cultural theories which posit that beliefs about efficiency or diligence drive institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). She argues instead that these institutions were shaped by conflicts over resources and power (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a nuanced examination of how power dynamics influence their development.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This suggests that institutions play a secondary role in facilitating technological change, with long-run economic growth driven primarily by other factors.

This qualification underscores the importance of institutions even within a framework that prioritizes technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on the role of institutions by noting that sovereign states play a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully. This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions are essential for managing the social and political challenges that arise from economic development; without effective institutions, even technological progress can be undermined by societal tensions.

This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between technology and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) both argue that colonial rule shaped institutional change in Africa through the negotiation and imposition of policies. This suggests that the type of colonial administration mattered significantly in shaping institutional frameworks. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) build on this by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions played a role in economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This implies that although colonial rule set the stage for institutional development, other contextual factors also influenced long-run economic growth in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. The authors argue that this legacy of mistrust significantly impacts social trust within African societies. This mistrust has deep historical roots, shaped by experiences such as the transatlantic slave trade. Austin (2008, p.15) further elaborates on how colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic actions influenced institutional prescriptions, suggesting that historical legacies are intertwined with contemporary institutional frameworks. This dynamic highlights how enduring mistrust can influence the effectiveness of institutions and their impact on economic outcomes. The persistent effects of the slave trade on trust underscore the importance of understanding historical contexts in shaping current social dynamics.

Historical legacies play a crucial role in shaping current institutional frameworks and their impact on economic outcomes. European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of different institutions across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Other regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast saw extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional development has had lasting effects on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The persistence of these historical legacies underscores the importance of understanding how past actions influence contemporary social and economic dynamics.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the concept of credible commitment, which underpins long-run economic growth. Institutions that provide secure property rights and enforce contracts are essential for fostering investment and economic development (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. These institutions reflect increased security in property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. The fiscal crises of historical periods, such as the Hundred Years' War, highlight how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement contribute to economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The impact of colonial administrations on institutional prescriptions also highlights the enduring effects of historical legacies. Colonial governments often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, which influenced the institutional frameworks they imposed. For example, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium illustrates how colonial administrations adapted their strategies based on local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic shows that historical contexts significantly shape current social dynamics and economic outcomes.

The reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Equilibrium institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth. This historical evidence shows that the design of institutions can significantly impact economic outcomes.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but also by the presence of progressive institutions. This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Austin 2008, p.15) shows that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing effective institutions.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is the critical role that institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the enduring effects of historical legacies on current institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Disagreement persists over whether distributional conflicts or efficiency best explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the case of indigenous commodity production in colonial Africa, which shows how adaptations in institutional prescriptions were driven by local economic actions and political incentives (Austin 2008, p.15).
