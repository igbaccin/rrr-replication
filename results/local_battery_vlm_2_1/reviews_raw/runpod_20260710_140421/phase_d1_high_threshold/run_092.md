The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. Institutions are defined as the rules that structure political, social and economic interactions. The live disagreement concerns whether institutions matter for growth at all, and if so, what kind of institutions promote it. One position argues that institutions, particularly those enforcing property rights and rule of law, are essential for fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is rooted in the idea that secure property rights encourage investment by ensuring that individuals can reap the benefits of their efforts. Another position posits that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper factors such as cultural beliefs or geographic endowments, which ultimately determine economic outcomes. A third position posits that institutions are tools for powerful groups to extract rents and maintain their political power, while also being drivers of growth. These divergent views highlight the complexity of understanding how institutions influence economic growth over the long term.

Institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms. North argues that the development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduces transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. contend that institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment were critical in shaping long-term economic outcomes in former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced view of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights the need to consider how institutions evolve over time in response to various factors, including indigenous economic and political action. Institutions are not static; they can change in response to incentives created by local conditions.

These perspectives underscore the importance of understanding the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic growth. The interplay between institutions and other factors, such as cultural beliefs and geographic endowments, adds layers of complexity to this relationship.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. The political institutions that specified and enforced these property rights were instrumental in creating a stable environment conducive to long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North's analysis highlights how the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament, which was critical for securing property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution involved establishing rules and creating mechanisms for their enforcement and modification, ensuring the regime's commitment to these rules. The success of this institutional framework is evident in the development of capital markets, where secure property rights fostered economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

These institutions have implications that go beyond immediate economic benefits. This credibility is essential for sustaining economic development over extended periods. For instance, the secure property rights established in seventeenth-century England provided a stable foundation for economic activity that persisted well into the future. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth highlights the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic performance.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. assert that European colonizers established institutions designed to extract resources from colonies with high mortality rates, which in turn hindered long-term economic development. Powerful groups of colonists crafted these institutions to maximize their rents while fostering growth. The extractive nature of these institutions means they did not encourage investment or enforce property rights effectively (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic is evident in colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where European powers set up states primarily for resource extraction, leading to persistent underdevelopment. Colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic growth. This disparity in institutional frameworks highlights how colonial policies shaped economic outcomes by determining which institutions were established and how they evolved over time.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These institutions, such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, resulted from power struggles among different social groups. In pre-industrial Europe, for instance, the persistence of guilds can be attributed to their role in protecting the interests of artisans against competition from other classes. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's argument that institutions are best understood as outcomes of conflicts over resource distribution (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The mechanisms through which these distributional conflicts shaped institutions involved the strategic actions of elites who sought to maintain their privileges, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman elaborate on this by showing how inequalities in New World societies were reinforced through government policies that restricted competition and favored elite interests (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies had lasting effects on institutional development, perpetuating inequalities over time.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge the oversimplification of historical causation in economic development by emphasizing that different periods and paths must be considered separately. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, illustrating how institutional prescriptions could shift based on indigenous dynamics. This mechanism demonstrates that institutions evolved in reaction to local conditions and the interests of various stakeholders. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this by noting that export production patterns varied significantly across countries due to factors such as transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation indicates that institutional frameworks were adapted to local contexts, influencing economic growth trajectories in nuanced ways. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution against overgeneralizing these patterns by emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of real wage divergence across different regions of colonial Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This qualification underscores that while institutional adaptations were crucial, their impact on economic growth was mediated by local economic conditions and historical specificities.

This focus on short-term poverty alleviation may neglect the deeper structural changes needed for sustained growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) together argue that targeted institutional reforms can significantly impact African economies by improving incentives for investment and enhancing public goods provision (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This approach highlights the importance of tailored policy interventions, such as those that address ethno-linguistic fragmentation and enhance the quality of education. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) point out that historical legacies, like the slave trade, have fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), which complicates the effectiveness of contemporary reforms. The persistence of this mistrust means that even well-designed institutional changes may encounter significant barriers to implementation and success.

The enduring impact of colonial rule on African institutions is another critical aspect of this literature. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British colonial policies were more conducive to developing formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). This historical context suggests that the legacies of colonial governance still influence contemporary institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. Yet, Hopkins's (2009, p.18) work on the new economic history of Africa indicates that while these legacies are important, they do not dictate current development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Instead, it is possible to leverage historical insights to inform modern policy reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people. This synthesis underscores the nuanced interplay between historical institutional legacies and contemporary economic development in Africa.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Simson 2019, p.2) do not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. They share the view that the role of women in economic growth is often overlooked, particularly during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system where European courts operated under common law while native courts codified customary laws; this process reinforced patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops. This institutional framework, which marginalized women's economic contributions, had significant implications for long-term economic development. Simson (2019, p.11) further qualifies that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive and that domestic military spending began to grow in the independence period and escalated towards the end of the 1960s and 1970s, which displaced both capital and recurrent spending. These dynamics underscore how institutional frameworks can shape economic priorities and resource allocation, even if they do not directly cause long-run growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) (Kuznets 1973, p.1) propose that economic growth is primarily driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological advancements. This perspective underscores that institutional frameworks, while important, are secondary to the role of technology in spurring long-term economic development. Kuznets elaborates on this by arguing that technological progress requires corresponding institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial, as it highlights how societies must adapt to harness the full potential of new technologies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement may not be sustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions play a critical role in sustaining it. Kuznets also points out that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to be possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, is crucial in resolving these conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society. This underscores how institutions facilitate the absorption of new technologies by managing societal tensions.

Broadberry and Gupta (Pamuk 2007, p.23) argue that merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe were key drivers of economic growth before 1800. These factors facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector, which was crucial for early modern economic development. Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) points out that institutional flexibility allowed regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities, further enhancing their economic development. This institutional adaptability contributed significantly to the divergence between northwestern Europe and other parts of the continent. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) highlights that the shift in economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea, such as England, led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader structural change driven by institutional factors. van Zanden also notes that this period did not show a clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This suggests that while institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, their impact on living standards was more nuanced.

The slave trades in Africa had an adverse effect on economic development by directly impacting institutional structures and economic activity (Nunn 2008, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, significantly hindered Africa's subsequent economic development. This duration is notably longer than the period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to around 1960. The slave trades disrupted local societies and institutions, preventing them from developing the necessary structures to support sustained trade and economic growth. Peters supports this view by highlighting that the reliance on foreign firms and intermediaries in Africa represents a significant departure from patrimonial dependence on decaying state institutions for political resources. This shift indicates that the slave trades depleted human capital and weakened local institutional frameworks, making it difficult for African societies to support trade with Europeans or develop their own economic systems. The adverse effects of the slave trades extended beyond demographic impacts; they fundamentally altered the institutional landscape, creating barriers to economic development that persisted long after the end of the slave trades. The impact on Africa's economic institutions was particularly pronounced in regions where centralized governments and well-developed markets were crucial for supporting trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context underscores the complex interplay between external forces like slave trades and internal institutional factors, shaping long-run economic growth trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to influence economic outcomes through various mechanisms. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These varied institutions significantly impacted current income per capita, demonstrating that institutional differences can persist and affect long-term economic trajectories. North argues that the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England and the development of secure property rights were critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges. This institutional framework was essential for the long-term capital market and overall economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic development is further illustrated by their role in shaping colonial economies. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that European settlers established extractive states in colonies with high mortality rates, which hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists while promoting long-run growth, highlighting the detrimental effects of extractive institutions on economic outcomes. Austin argues that colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production and adjusted institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This adaptability in institutional frameworks shows how institutions can evolve in response to local conditions, influencing long-term economic development.

The historical evidence from African regions affected by the slave trades further illustrates the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth. Nunn's work highlights that these external forces fundamentally altered the institutional landscape, creating barriers to economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The persistence of these barriers long after the end of the slave trades underscores the importance of historical institutional frameworks in shaping contemporary economic outcomes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is best understood through the lens of empirical evidence from former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in reversing relative incomes among these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development, illustrating how specific historical events shaped economic trajectories. Austin's work on African regions further supports this by showing that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions to local conditions, influencing long-term economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-term economic growth and are a product of the political and economic environment in which they operate.
