The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty across nations. Institutions are both rules and enforcement mechanisms; they shape incentives for investment and innovation, which are crucial for economic growth. Two positions stand out: one emphasises institutions as the primary determinant, while another stresses geographical factors or population dynamics as key influences on long-run economic outcomes. The disagreement hinges on whether institutional frameworks can be shaped by external forces such as colonial policies (North and Weingast 1989, p.29) or if they are more endogenous and influenced by internal socio-political structures.

The debate over institutions and growth is rooted in the understanding that different types of institutions, those that enforce property rights versus those that extract resources, have distinct impacts on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that "Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions" (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that the creation of secure property rights is critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. Both these perspectives highlight the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They contend that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks based on their mortality rates and settlement patterns. In areas with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted over time, shaping economic outcomes by discouraging investment and stifling growth. In regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans settled in large numbers, they established institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment, fostering long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) analysis of population density and prosperity further complicates this narrative by highlighting how European policies were influenced by local conditions. This interaction between institutional design and local factors underscores the multifaceted nature of colonial influence on economic growth.

The pivotal role of financial institutions in shaping economic growth is evident through the development of secure property rights and reduction of transaction costs. This financial revolution was critical to establishing a robust capital market, which in turn drove economic expansion. The creation of new financial instruments facilitated more efficient market exchanges, highlighting how institutional changes can significantly impact economic performance over time. For example, the English fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War led to a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues (North 1989, p.9). This political model underscores how institutional adaptations can address immediate crises while laying the groundwork for long-term growth.

North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs per exchange. As economies grow, increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic illustrates how institutional frameworks can adapt to support economic growth by facilitating more efficient market operations. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not always straightforward; they note that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly at parity with England, suggesting that institutional changes alone do not guarantee superior economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth, highlighting that while financial and political institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness is contingent on broader socio-economic factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) assert that the expansion of useful knowledge is the primary engine of economic growth. This technological progress requires institutional frameworks to facilitate its efficient use, but the relationship between technological change and institutions is reciprocal: technological advancements drive the need for institutional adjustments, while effective institutions promote further technological innovation. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that the pace and sectors affected by technological changes vary across different periods and regions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This variability implies a complex interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation. Mokyr (2005, p.49), meanwhile, acknowledges that without complementary institutional progress, economic improvement may not be sustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19) show that the slave trade has had a persistent negative effect on African economic development. The slave trade's role in shaping Africa's underdevelopment is not merely about extracting labor; it also involves an intergenerational legacy of mistrust, which continues to affect economic outcomes today. This mistrust encompasses both interpersonal relations and broader social institutions, including those related to economic activity. The authors find that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates on this point by noting that centralized governments and well-developed markets were crucial for facilitating trade in pre-colonial Africa. This implies that the disruption caused by the slave trade affected immediate economic activities and hindered the development of robust institutional frameworks necessary for sustained growth. The persistence of mistrust, therefore, acts as a significant barrier to effective institution-building, which is essential for long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that the historical trajectories of African economies have been shaped by complex interactions between institutional frameworks and economic activities. This perspective challenges simplistic narratives about Africa's economic development, highlighting that different historical periods and paths have unique characteristics that influence economic outcomes. For instance, colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, illustrating how institutions evolved based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay suggests that institutional frameworks in Africa were not static but responded to the evolving economic landscape.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. The role of institutions is crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores how historical institutional frameworks can shape contemporary economic policies and outcomes in Africa, highlighting the importance of understanding past trajectories to inform future reforms. By examining the nuanced interactions between institutions and economic activities over time, this approach offers valuable insights into the complexities of African economic history, suggesting that effective institution-building is key to long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Frankema 2012, p.15) identify that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a distinctive impact on the development of formal education. This influence varied across African regions based on local political structures and socio-economic factors. The demand for missionary education, for instance, arose from complex cost-benefit analyses made by both macro-level political leaders and micro-level families.

While British rule facilitated educational development in certain areas, it also created structural conditions that influenced long-term economic outcomes. Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights how the introduction of new governance structures impacted African families' decisions regarding formal education. This institutional change was pivotal in shaping both short-term and long-term socio-economic trajectories (Frankema 2012, p.15). The lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates for Africa complicates a comprehensive understanding of these dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) address this gap by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies from 1880 to 1965 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach reveals that while colonial education policies had specific impacts, they did not uniformly determine long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of poverty in Africa is thus a complex issue influenced by multiple factors beyond institutional frameworks alone, including the distribution of income and broader historical conditions.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that historical national accounting provides essential insights into African economic development. This is shared by Broadberry and Gupta's finding that high productivity in the traded goods sector may be seen as arising through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). These patterns of growth were tied tightly to foreign trade and export markets, which varied between countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation in economic performance across Africa was influenced by the market for exports. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, Broadberry and Gupta argue that living standards increased through the falling relative price of manufactured goods rather than through increased consumption of basic foodstuffs such as grain; this suggests that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly across different sectors. The relative price of manufactured goods fell more significantly than the wages of unskilled laborers, which suggests that this pattern of growth may have spread unevenly across different sectors.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern property rights and enforce contracts. Institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are integral to economic growth (North 1989, p.6). For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations during the English financial revolution significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby fostering a more secure environment for long-term capital markets and economic expansion (North 1989, p.9).

The impact of institutions on economic performance is evident in colonial contexts. In colonies where Europeans established extractive institutions, current income per capita remains lower than in areas with inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder long-term growth depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms.

The evolution of political institutions is crucial for fostering secure property rights. In seventeenth-century England, the development of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in creating a stable environment that supported economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This period saw the establishment of credible commitments by the regime to enforce property rights, which was instrumental in promoting market exchanges and economic development.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term development is further underscored by the interaction between institutional changes introduced during colonialism and the opportunity for industrialization. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies shows that these interactions played a central role in their long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic trajectories, with extractive institutions often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence from Africa shows that institutional reforms can significantly impact economic outcomes. The new economic history of Africa reveals that "good" institutions provide essential incentives for investment, which is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments; rather, the presence of progressive institutions and the rule of law are more critical factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The disagreement lies in whether institutional changes introduced by colonialism or the evolution of political institutions in specific historical contexts are more influential. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth were a significant factor in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the creation and enforcement of secure property rights during the English financial revolution, which demonstrates how institutional frameworks can foster economic development by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).
