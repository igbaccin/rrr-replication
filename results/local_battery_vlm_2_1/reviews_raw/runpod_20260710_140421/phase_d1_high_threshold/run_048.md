The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development studies. The intellectual stakes revolve around understanding how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder sustained economic prosperity over extended periods. A central live disagreement lies between those who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who argue that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic determinants, are more influential. This debate is crucial for policy-makers aiming to promote long-term economic development, as it shapes strategies for institutional reform and intervention.

Different streams of scholarship offer varying perspectives on this question. One stream emphasizes the direct causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting how specific institutional arrangements can either facilitate or impede long-run prosperity. Another stream posits that distributional conflicts within societies are key to understanding core economic institutions. A third stream suggests that credible commitment by political regimes is critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Each of these perspectives contributes unique insights into the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, offering a nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks shape long-run growth trajectories. The first stream's claim about institutions' direct impact on growth stands in tension with rival explanations that highlight other factors such as cultural beliefs or geographic determinants. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. posit that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes. In some cases, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In some cases, extractive states were established to swiftly move resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in colonial institutions had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutional differences shaped by colonial powers persistently affected current income per capita of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, is crucial for understanding the long-run economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. also observe that equilibrium institutions may not always promote economic development; they might be designed to maximize rents for European colonists and long-term growth is not guaranteed (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Thus, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes today, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. This development facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights over time, which is critical to both long-term capital markets and overall economic expansion. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflected increased security in the temporal dimension of property rights, fostering a more robust capital market. As these institutions develop, they lower per-transaction costs while increasing the overall volume of exchanges, which is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can shape economic trajectories by creating an environment conducive to market exchanges and capital accumulation. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) advise that the influence of institutions on economic growth is not always direct; other factors, such as the commitment of regimes to enforce rules, also have a significant role (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth; Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) agree that these factors work in tandem to drive sustained economic improvement. Institutions play a vital role in mediating conflicts arising from technological change, ensuring that these disruptions do not destabilize societal structures. Kuznets shows that without effective institutions, economic growth can be hindered by social unrest or political instability; he argues that the modern national state is essential for peacefully resolving such conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr adds that institutional progress complements technological advancements, preventing rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic gains and ensuring sustained improvement. He highlights that without these complementary institutions, economic growth could be limited or even reversed by coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Mokyr's emphasis on the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs further underscores the importance of institutional adjustments in facilitating technological progress. By creating and maintaining structures that lower barriers to knowledge sharing and innovation, societies can more effectively harness new technologies for economic growth. Mokyr also advises that this process is continuous and requires ongoing adaptation and improvement of institutions to keep pace with technological changes (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while technological progress is a driving force behind economic growth, the effectiveness of institutional adjustments determines how fully societies can capitalize on these advancements.

Austin and Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This approach overlooks the nuanced interactions between institutions, economic policies, and local responses during colonial rule. Austin highlights that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, which could lead to higher production equilibriums than anticipated by theories focusing solely on exogenous factors (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic adaptation suggests that the effectiveness of institutional changes is contingent upon local context and endogenous responses.

Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize the importance of understanding long-term trends in economic development, including income distribution. They note that a deeper grasp of these trends can reveal whether historical or geographical conditions have had temporary or structural effects on African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective is crucial for assessing future growth prospects, as it underscores the need to consider both institutional legacies and contemporary adaptive processes.

One implication of this nuanced view is that the path dependence of institutions may not be as rigid as some theories suggest. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies indicates that economic outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including local adaptations to colonial policies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This finding qualifies the notion of strict path dependence by showing that institutions can evolve in response to endogenous forces, even within the constraints of colonial rule.

The slave trade in Africa has been shown to have had profound and enduring effects on contemporary trust levels (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the extensive history of slave trading in Africa led to a deeply ingrained culture of mistrust, which persists today. This mistrust is internalized within individuals, shaping their attitudes and behaviors (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The impact of this historical legacy on economic development is significant; Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that the slave trade's influence on intergroup trust is substantial. This mistrust affects various dimensions of social and economic life, including the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The internalization of mistrust, however, is at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), underscoring the deep-seated nature of this cultural legacy. This finding qualifies the extent to which contemporary economic policies can address the underlying mistrust, suggesting that addressing the historical roots of mistrust may be crucial for long-term economic development.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues. This perspective is shared by authors who have examined the New World societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts led to institutional arrangements that reflected the interests of powerful groups along with efficiency considerations alone. For example, serfdom and guilds in Europe were efficient responses to economic conditions but also shaped by the power dynamics between landlords and peasants or between masters and apprentices. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) illustrate this mechanism by showing how New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic suggests that economic institutions are outcomes of rational efficiency-seeking behavior but are deeply embedded in the social and political structures that reflect power imbalances.

The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding long-run economic growth. For instance, if institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations, then policies aimed at fostering economic growth may need to address these underlying power dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) underscores the importance of incorporating institutional analysis into long-term research programs, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve can provide insights into sustained economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to structure incentives and constrain behavior. This perspective is evident in the work of North (1989, p.9) who argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) build on this idea, emphasizing that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks, which in turn significantly influenced current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illustrate how institutions shaped by colonial powers, particularly those designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight the importance of political institutions that ensure secure property rights and enforce rules governing economic exchange. They show that credible commitments underpin growth by fostering a stable environment for investment and market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The evolution of institutions is also influenced by distributional conflicts, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues. She contends that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped more by power dynamics than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view by noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating a complex interplay between power and institutional design (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had lasting effects on economic development. This is evident in the case of former colonies where extractive institutions persisted, significantly impacting current income per capita (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the variation of institutional frameworks across different colonization strategies and their measurable impact on long-run economic outcomes.
