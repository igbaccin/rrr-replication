The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has captivated scholars for decades. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods and why some nations prosper while others lag behind. One live disagreement concerns whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, such as geography or culture, or if they are endogenous to political and economic processes. Proponents of the latter view argue that institutions can be deliberately designed and modified to promote growth, whereas those who emphasize exogenous factors suggest that institutional change is more constrained by underlying conditions that are difficult to alter. Institutions are dynamic entities that influence and are influenced by economic development over time (Author Year p.N). This interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how different types of institutions, such as property rights and legal systems, can either foster or hinder economic progress.

The historical context in which institutions evolve is crucial to understanding their impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies. Settler colonies, such as the United States and Australia, developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas extractive states were established in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole (North 1989, p.9). This divergence in institutional development had lasting effects on current income per capita. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical perspective underscores how institutional changes can have profound and enduring effects on economic outcomes.

North and Weingast corroborate this by highlighting how these developments fostered secure property rights, thereby driving long-run economic growth. This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, was a critical juncture. This institutional change reflects a broader pattern where political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs per exchange. As specialization and division of labor increase, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6).

Nevertheless, North and Weingast caution against overstating the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes. They point out that recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that England and France were almost at parity in economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This implies that institutions including those governing public choice interacted with other factors to produce observed outcomes (Author Year p.N). The implication is that while institutional changes are crucial, their effects on economic growth are complex and contingent on broader historical and political contexts.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to economic divergence by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta support this view, suggesting that the Black Death triggered institutional flexibility in northwest Europe, enabling regions like Holland to rapidly adapt and commercialize. This institutional flexibility allowed new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to thrive under weak monopolies. Tuscany's economy stagnated as its political authorities strengthened existing monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The differential response to the Black Death highlights how regional institutions shaped economic trajectories in Europe. One downstream implication of this divergence is that it suggests a pathway to growth independent from institutional factors, such as the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity gains in northwest Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This pattern underscores the importance of understanding both structural changes and institutional flexibility when examining long-run economic growth.

Mokyr highlights that technological progress has been the primary driver of sustained economic growth over long periods. This view contrasts with institutional explanations, instead emphasizing how technological advancements have required societies to make significant adjustments in their institutions and ideologies for efficient use. The mechanism by which technology drives growth involves epochal innovations that spread across regions, necessitating adaptive changes in societal structures. Kuznets notes that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving conflicts arising from these technological shifts without excessive costs to the political fabric of society. This implies that while institutions may not be the primary driver, they play a crucial role in facilitating the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. The sovereign state, with its authority and community of feeling, is pivotal in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr further elaborates on this by suggesting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may not be sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment where technological progress can thrive, even if they are not the initial drivers of growth. The role of institutions is thus intertwined with technological change, highlighting a pathway for long-run economic growth that integrates both factors. Kuznets (1973, p.1) reports, “Nor is modern technology compatible with the rural mode of life, the large and extended family pattern, and veneration of undisturbed nature.”

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) together with Frankema and van Waijenburg (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge the narrative of persistent poverty in Africa by highlighting that many African economies had achieved per capita incomes above subsistence levels before 1950. This challenges the notion that structural impediments have consistently hindered economic growth, suggesting instead that historical economic performance was more dynamic than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that export production patterns were tightly linked to foreign trade and political institutions. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This highlights the role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes, aligning with broader discussions on how institutions can drive or hinder long-run economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to assess the persistence of poverty. Their findings suggest that pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates are crucial for understanding long-term economic development in Africa, indicating that historical data can provide valuable insights into contemporary economic conditions. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth encompasses the broader economic and political frameworks that influence export production and wage levels.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights for institutional reforms aimed at alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). By examining historical data, scholars can identify how institutions shaped long-run economic growth and inform current policy decisions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the development of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective aligns with Simson's (2019) analysis, which challenges the prevailing narrative of clientelism and rent-seeking in East Africa's post-independence budget policies. Simson argues that the growth in military spending and debt burden were responses to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This nuanced understanding of historical budgetary decisions highlights the importance of contextual factors in institutional development. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) adds a layer of complexity by emphasizing that British colonial rule was more conducive to developing formal education systems than other colonial powers, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions can have enduring effects on economic development (Frankema 2012, p.15). Therefore, historical analysis reveals that institutional reforms must consider both the contemporary context and the enduring impacts of past policies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that colonialism's institutional legacy has shaped long-run economic growth through the establishment of distinct institutions in colonies based on European mortality rates. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2001, p.27) show that extractive institutions were more likely to be imposed in areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, as these regions were often seen as less suitable for settlement and thus governed with the primary aim of resource extraction. This institutional framework, characterized by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities, hindered long-term development. These historical institutions persist, influencing contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The enduring impact of colonial institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where those with extractive institutions lagged behind those with more inclusive ones (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that this institutional legacy interacts with other factors such as population density and prosperity.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Akyeampong and Fofack (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), show that institutions shaped by colonial rule and gender dynamics significantly influenced economic development in New World societies. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the unequal distribution of resources and power in these societies was reinforced through institutional frameworks, which created conditions where elites could maintain their privileged status. These policies restricted competition, thereby perpetuating high levels of inequality. Akyeampong and Fofack further illustrate how gender dynamics played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa. In the Gold Coast, for example, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutionalized gender disparity was reinforced through customary law under colonial rule, which codified and entrenched patriarchal structures. Akyeampong and Fofack also highlight that the invention of custom during colonial times solidified women’s subordination in the legal framework (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20), underscoring how institutions shaped by colonial rule can have profound impacts on economic development by reinforcing existing inequalities. The enduring effects of these institutional frameworks are evident in the persistent disparities seen today, demonstrating the long-term influence of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

The enduring effects of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes are evident in the persistent disparities seen today (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These institutions, shaped by colonial rule, often reinforced existing inequalities and entrenched patriarchal structures. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as secure property rights foster economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), but Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The legacy of these colonial institutions persists in the form of entrenched inequalities and underdevelopment, demonstrating how historical institutional frameworks continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the fact that the development experience of former colonies was influenced significantly by the interaction between institutions and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8) by providing incentives for investment. The evidence shows that "good" institutions are crucial to providing these incentives, which are essential for development.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter significantly for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Disagreement remains over the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth as secure property rights foster economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the differing outcomes of extractive versus inclusive institutions in former European colonies, which shows how the interaction between institutions and industrialization played a central role in long-run development.
