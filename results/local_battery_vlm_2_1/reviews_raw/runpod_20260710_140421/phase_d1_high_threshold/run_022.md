The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that addresses how societies develop and sustain prosperity over extended periods. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. One position holds that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, create an environment conducive to investment and economic activity. Another view posits that institutions alone cannot explain long-run economic growth; rather, they interact with other factors in complex ways. A third perspective suggests that the specific historical context and path dependencies significantly influence how institutions affect economic outcomes. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for grasping the mechanisms behind sustained economic development. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing transaction costs and property rights (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North's work underscores the importance of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement. The creation of the Bank of England exemplifies this, as it led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs due to increased security of property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This mechanism is crucial because it enhances the efficiency of market exchanges and encourages investment by reducing uncertainty. The specific historical context and path dependencies significantly influence these dynamics. For instance, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies. This variation underscores how the initial conditions and historical context can shape the long-term economic trajectory of a region.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that seventeenth-century England's institutional evolution fostered secure property rights, which underpinned economic growth. This process was driven by the development of institutions governing public choice, as North and Weingast note that "a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules" (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The commitment to secure property rights created an environment conducive to investment and economic activity. This institutional framework ensured stability and facilitated market development, as seen in capital markets' performance during this period. The impact of institutions on inequality merits attention. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that in New World societies, particularly those with greater initial inequality, elites used government policies to maintain their advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional bias restricted competition and reinforced existing inequalities, illustrating how institutional frameworks can both foster growth and perpetuate disparities. Thus, the evolution of institutions governing public choice is pivotal for understanding long-run economic growth, but its effects are nuanced by the broader social and political context in which they operate.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Hopkins  (2009, p.18) argue that distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and Africa than efficiency does. In this view, institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communities were primarily shaped by power struggles among different social groups. This perspective suggests that the effectiveness of these institutions can be complicated by factors such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation, which can either enhance or hinder institutional performance depending on the context (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) builds on this by emphasizing the importance of good institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how distributional conflicts influence the development and effectiveness of economic institutions over time.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) examine how colonial rule influenced African economies by focusing on outcomes such as per capita income levels and educational development. They find that British rule was more conducive to the growth of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, which had a positive impact on economic performance. This is because demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities in the colonial economy.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate that patterns of economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa during the colonial era were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of growth varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These variations highlight how specific institutional arrangements under colonial rule could either facilitate or hinder economic development. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this by noting that the benefits of formal education were not uniformly distributed across different regions and social groups. This suggests that while formal education systems contributed to overall economic growth, their impact was moderated by underlying social and political dynamics.

This nuanced understanding of colonial impacts on African economies underscores the importance of considering both institutional frameworks and socio-economic conditions in assessing long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) together with Austin (2008, p.15) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the role of colonial policies and labor dynamics. This approach emphasizes that the impact of European rule on African economies varied based on local conditions and colonial strategies. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with their fiscal interests. This dynamic highlights how colonial administrations adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political actions. The focus on labor dynamics and colonial policies provides a nuanced understanding of African economic development that goes beyond institutional determinants alone.

The role of institutions cannot be entirely dismissed. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that while the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods, it still offers valuable insights into the long-term economic growth patterns. This perspective suggests that institutional frameworks, though not the sole determinant, played a significant role in shaping African economies during colonial rule. The interaction between colonial policies and local dynamics reveals that institutions were both influenced by and influential on economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further elaborate on this interplay by emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of real wage divergence across British Africa, which is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This approach underscores the importance of examining historical data to understand long-term welfare development in Africa. By reconstructing real wage levels and trends, it becomes possible to assess how specific historical conditions have impacted African economies over time.

This focus on historical data and labor dynamics provides a comprehensive view of African economic development that integrates both institutional frameworks and local socio-economic conditions.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that the accumulation of knowledge and technological progress are the mainsprings of economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' (1973) assertion that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which requires institutional adjustments for efficient use. These technological advancements necessitate significant shifts in societal structures, including changes to rural lifestyles and family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This process is characterized by epochal innovations, major breakthroughs in human knowledge, that spread widely and sustain long-term growth. However, Kuznets cautions that the integration of these technological changes into society can lead to conflicts that must be resolved peacefully for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is fundamental, its benefits are contingent on effective institutional mechanisms that manage societal transitions and resolve conflicts. The success of long-term economic development thus relies on both the generation of new knowledge and the ability of institutions to adapt to and integrate these advancements into society's fabric.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to manage societal transitions and integrate technological advancements effectively (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. illustrate how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic highlights that institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; he shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation underscores the importance of flexible institutions that can evolve with changing circumstances, which is crucial for sustained economic growth. North emphasizes the critical role of secure property rights in facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

North's work on transaction costs provides a framework for understanding how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. extend this by showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups and not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the nature of institutional design is pivotal in determining whether an economy will thrive or stagnate. Hopkins highlights that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Together these findings show that institutions matter profoundly in economic growth.

The precise historical evidence that settles this debate comes from the comparison of extractive versus inclusive institutions across different colonial settings and their long-term impacts on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15).
