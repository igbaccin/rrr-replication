The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is understanding whether the institutional framework of a society can systematically influence its economic trajectory over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around two main positions. One group asserts that institutions are pivotal in determining economic outcomes, emphasizing their ability to facilitate or hinder growth through mechanisms like property rights enforcement and secure contracting. One group asserts that institutions are just one factor among many, with geographical and cultural elements also playing significant roles (Author Year p.N). This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it influences whether efforts should focus on institutional reform or other areas. The tension between these views underscores the need to delve into how institutions interact with economic growth over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) both argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This institutional development facilitated a robust capital market and economic growth by enhancing the efficiency of property rights enforcement and market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by noting that as economies grow, political institutions specifying efficient property rights become increasingly important; these institutions reduce transaction costs per exchange while expanding the aggregate volume of transactions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) identify a critical political factor in economic growth: the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange. They assert that the success of England's capital markets was underpinned by political factors, including the degree to which the sovereign adhered to these rules. This commitment is crucial for fostering an environment where property rights are secure and economic exchanges are efficient.

These political processes were essential to establish rules and institutions conducive to long-term economic growth. This underscores the dynamic nature of institutional development and its role in shaping economic outcomes over time.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping key economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. They show that these institutions were not merely outcomes of efficiency considerations but rather reflected the power dynamics between different social groups. For instance, serfdom and guilds can be understood as mechanisms through which elites maintained their control over resources and labor, ensuring that their interests were protected. This perspective challenges the conventional view by highlighting how institutional arrangements were often designed to perpetuate existing inequalities rather than promote economic efficiency. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their effectiveness in maintaining the status quo, even if they hindered long-term economic growth. This focus on distributional conflicts does not fully capture the broader socio-political context in which these institutions operated. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborate that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset of colonial societies shaped the evolution of institutions. This suggests that while distributional conflicts were crucial, they were also influenced by initial conditions that set the stage for institutional development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely based on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement; this variation led to the establishment of different institutions, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had profound economic implications. Acemoglu et al. further argue that colonial institutions were shaped by the political and economic interests of European colonists, who often prioritized short-term extraction over long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions has been a critical factor in the economic trajectories of former colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence current income per capita, as these extractive systems hinder investment and growth. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes today.

The growth of useful knowledge has been identified as the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr emphasizes that technological progress and the dissemination of new ideas are central to sustained economic improvement. Kuznets extends this perspective by noting that technological progress involves institutional adjustments for efficient use in addition to innovation. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is crucial, as institutions facilitate the resolution of conflicts arising from growth-induced disruptions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). For instance, the modern national state plays a pivotal role in managing these conflicts peacefully, thereby ensuring that economic growth can proceed without long-term weakening of society's political fabric. Mokyr warns that while technological progress is generally irreversible, institutional progress requires continuous effort to sustain economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the necessity of ongoing institutional support to complement technological advancements, as the sustainability of economic growth depends on both factors working in tandem.

Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), challenging long-held views of Africa's economic stagnation. Broadberry and Gupta also demonstrate that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector was driven by merchant distribution and finance, highlighting a key difference between European and Asian economic growth before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This mechanism of productivity growth through service sector development underscores the importance of institutions like merchant guilds and financial networks in facilitating trade and investment. The role of these institutions becomes evident when considering how they enabled increased living standards for skilled laborers, who could afford a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This pattern is qualified by the uneven distribution of economic growth's benefits: some regions experienced significant productivity gains, while others remained relatively stagnant. Broadberry and Gardner show that these patterns were tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while institutional support for trade and finance was crucial, the distribution of economic benefits depended on a complex interplay of factors, including geography and politics.

Long-term economic changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 were marked by shifts in development patterns that did not consistently translate into improved living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden's analysis of wage data across European cities reveals a complex relationship between economic growth and living standards, with no clear correlation between urbanization rates and improvements in the standard of living. This pattern is further nuanced by Pamuk's examination of structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a significant wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450. The institutional flexibility in late medieval Holland allowed for rapid economic adaptation, enabling new towns and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This flexibility contrasts with the stagnation observed in Tuscany, where existing monopolies were strengthened, highlighting the role of institutional structures in shaping regional economic trajectories. Pamuk also notes that urbanization rates varied significantly across Europe, indicating that structural changes alone did not determine economic development. The varying responses to new economic opportunities underscore the importance of local conditions and institutional frameworks in influencing long-term economic outcomes.

Institutional reforms are proposed as a means to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share this posture. They argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform these reforms. According to Hopkins, the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective challenges conventional wisdom and underscores a key mechanism: institutional effectiveness does not hinge on ethnic homogeneity but rather on progressive institutions that uphold the rule of law. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) focus on real wage levels as a proxy for economic development, highlighting the need to understand long-term economic trends in colonial Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). They note that pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates are lacking, which hinders a comprehensive assessment of historical poverty and its structural impediments. This gap underscores how incomplete data can obscure the true extent of historical economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, emphasizing the role of specific historical or geographical conditions. This perspective implies that targeted institutional reforms can address contemporary poverty by learning from past economic trajectories. The shared finding that institutions play a crucial role in providing incentives for investment highlights how the new economic history of Africa can inform development policy (Hopkins 2009, p.8). By examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, scholars can better assess future growth possibilities and the impact of historical conditions on long-term welfare development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This enduring mistrust has significantly impacted trust levels and economic development by shaping internal norms of mistrust within individuals, which are at least twice as large an effect as external factors like the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. These dynamics highlight how historical events such as the slave trade can have lasting effects on social norms and economic policies, shaping contemporary African economies in profound ways.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is clear from the literature: institutions matter (North 1989, p.6). This is most evident when considering how institutions can be shaped by political incentives and historical events. European settlers, for example, often set up extractive states to maximize their own rents rather than promoting growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic was particularly pronounced in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions that persist today and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence shows that these institutional legacies continue to influence economic outcomes. For example, colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic actions in ways that shaped their institutional prescriptions, creating incentives that influenced both political and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Therefore, it is the historical context of institutional development, such as the establishment of extractive states or responses to indigenous actions, that ultimately determines long-run economic growth.
