The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, as it addresses how societies can foster sustained prosperity. At stake intellectually is understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes over extended periods. A live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that institutions evolve to maximize economic efficiency and growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Others contend that these institutions are more likely designed to serve the interests of politically powerful groups, even if this does not always promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The debate extends further into whether institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the development trajectories of former colonies. A crucial aspect of this discussion is how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes and their lasting impacts on economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutions' influence on long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs, which are essential for fostering a productive economy. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England dramatically reduced transaction costs by increasing the security of property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize how European colonists established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional frameworks significantly affected current income per capita and industrialization potential. North and Weingast highlight that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, and institutions that enforce these rights can lead to remarkable success in capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England is a testament to how institutional changes can foster long-term economic development by providing the necessary incentives for investment.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of long-term property rights. This institutional development facilitated a robust capital market and spurred overall economic growth. This institutional framework supported markets and provided incentives for investment. North and Weingast's work highlights that the success of these institutions is evident in capital market evidence, demonstrating a clear link between secure property rights and long-term economic development. Other factors can nuance this relationship. This suggests that while secure property rights are a significant factor in economic growth, they are not the sole determinant.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers implemented different strategies based on mortality rates, leading to diverse institutional frameworks in their colonies; in places like the United States and Australia, they established institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment, whereas in regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. This institutional legacy is evident in contemporary economic disparities; for instance, the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the interaction between colonial-era institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these extractive institutions were designed to consolidate power and resources for European colonists, a dynamic that continues to influence economic outcomes today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is driven primarily by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective posits that technological progress has been a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods, spreading to substantial parts of the world. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for efficient use of innovations; for example, Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that epochal innovations have required significant adaptations in societal structures and ideologies to facilitate economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that coordination failures can hinder technological progress unless complemented by institutional advancements. Such complementary institutions are essential for preventing rent-seeking behaviors that could erode the benefits of technological improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets's observations on the role of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts underscore this point, highlighting how institutional frameworks can either support or impede economic development by managing social tensions arising from technological change. This dynamic suggests that while institutions may not be the fundamental cause of growth, they play a critical role in sustaining and amplifying the benefits of technological progress, thereby shaping long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between useful knowledge and complementary institutions remains pivotal for understanding the mechanisms driving sustained economic growth over extended periods.

Distributional conflicts were central to shaping the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share a view that these conflicts played a pivotal role in determining institutional frameworks. They trace this pattern to how distributional conflicts influenced institutional structure, particularly affecting serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that these institutions were shaped by power dynamics between different social groups rather than purely by efficiency considerations. The persistence of inequality in these societies was reinforced through government policies that restricted competition and maintained elite advantages. This dynamic is particularly relevant when considering how institutions can either support or impede economic development by managing social tensions arising from technological change, as discussed previously. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further argue that the initial conditions of inequality in these societies had lasting effects on their institutional frameworks (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the legacy of distributional conflicts can have long-lasting implications for economic institutions, shaping their initial forms and evolution over time. The interplay between useful knowledge and complementary institutions remains pivotal for understanding the mechanisms driving sustained economic growth over extended periods.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Frankema 2012, p.15) focus on the role of women in African economic development and the impact of British colonial educational policies. They argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, particularly through their involvement in agriculture. Akyeampong and Fofack note that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production under customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor had significant implications for economic development, as women's agricultural contributions were crucial for sustaining local economies. This educational infrastructure laid the groundwork for future economic development by enhancing human capital. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The interplay between gender dynamics and institutional frameworks during colonial rule shaped long-term economic trajectories in Africa. While British educational policies fostered formal education, the entrenchment of patriarchal customs through legal systems constrained women's economic participation.

Institutional reforms in Africa have been proposed as a means to alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). They are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that "good" institutions matter because they are essential for delivering broad-based public goods and services, which in turn foster economic growth by incentivizing investment. The new economic history of Africa has highlighted the importance of targeted policy changes to address economic disparities (Hopkins 2009, p.18). These reforms must navigate complex dynamics such as patrimonial governance structures that treat government resources as private property (Simson 2019, p.11). The effectiveness of institutional reforms depends on their ability to transcend these patronage structures and deliver public goods equitably. This underscores the need for a nuanced approach to institutional reform in Africa, one that recognizes both its potential and the challenges it faces.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs, as North argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which highlight how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, significantly impacted current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evolution of institutions governing public choice further underscores their importance. North and Weingast show that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing exchange and how these rules are enforced and changed, with credible commitment being crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence from capital markets in England illustrates the transformative power of institutional changes. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Institutional reforms must address the complex dynamics of patrimonial governance structures that treat government resources as private property, a challenge highlighted in both colonial and post-colonial contexts. Ogilvie emphasizes the need to transform institutions from mere fashion accessories into indispensable instruments for economic historians, suggesting that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
