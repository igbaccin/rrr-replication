The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over time. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder sustained economic development. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more decisive role.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to vastly different institutional outcomes in colonies, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas and more inclusive ones elsewhere, significantly affecting current income per capita. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that understanding long-term growth requires a nuanced view of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over extended periods. This debate highlights the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth, where historical context and specific institutional developments are crucial. Institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth because they provide the framework within which economic activities occur, influencing everything from property rights to market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In their seminal work, Acemoglu et al. 2001 explain that mortality rates among Europeans were the primary driver of institutional variation across colonies; high mortality led to extractive institutions because settlers did not expect to stay long and thus had little incentive to invest in growth-promoting infrastructure (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For example, colonies like the Congo or Gold Coast had extractive states designed for rapid resource extraction. Lower mortality rates in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand enabled Europeans to establish institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al. 2001 show that these institutional frameworks were crucial because they directly affected economic outcomes; colonies with extractive institutions tended to have poorer long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. provide further support for this view, arguing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to colonial institutions. This finding highlights a critical mechanism: institutional design during colonial periods significantly impacted economic development trajectories because it shaped the incentives and opportunities available to local populations over generations. Acemoglu et al. 2001's findings are robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on executive power, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These results underscore that the economic legacies of colonialism are deeply rooted in institutional frameworks established during this period.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments were pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This financial revolution facilitated market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn reduced per-transaction costs while increasing the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets, where the commitment to rules by the regime or sovereign played a critical role (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Political institutions underpinning economic growth are about more than just the rules governing exchange; they also involve how these rules are enforced and changed.

Shared beliefs shape institutional structures but are not identical to them; the two can diverge, as shown by the persistence of inefficient institutions like guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Institutions may be inefficient even when they align with cultural values and beliefs, such as Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivating craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The mechanism through which ideologies drive economic change involves changes in societal rhetoric that permit experimentation and open discourse. This ideological shift enabled people to experiment freely and share their findings openly without fear of disapproval; this rhetorical environment was a necessary condition for the uptake of new techniques and institutions like electric motors or research universities (McCloskey 2015, p.3). Such shifts were pivotal, as evidenced by Pope Pius IX's condemnation in 1864 of progress, liberalism, and modern civilization. Despite the condemnation, these ideas had already transformed many economies, demonstrating that ideological changes can precede and drive economic change (McCloskey 2015, p.4).

Ideologies shape economic growth through their influence on societal rhetoric, which is a necessary condition for experimentation and innovation. This rhetorical environment fosters the development of new techniques and institutions by enabling open discourse and reducing fear of disapproval; it allows for "the rule of the betterers." However, this process may not be linear or immediate.

Mokyr and Kuznets share that the growth of useful knowledge is a fundamental driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This process requires institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use of innovations. Institutions must facilitate the diffusion of new technologies and support the development of human capital, as technological progress alone does not guarantee sustained growth. Kuznets identifies that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, require institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr notes that without these institutional changes to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited; rent-seeking could erode gains. The process is not linear or immediate: Kuznets shows that conflicts arising from technological change must be resolved without excessive costs to maintain political stability and allow for modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr's point about coordination failures suggests that institutional progress must address these issues to ensure the sustainability of economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Nunn and Wantchekon's research shows that the slave trade in Africa had significant adverse effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2); Nunn and Wantchekon show that these effects were not merely due to colonialism or recent events. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust was cultivated through intergroup dynamics: the historical exploitation created enduring social norms that continue to impact economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn argues that these institutional legacies are deeply rooted in Africa's history, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. The impact of these historical institutions is further complicated by the fact that trust is a critical factor in economic development. Recent studies have documented the importance of trust for various aspects of economic activity, including international trade and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn's research highlights that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external factors, indicating that while trust is important, the legacy of historical exploitation presents a unique challenge for African development. This points to the enduring nature of institutional legacies and their profound impact on long-run economic growth, even in the absence of direct colonial institutions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) along with Frankema (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This is evident in the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education under new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions created a demand for missionary education, which was tied to the development of export production and foreign trade. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that British colonial rule, despite its exploitative nature, did have some positive impacts on economic development in sub-Saharan Africa. These gains were not uniform across all regions; they were often tied to specific local endowments and transport costs. The legacy of British rule in fostering formal education and export production highlights the complex role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

The literature on institutional reforms and African poverty explores how historical and contemporary factors influence development (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This argument is particularly relevant in understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, where colonial legacies have left a lasting impact on institutional frameworks. Colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, which shaped the incentives for indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) builds on this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores this point by noting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by more than just ethno-linguistic fragmentation; it also depends on the effectiveness of institutions. Therefore, societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional, or heterogeneous and capable of upholding the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight suggests that institutional reforms must consider the specific contextual factors within African countries to be effective in eliminating poverty. This historical perspective is valuable for understanding how contemporary institutions can be shaped to foster economic growth and development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to create stable frameworks for investment and development. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly across different regions, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions, which enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, were set up in areas like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, whereas extractive states with rapid resource transfer to the metropole were established in colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast. This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and enforce them effectively, which is crucial for market exchanges and long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.6).

The historical evolution of institutions also plays a critical role in shaping economic outcomes. Austin highlights that colonial administrations sought fiscal security by encouraging their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo, which often led to changes in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective aligns with North's view that political institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, leading to falling transaction costs and economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The specific historical context of Africa further illustrates how colonial institutions have shaped long-run economic outcomes. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight suggests that contemporary institutions must be designed to foster economic growth and development, building on the lessons learned from historical institutional frameworks. Austin posits that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15).

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which are essential for market exchanges and long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin counters this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

The specific historical evidence and empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the variation in colonial strategies across different regions, which directly influenced current income per capita. This shows that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, which had long-lasting effects on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
