The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians alike. At stake intellectually are the fundamental drivers of economic development and the mechanisms through which societies achieve sustained prosperity. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary determinants of long-term economic growth or if they operate as intermediaries for other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements. Some scholars argue that institutions directly influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, enforcing property rights, and facilitating market exchanges. This perspective emphasizes the importance of credible commitments underpinning growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North 1989, p.6). Others contend that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, where factors like population density, resource endowments, and historical contingencies play equally crucial roles. The debate is further complicated by the idea that institutions can be both efficient and extractive, depending on who benefits from them and how they are designed (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Understanding these dynamics is essential for formulating effective policies aimed at fostering economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the emergence of financial institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs. The creation of these institutions led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This development was critical for fostering economic growth by enabling long-term capital markets and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the success of these financial innovations can be traced to political factors such as a regime's commitment to binding rules, which underpinned the development of efficient property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The emergence of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates that these institutional changes also led to an increasing proportion of transactions sector in GNP as specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic suggests that while financial institutions were instrumental in reducing transaction costs, the broader economic impact was mediated by the evolving political landscape.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a common view that technological advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is grounded in the idea that the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels innovation, which in turn drives productivity gains. Mokyr's framework highlights the role of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements, suggesting that institutions facilitate the efficient use and diffusion of new technologies. This dynamic is crucial because it addresses coordination failures and ensures that economic improvements are sustainable over the long term (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets emphasizes that these institutional adjustments must occur without excessive social conflicts or weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This means that while technological progress is essential, the institutional landscape must adapt in a way that minimizes disruptions and maintains social cohesion. The interaction between technology and institutions is multiplicative; one's effectiveness depends on the other's supportive role.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are central to understanding core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie's focus on pre-industrial Europe highlights how these conflicts shaped institutions like serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. These institutions were influenced by the power dynamics between different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this perspective to New World societies, showing that initial inequalities led to institutional frameworks that perpetuated these disparities through various government policies. The implications of these findings are significant: they suggest that the persistence of inequality is not just a consequence of inefficient institutions but is often actively maintained by those in power. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how distributional conflicts can lock societies into specific institutional trajectories, which may hinder long-term economic growth and development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The historical impact of the slave trade on African societies is profound. Nunn (2008, p.2) asserts that the four concurrent slave trades in Africa over nearly 500 years profoundly shaped the continent's subsequent economic development, with this period far outlasting the duration of colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extensive history of exploitation and forced migration fostered deep-seated mistrust among African populations. Nunn and Wantchekon elaborate that this mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms, making it at least twice as impactful (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The pervasive nature of mistrust, therefore, undermines social cohesion and collaborative efforts essential for economic development. Consequently, the legacy of the slave trade continues to hinder Africa's long-run economic growth by perpetuating a culture that inhibits trust and cooperation.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies Africa's economic development by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin shows that the thesis overlooks the varied colonial experiences in Africa, which were not uniformly detrimental to economic growth. Colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating incentives for local economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). Governor-General Clifford opted for higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. This highlights how colonial institutions could adapt to local conditions, fostering growth in specific contexts. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Englebert concludes that ethnic fragmentation does not significantly affect public goods provision by African governments; the quality of institutions is more critical than demographic factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, their impact on long-run economic growth was contingent on local adaptations and institutional reforms.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the notion of persistent African poverty by examining historical economic data and trends, arguing that poverty was influenced by specific historical factors. They emphasize the importance of understanding long-term economic development patterns in Africa, including income distribution, to assess future growth potential. This approach highlights how structural impediments are not permanent determinants of poverty. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further support this perspective by showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century. They identify export production as a key driver of economic growth in these regions, noting that its timing and scale varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, their impact on long-run economic growth was contingent on local adaptations and institutional reforms. This underscores the complex interplay between internal and external forces in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered the development of formal education through altered social and political institutions. This shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, which highlighted the advantages of formal education in the new institutional context. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this perspective by examining East Africa's post-independence budget policies, noting that while formal education expanded under colonial rule, significant shifts in public expenditure occurred subsequently (Simson 2019, p.11). These shifts included increased military spending and subsidies, which displaced both capital and recurrent spending. This pattern suggests that while colonial institutions may have initially promoted education, post-independence policies altered the trajectory of economic development by redirecting resources away from productive investments.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Bolt and van Zanden (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) argue that long-term structural changes influenced economic performance in Europe during the era of the Black Death. This period saw significant shifts in urbanisation rates and institutional flexibility, which were key indicators of productivity and economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.23) shows that by 1450, a wage gap had emerged between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent. The Black Death initiated these changes; it disrupted existing economic structures and created new opportunities for growth. The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland allowed for rapid adaptation to these new economic conditions, enabling them to flourish while other areas stagnated. This dynamic highlights how structural changes can drive economic performance over the long run. For example, Pamuk (2007, p.23) illustrates that the political authorities in Tuscany worked to strengthen existing monopolies, whereas Holland's weak monopolies fostered rural manufacturing and new towns. The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland was a critical factor in their ability to respond rapidly to economic changes. Bolt and van Zanden note that the margins of error in estimating national accounts increase as one goes further back in time (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). These errors can affect the accuracy of long-term economic growth measurements, suggesting that while structural changes are important, their impact on economic performance must be interpreted with caution. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex and multifaceted, influenced by both short-term disruptions and long-term structural adaptations.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9); (van Zanden 1999, p.1), focus on the early modern European economic development, highlighting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. They underscore the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity in the traded goods sector of northwestern Europe. This highlights how institutional changes, particularly those related to trade and finance, played a crucial role in shaping early modern economic growth. Van Zanden further elaborates on this by noting that the focus of economic development shifted towards countries bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift was marked by significant changes in relative price levels of basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader impact on living standards and economic structures. This implies that although institutional frameworks were pivotal in fostering economic growth, their impact on broader societal well-being was not straightforward.

This is shown by the impact of extractive institutions on resource allocation and management (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and the importance of secure property rights for facilitating economic activity (North 1989, p.9).
