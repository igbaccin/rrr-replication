The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. The stakes are intellectual: to understand the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity over centuries. At the heart of this debate lie two competing positions. One position argues that institutions are central to economic development: they create incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity gains, thereby fostering long-term growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These scholars propose a causal mechanism where secure property rights and effective enforcement enable markets to function efficiently, reducing transaction costs and encouraging economic activity (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North 1989, p.6). The rival position holds that institutions reflect underlying economic factors and are primary drivers of economic growth. This view suggests that institutions are endogenous: they emerge in response to deeper structural forces such as technological change or demographic shifts, and therefore do not independently shape long-run economic outcomes. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are exogenous determinants of growth or merely epiphenomena of other causal processes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The key mechanism they identify is the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they implemented extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This approach prioritized short-term resource extraction over long-term economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions is crucial because they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, creating a lasting impact on the economic trajectory of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.19) show that this institutional legacy is robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) caution that while institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal, they also interacted with other factors such as population density and prosperity, which influenced European policies and, consequently, the economic outcomes in these colonies.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.9) traces the creation of the Bank of England as a critical juncture that led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional change was driven by fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated a trade-off between certain rights and parliamentary revenues (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast elaborate on this dynamic by highlighting that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic growth. The political commitment to enforce rules governing economic exchange was crucial for this development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). However, North and Weingast caution that while institutional changes were significant, they did not solely determine economic outcomes; the extent of a regime's commitment to these rules also played a critical role.

North (1989, p.9) further argues that as economies grow, transaction costs per unit should decrease due to increasing specialization and division of labor. The development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is thus not just a static condition but an evolving process that adapts to the growing complexity of the economy.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in Africa fostered the development of formal education systems. This institutional shift drove demand for missionary education. The impact of British colonial rule on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa was not uniform across all colonial powers. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a positive economic outcome from these institutional changes. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further evidenced by the divergence in real wage levels across British Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This divergence suggests that specific historical and geographical conditions influenced long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Distributional conflicts are argued to be a more fundamental driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) maintains that "beliefs are not institutions" and argues against the game-theoretic view, which asserts that beliefs can constitute institutions. This focus on distributional conflicts reveals a mechanism where elites used government policies to restrict competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. As such, the persistence of inequality can be seen as an outcome of these institutional arrangements, which were designed to protect elite interests (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elucidate that the variation in factor endowments across different societies led to distinct levels of inequality from the outset. These initial conditions set the stage for institutional differences that would later impact economic development trajectories. The influence of distributional conflicts on institutions is thus evident in how elite interests shaped policies and structures, which in turn reinforced existing inequalities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern underscores the enduring role of distributional conflicts in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on institutional frameworks.

Van Zanden's work (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) on economic performance measurement highlights the importance of collaborative research and historical data analysis in understanding long-term economic growth. The skill premium, as van Zanden argues, is a critical predictor of long-term economic performance. This premium reflects the costs associated with human capital investment, including training periods during which apprentices do not earn income. Institutions that guarantee fair execution of apprentice contracts, such as guilds in Western Europe, played a significant role in this process (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills but were also criticized for monopolizing these skills and restricting entry into trades. The net effect of these institutions on economic performance is thus complex, balancing skill development against potential market distortions. This complexity extends to the broader implications of institutional frameworks on long-term economic growth, where the benefits of skill enhancement must be weighed against the costs of restrictive practices.

The collaborative nature of economic performance measurement underscores the need for ongoing scholarly engagement and data refinement. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) emphasize that Maddison's pioneering work in this area induced many scholars to engage with these themes, leading to a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth. The skill premium thus serves as both an indicator of past economic performance and a catalyst for future research, driving deeper understanding of institutional impacts on long-term growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share a common ground that historical factors influence economic outcomes in Africa, but they do not directly address the fundamental role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). They both agree that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping African economies. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that European settlers' presence and competition for labor influenced commodity production, leading to institutional changes by colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective highlights how historical legacies and institutional frameworks shape economic development trajectories. However, Austin's work underscores the complexity of these interactions, showing that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local economic and political actions. This adaptability suggests that institutions evolve in response to changing circumstances. This aligns with broader arguments about the role of credible commitments in sustaining long-term growth.

The slave trade has been argued to have fostered an entrenched culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect contemporary levels of trust and thereby influences long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) establish that the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust is primarily driven by internal norms of mistrust. This internalization of mistrust creates a persistent barrier to economic development, as trust is a critical component for fostering economic cooperation and investment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn supports this perspective by highlighting that the duration and scale of Africa's slave trades were substantial, spanning nearly 500 years. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted local economies and social institutions, leaving lasting impacts on African development trajectories. Nunn also notes that the presence of centralized governments and well-developed trading networks facilitated trade with Europeans in some regions such as the Kongo kingdom despite the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). The ability of these centralized states to adapt and engage in trade suggests that while the slave trades had adverse effects, local institutions played a role in mitigating some of these impacts.

Broadberry and Gupta, together with Pamuk, argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This divergence was driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. These institutional developments facilitated the spread of fruits from early modern development, which were not evenly distributed across all social classes. For instance, skilled labourers enjoyed larger surpluses over basic food needs compared to unskilled labourers (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Structural changes initiated by events such as the Black Death also influenced these differences in economic outcomes. Pamuk shows that this event led to long-term structural changes that resulted in a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This wage gap was further reinforced by institutional changes, including high levels of investment in agriculture (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15).

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. The mechanism through which this occurs involves the reduction of access costs to new ideas, which fosters innovation and economic improvement. This is achieved by the emergence of formal institutions that facilitate the dissemination of knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). For example, the establishment of scholarly societies in Europe during the 17th and 18th centuries played a crucial role in reducing access costs to new ideas, thereby accelerating technological progress. Mokyr highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements would be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while institutions are crucial for sustaining economic growth, their primary function is to support and facilitate technological advancements.

Institutions facilitate economic growth by establishing stable property rights that support investment and market exchanges. The role of institutions is to reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework fosters specialization and division of labor, increasing the volume of transactions and thereby promoting economic development. Austin shows that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates: extractive states where rapid resource transfer was prioritized or settler societies with rule-of-law enforcement (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights how institutional choices made by colonial powers shaped long-term economic trajectories.

Institutional quality and economic outcomes are closely linked, as evidenced by the divergence in income per capita among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote growth, were established where mortality rates were high (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, leading to different institutional frameworks in colonies with varying levels of development potential (Austin 2008, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that the economic performance of former colonies is strongly correlated with the nature of institutions introduced during colonial rule.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the experiences of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding power dynamics in institutional formation. Hopkins highlights the significance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.18).

A shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter for economic growth because they provide a stable framework for market exchanges and secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). The precise mechanisms by which institutions shape long-run economic growth remain contentious. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts were more critical than efficiency in shaping pre-industrial European institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), while Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The measurement of income per capita among former colonies shows that the nature of institutions introduced during colonial rule significantly impacted long-term economic trajectories, demonstrating that colonial policies and institutional frameworks had enduring effects on economic development.
