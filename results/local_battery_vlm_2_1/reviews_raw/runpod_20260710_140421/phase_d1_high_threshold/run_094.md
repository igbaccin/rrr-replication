The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with debates centering on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if they operate alongside other factors. Some argue that institutions matter fundamentally because they shape incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. Others posit that institutional quality is merely one factor among many, including geography and culture, which collectively influence long-run growth. A third group suggests that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. The stakes of this debate are high: understanding the causal mechanisms behind economic growth can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering development in both developed and developing nations. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are autonomous forces or merely tools used by other underlying determinants.

This review begins with the claim that institutions matter for long-run economic growth because they determine how resources are allocated and incentives are structured within an economy. North argues that economic growth is driven by institutions that reduce transaction costs, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and enable specialization (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s assertion that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current income levels. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth by failing to encourage investment and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Institutions that enforce property rights and rule of law are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary security for long-term investments. These findings underscore the critical role institutions play in shaping economic outcomes over extended periods.

Some scholars argue that institutions influence long-run economic growth in complex ways. Ogilvie posits that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are key determinants of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This view challenges the notion that efficient institutions naturally emerge to promote growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced understanding is needed for how institutions interact with other factors over time. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, highlighting that institutions may be influenced by a broader set of historical and contextual factors (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth must therefore be understood within a dynamic framework where various factors interact over time.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth. This process enabled markets to flourish through increased specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. The emergence of these financial institutions was a response to broader fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). They also caution that other factors, such as bureaucratic structures, can influence economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast's work underscores that while financial institutions played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, their effects must be considered within the broader context of political and historical factors.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial rule was determined by European mortality rates and shaped long-run economic outcomes. In colonies with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Conversely, in areas where Europeans settled and established property rights, like the United States and New Zealand, economic growth was fostered (Acemoglu et al., 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27); Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33 (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)). The persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended highlights their enduring impact on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. also note that the interaction between these institutions and opportunities for industrialization played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.6).

The institutional framework established during colonial rule was about economic exploitation as well as consolidating political power. The legacy of these institutions continues to influence economic performance today, underscoring the profound impact of historical institutional changes on contemporary development trajectories.

Distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions, providing an alternative to efficiency-based explanations for core economic structures like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts over power and wealth distribution drove the institutional framework of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective underscores how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that prioritize the maintenance of existing power structures over economic efficiency.

In New World societies, this dynamic is evident in the way institutions were shaped by inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that societies with greater initial inequality developed institutions that reinforced elite control through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These policies restricted competition and limited opportunities for upward mobility, thereby perpetuating economic disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, rooted in distributional conflicts, maintained the status quo and hindered long-term economic growth by stifling innovation and investment. The legacy of these institutions continues to influence contemporary development trajectories, illustrating how historical distributional conflicts can have enduring impacts on economic performance.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that Africa's slave trades have had profound adverse effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). They propose a mechanism where the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and hinders economic growth. This mistrust is rooted in the historical trauma of enslavement, where communities were forcibly separated and exploited, leading to deep-seated suspicions that impede social cooperation (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of these institutions continues to influence contemporary development trajectories, illustrating how historical distributional conflicts can have enduring impacts on economic performance. Nunn identifies the duration of slave trades as a critical factor, noting that they spanned nearly 500 years, significantly longer than the period of colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extended period allowed for deep institutional changes in African societies, which were not undone by subsequent historical events. The economic impact of these enduring institutions is evident in contemporary Africa's underdevelopment, where mistrust and lack of social cohesion persist as significant barriers to growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that economic growth is primarily driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that technological progress serves as the mainspring of long-run economic development. Mokyr highlights the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, facilitating its dissemination and application across societies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that modern technology necessitates significant institutional adjustments, including shifts in societal values and political structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These adaptations are crucial for integrating technological innovations into existing economic systems without incurring excessive social or political costs.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional change is evident in the historical record. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that epochal innovations, such as those during the Industrial Revolution, required not only technical advancements but also profound changes in societal norms and governance structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr's analysis of scholarly societies underscores how institutions can lower barriers to knowledge sharing, thereby accelerating economic growth. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by pointing out that such institutional progress must be sustainable and irreversible to avoid being undermined by rent-seeking behaviors (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The long-term sustainability of economic growth hinges on the ability of societies to manage the conflicts arising from technological change. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that resolving these conflicts peacefully is essential for maintaining political stability, which in turn supports sustained economic development. This perspective aligns with Mokyr's view that institutional progress complements technological advancements, ensuring that the benefits of new knowledge are fully realized. As a result, societies that can effectively integrate technological innovations into their institutional frameworks are more likely to achieve enduring economic growth.

The impact of colonial rule on African education and poverty reveals that British colonial policies were particularly influential in shaping formal educational development (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows how the establishment of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African communities. This institutional framework encouraged African political leaders to prioritize missionary education, thereby fostering a more robust educational infrastructure than in other colonial contexts. The demand for such education was driven by cost-benefit calculations that shifted with the introduction of these new institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This analysis underscores that while structural impediments to growth were present, they did not uniformly impede long-term welfare improvements (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The persistence of poverty is more complex than a simple lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates suggests; it requires understanding how specific historical conditions influenced economic trajectories.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge conventional narratives about African economic performance by revisiting historical data and policy assessments, offering nuanced perspectives on the region's economic trajectory. This perspective is shared with Simson 2019(Simson 2019, p.2), who argues that East Africa’s post-independence budget policies were not exclusively clientelist or unproductive. These factors shaped budgetary decisions and economic outcomes in ways that complicate simplistic narratives of clientelism and rent-seeking. Simson 2019(Simson 2019, p.11) further illustrates this point by detailing how Kenya's subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. This shift underscores the complex interplay between economic policy and regional dynamics, which Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also acknowledge through their analysis of how export production growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The nuanced understanding of African economic performance requires acknowledging these multifaceted influences on budgetary policies and economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting the complexity of African development paths. They argue that colonial administrations and indigenous commodity production played significant roles in shaping these trajectories. The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic encouraged colonial administrations to adopt institutional prescriptions that responded to local economic and political incentives, fostering a higher production equilibrium than would have been achieved otherwise.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms by highlighting how 'good' institutions provide crucial incentives for investment. This variation underscores the need for nuanced institutional reforms tailored to specific historical and local contexts. By understanding these complexities, African nations can better harness their own development paths, informed by both colonial legacies and indigenous economic activities.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for investment and economic activity. Credible commitment underpins growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Institutions that secure property rights facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.9). The historical context of institutional formation is crucial; the English financial revolution, for instance, led to a significant decline in transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9).

The adoption and effectiveness of institutions vary across different contexts. The path to economic growth is not uniform due to differences in colonization strategies and the resulting institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.2). European settlers established extractive states in some colonies while setting up institutions that enforced the rule of law in others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation highlights the need for nuanced institutional reforms tailored to specific historical and local contexts.

A key point of agreement among scholars is that institutions matter significantly for economic growth. The role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is well-documented, as "good" institutions are crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. While some argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in certain colonies, others contend that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement involves comparing the economic outcomes of former European colonies with differing institutional frameworks. This comparison shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists were a significant factor in the development trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
