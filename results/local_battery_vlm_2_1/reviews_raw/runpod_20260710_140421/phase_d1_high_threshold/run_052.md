The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and political scientists. At stake is whether institutions are the primary driver of economic development or if they merely play a supporting role to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancement. Institutions are defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal constraints (laws, regulations) and informal constraints (norms, conventions). The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth; that is, whether they are shaped by economic factors or shape economic outcomes. Some argue that institutions are a product of economic development, while others contend that they are the fundamental cause of economic prosperity or stagnation.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder development as they do not promote long-term growth. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who argue that secure property rights fostered by credible commitments are crucial for economic growth. Institutions influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, reducing uncertainty, and facilitating market exchanges. For example, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th century England led to a decline in transaction costs, which was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This highlights how institutions can create an environment conducive to economic activity by providing stability and predictability.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is bidirectional. Institutions themselves are shaped by historical, political, and cultural factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied widely due to differences in mortality rates, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others they set up extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received more beneficial institutions experiencing higher income per capita today.

The importance of institutions is further underscored by their impact on trust and social cohesion. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and affects economic development. Trust is crucial for economic development as it facilitates cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and enhances the efficiency of market exchanges (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Institutions that foster trust can therefore have a profound impact on long-run economic growth by creating an environment where individuals are more likely to engage in productive activities.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between formal and informal constraints. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to align with existing norms and conventions. In some cases, informal institutions may be more important than formal ones in shaping economic outcomes. For example, societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This underscores how the interplay between formal and informal constraints shapes institutional effectiveness and, consequently, long-run economic growth.

The evidence from various studies indicates that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. They influence economic outcomes by providing stability, reducing uncertainty, facilitating market exchanges, and fostering trust. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, shaped by historical, political, and cultural factors. Understanding this dynamic is essential for designing effective policies that promote long-term economic development.

The type of institutions established during colonization were pivotal to the resulting economic outcomes of these regions. Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and population densities, which led to either extractive or inclusive institutions. This institutional framework persisted over time, significantly affecting the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions is not merely a consequence of geographic factors but is deeply rooted in the historical policies and practices of colonial powers.

The impact of these colonial institutions on long-run economic growth can be seen through their influence on industrialization and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely linked to institutional changes introduced during colonialism, highlighting how extractive institutions hindered industrialization and economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic underscores the enduring legacy of colonial policies on economic development, where initial institutional frameworks set the stage for future economic performance. The extractive nature of these institutions often led to a focus on short-term gains rather than long-term growth, perpetuating cycles of underdevelopment in many former colonies. One downstream implication is that post-colonial economic policies must address these historical institutional legacies to foster sustainable development. This requires immediate policy interventions as well as long-term strategies aimed at building inclusive institutions that support broad-based economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) assert that the development of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs. These institutions facilitated economic growth by providing secure property rights and fostering long-term capital markets. This institutional shift was particularly significant as it addressed the fiscal crises that had plagued the country during earlier conflicts like the Hundred Years' War, thereby enhancing economic stability.

The evolution of public choice institutions in England further underscored the importance of secure property rights. This institutional framework governed how these rules were enforced and ensured that they could be adapted over time, fostering an environment conducive to market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of this institutional setup is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, which reflected broader economic growth and stability.

North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by noting that as economies grow, the volume of transactions increases due to greater specialization and division of labor. The development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges is thus a key driver of long-term growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution against overstating the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes. They suggest that while the political factors underpinning economic growth were crucial, the bureaucratic structures inherited from previous regimes were not necessarily hindrances to development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic outcomes.

The creation of financial institutions in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering an environment where long-term capital markets could flourish.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. Mokyr's focus on the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth aligns with Kuznets' emphasis on technological progress as a key driver of economic development. Both authors highlight that the integration of new technologies requires institutional and ideological adjustments to ensure efficient use of innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Institutions play a role in facilitating these adjustments, but their primary function is to support the adoption and diffusion of technological advancements rather than being the direct drivers of growth. Mokyr further illustrates that the formal institutions meant to reduce access costs were crucial for sustaining economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets underscores that modern technology demands significant institutional changes, such as the emergence of the sovereign state, which plays a pivotal role in resolving conflicts arising from technological progress without undermining societal stability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr also acknowledges that while institutional progress complements technological advancement, it is not always linear or irreversible.

Frankema argues that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). This institutional framework was shaped by the demand for missionary education and the political calculations of African leaders. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that, despite the common narrative of widespread impoverishment, some sub-Saharan economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that the institutional changes introduced during British rule may have contributed to economic improvements in these areas. However, Simson (2019, p.2) notes that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not always aligned with productive investment but instead focused on consumption and military spending (Simson 2019, p.11). This implies that while colonial institutions might have laid some groundwork for economic development, the sustainability of this growth depended on post-colonial policy choices.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa has had lasting effects on African societies, particularly through the creation of a culture of mistrust. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experiences of enslavement. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates that well-developed markets and trading networks, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, were crucial for sustaining trade with Europeans. This suggests that pre-colonial institutions and economic structures played a significant role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory before colonial rule began. The slave trade disrupted these institutions, creating lasting barriers to economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) propose that the new economic history of Africa can guide institutional reforms to alleviate poverty among the continent's bottom billion people. They identify a specific mechanism: improving investment incentives through good institutions. Institutions are crucial in shaping economic growth because they influence how resources are allocated and utilized within societies.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) examine real wage levels and trends across British African colonies to understand the long-term picture of economic development. They find that poverty in Africa is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments; rather, it varies over time and space. This insight suggests that targeted institutional reforms can address specific historical or geographical conditions that have temporarily affected African welfare development.

The new economic history of Africa highlights how understanding historical patterns in economic development can inform contemporary policy-making, offering a pathway towards reducing poverty through strategic institutional changes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic interactions and incentivize investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions depending on mortality rates, which significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This insight aligns with North's argument that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth. North highlights the English financial revolution as a key example where institutional changes reduced transaction costs and fostered market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002) work, which shows that colonial policies introduced extractive institutions in many former colonies. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This finding is supported by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in economic development is also evident in Africa, where historical patterns have informed contemporary policy-making. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that understanding these patterns can guide institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon's work, which shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today. This historical legacy accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting how past institutional failures can have enduring economic consequences (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions are fundamental to long-run economic growth because they establish the rules governing economic interactions and provide incentives for investment. There remains a disagreement over whether the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies is primarily due to colonial policies or endogenous factors within these societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This debate is settled by Nunn and Wantchekon's empirical comparison of intergroup trust and external environments across different regions in Africa, which shows that internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channel.
