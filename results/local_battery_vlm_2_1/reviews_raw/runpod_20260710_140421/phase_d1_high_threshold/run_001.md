The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has sparked significant debate among scholars. At stake intellectually is the question of whether institutional frameworks are fundamental drivers of sustained economic development or merely ancillary factors. One position argues that institutions play a pivotal role by establishing rules and enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges and secure property rights, thereby fostering growth (North 1989, p.6; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Another perspective posits that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent on broader historical and cultural contexts, which can either amplify or mitigate their effects. The disagreement hinges on the extent to which institutions are autonomous determinants of economic growth or if they must interact with other factors such as geography, culture, and historical legacies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This interplay underscores the complexity of understanding how institutions influence long-run economic outcomes.

The evolution of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, which were crucial for long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that these factors had significant implications for economic development. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a turning point by dramatically reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating secure property rights over time (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution involved both economic exchange and political commitment to enforce rules governing these exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the success of these institutions hinged on the regime's commitment to upholding property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The Bank of England's role was pivotal as it provided a stable financial framework that supported long-term capital markets. North (1989, p.9) notes that while financial institutions were critical, their impact varied across different historical contexts. This highlights that while the evolution of financial institutions is crucial for long-run economic growth, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader historical and political context.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) central claim is that colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by European policies and varied widely based on settlement patterns and mortality rates among Europeans. Colonies with high mortality, such as those in Africa, were more likely to develop extractive institutions designed for rapid resource extraction. Low-mortality colonies like the United States saw inclusive institutions established that protected property rights and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional design had profound implications for economic development. As a result, former colonies with extractive institutions have struggled with lower income per capita and slower growth. The relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) work underscores that while institutional legacies are powerful, they interact with other factors such as population density and industrialization opportunities. This interaction can either reinforce or mitigate the impact of initial institutional conditions on long-term development trajectories.

Mokyr posits that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). The growth of knowledge and technology is the main driver of economic development, as Kuznets also argued in his seminal work on technological progress and economic growth. Kuznets notes that "epochal innovations" are pivotal for sustained growth over long periods; these innovations transform economies by introducing new technologies and production methods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge involves both technological breakthroughs and institutional progress that facilitates efficient use of this knowledge. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial for sustained economic improvement; without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by inefficiencies or rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets highlights that resolving growth-induced conflicts within society is essential for modern economic growth to be possible. The sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. This underscores the importance of institutional stability and effectiveness in harnessing technological progress for long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The historical impacts of the slave trade on Africa's economic development highlight that the adverse effects of slavery are a significant rival explanation to institutional factors in understanding long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trades in Africa, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had profound and lasting effects on the continent's economic trajectory. The disruption caused by the slave trade extended beyond immediate economic losses; it also fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust that persists to this day. The resulting lack of trust has significant implications for economic development, as it hinders cooperation and undermines the effectiveness of institutions.

Broadberry and Gardner highlight that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This challenges previous characterizations that colonial rule uniformly impeded economic development. Broadberry and Gardner's findings are supported by Frankema, who argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African families and leaders. This educational advancement had significant implications for economic growth, as it fostered a skilled workforce capable of driving technological progress and institutional reforms. Broadberry and Gardner note that these patterns of growth were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by examining colonial rule's institutional legacies in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). He argues that colonial institutions shaped African economic trajectories in complex ways. Austin shows that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production and adapted their policies based on local incentives. This dynamic was influenced by both the fiscal needs of colonial administrations and the economic actions of indigenous populations (Austin 2008, p.15). They reconstruct real wage levels across British African colonies to assess the impact of colonial rule on welfare development. Their findings suggest that the effects of colonial institutions were neither uniform nor irreversible, indicating that African economic trajectories were shaped by a combination of institutional legacies and local adaptations. The implications of these studies underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions interacted with local conditions to shape long-term economic outcomes in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their influence on property rights and economic development. North argues that the creation of secure property rights is critical for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, with some colonies benefiting from institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment while others were subject to extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The divergence in economic trajectories between these colonies underscores the profound impact of institutional legacies on long-term economic outcomes. The historical evidence from colonial rule demonstrates that institutions shaped by European settlers had lasting effects on the economic development of their former colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of institutional frameworks on growth.

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing the complexity of historical paths and the need for a nuanced understanding of how different periods interact to shape economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is complemented by North's argument that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The interaction between colonial institutions and local conditions further complicates the picture. Austin shows that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial governments' interests, leading to institutional adaptations that responded to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding how institutions interact with local conditions to shape long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by their impact on trust and social norms. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from the slave trade demonstrates that institutional legacies can have profound and lasting effects on social norms and economic development. The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether the impact of colonial institutions is uniform or varies based on local conditions. Austin shows that Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This specific historical evidence from colonial rule resolves the disagreement by demonstrating that institutional legacies interact with local conditions in complex ways, shaping long-term economic outcomes through their impact on trust and social norms.
