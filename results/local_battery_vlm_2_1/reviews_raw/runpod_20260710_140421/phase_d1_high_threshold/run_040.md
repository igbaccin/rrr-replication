The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake intellectually is an understanding of how political and social structures can either facilitate or hinder sustained prosperity over generations. One live disagreement centres around the causality between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes: do institutions drive growth, or are they merely epiphenomena of deeper underlying forces? A second debate concerns the nature of these institutions themselves: are they primarily shaped by exogenous factors like geography, or do endogenous political dynamics play a more significant role? The answers to these questions have profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of historical economic trajectories. While some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutional quality in determining economic performance, others point to the complex interplay between institutions and other factors such as culture and colonial legacies. This interplay suggests that institutions operate within a web of historical, political, and social forces (Author 2015).

The literature on institutions and growth often highlights how different types of institutions can either promote or hinder economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective is complemented by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they shape transaction costs and property rights, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and enhance economic growth. North's work underscores that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for a growing economy, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) indicates that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth by shaping the environment in which economic activities occur.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). The development of new financial instruments during this period, such as the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs. North and Weingast highlight that these institutional changes were driven by political factors, particularly the commitment of the regime to enforce property rights effectively (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This enforcement mechanism is crucial as it ensures that economic rules are established and reliably upheld. The implications of this institutional framework reach beyond rule enforcement. This suggests a broader impact on economic growth through the reduction of transaction costs and the expansion of market activities, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12); McCloskey agrees (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. For instance, Ogilvie attributes the persistence of serfdom and guilds to power struggles between different social groups. McCloskey supports this view by highlighting that ideologies, not just interests or institutions, significantly influenced economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This suggests that the ideological landscape of a society can either facilitate or hinder institutional changes aimed at promoting economic growth. Ogilvie emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts; McCloskey points out that broader cultural shifts were also crucial. For example, the Protestant Reformation reduced the power of guilds and altered societal beliefs about work and innovation. This ideological shift fostered an environment more conducive to economic experimentation and growth. Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing these cultural changes, noting that institutions themselves can be both a cause and effect of broader societal ideologies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The interplay between distributional conflicts and ideological shifts thus reveals a complex dynamic where institutions are shaped by and in turn shape the economic landscape.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping local governance structures and resource allocation mechanisms (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). According to Acemoglu et al., European settlers established different colonial institutions depending on mortality rates. In regions with low mortality, they created inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. This institutional divergence persisted over time, with extractive institutions perpetuating inequality and underdevelopment in many former colonies.

Acemoglu et al. further highlight that these equilibrium institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional design reflected the interests of politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining control over resources and labor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of such extractive institutions can be attributed to the reluctance of colonial powers to initiate changes that would not directly benefit them, despite potentially fostering economic development in the colonies.

The legacy of these colonial institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that this reversal was driven by industrialization opportunities and institutional interactions during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth varies across former colonies. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions influenced development vary depending on local conditions and historical contexts. In some cases, the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers has been resilient, continuing to shape economic outcomes even after independence.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality have restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. They highlight how different factor endowments across colonies led to varied degrees of inequality from the outset, influencing institutional development. The initial abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor resulted in high average living standards, but also extreme wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality was compounded by institutions that favored elites, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades exacerbated these inequalities, with adverse effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The presence of centralized governments and developed trading networks in some regions facilitated trade and potentially mitigated some institutional barriers to growth. The overall impact of inequality and restrictive institutions persisted, shaping long-run economic trajectories in these societies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that institutional change in African colonies was influenced by both endogenous initiatives and exogenous factors. The introduction of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to shifting economic opportunities and governance structures. This institutional shift was crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. These variations highlight that while institutional change was pivotal, its impact on economic development was mediated by other contextual factors, including local resources and infrastructure. This interplay underscores the complexity of colonial legacies in shaping African economies, where both endogenous initiatives and exogenous influences played significant roles.

Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust in Africa that endures to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This longstanding mistrust, which originates from both internal norms and external environmental factors such as weak institutions, is responsible for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust within African societies. Austin argues that colonial administrations often prioritized fiscal security over fostering robust institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15). As a result, even when indigenous economic activities were encouraged, they did not necessarily lead to sustained growth due to the absence of supportive institutions. This dynamic illustrates how historical mistrust and contemporary governance failures intertwine to create deeply embedded institutional weaknesses that hinder long-term economic development in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to extractive states in high-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth, illustrate how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin highlights that colonial administrations often prioritized fiscal security over robust institutional frameworks. This focus on short-term gains at the expense of long-term development shows how historical institutional choices have lasting effects on economic trajectories(Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's emphasis on secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). He argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights are crucial for reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that extractive institutions impede long-term growth by failing to enforce secure property rights and contract enforcement mechanisms(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development. He argues that different historical periods and paths must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced view is supported by Ogilvie, who emphasizes the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds(Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Both authors show that institutions are shaped by political and social dynamics.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Acemoglu et al.'s institutional framework lies in the measurement of property rights enforcement and contract mechanisms. Hopkins shows that good institutions, defined as those providing incentives for investment through secure property rights and effective governance, are crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This evidence supports North's argument that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges(North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6), thus settling the disagreement by providing a clear empirical basis for understanding the role of institutions in long-run economic growth.
