The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding how societal structures and rules influence the trajectory of economies over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions determines economic outcomes by influencing investment, innovation, and productivity. Others contend that institutions are shaped by deeper historical and cultural forces, which in turn drive economic performance. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies globally.

Institutions matter fundamentally because they shape the incentives and constraints within which individuals and firms operate (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Institutions encompass formal rules such as laws and regulations, as well as informal norms and conventions that guide behavior. North argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This historical perspective is complemented by Acemoglu et al. who show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with significant long-term effects on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These findings suggest that the nature of institutions introduced during colonial periods has enduring impacts on economic development. The persistence of these institutions highlights how initial conditions and historical events can shape long-run economic outcomes through institutional pathways.

In colonies where Europeans settled, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were established; extractive states were set up in regions with high mortality rates to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These differing institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, underscoring the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. along with other scholars (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence resulted from deliberate institutional frameworks established by Europeans based on their mortality rates and settlement patterns. These extractive institutions were not merely a byproduct of geographical factors but were actively designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than fostering long-run growth. This deliberate institutional design had profound and lasting effects on economic outcomes in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions, even after the colonial period ended, highlights their enduring impact on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings suggest that the initial conditions set by colonial powers, particularly the type of institutions they established, were crucial in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9), along with North (1989, p.9), argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This transformation was driven by a shift in political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms. These changes reflect an increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this shift; Parliament traded certain rights in return for revenues to address these crises (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of these institutional changes can be seen in the development of economic institutions that facilitated market exchanges, further reducing transaction costs. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution against overemphasizing the revolutionary nature of these changes by noting that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England’s (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This comparison suggests that while institutional changes were significant, other factors also played a role in economic development. The commitment of the regime to the rules governing property rights and their enforcement is crucial for understanding how these institutions fostered growth. This credible commitment underpins the long-term stability necessary for sustained economic expansion (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Therefore, the evolution of secure property rights in England highlights the broader implications of institutional design on economic outcomes, setting a precedent for future studies on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality tended to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The extreme differences in factor endowments across these societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to societies characterized by extreme wealth, human capital, and political power inequalities from their inception (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that Africa's slave trades had a lasting impact on economic development. This pattern of inequality in New World societies was reinforced through the evolution of institutions that favored elites, limiting opportunities for broader economic participation and growth. The persistence of these institutional arrangements over time highlights how initial conditions can shape long-term economic trajectories (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn qualifies this by noting that centralized governments with well-developed markets and trading networks were better equipped to support trade with Europeans; suggesting that a strong central authority could mitigate some of the negative effects of inequality (Nunn 2008, p.2). This underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes, even within the context of highly unequal societies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African economies were more economically developed by the early twentieth century than previously thought. They show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence during this period (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This challenges previous characterizations of persistent poverty in Africa, highlighting periods of growth tied to foreign trade and export markets. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that the timing and scale of economic growth were influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), suggesting a complex interplay between natural resources and institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further emphasize this point by noting that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This implies that the economic performance of African economies was more dynamic than often portrayed, with growth patterns influenced by various factors including trade and institutional settings. Broadberry and Gardner also acknowledge that these growth periods were often tied to external markets, indicating a dependency on foreign demand which could limit sustained internal development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This underscores the importance of understanding both the opportunities and constraints presented by trade dependencies in shaping long-term economic outcomes in Africa.

Frankema shows that British colonial rule was more favorable to formal education development in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.15). The establishment of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African communities. This shift influenced both macro-level decisions by African political leaders and micro-level choices made by families, ultimately fostering a more conducive environment for educational advancement (Frankema 2012, p.1; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The legacy of this institutional framework is evident in the higher levels of trust within societies that experienced British rule, as Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate. They argue that the slave trade, which was prevalent under other colonial powers, contributed to a culture of mistrust, an internal norm that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal norm of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel of mistrust, such as the rule of law and trustworthiness of others. The impact of British rule on education and trust highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-term economic outcomes by influencing both educational development and social norms.

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women made significant contributions to economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The marginalization of women in land access for cash crops under colonial rule had a complex impact on the economy. Women were often relegated to less productive lands, which affected their ability to contribute to commercial agriculture. This pattern was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified during colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack discuss how women's contributions were crucial for economic development despite these challenges. For instance, women played a pivotal role in the cocoa revolution in the Gold Coast (present-day Ghana), where farmers migrated to secure land for cocoa cultivation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This migration was instrumental in making the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa by 1911. The new economic history of Africa underscores that good institutions, including those supporting women’s contributions, are essential for providing incentives for investment and development.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr share this posture (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights technology as a pivotal driver of long-run economic growth. They propose that technological progress creates new possibilities for productivity gains, which in turn stimulate economic expansion. Institutions play a role by facilitating the efficient use of innovations and resolving conflicts arising from technological changes. Kuznets argues that modern technology requires institutional adjustments to mitigate conflicts between traditional ways of life and new economic realities (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are crucial because they enable societies to harness the full potential of technological advancements without incurring excessive costs or weakening their political fabric. Mokyr further elaborates on this by emphasizing that coordination failures can hinder economic growth if not addressed through institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions are necessary for sustaining it by ensuring that new knowledge is effectively utilized and disseminated.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency does (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie and Austin both identify how economic institutions can be shaped by the distribution of power among different groups. This dynamic is evident in the way pre-industrial European institutions evolved to manage conflicts over resources and wealth, rather than simply optimizing efficiency. For instance, guilds and serfdom were mechanisms for managing social and political tensions as well as efficient responses to economic needs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating how institutions can be shaped by the distributional conflicts they aim to resolve. Furthermore, Austin notes that colonial governments often encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent, illustrating how institutional changes can be driven by the need to maintain fiscal security and manage conflicts over resources.

These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), illustrating how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic development. This is further supported by North's argument that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. argue that this reversal was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
