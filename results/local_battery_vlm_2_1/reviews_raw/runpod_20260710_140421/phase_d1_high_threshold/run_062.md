The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, as it directly challenges neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants of growth. Proponents of the institutional approach argue that institutions, such as secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, are crucial for fostering investment, innovation, and overall economic development. There is no consensus on how these institutions emerge or whether they are primary drivers of economic growth. Some scholars contend that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and values, while others point to distributional conflicts as a more compelling explanation for institutional design. The debate hinges on whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by the political and social dynamics of power distribution.

Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North's assertion that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). However, this view is challenged by Austin who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique highlights the need to consider a broader range of factors, including indigenous economic and political action, in understanding institutional development (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The debate underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics is essential for comprehending long-run economic performance.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established extractive institutions in high-mortality regions to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, while in low-mortality areas they created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional divergence had profound effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, leading to persistent economic disparities among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings highlight the enduring impact of colonial policies on institutional development and economic performance in postcolonial societies. The interplay between European settlement patterns and institutional design shaped the trajectory of economic growth in these regions over centuries.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This institutional change occurred as Parliament secured control over taxation to raise revenue during the Hundred Years' War, which led to a "trade" of certain rights in exchange for revenues. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments facilitated market exchanges with falling transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These changes resulted from broader political transformations that ensured credible commitment to property rights enforcement in addition to economic shifts. North and Weingast's argument is supported by evidence from capital markets, which shows that these institutional changes significantly impacted economic performance. This pattern of institutional evolution underscores the importance of credible commitment in sustaining economic development over centuries. This does not imply that other factors are irrelevant; it shows how political institutions can shape economic outcomes by providing a stable framework for property rights and market exchanges. This institutional development is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth, as it highlights how secure property rights can drive economic performance through enhanced market efficiency and capital accumulation.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shape long-term economic outcomes in Africa, emphasizing that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further elaborate on this by reconstructing real wage levels and trends across British African colonies to assess the extent of poverty and its causes. Their work reveals that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that specific historical or geographical conditions may have cast a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This implies that institutional reforms grounded in historical context can mitigate the impact of these conditions on contemporary economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) together with Broadberry and Gupta (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) argue that inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power was a key driver of long-term economic divergence. They focus on how initial factor endowments shaped the development of institutions that either facilitated or hindered growth. Sokoloff and Engerman note that the extreme inequality present from the outset in many New World societies led to institutional arrangements that favored elites at the expense of broader competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This skewed distribution of power and resources restricted economic dynamism by limiting opportunities for entrepreneurship and innovation. The persistence of inequality in the New World, as Sokoloff and Engerman show, was reinforced by policies that protected elite interests. These policies included restrictions on land ownership, barriers to education for the lower classes, and limited access to credit markets, all of which stifled economic mobility and innovation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Broadberry and Gupta qualify this perspective by noting that differences in geographical conditions also played a role in shaping these institutional outcomes (Broadberry & Gupta 2006, p.14). For example, they argue that the warm climate in southern India reduced the need for certain goods, influencing agricultural productivity and wage levels (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that while inequality was a significant factor, it interacted with other environmental and institutional variables to produce divergent economic trajectories. In sum, the interplay between initial conditions of inequality and institutional development was crucial in shaping long-run economic growth paths across different regions.

Austin's critique of institutional explanations for economic growth highlights the oversimplification of causation in these frameworks (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These scholars argue that institutional approaches often compress different historical periods and paths into a single narrative. Austin shows how this compression obscures the complexity of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over time. For example, Austin notes that colonial governments' responses to indigenous economic actions could lead to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie builds on this critique by pointing out that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency alone. She argues that the persistence of serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be attributed to power dynamics rather than purely economic factors, such as a shared belief system (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that institutional explanations must account for the political and social contexts in which they operate. However, Ogilvie also acknowledges the need for institutions to be more deeply integrated into long-term research programs. She argues that institutions should become an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), highlighting the importance of a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics. This perspective underscores the necessity of moving beyond simplistic causal narratives and incorporating a broader range of factors into analyses of long-run economic growth.

The slave trade is argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the legacy of mistrust generated by centuries of African slave trades has enduring effects, contributing to Africa's underdevelopment. They argue that this mistrust is rooted in both external factors such as the rule of law and internal norms of behaviour. Their estimates indicate that the internal channel of mistrust, shaped by historical experiences, is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores the deep-seated nature of the problem, suggesting that overcoming it will require addressing both structural issues and deeply ingrained cultural attitudes. Nunn highlights the prolonged impact of Africa's slave trades compared to colonial rule. He notes that the period of African slave trade lasted nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900 whereas official colonial rule spanned approximately 75 years from 1885 to around 1960 (Nunn 2008, p.2). This temporal contrast underscores the enduring legacy of the slave trade on African development. However, Nunn (2008, p.2) also notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while the slave trade had adverse effects, the presence of strong institutions could mitigate some of these impacts.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and effectiveness of resource allocation, investment, and productivity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks across colonies, ranging from extractive states that prioritized rapid resource transfer to institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North (1989, p.9) argues that economic growth is fundamentally linked to the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6).

The historical context shows that the presence of strong institutions can mitigate adverse impacts. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive but are shaped by politically powerful groups that maximize their rents rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Austin's (2008) argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the importance of understanding the specific institutional contexts of each period. For instance, colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions is further underscored by the fact that they can significantly influence long-term development trajectories through economic policy and governance. Hopkins (2009, p.18) shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter significantly in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions are often extractive and designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-term growth, highlighting the importance of institutional design (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that good institutions provide crucial incentives for investment, essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the variation in colonial strategies and their long-term economic impacts. For example, the presence of strong institutions in some colonies mitigated adverse effects from extractive policies, as shown by Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) analysis of different colonization strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
