The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure social interactions, drive economic performance over extended periods. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes like political dynamics, and how these institutions subsequently influence growth. One position argues that institutions designed to extract resources can persist and significantly affect current income levels, while another posits that the evolution of secure property rights through institutional changes is crucial for fostering economic development. A third perspective posits that distributional conflicts are key drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, with efficiency concerns also playing a role. These differing views highlight the complexity of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth and underscore the need to delve into specific mechanisms by which institutions operate.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth, emphasizing the political factors governing secure property rights. They focus on how institutional changes in seventeenth-century England fostered a regime committed to enforcing rules, thereby facilitating market development and economic growth. This perspective is complemented by North's broader argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North highlights that the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and supporting long-term capital markets essential for economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This criticism targets approaches that attribute economic outcomes primarily to institutional factors without considering the complex interplay of historical contexts. Austin's view aligns with North and Weingast's emphasis on the importance of credible commitment in fostering growth, as it suggests that institutional prescriptions can evolve in response to indigenous economic and political action (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with historical contexts to shape long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This dynamic is evident in the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this institutional evolution; the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues laid the groundwork for subsequent economic developments. The development of capital markets in England provides compelling evidence of this institutional impact; recent research has shown that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with Britain. The elaboration of this theme is further qualified by the recognition that institutional changes are not inherently revolutionary; rather, they are part of a broader historical process that includes bureaucratic structures and political commitments.

Colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies by introducing enduring institutional frameworks that either fostered or hindered development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate this through the divergence in colonization strategies across different regions. They find that European settlers established extractive states in colonies where mortality rates were high, while setting up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in places with lower mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence was driven by the Europeans' strategic interests: in high-mortality areas, they aimed to extract resources rapidly, whereas in low-mortality regions, they sought long-term settlement and development. These initial institutional choices had lasting effects on economic outcomes. This suggests that the extractive nature of colonial institutions was a direct response to the political and economic interests of the colonizers, which ultimately shaped the economic trajectories of the colonies.

Acemoglu et al. show that these institutional differences persisted over time and significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions implies that initial colonial policies had lasting impacts on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. note that population density and prosperity in the colonies also played a role in shaping European policies, influencing whether extractive or inclusive institutions were established (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interplay between institutional design and local conditions underscores the complexity of colonial impact on long-run economic growth. The legacy of these institutions continues to affect economic outcomes in former colonies today, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks.

Ogilvie and Pamuk both argue that distributional conflicts shaped pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Ogilvie focuses on how these conflicts drove the development of key institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. These institutions were shaped by power struggles and resource distribution within society, not just efficiency considerations. Pamuk elaborates on this by showing that institutional flexibility in regions like Holland allowed for rapid economic adaptation post-Black Death, while regions with stronger monopolies, such as Tuscany, lagged behind (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This highlights how institutional rigidity or flexibility, shaped by underlying distributional conflicts, influenced regional economic trajectories. Pamuk's analysis of urbanization rates further underscores this point, demonstrating that regions with higher urbanization and productivity were those where institutions allowed for greater adaptability and less monopolistic control (Pamuk 2007, p.16).

Moreover, the impact of these institutions extended beyond immediate economic outcomes to long-term structural changes. This dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional design, and economic outcomes underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets explains that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns. He shows that the interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustained economic growth over long periods. Mokyr illustrates how institutional progress complements technological advancements, suggesting that without such coordination failures, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress is underscored by Kuznets's assertion that modern economic growth requires resolving conflicts induced by growth without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further argues that rent-seeking behavior can erode economic improvements if not mitigated by institutional advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay highlights how institutions shape long-run economic growth through their role in managing technological progress and societal conflicts.

The slave trade has been identified as a significant factor in shaping Africa's economic underdevelopment through its lasting impact on social trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists to this day. This mistrust reflects both the untrustworthiness of others and internal norms of mistrust within individuals. The historical context is important here: for nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, Africa experienced four simultaneous slave trades. This period significantly outlasts the era of official colonial rule, which spanned approximately 75 years from 1885 to 1960 (Nunn 2008, p.2). The economic institutions that evolved during this time were not conducive to supporting large-scale trade, as evidenced by Nunn's historical analysis. The Kongo kingdom is a notable exception; its centralized government and well-developed markets allowed it to support trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context underscores the deep-seated impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic trajectory, suggesting that the legacy of mistrust continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This view suggests that the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex than previously acknowledged. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience are deeply rooted in each country's unique trajectory, stretching back to its original industrialization. This perspective underscores that the adoption of growth-favorable institutions varies across countries due to distinct historical contexts and political dynamics (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman support this by showing how factor endowments influenced institutional development in New World societies, leading to varied degrees of inequality. The specific focus on how environmental differences shaped societal structures reveals that initial conditions significantly impact the trajectory of economic growth. Austin's analysis further illustrates this complexity through colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic and political actions, which often led to shifts in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay indicates that institutions evolve in response to internal and external pressures, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

Institutions are fundamental in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing rules and incentives that govern behavior and resource allocation. North's seminal work underscores the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The dynamic nature of institutions is evident in how they evolve in response to internal and external pressures. Austin illustrates this through the adaptive responses of colonial administrations to indigenous economic actions, which often led to shifts in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is crucial as it demonstrates that institutions can change over time, reflecting societies' evolving needs and contexts.

The role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth is another key aspect highlighted by North and Weingast. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in ensuring secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which show that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often hindered long-run growth by not fostering private property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The complexity of institutional impacts is further illustrated by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This view challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations, highlighting instead the role of power dynamics and political struggles in shaping institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's analysis complements this by showing how colonial administrations responded to indigenous actions, illustrating the interplay between power and institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15).
