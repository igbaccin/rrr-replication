The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development and historical studies. The stakes lie in understanding whether the quality of institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic progress over extended periods. This debate has produced two rival positions: one that prioritizes institutional changes as a key driver of economic growth, and another that underscores other factors such as population density and prosperity (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal rules, informal norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape behavior within societies. The first position posits that institutions matter because they affect economic outcomes by shaping incentives for investment, productivity growth, and technological innovation. This perspective underscores how secure property rights and effective governance can foster an environment conducive to long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.6). The rival position argues that institutions are important but other factors such as population density play a more significant role in determining long-run economic growth. This perspective highlights the importance of demographic factors and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This disagreement is crucial for understanding how societies can achieve sustained economic development, as it provides insights into whether institutional reforms should be prioritized over other policy interventions.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established in colonies was determined by European mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions focused on rapid resource transfer. These institutions persisted and had a lasting impact on economic development. For instance, colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand saw the establishment of rule-of-law-enforcing institutions that encouraged investment, while others, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subject to extractive states designed for rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and geographic factors alone do not account for this (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The persistence of extractive institutions was influenced by local political dynamics as well as European policies. Acemoglu et al. note that politically powerful groups might resist institutional changes if they feared losing their political power or did not stand to benefit directly from economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic highlights how the persistence of extractive institutions is shaped by both colonial policies and local political structures, which can hinder long-run economic growth. Therefore, understanding the role of institutions in shaping economic growth requires considering both the initial conditions set by colonial powers and the endogenous dynamics within colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that financial institutions were pivotal in fostering long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. This institutional development was driven by political factors that ensured credible commitment to property rights enforcement.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) qualify the impact of financial institutions by emphasizing that their success hinges on political factors. Specifically, the degree to which regimes are committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange is crucial. This commitment ensures a stable environment where property rights are secure, thereby facilitating economic growth. These findings underscore the importance of political stability and institutional credibility in shaping economic outcomes.

North observes that the development of institutions specifying efficient property rights should lead to falling costs per transaction, despite an increasing volume of exchanges due to specialization and division of labor. This dynamic reflects a growing proportion of transactions relative to GNP, as market exchanges become more prevalent (North 1989, p.6). The evolution of political institutions that support secure property rights thus fosters economic growth by reducing transaction costs and promoting efficient markets.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight how the accumulation of knowledge is a fundamental driver of economic progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements are the fundamental drivers. The generation and diffusion of new ideas and technologies fuel productivity gains and structural transformations in economies. A key mechanism through which this occurs is the reduction of access costs to knowledge through the development of formal institutions like scholarly societies (Mokyr 2005, p.49). These institutions facilitate the dissemination of useful knowledge, thereby accelerating technological progress.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this point by highlighting that economic growth requires resolving conflicts arising from technological changes through effective political institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Institutions play a crucial role in managing the societal disruptions caused by rapid technological change, ensuring that economic growth is sustainable and does not lead to long-term weakening of the political fabric. Without such institutional support, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, they must be accompanied by robust institutions that can manage the social and political challenges they present. Kuznets (1973, p.1) asserts that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts.

Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.2) and Ogilvie's study (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) provide detailed analyses of how specific institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe influenced economic outcomes. The shared posture of this literature is that it examines particular historical contexts to elucidate how institutions influenced economic growth, rather than focusing on the universal role of institutions across time and space. Austin's analysis of colonial economic policies highlights one mechanism by which institutions can affect growth: fiscal incentives shape institutional design, which in turn influences production equilibrium. This dynamic is evident in Austin’s case study of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium despite Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective suggests that institutional structures were shaped by underlying social and political dynamics as well as being efficient responses to economic conditions. This approach does not directly address whether these specific institutional arrangements can be generalized to explain long-run economic growth universally. Rather, it offers nuanced explanations for historical phenomena that might otherwise seem anomalous within broader theories of institutional economics. The implication is that while institutions are crucial, their effects on growth may vary widely depending on the specific historical and social context in which they operate.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) illustrate that colonial rule imposed new policies and altered local economic dynamics through negotiation and imposition. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights how colonial institutions influenced African development by affecting the incentives for investment, which are essential for development. This influence was not uniform; it varied depending on the specific colonial power and its approach to governance. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that British rule was more conducive to developing formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This differential impact suggests that the quality of institutions imposed by colonial rulers had significant implications for long-run economic outcomes, as evidenced by the varying levels of per capita income across Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production during this period were influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial policies and local conditions, shaping the institutional landscape and economic trajectories of African nations.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), Nunn (2008, p.2), and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) explore how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequality, highlighting the nuanced role of institutions in economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman posit that New World societies with greater initial inequality developed institutions that favored elites. This dynamic restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, which in turn shaped long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional bias towards elites was deeply influenced by political power structures as well as being a product of economic factors. This concentration of power led to policies that limited opportunities for broader economic participation, thereby perpetuating inequality.

The impact of these institutions on long-term development is further illustrated by Nunn's analysis of Africa’s slave trades. These trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had a profound adverse effect on subsequent economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). The centralized governments and well-developed markets in regions like the Kongo kingdom facilitated trade with Europeans, highlighting how institutional structures could either support or hinder economic growth. The broader implications of these institutions extend beyond immediate economic outcomes; they also shaped social dynamics that perpetuated inequality. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack note that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified the subordinate status of women through customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional bias affected economic participation and reinforced broader social inequalities. The de-privileging of women in access to land for cash crops under colonial rule exemplifies how institutions can perpetuate inequality by limiting opportunities for certain groups, thereby influencing long-term economic trajectories (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These institutional frameworks, shaped by historical contexts and power dynamics, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities across different groups within society. The nature of these institutions determines whether they facilitate or hinder economic development. Acemoglu et al argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law-enforcing institutions, long-term growth was encouraged (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up with the primary goal of rapidly transferring resources back to the metropole. This difference in institutional frameworks had a lasting impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin also demonstrates that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptability suggests that institutions can change based on local dynamics and incentives (Austin 2008, p.15).

North’s work emphasizes the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which is essential for economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). North argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6).

Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-based explanations for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Instead, she posits that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie advocates for incorporating institutions into long-term research programs to understand their impact on economic outcomes fully (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Acemoglu et al. further demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and geographic factors do not account for this (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). They emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which can hinder long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Hopkins highlights the importance of good institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. He argues that poor institutions are linked to Africa's dismal economic record and suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to influence resource distribution and opportunities. All sources agree that credible commitment underpins growth, but they disagree on whether institutions are shaped primarily by efficiency or distributional conflicts. The historical evidence in Acemoglu et al shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Ogilvie’s work demonstrates that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, challenging efficiency-based explanations. The empirical comparison of colonial strategies in Acemoglu et al and the institutional adaptations documented by Austin settles this disagreement, showing that institutions evolve based on local dynamics and incentives rather than being solely driven by efficiency considerations (Austin 2008, p.15).
