The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in development economics. The stakes revolve around understanding whether and how institutional frameworks can drive sustained economic progress over extended periods. This question has sparked intense debate between scholars who posit that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who argue for alternative factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits. At the heart of this disagreement lies the issue of causality: do institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic outcomes, or are they merely epiphenomena of deeper underlying forces? The debate hinges on whether institutions can be harnessed to promote growth or if other variables must be prioritized.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes that institutional frameworks set by colonial powers have had enduring effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the policies pursued by European colonizers, influenced by population density and prosperity, played a central role in shaping the long-run development of these regions. This perspective is echoed by North's work, which underscores the significance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth through secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). Both streams argue that the institutional legacies left behind by colonial powers have had profound impacts on contemporary economic outcomes.

The importance of credible commitment underpins this argument. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) contend that political factors, particularly the degree to which a regime is committed to enforcing rules, are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective echoes Acemoglu et al.'s claim that extractive institutions, crafted to maximize rents for European colonists and not long-run growth, have endured in many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding is that the nature of colonial institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, has significantly influenced economic development paths over centuries.

North (1989, p.9) argues that specific institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time. This financial revolution, driven by political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.9) specifically highlights how the Bank of England's establishment and subsequent financial innovations were pivotal in reducing transaction costs.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate on this institutional framework, emphasizing the role of credible commitments by regimes to enforce rules governing public choice. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact can be contextual and dependent on other factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective suggests that the accumulation of knowledge and technological progress create a foundation for sustained economic growth, as new ideas and innovations drive productivity gains and structural transformations. This shared finding is exemplified by the role of technological progress in reducing access costs and fostering coordination. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also observes that without institutional progress alongside technological advancements, economic improvements may lack sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this point, suggesting that modern economic growth is possible only if conflicts induced by technological changes are resolved peacefully. This implies that while technology and knowledge drive growth, institutions play a crucial role in managing the social and political challenges that arise from these advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) contend that colonial institutions influenced long-term economic outcomes by creating extractive states focused on resource extraction and not sustainable development. This contrasts with colonies where settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. The persistence of these institutions has had profound effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, often leading to stagnation or underdevelopment. This implies that while extractive institutions were detrimental, the potential for industrialization could mitigate some of their negative impacts on economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) highlight how historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories. They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power, which characterized many New World societies from their inception, was a direct result of European colonial policies. These policies often favored elites, creating institutions that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that centralized governments and well-developed markets facilitated trade in regions like the Kongo kingdom. This suggests that where institutions were more inclusive and supported broader economic participation, they could mitigate some of the negative impacts of colonial policies on long-run development. Where such institutions were absent, the persistence of inequality became a significant barrier to economic growth as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) observe. This underscores that the legacy of colonial institutions, shaped by historical contexts, continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes in profound ways.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) together with Frankema (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15), argue that British colonial rule in Africa fostered formal education more robustly than other colonial powers. This educational development was tied to broader economic trends; export production, influenced by factors such as transport costs and political institutions, played a crucial role in shaping growth patterns across different regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further note that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this view by noting that the demand for missionary education was driven by complex cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that while British colonial rule may have been more conducive to formal education, the broader economic impact varied significantly depending on local contexts. The legacy of these educational institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes in profound ways.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This focus on poverty reduction is crucial because it highlights the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, even if the long-term growth implications are not always explicitly stated. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this view by arguing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, showing that specific historical or geographical conditions have cast temporary effects on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This reconstruction provides valuable insights into the economic conditions under which institutional reforms could be most effective. This qualification underscores the complexity of implementing successful institutional reforms in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented by several scholars who emphasize the importance of institutional frameworks that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution is crucial for economic growth because it facilitates long-term capital markets and encourages investment. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These inclusive institutions, which enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, were instrumental in fostering economic development in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand.

The significance of institutional quality is further underscored by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. They demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of their effectiveness in promoting economic growth. This historical evidence highlights how credible commitment to institutional rules can underpin long-term economic prosperity. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is particularly relevant in the context of colonial development, where institutions were often shaped by European colonists' interests.

The effectiveness of institutional reforms can depend on factors beyond ethnic diversity. Hopkins notes that ethnolinguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding (Frankema 2012, p.15; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) suggests that successful institutional reforms require a nuanced understanding of local contexts and the specific challenges faced by different regions. Frankema argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, highlighting how colonial institutions influenced educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.15). This historical evidence shows that the impact of institutions on economic growth can vary significantly depending on their design and implementation.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by comparing former European colonies. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth, and Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that inclusive institutions foster long-term prosperity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement concerns how extractive institutions persist despite their negative impact on economic development. The historical evidence from former colonies shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists were prevalent in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This comparison reveals that the persistence of extractive institutions is tied to the interests of politically powerful groups, which highlights the importance of institutional design in promoting long-run economic growth.
