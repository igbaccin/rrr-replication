The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether institutions are a primary driver of economic outcomes or if their effects are secondary to other factors such as geography or culture. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions can be shaped by political power dynamics, and how these institutions in turn influence long-term economic development. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups may not necessarily promote growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while others emphasize the importance of secure property rights as a fundamental driver of economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). A further position contends that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent upon historical and contextual factors, such as the presence of European settlers in colonies and their influence on local institutional development (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The debate also extends to whether institutions can be transformed into a crucial tool for understanding long-term economic trends (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), or if they are merely one among many factors that influence growth, alongside geography, culture, and human capital. This question is central to the study of economic development, as it determines how resources should be allocated to foster sustainable growth. The interplay between institutions and other determinants remains a critical area of inquiry, with scholars seeking to understand how these factors interact over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This institutional divergence was driven by mortality rates: high mortality led to extractive institutions, while low mortality fostered more inclusive ones; these institutions persisted over time and continue to affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These colonial institutions influenced long-run growth by establishing equilibrium institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) advise that measuring these institutional differences requires careful consideration of various indicators such as constraints on executive power and property rights to capture the nuances of institutional quality. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, the specific mechanisms by which they did so are complex and multifaceted.

Building on these insights, North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) highlight the pivotal role of financial institutions in reducing transaction costs. This reduction in transaction costs, facilitated by new financial instruments, was pivotal for long-run economic growth. This dynamic suggests that as economies develop, the proportion of GNP devoted to the transactions sector will expand (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that the institutional changes in England were not universally revolutionary; other factors, such as bureaucratic structures, may also play a significant role.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both assert that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets's analysis reveals that technological progress requires societal adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from modernization, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This involves both invention and the diffusion and application of new knowledge, which in turn demands an adaptable society willing to accept change. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by noting that institutional progress must complement technological advancements for economic improvement to be sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while institutions are important, they play a secondary role in supporting the primary driver of economic growth: technological and intellectual progress. This suggests that while institutions may not drive economic growth directly, they are essential for creating an environment where technological progress can flourish without societal disruptions.

The adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development challenge this perspective, as noted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade has had a lasting impact on African economies, with negative effects persisting through reduced levels of trust. This mistrust arises from both external factors such as rule of law and internal norms shaped by the historical experience of enslavement. Nunn and Wantchekon further assert that these internal norms are at least twice as significant as external environmental factors in shaping contemporary levels of trust within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that the legacy of mistrust, rooted deeply in the historical trauma of slavery, continues to hinder economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation. This extended duration implies that the economic impacts of slavery are more profound and pervasive than those of colonialism. Nunn (2008, p.2) also notes that centralized governance and well-developed trading networks were crucial in facilitating trade during periods when Africa's slave trades occurred (Nunn 2008, p.19). The implication is that while institutions play a role in economic development, their influence must be understood within the broader context of historical factors such as slavery that have shaped African societies over centuries.

Institutions are not static; they evolve in response to various factors. For instance, colonial administrations adjusted their policies based on local economic and political dynamics. This adaptive behavior is evident in instances where colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie's work complements this by emphasizing that distributional conflicts often drive the formation and persistence of core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

These findings suggest that institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe were influenced by a complex interplay of local conditions and adaptive responses to historical contingencies. This perspective challenges simplistic views of institutional change as driven solely by efficiency considerations or exogenous cultural factors. Instead, it highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to economic and political pressures.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff & Engerman (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) build on this by showing how British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African leaders and families alike. This shift in perception was driven by a complex interplay of cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). In New World societies with greater inequality, elites used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their privileged status (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutional mechanisms were crucial in shaping long-run economic growth. The persistence of inequality in these societies highlights a downstream implication: the evolution of institutions that reinforced existing power structures could limit broader educational access and economic opportunities.

The literature on African economic development and colonial legacies explores various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) asserts that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, arguing that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective is supported by Simson's analysis of East Africa’s post-independence budget policies, which challenges the narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services (Simson 2019, p.11). These findings collectively suggest that the quality of public goods provision in African societies is not necessarily tied to ethnic fragmentation but rather to the effectiveness of institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this by highlighting that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa could provide more insight into the long-term picture of economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, other historical and geographical conditions may also influence long-term African welfare.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) extend this analysis by showing that historical economic performance in Africa and Europe can be understood through per capita income levels and trade patterns. Broadberry and Gardner's work demonstrates that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence, challenging previous narratives of persistent poverty. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. These insights reveal that economic development in both regions was driven by factors such as trade and market integration, which facilitated growth in export production. The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). While these factors underscore the importance of trade and market integration in shaping long-run economic outcomes, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) also note that institutional change was a key driver of investment in agriculture, which led to high agricultural labour productivity in developing parts of north-western Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The extent to which these institutions influenced African economies remains an open question.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth through their influence on property rights and market exchanges. North highlights that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects a broader trend where political institutions establish efficient property rights, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and drive economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie underscores the importance of understanding how these institutions evolve over time. She advocates for transforming institutions from mere fashion accessories into indispensable tools for economic historians to employ in their long-term research programs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions is crucial in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of either extractive states or institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These divergent institutional paths significantly influenced current income per capita levels. Austin further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability shows how institutions can evolve to meet the needs of different societies, ultimately impacting long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is also evident in the context of African economies. Hopkins emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The extent to which these institutions have influenced African economies is a subject of ongoing debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Institutions underpin economic growth by shaping property rights, facilitating market exchanges, and providing incentives for investment. The precise historical evidence of European financial revolution shows how institutions reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights. This is a shared finding with Austin's analysis of colonial administrations adapting to indigenous actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15), which settles the disagreement over the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions in African economies.
