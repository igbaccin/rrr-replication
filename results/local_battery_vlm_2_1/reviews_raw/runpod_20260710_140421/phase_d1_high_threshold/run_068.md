The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been extensively debated among economists and historians. The stakes are high because this question directly informs our understanding of how societies develop over time and the policies that might foster sustainable economic progress. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.9), emphasizing how the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights. This institutional framework fostered a long-term capital market critical for economic growth, illustrating how secure property rights can drive long-run development.

Building on this, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argued that financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs. This perspective highlights how the creation of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution was crucial for economic development. The impact of these institutions is evident in the development of economic institutions that facilitated market exchanges, thereby reducing costs per transaction and increasing the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors by showing how political institutions governing enforcement and change were critical in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They argued that the success of these institutions is evident from capital market evidence, which indicates a remarkable transformation in economic performance. Recent research has upgraded France's pre-revolutionary economic performance, suggesting that institutional changes may have been less revolutionary than initially thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The evolution of financial institutions and the development of new financial instruments played a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, thereby driving long-run economic growth. This perspective underscores how the creation of institutions like the Bank of England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and promoting economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) assert that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and settler motivations, leading to divergent institutional outcomes. This approach contrasts with extractive states in regions like the Congo or Gold Coast, where European powers focused on rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences persisted over time, shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) extend this analysis by documenting the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and linking it to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. This interaction between institutions and economic development suggests that institutional legacies from colonial times continued to influence growth patterns long after independence. These findings underscore how initial conditions and colonial policies created enduring institutional legacies that continue to shape economic outcomes today.

This institutional perspective on long-run growth is crucial for understanding the diverse economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the need to consider historical institutions when analyzing contemporary economic development.

Expanding on this institutional perspective, Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts within societies fundamentally shape core economic institutions. These authors present the case that inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power are central to understanding how institutions evolve and affect development over time. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that efficiency considerations alone cannot account for the persistence of institutions like serfdom or craft guilds; instead, these institutions must be understood as products of underlying conflicts between different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illustrate this dynamic by showing how initial differences in factor endowments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, which in turn influenced the development of institutions that either reinforced or mitigated these inequalities. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns; instead, it highlights the role of power dynamics and distributional conflicts in institutional formation. The implications of this framework can be nuanced. For instance, while Ogilvie critiques the game-theoretic view of beliefs as institutions, Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis underscores that initial economic conditions can set societies on divergent paths of development, with institutions playing a crucial role in perpetuating or altering these trajectories. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are pivotal, the specific historical context in which they unfold is equally important.

To further illustrate this point, Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Hopkins 2009, p.18)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) examine the African economic development by focusing on specific historical and social factors. They argue that these factors can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This focus includes the role of women and the impact of colonial rule, which highlight how African societies have navigated economic development through distinct paths. This contribution was shaped by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule, which relegated women to marginal lands for food farming. These dynamics underscore the complex interplay between gender roles and economic outcomes in Africa's historical context. Hopkins notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This nuanced view suggests that while historical and social factors are crucial, the specific institutional frameworks that emerge from these dynamics are equally important in shaping long-run economic growth. Therefore, understanding African economic development requires a multifaceted approach that considers both historical context and institutional evolution.

In this context, the slave trades had an adverse effect on Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade led to a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust stems from both the immediate environment and internal norms shaped by historical experiences. The slave trade's impact on trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of its reduced-form effect on intergroup trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon assert that this mistrust persists to this day, highlighting a lasting legacy of the slave trades. This finding underscores the deep-seated nature of the cultural shifts induced by historical events, suggesting that economic development in Africa is hampered by enduring social attitudes. Internal norms of mistrust are one factor in the trust deficit; external environmental factors also contribute significantly (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This dual mechanism, internal and external, indicates a complex interplay between historical traumas and contemporary socio-economic conditions. The persistent mistrust and lack of social cohesion, fostered by centuries of slave trades, continue to impede economic growth in Africa.

The adverse effects of the slave trade on African development extend beyond cultural shifts alone; they also encompass institutional weaknesses. This disruption was particularly detrimental because it occurred over a prolonged period, spanning nearly 500 years. Colonial rule, often cited as a primary cause of Africa's underdevelopment, lasted only about 75 years. The institutional legacies of the slave trades are thus more profound and enduring than those of colonialism (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context suggests that addressing the deep-seated issues stemming from the slave trades requires economic reforms and a comprehensive approach to rebuilding social trust and strengthening institutions.

The impact of colonial education on African economic development varied significantly across different colonial powers. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) show that British rule was particularly effective in promoting formal education compared to other colonial administrations, which had more negative impacts on educational attainment. This effectiveness stemmed from the unique institutional framework established by British colonizers, which encouraged local demand for missionary education through altered social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions influenced African political leaders' cost-benefit calculations about formal education at both macro and micro levels, thereby enhancing the perceived benefits of education.

The long-term effects of this educational legacy are evident in the real wage trends across British African colonies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstructed real wage levels for nine British African colonies from approximately 1880 to 1965, revealing a divergence that highlights the structural impact of British colonial education policies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This educational foundation laid by British rule contributed to a more robust economic development trajectory in these regions. British colonial education had positive effects on long-term African welfare development; however, broader historical and geographical conditions also played significant roles in shaping this trajectory.

The legacy of British colonial education policies thus underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth, even amidst the complexities of colonial rule.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce contracts, which are crucial for investment and development. North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was pivotal in facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonists established different institutions based on mortality rates, with some colonies receiving extractive states while others benefited from institutions that enforced the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic development is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings are supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.29), who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment underpins long-term economic development by providing stability and predictability.
