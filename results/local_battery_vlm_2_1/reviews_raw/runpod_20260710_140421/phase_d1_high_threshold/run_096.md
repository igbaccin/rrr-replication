The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. This debate hinges on the relative importance of institutional frameworks versus other factors such as geography, culture, and initial conditions. A central disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who propose rival causes like culture or geographical factors. Institutions can be defined broadly to include formal rules, informal norms, and enforcement mechanisms. The debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. Understanding whether institutions are the key factor can guide efforts in institutional reform and economic planning. The live disagreement centres around whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors such as culture and geography play a more significant role. This question is particularly relevant for developing countries seeking to understand the path to sustained economic prosperity. For example, Acemoglu et al.'s work on colonial institutions highlights how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes that persist today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These differing institutions shaped long-term economic trajectories by determining whether rule of law and investment were encouraged or hindered.

The importance of institutions is further underscored by North’s argument that they are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North argues that the development of new financial instruments, such as those introduced during the English financial revolution, significantly lowered transaction costs. This reduction was critical for economic growth because it enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were driven by political factors, including the trade-offs made to secure revenues and resolve fiscal crises. North’s analysis aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that extractive institutions can persist and negatively impact economic development. For instance, in colonies where Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, long-term growth was stifled (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic progress depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the colonial context. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they set up extractive states designed to transfer resources back to Europe. These differing institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received more beneficial institutions experiencing higher income per capita levels today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast’s analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of public choice institutions was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth because it ensures that rules are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon processes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional stability is essential for long-term economic development as it provides a predictable environment for investment and market exchanges.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends beyond colonial contexts. Acemoglu et al.'s work on the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the enduring impact of institutional changes introduced during colonialism (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). They argue that these institutional legacies are more significant than geographic factors, which have often been cited as primary determinants of economic development. This perspective is reinforced by North and Weingast’s emphasis on the political factors underpinning economic growth, including the commitment to rules and their enforcement mechanisms (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings suggest that institutions play a critical role in determining long-term economic trajectories, regardless of initial conditions or geographical advantages.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by the varying strategies Europeans employed in their colonies, which ranged from settler societies to extractive states. This institutional framework persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, demonstrating the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al also note that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who may have obtained fewer rents with institutions of private property or feared losing political power if there was institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

They posit that the development of new financial instruments, such as the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade-off where certain rights were ceded to Parliament in exchange for revenues, marking a pivotal shift in political institutions. This institutional change facilitated market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms, leading to falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast also note that the success of these institutions depended on both their inherent efficiency and the regime's commitment to uphold them (Author Year p.N).

Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Broadberry and Gardner also note the importance of understanding historical determinants in shaping economic performance in Africa. Broadberry and Gardner's work on national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa shows that there has been significant available data, challenging the notion that African economic history is under-researched (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This data highlights the variability in export production tied to foreign trade and market factors, including political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), which suggests that institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. For instance, the timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These variations imply that effective institutions could have facilitated economic development by providing stable frameworks for trade and investment. Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of real wage trends to assess future growth possibilities (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact on long-run economic growth may be mediated by other factors such as labor market conditions.

The importance of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes is evident. Frankema and van Waijenburg's emphasis on real wage trends highlights the need to consider multiple factors when assessing long-run economic growth in Africa.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The core economic institutions, serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by struggles over resources and power rather than efficiency considerations alone. In Latin America, for instance, institutional frameworks evolved to maintain elite advantages through various government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by efficiency; instead, it highlights how they can be moulded by underlying social structures and power dynamics. Ogilvie emphasizes the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions as dynamic entities (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development underscores the complexity of economic growth in pre-industrial societies. This suggests that while efficiency may be a factor, it is not the sole determinant of institutional design; power dynamics and resource allocation play critical roles, shaping institutions in ways that can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trades in Africa have had a lasting detrimental impact on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This effect is mediated through the erosion of trust within societies; Nunn and Wantchekon's research shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop, which persists today. This mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, suggesting it is a significant factor in Africa's economic underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The mechanism by which this occurs involves both external and internal channels; however, the internal channel, shaping individuals' norms of mistrust, is at least twice as large as the external channel. This finding underscores the deep-rooted nature of the problem, as it requires addressing entrenched cultural attitudes in addition to changing external conditions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates that this mistrust is not an isolated phenomenon but part of a broader historical context where Africa's economic institutions were undermined by the slave trades, which lasted nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of colonial rule. Consequently, understanding the legacy of these historical events is crucial for addressing contemporary economic challenges in Africa, as the roots of mistrust run deep and require comprehensive solutions that go beyond mere institutional reforms.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the insight that technological progress can transform economies by introducing epochal innovations, which are dominant sources of sustained long-term growth. These innovations spread widely, influencing substantial parts of the world through their adoption and diffusion within growing economies. The institutional and ideological adjustments required to efficiently use these innovations differ across centuries and regions, suggesting that the interplay between technological changes and societal adaptations is crucial for economic development.

The role of institutions in this process is active: they facilitate the resolution of conflicts arising from growth-induced disruptions. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern economic growth is possible only if these conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, emphasizing the need for a strong political fabric within society. This implies that while technological advancements drive growth, institutions play a critical role in managing the social and political challenges that accompany such progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this idea by recognizing the possibility of coordination failures, suggesting that policies to rectify these failures are essential for sustaining economic improvements. The growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic than technological progress, indicating that institutional progress must complement technological advancements to ensure long-term sustainability. This underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment where technological innovations can flourish and contribute to sustained economic growth.

Institutions facilitate economic growth by shaping the incentives that guide individual behavior and collective action. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show how European colonization strategies led to different institutional frameworks in colonies, with some fostering investment and rule of law while others established extractive states. These institutions persistently affected economic outcomes, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on long-run growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) reinforces this perspective by highlighting how the creation of financial instruments and secure property rights in England reduced transaction costs and stimulated economic growth. Institutions that enhance security and reduce uncertainty are crucial for fostering a stable environment conducive to investment and innovation.

The role of institutions extends beyond mere efficiency considerations, deeply influencing distributional conflicts and power dynamics within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts in addition to efficiency alone. This perspective underscores how institutions are embedded in broader social and political contexts. These institutions reflect and reinforce power structures, which in turn shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) further illustrate this point by showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This highlights how institutions can either foster or hinder economic development based on their design and the interests they serve.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide a compelling case for the importance of credible commitment in institutional design, arguing that secure property rights are essential for economic growth. They show how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered an environment where property rights were protected and markets could flourish. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) complement this by demonstrating how historical events, such as the slave trade, can shape cultural norms around trust and cooperation, which are foundational for economic development. The shared finding is that institutions matter profoundly in shaping long-run economic growth because they create an environment where investments and innovations flourish (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), whereas North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets shows how institutional changes in seventeenth-century England significantly impacted economic performance, providing a clear indication that institutions per se were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.
