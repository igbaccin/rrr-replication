The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding historical and contemporary economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules that govern behavior, can either facilitate or hinder economic progress by influencing investment decisions, technological innovation, and resource allocation. A key disagreement centers around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes driven by political and economic forces. One position argues that institutions are the product of historical contingencies and colonial legacies, while another emphasizes the role of internal political dynamics and distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different types of institutions in colonies, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas persisting to affect current income levels. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic performance. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. While Acemoglu et al. focus on the long-term effects of colonial institutions, Austin suggests a more nuanced view of institutional evolution, where colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions.

A key implication is that understanding the role of institutions requires examining both exogenous shocks and endogenous responses. The historical context matters, but so do the adaptive mechanisms within societies.

The evolution of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, which were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) argues that the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs. These institutions facilitate market exchanges, leading to a decrease in transaction costs per exchange while increasing the aggregate volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic highlights how institutional changes can fundamentally alter economic performance. The implications are clear: the development of robust financial institutions and secure property rights are key drivers of long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate that distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that power struggles between different social groups better explain the persistence of serfdom and guilds than efficiency considerations alone. This perspective is echoed by Akyeampong and Fofack's work on African women, who show how patriarchal alliances and customary law codified during colonial rule reinforced gender inequalities in land access (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutional changes can be driven by the need to maintain power structures and economic efficiency. The implications of this perspective are significant: it suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires examining not just the formal rules but also the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts that shape these institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) caution that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they must be analyzed within the broader context of colonial legal systems and patriarchal alliances.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These colonial legacies persisted, affecting current income per capita. The interaction between population density and prosperity influenced the policies Europeans pursued, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights how initial institutional frameworks laid down by European colonizers were pivotal in shaping long-term economic trajectories. These institutions evolve over time and can be influenced by local political dynamics and power struggles. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between colonial legacies and endogenous institutional changes, suggesting that understanding economic development requires a nuanced view of how historical institutions shape contemporary outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time through the establishment of elite-advantaging policies in New World societies. This claim is shared with Nunn's argument that Africa’s slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, shaping subsequent economic trajectories (Nunn 2008, p.2). The mechanism of persistence involves the entrenchment of institutional frameworks that favor elites. This institutional bias towards elites has been shown to persist over centuries, influencing long-term economic development by perpetuating inequality and stifling innovation. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offer a nuanced view of the persistence of these historical inequalities. They highlight that the mistrust fostered by the slave trade in Africa has had a significant impact on intergroup relations, which in turn affects economic development. This cultural legacy of mistrust can be as detrimental to economic growth as the institutional barriers erected by elite-advantaging policies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These findings suggest that the persistence of historical inequalities is an institutional issue and also a deeply ingrained cultural one. The downstream implication is that addressing these legacies requires multifaceted approaches that tackle both institutional reforms and cultural shifts to effectively promote economic development.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) argue that the Black Death instigated long-term structural changes, leading to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence was driven by demographic shifts and institutional factors alone. Pamuk highlights that the economic transformation in Holland during this period was facilitated by institutional flexibility, allowing new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to thrive under weak monopolies. Meanwhile, Tuscany's economy stagnated as its political authorities strengthened existing monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23).

The structural changes initiated by the Black Death had profound implications for economic development. Pamuk's emphasis on institutional flexibility in Holland suggests that regions with adaptable institutions were better positioned to capitalize on new economic opportunities. Broadberry and Gupta point out that the benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This finding indicates that while demographic shifts played a crucial role, the institutional environment also influenced how these changes manifested economically.

These insights suggest that the interplay between demographic shifts and institutional flexibility was critical in determining early modern economic divergence. The uneven distribution of economic benefits underscores the complexity of factors influencing long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods. This perspective highlights that the thesis does not fully account for the complexity of institutional evolution across diverse contexts, such as European versus African colonial settings. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography has struggled with quantitative testing and explaining why some countries adopted growth-enhancing institutions while others did not (Austin 2008, p.2). This mechanism is crucial because it underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how different historical paths and institutional frameworks influenced long-term economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) extend this argument by examining real wage levels and trends in British African colonies, demonstrating that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The divergence in economic development between regions was influenced by contextual factors and immutable institutional barriers. However, Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this perspective by showing that colonial administrations could change their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between institutions and indigenous agency (Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is subject to local adaptations and responses.

Mokyr contends that the growth of useful knowledge fuels economic development, while Kuznets highlights how advancing technology necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional changes reveals a mechanism where technological progress creates new opportunities but also challenges existing social structures. For instance, Kuznets observes that modern technology often conflicts with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This conflict can induce growth-induced tensions that require resolution through political means. Kuznets further emphasizes the role of the sovereign state in peacefully resolving such conflicts, underscoring the importance of political stability in sustaining economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is generally irreversible, institutional progress can be more erratic and subject to setbacks. He argues that without complementary institutional advancements, the benefits of technological progress might be limited or even reversed due to rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on effective institutional adaptations.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that African institutions evolved in response to both endogenous and exogenous factors during the colonial period. These influences shaped the quality of public goods provided by African governments. The interplay between local political dynamics and external pressures, such as missionary education initiatives under British rule, drove this evolution. The demand for formal education among Africans was influenced by new social and political institutions that altered perceived benefits (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shift in educational priorities was linked to broader economic developments, including the growth of export production tied to foreign trade markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development is complex. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, indicating a more nuanced picture of economic performance than previously thought. This highlights that African countries' economic trajectories were shaped not only by their colonial experiences but also by the institutional frameworks they inherited or adapted from those experiences.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly influenced by their historical development and interaction with other factors, such as colonial policies and local conditions. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions encouraged investment and protected property rights, while extractive ones prioritized resource transfer to the metropole and inhibited economic development. This framework underscores how initial conditions shaped by colonial experiences determined long-term economic trajectories. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). He contends that the institutional evolution in older industrial economies reflects patterns and paths going back to their original industrialisation, emphasizing the importance of understanding the political delivery of secure private property rights. This perspective highlights that institutions are not static but evolve over time, influenced by both internal and external factors.

The historical development of institutions is crucial for understanding economic growth. North argues that institutions matter because they shape transaction costs, which in turn affect economic performance (North 1989, p.6). He shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in determining the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development, illustrating how historical institutional frameworks can either support or hinder economic growth. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). She argues that institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by political struggles rather than purely economic considerations.

The impact of colonial institutions on African economies is evident in the sustainability of growth. Hopkins notes that while some African economies showed signs of growth, their long-term sustainability depended on the quality of institutions developed during and after the colonial period (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions were likely designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence shows that the institutional frameworks inherited or adapted from colonial experiences significantly influenced African countries' economic trajectories. Austin highlights that where European settlers were not present, indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and fiscal security encouraged changes in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is agreed to be pivotal by all cited sources; North shows that institutions shape transaction costs, which affect economic performance (North 1989, p.6). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which these institutions operate and their interaction with other factors. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2), thereby overlooking the nuanced interplay between institutional evolution, investment, policy experience, and local conditions. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of former European colonies, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in determining the reversal in relative incomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This measurement type reveals how different institutional frameworks shaped long-term economic trajectories, underscoring the critical role of institutions in economic growth.
