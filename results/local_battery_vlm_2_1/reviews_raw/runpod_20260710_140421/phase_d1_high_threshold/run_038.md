The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians. At the heart of this disagreement lies the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure political, social, and economic interactions, drive economic development over the long term. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutional frameworks in fostering growth, while another challenges this view by highlighting alternative factors such as geographic determinants or exogenous shocks. Understanding how institutions influence economic outcomes is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at promoting sustained economic development. A key point of contention revolves around whether institutions are endogenous to economic conditions or exogenously determined by historical and political factors.

One stream of scholarship argues that the nature of colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) posits that European settlers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, leading to varying economic outcomes. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, while in areas with lower mortality rates, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on current income per capita, demonstrating how colonial policies shaped long-term economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, offering a nuanced view that acknowledges the complexity of institutional evolution over time. These perspectives collectively underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

Moreover, the role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is highlighted as critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was pivotal in fostering economic growth by enhancing market exchanges and facilitating long-term capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth, suggesting that the political factors governing how rules are enforced and changed are essential for sustaining economic development. These findings align with the broader argument that institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms.

In addition to the direct impact of colonial institutions, the interaction between institutions and other factors such as industrialization has been shown to be central to long-run development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins highlights that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty by understanding the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional quality in fostering long-term economic growth.

Colonial institutions introduced by European powers profoundly shaped long-term economic trajectories in their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates among Europeans led to significant economic disparities between these colonies and those where settlers established more inclusive institutions. This pattern is evident in the divergent trajectories of colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand versus those in sub-Saharan Africa or Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Colonial powers established institutional frameworks to maximize resource extraction for their benefit, strategically designed to suit local conditions. This extractive approach stunted long-term economic growth and development in these regions.

The mechanism through which these institutions operate involves the persistence of political and economic structures that favor a small elite at the expense of broader societal development. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that even when local conditions were favorable, the institutional legacies left by colonial powers hindered economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). For instance, regions with higher population densities and prosperity under colonial rule often saw their development opportunities constrained by institutions that did not encourage investment or technological advancement. This dynamic underscores how the initial design of extractive institutions can have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory.

These policies, designed to facilitate resource extraction rather than sustainable development, have left enduring imprints on economic structures. This historical perspective highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-term economic outcomes, emphasizing the importance of inclusive and growth-oriented institutional frameworks in fostering sustainable development.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, while Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. One specific mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced African economies is via their effect on local demand for education. For example, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions could foster economic growth in certain contexts (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional change in Africa.

The institutions in New World societies with greater inequality are argued to have restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) assert that European colonies in the New World were characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception. This inequality was reinforced through institutional structures that favored elites, not merely a result of initial conditions.

Sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, located just south of the Zaire river (Nunn 2008, p.19). Because the Kongo kingdom had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, it was able to support trade with the Europeans. This perspective underscores how institutional factors can have long-lasting impacts on economic trajectories, even when other exogenous variables are held constant.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This culture is rooted in both external factors (such as rule of law) and internal norms, with the latter being at least twice as impactful on trust as the former (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The legacy of slavery has shaped African societies' social dynamics by fostering enduring mistrust between groups. Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that this mistrust has also influenced economic development trajectories in Africa. Colonial administrations' responses to local economic and political actions were pivotal in shaping institutional frameworks, which in turn affected the production equilibrium. This historical mistrust, combined with colonial policies, has had profound implications for contemporary African economies, hindering growth by limiting cooperative behavior and undermining collective action. The persistence of this mistrust highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on modern economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is pivotal in driving long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both posit that technological advancements are fundamental to sustained economic development. This perspective posits that the accumulation of intellectual capital is the primary engine of growth, not institutional frameworks. The mechanism through which this operates involves significant institutional adjustments to facilitate the efficient use of new technologies. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology requires substantial ideological and institutional changes to integrate into existing economic systems effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr's emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge aligns with Kuznets' observation that technological progress necessitates complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Technological advancements can create conflicts that must be resolved peacefully for economic growth to continue, this process has challenges. This suggests that while technology and knowledge are primary drivers, the institutional environment plays a crucial role in managing the social and political implications of these advancements. The complexity of integrating new technologies into existing frameworks underscores the importance of adaptive institutions in supporting long-term economic development.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing and enforcing property rights that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This process is evident in the historical development of financial institutions like the Bank of England, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9), key factors that North argues are critical for economic growth. The evolution of such institutions reflects a broader pattern where political stability and secure property rights create an environment conducive to investment and long-term economic development.

The historical evidence from colonial settings further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive ones that encouraged investment to extractive ones designed to maximize resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions, with inclusive institutions fostering long-term growth while extractive ones impeded it. This is supported by Austin's analysis, which highlights how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, influencing the development of institutions that either facilitated or hindered economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15).

The reversal of fortune experienced by former European colonies underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. argue that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century was crucial in determining the long-run development paths of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of political institutions in specifying and enforcing property rights, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions in managing technological advancements and social conflicts is also crucial. The creation of new financial instruments and the development of secure property rights were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and provided a stable environment for investment, essential for long-term economic development.

The specific historical evidence that settles these disagreements lies in the comparative analysis of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al.'s work on the differing colonization strategies and their long-term economic impacts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) provides a clear example of how inclusive institutions can drive growth, while Austin's detailed examination of institutional adaptations in colonial settings (Austin 2008, p.15) highlights the dynamic nature of institutional development.
