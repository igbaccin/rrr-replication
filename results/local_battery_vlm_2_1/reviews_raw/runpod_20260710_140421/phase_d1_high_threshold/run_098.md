The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake are fundamental explanations for why some economies prosper while others stagnate. A central disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutional factors and those who argue that other variables, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play more decisive roles. Some scholars contend that institutions shape long-term economic outcomes by influencing property rights, investment incentives, and transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.2). Others propose that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs drive the formation of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while still others focus on the political commitment to secure property rights as a critical factor in fostering growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The debate over institutional primacy is further complicated by rival explanations, such as those focusing on demographic factors or colonial policies. These perspectives suggest that population density and prosperity influenced European colonization strategies, which in turn shaped the institutions of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The enduring effects of these institutions are evident in current income per capita disparities among countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

One stream of scholarship asserts that institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth by influencing property rights and transaction costs. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, hinder long-term growth. In contrast, inclusive institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment foster economic development.

The impact of institutional quality on economic performance is further illustrated by the historical evolution of financial systems. This institutional change was driven by political factors, including the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises. The resulting secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms were crucial for the development of a long-term capital market and sustained economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England provides additional evidence for the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

Another stream of scholarship identifies antecedents that trigger the formation of institutions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that colonial policies, rather than institutional factors alone, played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes in former colonies. The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, which encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15).

The interaction between institutions and other factors, such as demographic changes or cultural beliefs, is complex. This perspective suggests that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the formation of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the influence of colonial policies on institutional development. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights the enduring effects of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic outcomes.

The live disagreement is whether it is institutions or other factors such as geography that shape long-term economic growth. The causal path from extractive institutions to low growth is well-documented, but the role of antecedents like colonial policies and demographic changes remains a subject of debate.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of colonial institution established was determined by European mortality rates and the resulting settlement patterns. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development; for example, colonies with extractive institutions experienced lower long-run growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions. The implications of this institutional legacy are profound, as the extractive nature of these institutions often persisted long after colonial rule ended, continuing to hinder economic growth in many former colonies. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic outcomes.

Financial innovation, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), was crucial in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. They fostered secure property rights and drove long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges. These institutions reduced the cost of transactions, facilitating increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.9). The development of political institutions that specified "efficient" property rights and provided effective enforcement was critical in this process (North 1989, p.6). This institutional evolution was not merely an economic phenomenon but also a political one, as it involved the commitment of regimes to rules governing economic exchange. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that England's success is evident from capital markets. However, they note that this does not mean other institutions were inherently hindrances to growth; rather, the critical factor was the regime's commitment to these rules. This suggests that while financial institutions played a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth, the political commitment to maintaining these institutions was equally important. This interplay between financial innovation and political stability underscores the complex nature of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Ogilvie's analysis focuses on how these institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were formed not to enhance efficiency but to manage conflicts over wealth distribution, particularly between elites and commoners. The emergence of such institutions can be seen as a means to mitigate class struggles by institutionalising power dynamics. For instance, the craft guilds in pre-industrial Europe were instrumental in regulating competition within trades, thereby maintaining the status quo for elite control. The institutions that evolved in these settings often reinforced existing inequalities rather than fostering growth. However, Ogilvie cautions against overgeneralising this mechanism. While distributional conflicts are a powerful driver of institutional formation, they do not universally determine economic outcomes. The specific historical context and the nature of power struggles within societies play crucial roles in shaping institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

Mokyr posits that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). He and Kuznets together find that technological progress, particularly epochal innovations, constitutes dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores the significance of intellectual advancements in shaping economic outcomes. While Mokyr highlights the role of institutions in facilitating access to knowledge and reducing coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54), Kuznets emphasizes that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts without weakening societal cohesion, thereby ensuring sustained economic improvement (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This means that although institutions may facilitate economic growth by promoting useful knowledge, their development is not guaranteed to follow a linear path. The implications are significant: while technological advancements provide a steady impetus for growth, the institutional framework required to harness these advances can be volatile.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that Africa's slave trades have left a lasting imprint on the continent's economic development. The slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. This mistrust is not merely an artifact of colonial rule or recent political instability but rather a direct consequence of the centuries-long slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon trace this mistrust to both external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internal norms of mistrust. They find that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This deep-seated mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it erodes social capital and hinders cooperation (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade's impact on trust levels in Africa is significant; Nunn and Wantchekon estimate that it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, in turn, affects various aspects of economic life, from international trade to firm management practices. The legacy of the slave trade thus rivals the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by creating a persistent barrier to social and economic cooperation.

This encouragement often resulted in higher production equilibria, contrary to the hypothesis posed by Nunn (2008, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), indicating a more complex economic landscape than previously characterized. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability suggests that the impact of colonial policies on African economies was nuanced and context-dependent, rather than uniformly detrimental. Such complexity highlights the need for more granular analyses that consider the specific historical and institutional contexts in which economic outcomes were shaped.

A central tenet of economic growth theories is that institutions shape long-run economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints within which individuals operate (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.2). Institutions define property rights, enforce contracts, and establish rules for market exchanges. The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms has been shown to facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment (North 1989, p.6).

Institutional arrangements can either foster or hinder economic growth depending on their nature. Extractive institutions, which concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, tend to impede growth by discouraging investment and innovation. In contrast, inclusive institutions that provide broad-based political representation and secure property rights promote economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15). For example, Acemoglu et al. describe how extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates led to persistent underdevelopment, while more inclusive institutions in settler colonies fostered long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of institutions in economic growth is not limited to property rights and market exchanges. Political institutions that ensure credible commitment and enforce rules governing economic activity are also crucial. North and Weingast demonstrate how the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England provided a foundation for secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2). This shows that the institutional framework governing public choice is as important as the rules governing market exchanges.

The historical evidence from colonial economies underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The variability in economic performance across former colonies, despite similar geographic endowments, highlights the role of institutions in determining economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, the reversal of fortunes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors alone. This evidence supports the view that institutions are a key determinant of long-run economic growth.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth lies in the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction played a central role in the long-term development of former colonies, showing that institutions are not just fashion accessories but indispensable instruments for understanding economic outcomes.
