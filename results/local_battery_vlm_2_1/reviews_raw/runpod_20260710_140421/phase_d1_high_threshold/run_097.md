The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergent trajectories of nations over time. At stake is whether and how institutional frameworks can be engineered to foster sustained economic progress. The central disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, arguing that certain types of institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are essential for stimulating investment and innovation. This perspective often highlights how these institutions can mitigate transaction costs and enhance market efficiency. Another position contends that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex, involving mediating factors such as cultural beliefs or historical contingencies.

Institutional quality directly influences economic outcomes by shaping incentives for investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.15) argue that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive governance structures. These institutional frameworks significantly affected the long-run economic performance of these regions, with inclusive institutions fostering growth and extractive ones hindering it. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) posits that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the influence of historical contingencies and cultural beliefs. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) suggests that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs played a significant role in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were efficient responses to economic conditions, deeply rooted in societal norms and power dynamics. This perspective highlights how institutional development is intertwined with broader social and political contexts, influencing long-run growth trajectories beyond simple efficiency considerations.

The relationship between institutions and growth can be understood through the lens of colonial legacies, where institutional changes introduced by colonial powers had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributable to the extractive institutions imposed during colonial rule. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, illustrating how institutional frameworks can have enduring impacts on economic outcomes.

The interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional reforms is crucial for understanding economic development in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) argues that the new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into the role of institutions in poverty reduction. By examining the institutional frameworks that have shaped African economies, it becomes clear that effective governance and rule of law are essential for fostering investment and development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered local perceptions of the benefits of formal education, making it a more attractive investment for African families and leaders. This shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations that considered both macro- and micro-level factors (Frankema 2012, p.15). This economic performance was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, which were influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in growth patterns across different regions highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local conditions. This suggests that while British rule contributed to higher per capita income levels through education, the economic outcomes were not uniform and depended on specific contextual factors.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that England's economic growth during the seventeenth century was underpinned by the development of secure property rights. They attribute this to the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in England. These institutions were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth because they provided a framework that specified efficient property rights and ensured their enforcement.

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, alongside new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution was a direct consequence of increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. This process highlights how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs over time as the economy grows and becomes more specialized. As a result, the size of the transactions sector increases, becoming a larger proportion of GNP due to increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6).

North and Weingast present evidence from capital markets that underscores the significance of institutional changes in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They emphasize that the success of these institutions is marked by how they govern both economic exchange rules and their enforcement. The degree to which a regime or sovereign is committed to these rules is a critical political factor, as it ensures credibility and stability in property rights. The relationship between institutional changes and economic growth is complex and context-dependent, as evidenced by the varied experiences of different regions under colonial rule.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth and that institutions themselves may be more of an outcome than a cause. This perspective challenges the conventional view by suggesting that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by underlying power dynamics rather than purely efficiency-driven considerations. The distributional conflicts that Ogilvie highlights are linked to broader patterns of inequality, which have been shown to influence institutional development in colonial settings. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) illustrate this dynamic through their analysis of New World societies, where initial inequalities led to institutions that favored elites and restricted competition. This process of institutional formation, driven by distributional conflicts, can perpetuate inequality over time, as seen in the persistence of elite-advantaging policies. However, while Ogilvie emphasizes the role of beliefs within this dynamic (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), Sokoloff and Engerman focus more on the economic and political factors that shape institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The interplay between these factors suggests that the relationship between inequality and institutional development is complex and multifaceted.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into one narrative. This compression obscures the complex interplay between indigenous economic actions and colonial policies. For example, Austin (2008, p.15) notes that when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production. This dynamic suggests that African economies actively shaped their own development trajectories through economic and political engagement with colonial powers.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) support this critique by reconstructing real wage levels to argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon. This finding underscores the agency of African societies in shaping their economic outcomes. The implication is significant: if African economies were more dynamic and responsive than often depicted, then current development strategies might benefit from recognizing this historical agency. This points to a nuanced relationship where both exogenous institutions and endogenous responses played crucial roles in shaping African economic trajectories.

Akyeampong and Fofack assert that African economic development is significantly influenced by women's contributions and colonial legacies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary laws, often marginalizing women's access to land and resources. This codification of customary law under colonial rule was pivotal in shaping economic activities during this period. Akyeampong and Fofack document how Krobo and Akuapem farmers migrated to secure farmland for cocoa production, illustrating the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast a leading cocoa producer (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). However, Hopkins underscores that the role of institutions is crucial in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The implication is that while women's contributions were significant, the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule played a critical role in shaping economic trajectories. This nuanced view suggests that both endogenous factors, such as women's economic activities, and exogenous influences, like colonial institutions, are integral to understanding African economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon assert that Africa's slave trades had profound adverse effects on long-run economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). They establish this claim by showing how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust has persisted to this day and significantly impacts contemporary economic outcomes, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn provides empirical evidence that Africa's slave trades shaped subsequent economic development. The negative impact of the slave trade on development results from external factors such as colonialism or more recent events, but also due to internal norms and institutional breakdowns. The slave trades disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to a legacy of underdevelopment that persists today. This disruption was particularly pronounced because the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of colonial rule.

Mokyr 2005 and Kuznets 1973 argue that economic growth is driven by technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49). This growth requires institutional adjustments to efficiently use innovations. Institutions must adapt to accommodate new technologies, ensuring that the benefits of these advances are not lost due to societal resistance or inefficiency. Mokyr emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress, economic improvements may be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for long-term economic development. Kuznets points out that conflicts arising from technological change must be resolved peacefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while institutional adjustments are necessary, they must also be managed carefully to prevent social instability. Mokyr further qualifies this by noting that economic reasonableness, the application of rational economic principles, is essential for sustainable growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that institutions must not only adapt to technological changes but also foster an environment where economic rationality thrives, ensuring that the benefits of technological progress are fully realized.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) focus on specific institutional dynamics in Africa without directly addressing long-run economic growth (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). They argue that budget policies and land systems are shaped by deeply embedded political structures. This is evident, for example, in the neopatrimonial tendencies where government resources are treated as private property of political patrons rather than public goods (Simson 2019, p.2). Peters (2004, p.19) further elaborates on how these dynamics are rooted in historical perceptions of African landholding practices and their impact on agricultural development. This suggests that institutional adaptation to technological changes involves navigating entrenched political structures. The extent to which these budget policies and land systems influence long-run economic growth remains an open question.

van Zanden (2009, p.19); (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) focus on methodologies for measuring and predicting long-term economic performance, rather than directly addressing the role of institutions in driving economic growth. They argue that skill premiums can be used as a predictor of long-term economic performance. The skill premium reflects the remuneration for investing in human capital, encompassing costs such as foregone income during training periods and other related expenses (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This perspective highlights how investments in education and training, facilitated by institutions guaranteeing fair execution of contracts, can drive economic growth. These institutions may also form local cartels, restricting entry into skilled trades and keeping wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Bolt and van Zanden highlight that estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error, which increase as one goes back in time. These errors are often based on subjective estimates of underlying data, complicating the task of accurately measuring economic performance over long periods (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). The implications of these methodological challenges suggest that while skill premiums offer a valuable metric for assessing long-term economic growth, their interpretation must account for potential biases and institutional influences.

McCloskey contends that ideas and ideologies were instrumental in driving economic change from 1600 to 1848, with the rise of open discourse facilitating technological progress (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This ideological shift allowed for experimentation and innovation, as people were permitted by their society's prevailing rhetoric to "have a go" and talk openly about experiments. The Industrial Enlightenment, marked by this openness, was crucial in fostering economic growth through intellectual innovation (McCloskey 2015, p.4). By the late nineteenth century, even institutions like the papacy began favoring capitalist ideas over socialism, reflecting the broader ideological shift that drove economic change.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce contracts, which underpin economic activity (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, fostering a growing economy through increased specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework is critical for market exchanges, as falling costs per transaction enable more complex economic interactions. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight that the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions in colonies significantly influenced current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their analysis shows that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some regions receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. This variation in institutional design directly impacted long-term economic outcomes.

The ideological shift towards openness and discourse facilitated by institutions was crucial for technological progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie emphasizes the need to view institutions as indispensable tools for understanding economic history, arguing that they shape the broader societal framework. Austin points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution. This perspective underscores how the adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth varied across countries, influenced by factors such as fiscal security and indigenous economic action (Austin 2008, p.15). The creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions is further underscored by their impact on long-run development and industrialization. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors alone. This highlights how institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins argues that understanding Africa's economic history through an institutional lens can inform reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people. This approach emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

A shared finding across these studies is that institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth by influencing property rights and transaction costs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). The disagreement lies in whether institutional changes are driven primarily by extractive or inclusive strategies. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income levels, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin's analysis suggests that institutional prescriptions can change in response to indigenous economic and political action, emphasizing the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. This disagreement is settled by comparing the measurement of property rights enforcement and transaction costs across different historical periods and regions, as evidenced by North’s financial revolution in England (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15).
