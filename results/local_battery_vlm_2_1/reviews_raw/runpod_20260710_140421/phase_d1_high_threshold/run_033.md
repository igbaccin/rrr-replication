The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses how structural frameworks can either foster prosperity or hinder it over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies the tension between institutional theories that emphasize the primacy of property rights and secure governance structures, and those that highlight distributional conflicts and cultural factors. The crux of the disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by power dynamics and shared beliefs. This question is intellectually significant because it determines how policymakers should approach economic development: should they focus on establishing robust property rights and governance, or address underlying social conflicts and cultural norms? Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly influenced their long-run economic trajectories. North (1989, p.9) (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) also contends that institutions, particularly those governing property rights and governance structures, are crucial for economic growth. This sets the stage for examining how different scholars have approached this question through various lenses.

Building on this foundation, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions are pivotal in economic growth by fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. This institutional evolution facilitated market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms. As a result, transaction costs per exchange decreased while the overall volume of transactions increased, driving economic growth through greater specialization and division of labor in the economy (North 1989, p.6). This nuanced perspective underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth and suggests that while secure property rights are important, they are just one piece of a larger puzzle.

Extending this idea, Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) propose that the accumulation of useful knowledge drives economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights technological progress as a fundamental force in long-term economic development. Mokyr underscores how institutional adjustments are necessary to complement technological advancements, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that the pace and sectors affected by technological changes vary across regions and centuries (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This variation necessitates flexible institutional frameworks that can adapt to these changes without excessive costs or long-term political weakening. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving conflicts arising from technological advancements through institutional means (Kuznets 1973, p.6). However, Mokyr cautions that while technological progress may be more predictable and irreversible, the development of economic reasonableness, defined as the ability to make rational economic decisions, is less so (Mokyr 2005, p.54) . This insight suggests that although technological innovation is a robust driver of growth, its benefits can be tempered by institutional inefficiencies or lack of adaptive capacity.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) explain that distributional conflicts can account for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. They posit that these institutions were shaped by the interests of elites rather than efficiency concerns. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate how in New World colonies, factor endowments led to extreme inequality, which in turn influenced institutional development. These inequalities were perpetuated through policies that restricted competition and favoured elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This process is illustrated by the persistence of institutions that maintained elite dominance, such as those governing property rights and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) advises against overemphasizing efficiency considerations in explaining institutional change. She emphasizes that distributional conflicts often drive institutional development, challenging the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective shows how economic institutions can be deeply rooted in power dynamics and social structures, as well as purely economic rationales.

Further complicating this narrative, the slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the African slave trade has caused a culture of mistrust within societies, which persists today. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that this adverse impact was not limited to social dynamics but extended to economic institutions as well. This prolonged exposure to slave trades disrupted local economic and social structures, stifling the development of institutions conducive to sustained trade. The Kongo kingdom's experience illustrates this point: its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets facilitated foreign trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn (2008, p.2) warns that such institutional development was rare due to the pervasive effects of slave trades. This implies that the legacy of the slave trades is not just a historical artifact but a persistent force shaping contemporary African economic institutions and their capacity for growth.

Following from this, colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes. Acemoglu et al.'s (2002) analysis shows that these institutional differences were crucial in shaping long-term economic development. This pattern is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where those with extractive institutions lagged behind. Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) study also highlights that the impact of these institutions can be measured using various metrics, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The interaction between these institutional factors and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This suggests that while colonial institutions had profound effects, their impact was mediated by subsequent economic opportunities and policies.

This leads to Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenging conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments but rather that it is shaped by complex interactions between institutional factors and economic opportunities. This perspective highlights the need for deeper understanding of historical economic paths beyond structural impediments. The interaction between institutions and economic growth can be seen through the lens of colonial policies, which often responded to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, colonial administrations seeking fiscal security tended to adjust their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic development was not uniform but varied based on local conditions and responses. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates complicates efforts to fully understand this relationship (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2), leaving questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered.

In response, Broadberry and Gardner's work shows that African economies exhibited substantial economic activity before colonial rule, challenging previous perceptions of a uniformly stagnant pre-colonial Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). For instance, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) found that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges the notion that Africans were predominantly living at subsistence levels during this period. Similarly, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argued that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, with high productivity in the traded goods sector arising through merchant distribution and finance as much as production. This suggests that economic growth in Africa and Asia may have been driven by factors beyond institutional frameworks alone.

This perspective implies that historical narratives of African economic stagnation might be overly simplistic. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability suggests that while economic activity was present, its distribution and impact were influenced by complex interactions with local conditions.

This leads to long-term structural changes and economic divergence in Europe between 1500 and 1800 being marked by significant shifts in economic development and urbanization rates (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden (1999, p.1) argues that these changes were not uniformly experienced across Europe, with varying impacts on the standard of living. Pamuk (2007, p.23) further elaborates that by 1450, a wage gap had emerged between the northwest and the rest of Europe. This gap was driven by long-term structural changes initiated by events such as the Black Death. These changes were not merely economic but also institutional: late medieval Holland's rapid response to new economic opportunities was facilitated by its unusual degree of institutional flexibility (Pamuk 2007, p.23). However, this institutional flexibility was not uniformly present across Europe; in Tuscany, for example, political authorities strengthened existing monopolies rather than fostering new ones. This pattern suggests that while long-term structural changes were pivotal, their impact on economic development varied significantly depending on local institutional contexts and responses (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The varying trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe highlight the importance of understanding how different regions adapted to these changes, with institutional factors playing a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights led to falling transaction costs, which in turn supported economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework was instrumental in fostering a dynamic economy where increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, North and Weingast highlight that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that regimes are bound by rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Institutions also play a crucial role in determining the long-term development trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional outcomes. In some cases, Europeans established extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer, while in others, they set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced current income per capita, illustrating how historical institutional choices persistently affect economic development.

The impact of institutions on long-run growth is further evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that this reversal was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms in driving economic growth. The historical evidence from capital markets, as discussed by North and Weingast, supports this view, showing that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The precise mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes remain a subject of ongoing debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutional changes alone can explain long-term economic growth, suggesting that a more nuanced understanding is necessary. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical evidence from colonial administrations settling indigenous commodity production illustrates how institutions evolved in response to local economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium shows that institutional prescriptions were not static but adapted to the incentives created by indigenous actions. This dynamic interaction between institutions and local contexts highlights the complexity of institutional influences on economic development.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of colonial administrations in different regions. The case studies from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.15), which use measurements of production equilibria and institutional adaptations, demonstrate how local contexts shaped institutional outcomes and economic development trajectories.
