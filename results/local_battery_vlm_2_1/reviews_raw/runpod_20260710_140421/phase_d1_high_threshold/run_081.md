Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating incentives for investment and facilitating market exchanges. The live disagreement among scholars centres on the mechanisms through which institutions exert this influence. One camp posits that secure property rights and credible commitment underpin growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Another camp suggests that the efficiency of institutions is often secondary to distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). The stakes are high: different understandings of institutional impact lead to divergent policy prescriptions for fostering economic development. To grasp this role, it is essential to examine how specific institutional frameworks have historically influenced economic outcomes.

North's work highlights the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and ensuring property rights (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). They illustrate this through the evolution of English political institutions, which secured property rights and facilitated market development. Moreover, North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions relied on a credible commitment to enforce rules, which was crucial for fostering long-term capital markets and overall economic development. The centrality of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run growth is further supported by Acemoglu et al.'s work, which shows how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. Settler colonies, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law, experienced significantly higher income per capita compared to extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence underscores the impact of institutional frameworks on economic development by illustrating how different colonial policies resulted in divergent long-term economic trajectories.

The role of institutions is not limited to securing property rights; they also shape economic outcomes through their interaction with cultural and distributional factors. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run growth is mediated by broader social dynamics. Austin builds on this idea by highlighting how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive process indicates that institutions are not static but evolve in response to changing circumstances, further influencing long-term economic trajectories. Hopkins extends this view by emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa illustrates how institutional reforms can inform policy and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This perspective underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their critical role in shaping long-run economic growth through adaptive responses to social and political changes.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, highlighting that the development of financial instruments and secure property rights can dramatically lower transaction costs, fostering long-term capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further substantiate this by showing that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This pattern suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions can be a significant barrier to long-term economic growth, as these institutions are designed to maximize short-term gains for colonial powers rather than fostering sustainable development. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policy interventions aimed at promoting economic growth in regions with historical extractive institutions.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth by ensuring "credible commitment" to the rules of exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This credible commitment was essential for long-term economic growth as it provided stability and predictability, encouraging investment and economic activity. This dynamic indicates that while secure property rights are crucial, the path to fostering them can vary significantly based on local contexts.

Broadberry and Gupta, together with van Zanden, take a different approach by examining the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This early divergence was driven by differences in productivity and economic development. Van Zanden's analysis shows that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries after 1740 due to shifts in economic focus towards North Sea bordering nations (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These findings suggest that early modern economic growth was unevenly distributed. However, van Zanden qualifies this pattern by noting that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period, challenging optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This implies that while productivity gains were significant, they did not necessarily translate into uniform improvements in living standards across different regions.

Simson's study of East Africa's budget policies reveals that post-independence economic strategies were often shaped by regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This perspective complements Nunn and Wantchekon's finding that the legacy of the slave trade has had enduring effects on African societies, including a culture of mistrust that persists today. These factors have collectively influenced how institutions function in Africa, with political patrons often using government resources to buy loyalty rather than invest in broad-based public goods (Simson 2019, p.11; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This patronage logic is a key mechanism through which economic policies impact long-term development trajectories. For instance, the growth of military spending in response to regional instability can divert funds away from productive investments, further entrenching patterns of mistrust and underdevelopment. However, Broadberry and Gardner's research on Anglophone African economies suggests that despite these challenges, many regions had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Simson 2019, p.11). This points to a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history, where periods of growth and decline are closely tied to shifts in export production and political institutions.

Frankema's examination of British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa, along with Hopkins' insights into institutional reforms, highlights the enduring impact of formal education on development trajectories. Both scholars (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that British rule fostered a more conducive environment for formal education compared to other colonial powers, a legacy that shaped Africa's economic growth and development. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced patriarchal alliances, further complicated access to land and resources for women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20), revealing a nuanced picture of how colonial institutions influenced gender dynamics in economic activities. This institutional legacy not only shaped educational outcomes but also had broader implications for economic development by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities.

Africa's slave trades played a significant role in shaping long-term economic development and poverty levels across the continent (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn argues that Africa’s slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had an enduring impact on African economies. Frankema and van Waijenburg's work underscores this point by highlighting that understanding the historical determinants of real wage divergences across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth prospects. However, this perspective is nuanced by Nunn's observation that sustained trade in Africa only began when Europeans encountered the Kongo kingdom, which possessed a centralized government and well-developed markets, a condition that facilitated foreign trade. This suggests that the economic impact of slave trades was not uniform across Africa but varied based on pre-existing institutional structures (Nunn 2008, p.19). The implication is that while slave trades were a significant factor in African underdevelopment, their effects were mediated by local governance and economic institutions, which could either amplify or mitigate these impacts. This interplay between historical factors and local conditions reveals a complex picture of Africa's economic development trajectory.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutional efficiency is the primary driver of economic growth. Instead, it posits that the unequal distribution of power and resources shaped institutions in ways that favored elites at the expense of broader economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing how initial inequality in factor endowments led to institutional frameworks that perpetuated elite advantages. These institutions restricted competition and stifled economic growth by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

However, while distributional conflicts shaped institutions, the specific mechanisms through which these conflicts operated varied across regions. For instance, Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe highlights how guilds and serfdom were tools used by elites to maintain control over labor and markets. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that in New World societies, institutional differences arose from varying degrees of inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This variation suggests that while distributional conflicts are a common thread, the local context significantly influences how these conflicts manifest institutionally. Ogilvie's emphasis on the role of beliefs and norms in shaping institutions further complicates this picture, indicating that cultural factors also play a part in determining institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Thus, understanding the long-run economic impact of institutions requires considering both distributional conflicts and the specific historical and cultural contexts in which they occur. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional development and its multifaceted influence on economic growth.

The accumulation of knowledge, particularly technological advancements, is crucial for sustained economic development. This perspective underscores the importance of innovation in propelling economic growth forward. Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge are essential for long-term economic development. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, spread to a substantial part of the world and significantly impact economic growth. This shared finding suggests that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development.

Moreover, Mokyr points out that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. Without such institutional support, the benefits of technological advancements could be limited or even eliminated by rent-seeking behaviors. This implies that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by appropriate institutional frameworks to maximize its economic benefits. Institutions play a critical role in facilitating the diffusion and efficient use of new technologies, thereby fostering long-term economic growth.

Kuznets qualifies this view by noting that modern technology often requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments can lead to conflicts within societies, which must be resolved peacefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in resolving such growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological progress is vital, its successful integration into the economy depends on effective institutional management of societal changes. Kuznets (1973, p.1) reports, “Nor is modern technology compatible with the rural mode of life, the large and extended family pattern, and veneration of undisturbed nature.” Mokyr (2005, p.49) reports, “None of these results are materially different whether we count journals by first appearance only or whether we weight them by years of survival, except that German periodicals become less important as average periodical life in Germany was substantially shorter.”

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs. Acemoglu et al. argue that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The latter, characterized by the rapid transfer of resources to the metropole, were particularly detrimental to long-run economic growth in colonies with high mortality rates. North highlights that the development of financial instruments and secure property rights, such as those established during the English financial revolution, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This underscores how institutions that provide security and enforce property rights are crucial for fostering long-term economic development.

The historical context of institutional change is equally important. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is further illustrated by Austin, who notes that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more nuanced interplay between local conditions and institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that institutions introduced during colonialism are key to understanding relative income levels among former colonies, is supported by the evidence presented in these studies. This suggests that historical institutional changes, driven by both external colonizers and local actors, have lasting effects on economic development.

Institutional change is not solely a product of efficiency but also arises from distributional conflicts within societies. Ogilvie contends that core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with North's view that political institutions specifying efficient property rights are essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutional design and distributional conflicts highlights the complexity of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth. Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, as noted by Ogilvie, can also block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, further complicating this dynamic (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the comparative analysis of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in colonization strategies led to distinct institutional outcomes, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding is supported by North's argument that secure property rights and reduced transaction costs are critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The reversal of fortune thesis, as illustrated by Acemoglu et al. and Austin, provides a clear historical context for understanding how institutional changes introduced during colonialism have shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies.
