The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks can determine a region's economic trajectory over centuries. One side posits that institutions are pivotal in fostering economic development by ensuring secure property rights, facilitating investment, and promoting market exchanges. On the other hand, some scholars argue that institutions alone do not explain economic growth; rather, they contend that population density, prosperity, and geographic factors play crucial roles as well. The debate hinges on whether institutional changes are the primary drivers of long-term economic development or if they operate in tandem with other socio-economic variables. In essence, it is unclear whether institutions are the main cause or merely one factor among many in shaping a region's economic future.

The importance of institutions in fostering economic growth is underscored by their role in securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is critical for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring secure property rights and fostering investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Institutions like the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, exemplify how institutional changes can have profound economic impacts. The creation of such institutions reflects increased security of property rights, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This theme is carried forward by Acemoglu et al. who highlight that the interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. Their work highlights how equilibrium institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, significantly influenced economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that secure property rights were pivotal for economic growth in England, and North (1989, p.9) demonstrates that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England lowered transaction costs. The English financial revolution was a critical development in securing property rights over time, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North's analysis highlights how the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade-off where certain rights were ceded to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for institutional changes that fostered economic growth. They argue that a critical factor in England's success lay in the political institutions governing how economic rules were enforced and changed. This credibility was essential for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth by encouraging investment and long-term planning. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth may be more nuanced than simply providing secure property rights.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established diverse institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment. The persistence of these institutional differences has had profound effects on current income per capita levels in former colonies.

The enduring impact of colonial institutions is evident in their influence on long-run economic development. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization played a critical role in shaping the long-term development trajectories of these regions. This perspective highlights the complex and often exploitative nature of colonial institutions, which were tailored to serve the interests of European powers at the expense of local economic progress.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that Africa's institutions, shaped by the legacy of colonialism and slavery, have influenced economic outcomes. The impact of colonial rule is evident in its structuring of social and political institutions; British colonial policies sometimes supported formal education more than other European powers did (Frankema 2012, p.15). This differential treatment created new perceived benefits that drove demand from African families and leaders (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19). These institutions were not always conducive to broad-based economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights how Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, have had lasting adverse effects on economic development. These trades disrupted social and political structures, creating conditions that hindered growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) together with Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by demonstrating that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) describe how export production, tied to foreign trade, was a primary driver of economic growth in many African countries during the colonial era. This growth was not uniform across all regions or time periods. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further support this by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, showing that economic development varied significantly. The variability in economic performance across different regions and time periods suggests that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played crucial roles in shaping these outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) point out that the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era has enabled recent work in African economic history to demonstrate this variability. This underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and geographical conditions that influenced long-term welfare development in Africa.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and its implications for African economic history highlight that different historical periods and paths have shaped Africa's economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn’s formulations. The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, suggesting that 'good' institutions are crucial. This variation underscores the importance of considering the nuanced ways in which institutions have evolved over time to shape long-run economic growth.

The Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and other parts of Europe by 1450, suggesting an alternative pathway to economic divergence independent of institutional factors (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Pamuk argues that the demographic shock of the Black Death created new opportunities for labour mobility and capital accumulation in regions like Holland. This flexibility allowed these areas to rapidly adapt to new economic conditions, fostering urban growth and commercial development. They note that merchant distribution and finance contributed significantly to high productivity in traded goods sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p. 9). This structural change was a fundamental shift driven by demographic and economic forces; it was not merely an institutional adjustment. The reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates further reinforced these effects (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). However, Pamuk points out that the flexibility of institutions also played a critical role; new towns could spring up under weak monopolies, while existing monopolies in regions like Tuscany hindered growth (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This institutional flexibility was key to harnessing the economic opportunities created by the Black Death and subsequent demographic shifts. The divergence between northwestern Europe and other regions thus reflects a complex interplay of demographic shocks, economic adaptations, and institutional responses that collectively shaped long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that the accumulation of practical and scientific knowledge has been pivotal in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is echoed by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights that technological progress, driven by advancing knowledge, necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These authors collectively argue that the evolution of technology, enabled by the growth of useful knowledge, is more fundamental than institutional frameworks in driving economic growth. The key mechanism here is the continuous generation and application of new technological advancements, which in turn require supportive institutions to resolve potential conflicts arising from these changes (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that modern technology demands significant societal adjustments; for instance, it is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family structures. This suggests that economic growth is contingent on technological progress and society's ability to adapt to these changes without weakening its political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that while technological progress can be relentless, institutional progress has been less monotonic and irreversible. This implies that although technological advancements are a critical driver of growth, the sustainability of economic improvement hinges on complementary institutional developments (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

One of the key findings in the literature is that institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the security and enforcement of property rights. North (1989, p.9) argues that the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) analysis, which shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth have hindered economic development in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth is another critical aspect highlighted by the literature. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the success of seventeenth-century England's economic growth was underpinned by political factors, particularly the regime's commitment to rules governing property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This idea resonates with North’s (1989, p.9) discussion on how institutions facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs are essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The interaction between technological progress and institutional development is also crucial. Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, which in turn can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) findings that differences in European colonization strategies led to varying institutions, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The precise role of institutions in shaping economic growth is evident in the historical evidence from capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This underscores that credible commitment underpins growth, as shown by the success of England's financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9).

The remaining disagreement concerns whether institutional development is monotonic and irreversible or subject to reversals. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that institutional progress has been less monotonic and irreversible (Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows how extractive institutions in former colonies with high mortality rates persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence from the capital markets of seventeenth-century England settles this disagreement.
