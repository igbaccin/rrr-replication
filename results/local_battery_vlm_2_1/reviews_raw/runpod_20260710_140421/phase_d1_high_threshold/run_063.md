The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms driving prosperity and stagnation over extended periods. Institutions are often seen as the backbone of economies, influencing everything from property rights to market regulations. The debate focuses on how different types of institutions affect economic outcomes. One position emphasizes that institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups can hinder long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Another position argues that the adoption and effectiveness of growth-promoting institutions are influenced by historical factors, such as colonial policies and population density (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2), suggesting a path-dependent process where initial conditions set the trajectory for institutional development. Some scholars suggest that the nature of institutions can be traced back to early historical events, such as European colonization strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The interplay between these factors highlights the complexity of understanding how institutions shape economic growth over the long run.

These institutions were established to maximize the rents of European colonists. The nature of these institutions varied widely depending on the colonization strategy employed. In some cases, such as in the United States and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In other regions like the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks was deeply influenced by European mortality rates and colonization policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19), which shaped the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes introduced during colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize short-term gains for colonizers, often at the expense of long-run economic growth. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes. The legacy of these extractive institutions persists in many former colonies, highlighting the critical role that colonial policies played in shaping economic development paths.

Institutional developments such as the creation of financial instruments and central banks were pivotal for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) argues that the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the consequent development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, which are critical to both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. This institutional change reflects an increased security of property rights over time. The success of England’s financial revolution was underpinned by a credible commitment to enforcing property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework fostered a stable environment for market exchanges, which in turn drove economic growth.

This perspective challenges neoclassical theories that primarily attribute economic development to population and savings. North (1989, p.9) notes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling transaction costs per exchange. However, while this institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, it is important to note that the size of the transactions sector itself grew as a proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This growth in the transactions sector highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping economic development paths. Therefore, the institutional framework established during England's financial revolution not only reduced transaction costs but also fostered an environment conducive to long-term economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights over time and facilitating market exchanges.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both see technological advancements as the primary driver of economic growth, arguing that the accumulation of useful knowledge underpins long-term economic development. This perspective posits that institutional frameworks are secondary to the role of technology in driving sustained growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.6) qualifies this perspective by underscoring the importance of resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully. He argues that the modern national state plays a crucial role in managing these conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society, suggesting that while technology is fundamental, institutional stability is also essential for sustaining economic growth. This implies that although technological progress is the primary driver, institutions play a vital supporting role in maintaining the conditions necessary for long-term economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates on this by recognizing coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them, indicating that institutional progress complements technological advancements. This suggests that while technology drives growth, effective institutions are needed to ensure that technological progress is sustained and not undermined by social or political instability.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. A specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutions was the role of shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights. These beliefs could either enable or hinder institutional development that guaranteed property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

A downstream implication is that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe evolved in response to changing power dynamics, even as they constrained those dynamics. Pamuk (2007, p.23) provides an example of this with the Black Death. It initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, which was not solely due to later developments (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This highlights how exogenous shocks could disrupt existing power structures and create opportunities for institutional change.

This does not imply that institutions were always malleable. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions like guilds could become entrenched over time, even if their initial purpose was to serve the interests of a dominant group (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that while distributional conflicts can drive institutional change, once established, these institutions can perpetuate themselves and resist further change.

The slave trades in Africa had profound adverse effects on economic development that challenge the notion that institutions are the sole fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is an internal norm that significantly impacts economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the duration of African slave trades was far longer than colonial rule, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. The adverse effects of these prolonged slave trades, including mistrust and weakened social institutions, are thus deeply entrenched in Africa's economic development trajectory. This kingdom had a centralized government and well-developed markets which were able to support foreign trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). These factors suggest that while the slave trades had adverse effects, some pre-colonial African institutions were conducive to economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) establish that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education was influenced by the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders at a macro level and African families at a micro level, which were altered by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions changed the perceived benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, as they offered new opportunities that were previously unavailable. This mechanism is consistent with the broader finding that colonial policies played a crucial role in shaping educational outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the factor endowment of colonies influenced the development of institutions, which in turn affected economic growth. This relationship was not uniform across all colonies; the specific historical context and political dynamics within each colony determined how these institutions evolved. This means that while British rule might have fostered formal education more effectively, the impact on long-run economic growth varied depending on local conditions and the specific policies implemented.

The economic performance of Anglophone African economies and the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 are central to understanding long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) challenge conventional narratives by highlighting historical patterns of growth and income levels. The availability of extensive colonial-era data for Africa, coupled with the significant role of foreign trade in shaping economic growth dynamics, underscores the complexity of these economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). For example, the timing and scale of export production varied significantly across regions due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). In Europe, merchant distribution and finance played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, with the service sector being particularly important. This highlights how institutional changes can drive investment and productivity gains, which is relevant for understanding the broader mechanisms of long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that the benefits of these developments were unevenly distributed, suggesting that while institutions fostered growth, they also created disparities in wealth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis is critiqued for oversimplifying causation and compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis does not account for why institutions favorable to economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others. This critique highlights a gap in understanding how institutional changes, particularly those driven by colonial policies, influenced long-term economic outcomes. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that where European settlers were absent, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production and adjusted institutional prescriptions based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that Africa's dismal economic record is linked to poor institutions. Thus, understanding the mechanisms by which colonial policies shaped African economies is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms today.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs, which in turn fosters economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that European colonies with extractive institutions experienced significantly lower income per capita due to the persistence of these institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The design and implementation of colonial policies play a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks. Austin highlights how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on local incentives, particularly when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is evident in cases where indigenous commodity production was encouraged to align with colonial interests.

The impact of institutions extends beyond economic policies to include broader societal structures. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts are a more accurate explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view underscores the complexity of institutional design and its multifaceted influence on economic outcomes.

The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies is linked to their historical design, which maximized rents for European colonists. Acemoglu et al. show that these equilibrium institutions were not conducive to economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical legacy continues to affect the economic trajectories of many developing nations.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into institutional reforms and their potential to alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with North's assertion that secure property rights are crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9), highlighting the enduring relevance of institutional frameworks.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by empirical evidence on the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies. Acemoglu et al show that European mortality rates led to the establishment of these institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin's findings demonstrate how colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local incentives, particularly when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15), settling the disagreement by showing that institutional adaptations were driven by both historical legacies and local conditions.
