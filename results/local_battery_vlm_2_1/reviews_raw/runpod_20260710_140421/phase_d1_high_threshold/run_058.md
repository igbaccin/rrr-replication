The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among scholars. At stake is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play more significant roles. Some argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current economic outcomes. Others posit that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, with distributional conflicts and shared beliefs playing equally critical roles in shaping economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.15) argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent economic underdevelopment. This perspective is supported by North's assertion that institutions matter because they shape property rights and transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin argues that focusing solely on institutional legacies oversimplifies the complex interplay of historical factors and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This debate highlights the need to consider multiple dimensions of institutional impact, including how they interact with other economic and social factors.

Acemoglu et al.'s work demonstrates that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by their analysis showing that variations in institutional quality, as measured by constraints on executive power, property rights, and the rule of law, correlate strongly with current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists while promoting long-term growth was not a priority. This perspective underscores how colonial policies created enduring institutional frameworks that continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a crucial role in determining the long-run development paths of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). It is important to consider that the impact of these institutions may vary depending on local conditions and subsequent historical events.

Building on this context-specific impact, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) contend that financial institutions played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. This development reflected increased security of property rights over time as well as being an economic phenomenon.

The role of financial institutions in reducing transaction costs is evident in the growing proportion of GNP allocated to the transactions sector, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. This trend highlights how efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are integral to economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast's analysis suggests that the impact of these institutions can be nuanced. Their findings indicate that while institutional changes were significant, they may not have been as revolutionary as initially thought when comparing England and France's economic performance before the French Revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the broader historical context in which institutions operate.

The implications of these findings are substantial. For example, the development of new financial instruments and institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs while enhancing the security of property rights over time.

In pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are key drivers of core economic institutions. Ogilvie proposes that institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by power struggles in addition to efficiency considerations alone. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial inequality led to institutional frameworks that favored elites and restricted competition. One specific mechanism through which these distributional conflicts operate is the use of government policies to entrench elite advantages. For example, in Latin America, institutions often reflected the interests of powerful landowners who used their influence to maintain control over resources and limit economic opportunities for others. This dynamic highlights how institutional inequality can perpetuate itself over time. Ogilvie cautions that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they should not be viewed as the sole determinant of institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Extending this idea to Africa, Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They challenge the idea that Africa's economic trajectory is solely determined by colonial institutions, suggesting instead that local responses to colonial rule played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. This dynamic highlights how local agency can influence institutional outcomes and economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg further illustrate this point by reconstructing real wage levels in British African colonies, showing that economic conditions varied significantly across different regions and time periods (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Austin notes that while these local responses were important, the broader institutional framework set by colonial powers still constrained economic possibilities. This suggests that while local agency can mitigate some of the negative effects of colonial institutions, it cannot entirely overcome them. Understanding this interplay between local action and colonial structures is crucial for appreciating the complex nature of Africa's economic history.

The slave trade had profound and enduring effects on African economic development by fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust within Africa (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust persists today. One specific mechanism through which the slave trade impacted trust is via intergroup dynamics. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion between different ethnic groups, creating lasting divisions and hostility. This internal norm of mistrust has been shown to be at least twice as large as the external channel of mistrust, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The persistence of this mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collective action (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrates that the duration of the slave trades in Africa spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, which is significantly longer than the period of official colonial rule. The legacy of mistrust created by the slave trade continues to impact Africa's economic growth today, as it undermines social capital and inhibits productive interactions within and between communities.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological innovation is the mainspring of economic growth, which Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports by showing that epochal innovations have driven sustained growth over centuries. Kuznets (1973, p.1), however, qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is often irreversible, institutional progress can be less monotonic and more susceptible to erosion from rent-seeking activities. This suggests that the sustainability of economic growth driven by technology depends on complementary institutional developments that support innovation. Thus, while technological progress is a key driver of long-term economic growth, its benefits are contingent upon supportive institutions that manage access costs to knowledge and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors.

The accumulation of useful knowledge through formal institutions can thus be seen as a critical factor in sustaining economic growth over the long term.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to manage transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights reduces transaction costs, fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al., who highlight how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are integral to sustaining long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evolution of institutions reveals their pivotal role in economic development. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was crucial for the emergence of a long-term capital market, which North identifies as essential for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by their influence on the distribution and accumulation of wealth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonies with extractive institutions experienced lower income per capita due to policies that maximized rents for colonists while promoting long-run growth was not a priority (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that institutions governing public choice, such as those in seventeenth-century England, were critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by showing that European colonies with high mortality rates often adopted extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that while institutions can drive long-term economic growth, their effectiveness depends on historical contexts and the specific policies they implement. Austin also shows colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in historical comparisons between European colonies with differing institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. show that colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists, experienced lower income per capita compared to those with inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are integral to sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.6).
