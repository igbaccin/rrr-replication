Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how societies organize production, distribution, and exchange of goods and services over extended periods. This question is central to understanding why some economies flourish while others stagnate, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or merely secondary factors influenced by other determinants such as geography or culture. A key disagreement revolves around how institutions are shaped: Are they driven by efficiency considerations or power dynamics? Some argue that institutions evolve to minimize transaction costs and maximize economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Others contend that distributional conflicts and the interests of powerful groups often dictate institutional design, even if these institutions do not promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The nature of institutions matters for economic growth. Institutions can be either extractive or inclusive. Extractive institutions concentrate power in the hands of a few, often at the expense of broader economic development, while inclusive institutions distribute political and economic power more widely, fostering an environment conducive to sustained growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The difference between these two types of institutions is critical because they shape long-term economic trajectories. For example, Acemoglu et al argue that extractive states were established in colonies with high mortality rates, where Europeans aimed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole rather than invest in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions has significantly affected current income per capita in those regions, highlighting how initial institutional choices can have enduring impacts on economic outcomes.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast argue that the political transformation of England during this period led to a significant reduction in transaction costs. North further elaborates that as political institutions evolved to specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, economic institutions emerged to facilitate market exchanges. This dynamic resulted in a growing proportion of transactions relative to GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). Consequently, the cost per transaction decreased, fostering an environment conducive to long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis underscores that a critical political factor for this success was the regime's commitment to rules governing public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework ensured that property rights were secure, which was essential for sustained economic development in England. The evolution of these institutions highlights how secure property rights, underpinned by credible commitments from the state, can drive long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the traditional view that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency concerns. This approach is aligned with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutional frameworks advantaged elites through policies that restricted competition. However, the persistence of inequality in these societies suggests that the institutions were not merely shaped by efficiency but also by the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts. The evolution of guilds in Europe, for instance, was influenced by the beliefs and power structures within societies, which led to rules that favored certain groups over others. This dynamic highlights how economic institutions are often the product of social and political struggles rather than purely economic efficiency considerations. In essence, the shaping of economic institutions is deeply intertwined with the broader social and political contexts in which they operate, influencing long-run economic growth through the lens of distributional conflicts. The role of institutions in economic development extends beyond mere efficiency to encompass the power dynamics that underpin societal structures.

Acemoglu et al. show that Europeans adopted distinct colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive institutions that fostered investment and rule of law or extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional divergence was driven by the economic incentives faced by European colonizers, who established institutions that maximized their rents rather than promoting long-run growth in the colonies. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that even when institutions are in equilibrium, they may still be extractive and not conducive to economic development, as they are shaped by politically powerful groups whose primary interest is rent maximization rather than growth promotion (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy has profound implications for understanding the contemporary economic disparities between former colonies, highlighting how historical institutional choices continue to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) focus on African economic performance and the impact of colonial rule, using historical data to challenge prevailing assumptions about Africa's economic history. They emphasize that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century; this is consistent with Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. These scholars highlight that the economic growth in these regions was often tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The real wage levels in British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965) provide a nuanced picture of economic development that challenges the notion of persistent poverty. The absence of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered regarding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. The historical data on real wages and export markets offer a critical lens through which to view the complex interplay between colonial institutions, economic performance, and long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) both show that historical factors have had profound effects on African economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causation by compressing different historical periods, suggesting that European settlers’ presence did not always lead to adverse outcomes for indigenous commodity production. For instance, colonial governments often encouraged local production when settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.2). This mistrust is not solely due to external factors like the rule of law but is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. The historical legacies of colonial policies and the slave trade have thus shaped contemporary African economies through mechanisms that go beyond simple institutional frameworks. The implications of this historical mistrust are vast, as it affects not only economic development but also social cohesion and political stability. Therefore, understanding these historical factors is crucial for comprehending the current state of African economies and for devising effective policies to address their challenges.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already under way before 1800, and this divergence is attributed to early modern economic growth driven by the service sector in north-western Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that merchant distribution and finance played a crucial role in increasing productivity within the traded goods sector. This economic expansion was facilitated by institutional changes that fostered investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Pamuk (2007, p.23) further elaborates on this divergence, noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions. These changes were underpinned by institutional flexibility that allowed new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to flourish (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The economic growth in northwest Europe was also marked by increased urbanization rates, indicating higher productivity and economic development compared to southern European regions (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This divergence underscores the importance of early modern institutions in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a posture that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores that technological advancements are pivotal in shaping long-term economic trajectories. Mokyr highlights how the growth of useful knowledge spurs economic development, while Kuznets identifies epochal innovations as dominant sources of sustained growth over extended periods. These advancements necessitate institutional adjustments to facilitate their integration into existing socio-economic structures. For instance, Kuznets notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns, suggesting that societal adaptations are essential for harnessing technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The role of institutions becomes evident in facilitating these adjustments. Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial part in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that economic development proceeds without excessive social costs or political fragmentation (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This alignment between technological progress and institutional frameworks is critical for sustaining long-term economic growth. Mokyr cautions that while technological progress can be monotonic and irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness is less predictable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that although institutions are necessary for leveraging technological advancements, their effectiveness in promoting sustained economic growth can vary significantly over time.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that influence technological progress and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that institutions designed to enforce rule of law and encourage investment have a significant positive impact on economic outcomes, as seen in the long-term prosperity of colonies like the United States and Australia. The critical role of institutional frameworks is further highlighted by Austin, who argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation and that long-term economic growth can only be understood through the lens of evolving institutions (Austin 2008, p.2).

The efficiency of institutions in promoting sustained economic growth can vary significantly over time. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate the importance of credible commitment to property rights for long-run economic development. They show that England's success was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, underscoring the critical political factor of regime commitment to rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is echoed in Acemoglu et al.'s work, which highlights how institutions can be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding among these sources is that while technological progress can be monotonic and irreversible, the effectiveness of institutions in promoting sustained economic growth depends on their design and enforcement mechanisms. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that institutions are an indispensable instrument for understanding economic history.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the interaction between population density, prosperity, and colonial policies. Acemoglu et al. show how these factors influenced institutional changes introduced by colonialism which reversed relative incomes among former European colonies (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction provides a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth through the lens of historical empirical comparisons, settling the disagreement on whether technological progress or institutional frameworks are more critical for sustained growth.
