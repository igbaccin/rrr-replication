The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because institutions can either foster or hinder economic development by influencing investment incentives and resource allocation. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes versus other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions. Some argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-term growth, while others contend that they merely reflect underlying social structures or historical contingencies. The debate highlights a critical question: can institutional changes be engineered to promote sustained economic development? Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) suggest that European colonization strategies led to different institutions in colonies, with extractive states hindering growth while inclusive ones fostered it. North (1989, p.6) posits that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth. Scholars debate the precise role of these institutional factors as they examine the interplay between institutions and other determinants of economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of specific institutions was critical for long-run economic growth in England. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, which is a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. They also observe that traditional interpretations may have overstated the revolutionary impact of English institutional changes by comparing them to France's economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This highlights a nuanced view where institutional developments must be evaluated within broader political and economic contexts.

The institutional framework plays a crucial role in facilitating this diffusion, as it reduces access costs to new technologies and ensures that innovations are efficiently utilized. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights how coordination failures can impede economic progress if institutions do not adapt to support technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes the importance of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby enabling sustained economic development. McCloskey (2015, p.3) notes that ideological shifts are essential for the uptake of new techniques and institutions, suggesting that the sustainability of technological progress relies on broader societal changes. While technological advancements drive growth, their impact is contingent upon complementary institutional and ideological developments.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They suggest that institutions are not merely efficiency-enhancing mechanisms but also tools for managing power dynamics among different social groups. In pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie argues that institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by the need to manage conflicts between landowners and laborers, in addition to purely economic considerations. This perspective challenges the view that institutions are primarily designed to enhance efficiency and productivity. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing how initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality, in turn, shaped institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating economic disparities. The persistence of these inequalities highlights the enduring impact of institutional design on long-run economic outcomes. Ogilvie cautions against oversimplifying the role of distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). While acknowledging their significance, she emphasizes the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and function within specific historical contexts. Ogilvie's call underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including cultural beliefs and political structures, when analyzing the institutional determinants of economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the varying mortality rates of Europeans across different colonial settings led to distinct institutional frameworks. In regions where European settlers faced high mortality, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in current income disparities among former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping long-run development trajectories.

The new economic history of Africa highlights that institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The centrality of institutions is underscored by their role in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This variability suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is mediated by other contextual factors. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, indicating that the specific nature of colonial institutions mattered significantly (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The implication is that the quality and type of institutions introduced by colonial powers had differential effects on economic outcomes.

Van Zanden's exploration of skill premiums highlights the importance of guilds as institutions that facilitated the transfer of skills across generations. These guilds also acted as local cartels, potentially restricting entry and maintaining higher wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dual role of institutions in fostering both positive and negative economic outcomes is a recurring theme. Pamuk illustrates this complexity through the structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to significant wage disparities across Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Pamuk's analysis suggests that institutional flexibility was crucial in enabling regions like Holland to rapidly adapt to new economic opportunities. This highlights how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic adaptation and growth. The interplay between skill development, institutional barriers, and economic outcomes underscores the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-term economic performance.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Simson (2019, p.2) argue that African economic development is shaped by complex interactions between historical factors and contemporary policies. They explore how colonial legacies have influenced economic outcomes in British Africa. The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic performance is evident in their analysis of real wage levels and trends across nine colonies during the period 1880-1965 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This period saw significant shifts in budget policies, with post-independence governments facing substantial debt burdens and increased military spending. Simson (2019, p.11) elaborates on this by detailing how Kenya's subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent expenditure. This pattern underscores the institutional challenges faced by African economies, where resources are often diverted from productive investments to consumption-based policies, reflecting patrimonial governance structures. The implications of these institutional dynamics are profound, as they highlight the need for comprehensive reforms that address both historical legacies and contemporary governance issues in order to foster sustainable economic growth.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and shape long-term economic growth. Austin's analysis reveals that colonial institutions often adapted in response to local economic and political actions, contrary to the notion that they were uniformly extractive (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when settlers were not present to compete for labour, demonstrating a more dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local economic interests. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings complement this perspective by showing that historical events like the slave trade have had enduring effects on social norms, including trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact of historical legacies suggests that contemporary economic issues are deeply rooted in past institutional frameworks, which continue to influence present-day economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of property rights and market exchanges. North argues that secure property rights are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who show that extractive institutions, which do not enforce property rights or encourage investment, hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical context of institutional development reveals that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms has been a key driver of growth. North's work on transaction costs highlights how efficient property rights and their enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the interactions between colonial policies and local economic interests. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is complemented by Austin's findings, which show that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, illustrating a more nuanced interaction between colonial rule and local economies (Austin 2008, p.15). These studies underscore the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

The enduring effects of institutions are also evident in the reversal of fortunes experienced by former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the relative income differences among these colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The shared finding is that historical legacies, particularly those related to institutional frameworks, continue to influence present-day economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Institutions matter because they create the framework within which economic activity occurs. North shows this by arguing that institutions are the rules of the game in a society or the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie demonstrates the role of distributional conflicts and shared beliefs in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These studies collectively show that institutions are active forces that shape economic outcomes over the long run.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this debate is the measurement of transaction costs. North shows that efficient property rights reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of extractive versus inclusive institutions provides a comparative framework for understanding how different institutional arrangements affect long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
