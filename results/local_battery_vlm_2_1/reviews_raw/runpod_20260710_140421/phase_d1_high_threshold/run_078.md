The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike, where 'institutions' refers to the rules and norms that govern social behavior. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of sustained economic development, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. Proponents of institutions argue that specific political and economic structures can foster growth by promoting secure property rights, encouraging investment, and ensuring rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Those who emphasize geography argue that natural endowments and climate significantly influence long-term economic outcomes. Another camp focuses on culture, positing that deeply ingrained social norms and values can either facilitate or hinder growth. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies in both emerging and developed economies. Understanding the causal mechanisms by which institutions shape economic growth can inform effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They argue that the type of institution established by colonists was determined by mortality rates and settler motives, which varied across regions. High mortality rates in tropical areas led to extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development. Lower mortality rates in temperate zones aided the establishment of institutions encouraging investment and protecting property rights. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic growth, as extractive institutions hindered long-term development by discouraging investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) further elaborate on this mechanism by showing how these institutions interacted with industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. Former colonies with extractive institutions struggled to industrialize, while those with inclusive institutions thrived. This interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities was pivotal in shaping long-run economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also emphasize that these institutional legacies persisted over time, influencing contemporary economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the differential impact of colonial institutions on their development paths. This persistence underscores the enduring effects of historical institutions on modern economic performance.

In the same vein as North (1989, p.9), they highlight how political factors underpinning economic growth are critical, including the regime’s commitment to rules enforcing property rights. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting economic development.

The creation of institutions like the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies this dynamic (North 1989, p.9). These financial innovations lowered transaction costs significantly, enhancing the security of long-term property rights. In turn, this increased security fostered a robust capital market and stimulated economic growth. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and enforce them effectively lead to falling transaction costs. This reduction in transaction costs is pivotal for economic growth as it allows for increased specialization and division of labor, multiplying the aggregate volume of exchanges.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) qualify this view by noting recent research that challenges traditional interpretations of British versus French economic history. They point out that if England and France had similar economic performance before the French Revolution, then institutions per se may not have been as revolutionary as previously thought. This nuanced perspective suggests that while secure property rights are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structures also play a role in economic growth.

The implication is that the interaction between institutional changes and broader socio-economic conditions shapes long-run economic trajectories.

However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both suggest that technological advancements rather than institutional frameworks are the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is rooted in the idea that the accumulation of useful knowledge spurs innovation, which in turn drives economic progress. Technological change requires adjustments in institutions, ideology, and social structures to be effectively harnessed for sustained growth. Kuznets highlights that technological progress often clashes with existing social norms and political systems, necessitating significant adaptations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting the importance of coordination failures and policies aimed at rectifying them (Mokyr 2005, p.54). These insights underscore that while institutions play a role in facilitating economic growth, it is the growth of useful knowledge that truly propels long-term economic development.

This perspective is echoed by Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who argue that historical factors such as colonial policies have significantly influenced African economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the complexity of institutional evolution, noting that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions. This adaptation was evident when Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations. The mechanisms through which these historical factors operate are multifaceted: Austin's analysis indicates that colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged subjects to have a material interest in the status quo, which shaped institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further elaborate on this by showing how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. The implications are profound: while historical factors such as colonial policies and the slave trade have left enduring marks on African societies, the specific institutional adaptations and cultural shifts they engendered continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Colonial policies and educational outcomes in Africa are examined without direct reference to how they shape institutions or contribute to long-run economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) jointly demonstrate that colonial rule, particularly British rule, significantly influenced the development of formal education in Africa. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, with African leaders and families weighing the advantages of formal education within the new institutional framework. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate that differentials in paths of development are central to understanding the varying growth records across societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The implications of these findings are significant: while colonial policies shaped educational outcomes, the institutional frameworks they introduced continue to influence contemporary African societies, albeit indirectly through their impact on education rather than directly on economic growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic activity and distribute resources. North's work highlights the critical importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between colonial policies and institutional frameworks shows how historical legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers introduced different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of extractive states in some colonies while fostering more favorable environments for investment and growth in others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. It is important to emphasize that institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Austin 2008, p.2). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or geographic factors. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6).
