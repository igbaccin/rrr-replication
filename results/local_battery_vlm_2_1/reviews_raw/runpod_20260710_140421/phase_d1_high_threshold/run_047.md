The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it addresses whether and how the rules that govern societies can drive prosperity over centuries. At stake is an understanding of why some countries thrive while others stagnate, and to what extent this disparity is rooted in institutional frameworks rather than purely geographic or cultural factors. Institutions are widely recognized for their impact on economic outcomes, but there is a live disagreement about how institutions shape growth. Some scholars emphasize the importance of extractive versus inclusive institutions; others highlight the role of transaction costs and property rights. Still, other scholars question whether the institutional approach can fully explain long-run economic growth without considering distributional conflicts or the influence of cultural beliefs. Understanding these debates clarifies how the institutional landscape influences economic development over time.

A key theme in this debate is the distinction between extractive and inclusive institutions, which fundamentally shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, leading to divergent economic trajectories among colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores how initial institutional setups can have lasting effects on a nation's economic development. North also emphasizes the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to significant economic advancements by enhancing security and efficiency.

The role of institutions is further complicated by their interaction with other factors such as population density and prosperity. Acemoglu et al. argue that these factors influenced European policies in colonies, which in turn shaped long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction indicates that institutions are active components within broader socio-economic conditions. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting how different historical periods and paths complicate causal relationships between institutions and economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscates the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional effects, considering multiple historical contexts rather than simplistic causal narratives.

Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective shifts focus from efficiency to power dynamics within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Such an approach suggests that the enduring impact of institutions on growth may be rooted in historical struggles over resources and rights, which shape institutional frameworks over time. Hopkins supports this view by emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, a crucial driver of development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores how effective institutions can foster economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and innovation.

Another critical aspect is the role of beliefs and cultural factors in shaping institutions. Ogilvie highlights that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure and can influence economic outcomes, suggesting a deeper layer of social dynamics at play (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interaction between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks adds complexity to the understanding of how institutions drive economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). This institutional development is evident in the creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the introduction of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. These changes reflect an increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework not only facilitated market exchanges but also ensured that the rules governing these exchanges were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, where secure property rights fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The development of financial instruments and the reduction in transaction costs are key mechanisms through which institutions drive long-run economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics in driving economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies by introducing different institutional frameworks that either fostered development or hindered it (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established diverse colonial institutions based on mortality rates, leading to the creation of extractive states in high-mortality areas and more inclusive institutions where Europeans settled permanently. These institutional differences persisted over time and had profound effects on economic outcomes. In locations such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that upheld the rule of law and encouraged investment, thereby fostering economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the historical context of former colonies, where European colonists designed institutions to benefit themselves at the expense of local economic development. The institutional frameworks established during colonialism thus had lasting impacts on the economic trajectories of these regions.

Acemoglu et al. document how the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was linked to industrialization opportunities, highlighting that institutional changes during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping long-term development outcomes. Therefore, understanding these historical institutional frameworks is crucial for comprehending contemporary economic disparities.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, highlighting how Africa's dismal economic record is linked to poor institutions. The role of these institutions extends beyond mere governance; they are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the importance of understanding colonial institutions' role in fostering economic growth, as these institutions influenced local endowments, transport costs, and political structures (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the type of colonial institution mattered significantly in shaping educational outcomes, which in turn influenced economic development (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The interplay between these factors highlights how exogenous influences like colonial policies can interact with local initiatives to shape institutional frameworks and thereby influence long-term economic growth.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to power dynamics within societies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate on this dynamic, showing how colonial societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective highlights a key mechanism: distributional conflicts led to institutional arrangements that reinforced elite power and maintained existing social hierarchies. The long-term effects of these institutions differed across various contexts. For example, in some cases, these institutions may have hindered economic growth by stifling innovation and competition, while in others, they might have facilitated certain forms of development by providing stability and predictability. The qualitative thrust of this approach is not entirely novel as it builds on traditional economic factors (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, challenging the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The slave trades' impact on African economic development was profound. These trades disrupted social structures and depleted human capital, leading to long-term adverse effects on productivity and economic growth. Austin argues that colonial governments' fiscal security needs led them to change institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic action, often encouraging commodity production when European settlers were absent. Thus, the effects of slave trades on African economies are likely more complex than simply hindering growth through institution disruption (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that poverty in Africa was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments but rather a result of specific historical conditions. This suggests that while the slave trades had significant negative impacts, their effects were not insurmountable barriers to future economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Mokyr argues that the accumulation of knowledge and its effective dissemination is crucial for sustained economic growth, particularly through the reduction of access costs to new ideas. This process includes both the creation of new knowledge and the development of institutions that support its dissemination. Kuznets highlights that technological progress requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations, noting that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural modes of life and large family structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts without weakening political stability. Mokyr extends this by recognizing coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them, emphasizing the need for institutional progress to complement technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further elaborates that without such institutional support, economic improvements could be limited or even reversed by rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where institutions must adapt to harness the full potential of new knowledge.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and effectiveness of resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions can persist over time, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin's findings add to this by emphasizing the importance of institutional evolution, noting that European settlers often established different types of institutions based on their interests in the colonies (Austin 2008, p.2). These institutions, whether extractive or growth-oriented, reflect the political and economic strategies of the colonizers. For example, in regions where European settlers were present to compete for labor, colonial administrations adopted policies that encouraged indigenous commodity production, fostering economic development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The dynamic interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the need for institutions to adapt to harness new knowledge effectively. North argues that secure property rights are fundamental to long-term economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). Ogilvie supports this view by highlighting how shared beliefs within a society can shape its institutional structure, influencing economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These insights suggest that institutions must evolve to support technological advancements and ensure sustainable economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic development is further illustrated by the impact of colonial policies on former colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how extractive institutions designed by European colonists maximized rents rather than promoting long-run growth, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins builds on this by showing that poor institutional quality is linked to Africa's dismal economic record, emphasizing the need for good institutions to provide incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Institutions matter because they determine the rules of the game in a society. North shows how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.6). Austin supports this view by noting that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15), illustrating how institutions can adapt to changing circumstances. These findings highlight the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering long-term economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions are central to economic development, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement centers on whether extractive institutions can be reformed or replaced by more growth-oriented ones. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth, suggesting that institutional change is necessary for sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin's research shows that colonial administrations could modify their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the possibility of institutional reform within existing systems (Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in their long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence highlights the importance of adaptive institutions that can support technological progress and foster economic growth over the long term.
