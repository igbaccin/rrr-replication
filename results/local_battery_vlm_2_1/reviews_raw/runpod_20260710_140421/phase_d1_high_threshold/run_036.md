The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The stakes are high because the answer to this question informs policy decisions about institution-building and economic development. One live disagreement revolves around whether institutions themselves drive economic growth or if they are merely a byproduct of other factors, such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Others contend that the impact of institutions on economic development is overstated and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs, play a more significant role in shaping institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The debate also involves the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Some researchers propose that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Others suggest that the type of institutions established, whether extractive or inclusive, determines their impact on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2).

The extent to which colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies is another point of contention. Some studies demonstrate a direct link between colonial institutions and current income levels, emphasizing the enduring effects of historical institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Others maintain that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical processes and does not fully account for the varied paths of economic development in former colonies (Austin 2008, p.15).

The relationship between population density, prosperity, and colonial policies adds another layer to this debate. Some scholars assert that these factors influenced the institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, which in turn affected long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights the interaction between institutions and other contextual factors in shaping economic development.

A key theme emerging from this literature is the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance. This suggests that the initial conditions and policies set by colonial powers have shaped long-run economic growth in profound ways, influencing current income levels and development paths.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonization, either extractive or inclusive, played a pivotal role in determining the economic trajectories of these regions. These institutions were not merely products of geographical factors but were largely shaped by European policies designed to maximize colonial rents (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The establishment of extractive institutions, which focused on resource extraction and rapid transfer to the metropole, hindered long-run economic growth in colonies with high mortality rates. This historical institutional framework persists today, affecting current income levels and development paths. The legacy of these colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

Institutional reforms are pivotal for African economic development, as they address systemic issues that hinder growth and poverty reduction (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can provide insights into institutional reforms. This is because it informs how to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa; this insight also resonates with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) argument about understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including income distribution. Both scholars agree that historical analysis can guide current policy. The role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Moreover, the new economic history of Africa has gained attention from development specialists because it highlights the importance of "good" institutions in fostering investment and growth. This includes understanding how specific historical or geographical conditions have affected African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) further notes that while some societies may be ethnically homogeneous and still dysfunctional, others can be heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This insight is crucial for understanding how institutional reforms can overcome ethnic fragmentation and foster economic growth in Africa.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) assert that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. Their success is evident in capital markets, where political factors underpinning economic growth include a regime's commitment to rules and enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective on institutional development and economic growth is qualified by the observation that institutions alone did not revolutionize economic performance; they worked in conjunction with other factors. This suggests that while financial institutions and secure property rights are critical, their impact on long-run economic growth is contingent upon broader economic and political contexts.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These scholars show that the persistence of institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communities can be better understood through the lens of power dynamics and elite interests. For instance, Ogilvie highlights how the "shared beliefs" about economic organization often reflect underlying conflicts over resource distribution and political control. Sokoloff and Engerman expand on this by demonstrating that inequalities in wealth and human capital create institutional environments that favor elites at the expense of broader societal growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is exemplified by the persistence of serfdom, which was maintained to preserve elite power structures. Ogilvie cautions against overgeneralizing this mechanism. She notes that while distributional conflicts are significant, they must be evaluated in conjunction with other contextual factors such as cultural beliefs and historical contingencies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions when analyzing how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both highlight that technological progress and institutional adjustments are key to economic growth. They argue that technological change requires complementary institutional frameworks for efficient use, which in turn sustain long-term growth. Kuznets suggests that modern technology necessitates significant societal changes including shifts away from rural lifestyles and large family structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr's analysis of the role of institutions echoes this perspective, emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements could be limited by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Kuznets also points out that these adjustments must occur without excessive costs or weakening of political structures, underscoring the delicate balance required for sustainable growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis together with Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) challenges the timing and causation of the great divergence. Austin argues that this narrative oversimplifies the complex interplay of institutions, investment, and policy experiences across different historical periods, a pattern he finds evident in both European industrialization and its overseas 'off-shoots' (Austin 2008, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further emphasize that the divergence was already underway before 1800. This early start to divergence, as Broadberry and Gupta show, is tied closely to merchant distribution, finance, and the role of the service sector in economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The mechanism by which these factors shaped long-term economic outcomes was through increased productivity in traded goods sectors, leading to uneven distribution of development benefits across different groups. This narrative underscores how early institutional changes influenced investment patterns, ultimately driving high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) qualify this mechanism by showing that export production varied significantly between and within countries due to factors like local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation highlights the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories over time.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing investment patterns and productivity (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is critical for fostering economic development. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework not only secured property rights but also enhanced the efficiency of market exchanges, which are essential for sustained economic progress.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is profound. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varied institutional frameworks in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The persistence of these colonial legacies underscores the enduring influence of early institutional changes on long-term economic development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is also significant. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions were shaped by the power dynamics and distributional struggles within societies.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes introduced during colonialism. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutional changes, rather than geographic factors, were crucial in determining long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over extended periods.

Austin challenges the notion that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical paths. He argues that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood within the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional influences on economic development.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can eliminate poverty. Hopkins shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This emphasizes the role of institutions in driving long-term economic growth and reducing poverty.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement comes from Acemoglu et al. who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). This empirical comparison demonstrates how institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth.
