The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses how societal structures influence prosperity over generations. Institutions are understood to be the rules and constraints that shape human interaction (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). At stake is whether institutions facilitate economic growth by providing incentives for investment or if they simply reflect underlying socio-economic factors. The live disagreement centres around the causal mechanism: do institutions cause long-run economic growth, or do other factors drive both institutional development and economic outcomes? Some scholars argue that institutions are pivotal in shaping economic growth through their impact on property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North 1989, p.6). Others contend that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This thesis suggests that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional legacies of colonialism varied based on mortality rates among European colonizers and their resulting strategies. This approach was detrimental to long-run economic growth because it prioritized immediate resource extraction over sustainable development. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita in these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century was central to the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights how institutional legacies can either facilitate or hinder economic growth over extended periods, underscoring the critical role of colonial institutions in shaping contemporary economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) demonstrate that institutions governing public choice in 17th century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They establish the role of credible commitment by the sovereign to enforce institutional rules as a critical factor underlying economic development. This institutional framework facilitated economic growth by enabling long-term capital markets. North and Weingast identify that the success of these institutions was remarkable as evidenced by capital markets, highlighting how political factors underpinning economic growth are crucial for market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). In this context, the evolution of secure property rights enabled by political institutions fosters economic growth through reduced transaction costs and enhanced market efficiency.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2) examine how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality contributed to the persistence of inequality. The authors argue that factor endowments shaped institutional evolution, leading to divergent paths of development across societies. These institutional frameworks restricted competition and hindered market development by concentrating power and resources among a small elite group.

The impact of these institutional structures is further illustrated when considering Africa's slave trades. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the adverse effects of these trades on economic development were profound, with long-lasting consequences (Nunn 2008, p.19). The presence or absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets significantly influenced a region’s capacity to engage in trade and foster growth. This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks shaped by initial inequalities can perpetuate underdevelopment over centuries. The persistence of inequality in societies with extractive institutions is a consequence of both historical circumstances and institutional design that reinforces power imbalances.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific changes aimed at improving governance and economic conditions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the significance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment. These institutions are crucial because they shape the quality of public goods provided by African governments, which is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but rather by the capacity to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) suggest that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa can offer insights into future growth possibilities. The authors' reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule highlights that structural impediments have not been persistent historical phenomena, contrary to recent literature emphasizing such impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28)(Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic development trajectories. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, suggesting a broader recognition of the need for institutional reforms (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This recognition is pivotal because it sets the stage for policy recommendations that incorporate governance and organizational structures, which are essential for fostering long-term economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa. This mistrust is rooted in the historical legacy of intergroup conflict and exploitation during the transatlantic slave trade, which has left lasting imprints on social norms and interpersonal relations (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This legacy has been reinforced by patriarchal alliances that codified women's subordinate status through customary law under colonial rule. As Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) explain, this codification process relegated women to marginal lands for food farming, further entrenching gender inequalities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This persistent mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. The historical legacy of the slave trade continues to shape contemporary African societies, influencing trust levels and economic outcomes.

This perspective is critical for understanding why certain institutions were adopted while others were not. Ogilvie's analysis underscores how serfdom, community structures, craft guilds and merchant guilds can be explained through distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). In this view, economic institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain or enhance their wealth and status. For instance, the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe can be attributed to landowners' efforts to secure a steady supply of labor, while craft guilds in Western Europe served to restrict competition and maintain high prices for skilled labor. Austin (2008, p.2) complements this by showing that colonial institutions were also shaped by distributional conflicts. In some African colonies, indigenous commodity production was encouraged when it aligned with the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutional prescriptions can change in response to local economic and political actions. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional outcomes thus reveals a complex landscape where power dynamics significantly influence long-run economic growth.

Mokyr's work (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1) highlights that technological advancements are the primary driver of economic growth, with the accumulation of useful knowledge being critical to this process. Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize that institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.6). The interplay between technological progress and institutions is essential: while technological advancements drive growth, institutions facilitate the adoption and diffusion of these technologies by resolving conflicts arising from economic change. This dynamic underscores how technological progress requires complementary institutional changes to ensure sustained economic development.

The sustainability of economic improvement depends on both technological progress and institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr notes that without institutional advancements to support technological innovations, economic growth can be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. This highlights a crucial condition: while technological progress is essential for long-run economic growth, its benefits are contingent upon supportive institutions that manage the social and political challenges associated with economic change. The need for institutions to adapt to technological advancements underscores their role in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment that supports technological progress and investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development, depending on their design and enforcement. North's work underscores this point by emphasizing that secure property rights are crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(North 1989, p.9) demonstrates that institutions matter because they provide the framework within which economic activities occur, influencing everything from investment decisions to technological advancements.

The role of institutions extends beyond merely facilitating economic transactions; they also manage social and political challenges associated with economic change. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions served to maintain the social order and distribute resources among powerful groups. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)(Ogilvie 2007, p.12) shows that institutions are not just about economic efficiency; they also reflect and reinforce power dynamics within society.

The historical evidence from colonial experiences further illustrates how institutions shape long-run economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) shows that poor institutions are linked to Africa's dismal economic record, emphasizing the need for institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2)(Hopkins 2009, p.8) underscores how institutions can either support or hinder long-run economic growth. This is particularly evident in the case of Africa, where colonial institutions have had lasting effects on economic development.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions are central to shaping long-term economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The precise remaining disagreement lies in how extractive institutions can persist and affect economic outcomes over time. This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of former European colonies, where Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The measurement of income per capita across these colonies provides clear evidence of how extractive institutions can undermine long-run economic growth.
