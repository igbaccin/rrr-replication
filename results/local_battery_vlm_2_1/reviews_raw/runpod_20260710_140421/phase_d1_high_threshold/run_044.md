The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, as it directly addresses how societies can achieve sustained prosperity. At stake intellectually is the understanding of the fundamental drivers of economic development, which has significant implications for policy-making and historical analysis. A live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more decisive role. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established in a society, whether extractive or inclusive, determines its long-term economic trajectory, while others contend that institutional changes are merely one piece of a complex puzzle involving multiple interacting variables.

Some arguments focus on the impact of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19)  argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions in colonies, with some being extractive and others encouraging investment. These institutional differences significantly influenced current income per capita levels. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) highlight how colonial policies shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, emphasizing that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities was crucial for long-run development. These perspectives underscore the enduring effects of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.

Some scholars propose rival causes to explain long-term growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts, not efficiency concerns, are key in shaping core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily designed for economic efficiency. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating that institutional evolution is more nuanced than often presented. This stream emphasizes that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of factors beyond simple efficiency considerations.

The role of transaction costs in economic growth is another critical area. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the creation of political and economic institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can lead to a decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating long-term economic growth through increased market exchanges. This perspective highlights how institutional frameworks can reduce barriers to economic activity by ensuring secure property rights and lowering transaction costs.

These discussions collectively underscore the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth. The interplay between historical institutions, distributional conflicts, transaction costs, and colonial policies shapes long-run economic trajectories in profound ways. Understanding these dynamics is essential for formulating effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development.

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective highlights the critical role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes. These institutional changes were driven by fiscal crises that necessitated a trade-off between certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. They argue that a sovereign's commitment to these rules is crucial for economic growth and the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Recent research has challenged the notion that institutional changes in England were uniquely revolutionary by highlighting France's comparable economic performance before the French Revolution.

The growth of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress, through its role in advancing human knowledge, facilitates sustained economic growth over long periods. This process necessitates institutional adjustments to efficiently integrate innovations into existing economies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights the need for these institutions to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes and promote a national state that can manage these transitions peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This dynamic is crucial because without such complementary institutions, the benefits of technological progress could be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) extends this idea by emphasizing that these conflicts can only be resolved if they do not excessively weaken society's political fabric; a strong sovereign state is pivotal in achieving this balance. This interplay between technological progress, institutional adjustments, and the role of the sovereign state underpins long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts were the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. These distributional conflicts shaped institutions that reinforced existing inequalities. For instance, in New World societies with high initial inequality, elite groups secured policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance. This dynamic is evident in the evolution of institutions that favored elites, such as those governing land distribution and labor markets (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective highlights that institutional outcomes are often the result of complex interactions between power dynamics and economic rationality.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) shared finding that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita is evident in the divergent strategies employed by European colonizers. These strategies varied based on factors such as mortality rates and settler motivations, leading to distinct institutional outcomes. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, often stifled long-run economic growth by discouraging investment and enforcing regulations that benefited the colonizers at the expense of local development. This institutional framework evolved in response to political dynamics and power struggles within the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions long after the colonial period highlights their enduring impact on economic structures, influencing contemporary income levels and economic performance. While Acemoglu et al.'s work underscores the role of initial conditions in shaping institutional trajectories, it also acknowledges that these institutions can evolve over time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evolution is influenced by both internal political dynamics and external economic pressures, suggesting that while colonial institutions laid a foundational framework, subsequent developments could either reinforce or mitigate their impact.

The literature on colonial rule in Africa highlights various impacts such as educational development and governance issues but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, underscoring that African societies can be ethnically heterogeneous yet still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective is crucial because it suggests that the quality of public goods provision is determined by the effectiveness of governance structures (Hopkins 2009, p.18). By the 1990s, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the development policy scope widened to incorporate governance, emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment. This shift highlights how institutional quality became a critical factor in economic development strategies (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The implication is that while colonial rule had varied impacts on African economies, the subsequent focus on governance and institutions suggests that these factors are pivotal in driving long-run economic growth, even if the initial conditions set by colonial powers were not uniformly beneficial.

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women's economic contributions are often overlooked in discussions of long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn further supports this view by emphasizing the significant impact of slave trades on Africa's economic development. The literature demonstrates a clear pattern: both women’s roles and the impacts of slave trades were shaped by institutional frameworks, including customary laws and colonial legal systems. For instance, colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through the codification of customary law, which marginalized women's access to land (Nunn 2008, p.2). This institutional framework limited women's economic contributions, as they were often relegated to less productive agricultural activities. Nunn’s work illustrates how centralized governance and well-developed markets, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, facilitated trade and economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). Societies lacking these institutions struggled to engage in sustained trade. This suggests that while African women played a crucial role in economic development, their contributions were constrained by institutional factors that also influenced the broader economic landscape.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Simson 2019, p.2) reassess economic performance in African economies by focusing on historical national accounting and post-independence budget policies. This approach challenges the prevailing narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services, offering a more nuanced view of Africa's economic trajectory.

This finding underscores the need to reassess the region’s economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner also emphasize the importance of foreign trade and export markets in shaping growth patterns, noting that the timing and scale of growth varied significantly across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Simson (2019, p.11) builds on this by showing how post-independence budget policies were shaped by regional instability and domestic dissent. For instance, military spending increased in response to these challenges, indicating that economic decisions were influenced by broader geopolitical factors.

Simson also notes the shift in expenditure patterns towards subsidies and interest payments, particularly in Kenya and Tanzania (Simson 2019, p.11). This highlights how budgetary decisions were driven by complex economic and political pressures. These findings suggest that while post-independence policies faced significant challenges, they were not uniformly unproductive or rent-seeking, as the narrative of bloated payrolls would imply. These insights into the complexity of African economies underscore the importance of historical national accounting in understanding long-term economic growth patterns.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, underscoring the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving early modern economic growth. They argue that high productivity in traded goods sectors arose through efficient distribution networks and financial systems, which played a crucial role in fostering economic development. This perspective is shared by van Zanden (1999, p.1), who emphasizes the varying patterns of urbanization and living standards across Europe during this period. Van Zanden's analysis reveals that economic development did not uniformly translate into improved living standards, suggesting complex interactions between economic growth and social welfare. Broadberry and Gupta further note that institutional changes facilitated investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Van Zanden (1999, p.9) qualifies this by pointing out that the shift in economic development towards North Sea countries like Holland and England was accompanied by significant price level changes. This implies that while institutional factors were important, they interacted with broader economic trends such as shifts in grain prices and trade dynamics. These insights suggest that understanding long-run economic growth requires considering not only institutions but also the intricate interplay between financial systems, urbanization patterns, and market dynamics.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. They highlight that understanding historical economic paths requires a deeper analysis beyond structural impediments. Colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic actions, which shaped their institutional prescriptions. This dynamic is evident in cases where European settlers were absent, and colonial governments encouraged commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective does not fully account for the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-term economic growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Peters (2004, p.19) both document how institutional frameworks shaped long-term economic outcomes in response to structural changes such as the Black Death. This institutional flexibility allowed for the rapid emergence of new towns and rural manufacturing, which was not seen in regions like Tuscany where existing monopolies were strengthened (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Peters 2004, p.19). Peters shows how perceptions of static 'custom' can create advantages for certain groups seeking to control landholding practices. These patterns suggest that the ability of societies to adapt their institutions in response to structural changes significantly influenced long-term economic trajectories, particularly urbanisation rates and wage gaps (Peters 2004, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.16). Peters (2004, p.19) also highlights that these adaptations were not always equitable. The push for agricultural development during the final decade of colonial rule often led to dissatisfaction among groups seeking to modernise farming practices (Peters 2004, p.5). This suggests that while institutional flexibility can drive economic growth, it may also exacerbate inequalities if not managed inclusively.

The skill premium is a critical indicator of long-term economic performance, reflecting the value of human capital and labor market dynamics van Zanden (2009, p.19) (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This premium emerges from the costs associated with training skilled workers, including foregone earnings during apprenticeship periods. When these costs are high, the skill premium tends to be higher, as it compensates for the significant investment in human capital. Van Zanden (2009, p.3) elaborates that this cost structure includes direct expenses as well as institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades. Institutional factors like guilds can either facilitate or hinder the transfer of skills by regulating access and ensuring fair execution of apprentice contracts. These institutions have been criticized for potentially monopolizing skills and restricting market competition, which could artificially inflate wages (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dynamic suggests that while strong institutions can support human capital development and long-term economic performance, they must also be balanced to avoid creating barriers that stifle growth. The skill premium's role as a predictor of long-term economic performance underscores the need for policies that enhance human capital investment without over-regulating labor markets, ensuring sustainable economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa created a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; McCloskey 2015, p.3). The authors show that this mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals' norms. This internalized mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it affects trust levels which are crucial for various aspects of economic activity (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; McCloskey 2015, p.3). McCloskey's work on ideologies and economic change further highlights the role of cultural factors in shaping economic outcomes. She argues that changes in ideological conditions allowed for greater experimentation and open dialogue, which were essential for economic development. While Nunn and Wantchekon focus on the negative impact of mistrust, McCloskey illustrates that positive ideological changes can drive economic growth. The interplay between these cultural factors, both negative and positive, suggests that addressing mistrust through ideological shifts could be a key strategy for enhancing long-term economic performance in regions affected by historical traumas such as the slave trade.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident from various historical and comparative analyses. Acemoglu et al. illustrate that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of extractive states versus inclusive ones shaped economic outcomes, demonstrating that institutional frameworks are pivotal for long-term economic development. This perspective aligns with North's argument on the importance of property rights and enforcement mechanisms in facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

North highlights how institutions like the Bank of England drove down transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth by ensuring secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security is crucial as it allows for long-term investment and economic stability. North's emphasis on institutions as a determinant of economic performance complements Acemoglu et al.'s findings, showing that inclusive institutions are essential for sustainable economic development.

The importance of institutional design is further underscored by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that this thesis oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative. This nuanced view suggests that the effectiveness of institutions varies across contexts, and their impact on economic growth must be evaluated within specific historical frameworks.

Moreover, Hopkins emphasizes the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa highlights how institutional reforms can inform policy changes that help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This underscores the practical implications of understanding institutional design and its impact on long-run economic growth.

Institutional frameworks also play a critical role in addressing historical traumas, as shown by Ogilvie's analysis (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The persistence of certain institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic progress, depending on their alignment with broader societal goals. This perspective complements the findings from Acemoglu et al. showing that positive ideological changes and inclusive institutions are key to overcoming historical traumas such as the slave trade.

The precise remaining disagreement between these perspectives is whether distributional conflicts or efficiency drive core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The shared finding is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth. The disagreement is resolved by empirical comparisons of institutional reforms and their impact on economic outcomes, as shown by Hopkins' analysis of African countries (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
