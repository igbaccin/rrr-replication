The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and political science. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules that structure human interaction, can determine a society's economic trajectory over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural traits, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions create stable environments that encourage investment and innovation, while others contend that institutions themselves are shaped by exogenous factors like climate or colonial history.

The debate is further complicated by the question of whether institutions operate through specific mechanisms to promote growth or if they simply reflect deeper historical or cultural patterns shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This review synthesizes key arguments from multiple streams of scholarship, focusing on how different perspectives illuminate the causal role of institutions in economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are critical for long-run economic growth. The formation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments exemplifies how institutional changes can lower transaction costs, fostering economic growth by enhancing market exchanges through secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that colonization strategies led to different institutions in colonies, with some promoting investment while others were extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Institutional frameworks can significantly affect economic outcomes. For instance, the establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and encouraging investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional framework is pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to sustained development. Acemoglu et al (2002) warn that institutions may hinder growth; they might be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and not foster long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the motivations behind institutional design and their implications for growth. Institutions can both drive and constrain economic progress depending on their structure and enforcement.

This institutional transformation was pivotal in setting the stage for subsequent economic developments, as evidenced by the secure property rights and economic growth fostered during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the commitment to rules by the regime underpinned economic growth, fostering an environment where markets could develop. This institutional foundation ensured that political factors, such as credible commitments, were integral to long-term economic success. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) observe that in New World societies, institutions often mirrored existing inequalities. These institutions favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic underscores the critical role of initial conditions and institutional design in shaping economic trajectories. The persistence of inequality in these societies suggests that while institutions can drive growth, their impact is contingent on how they are structured to address or exacerbate existing social disparities.

Technological advancements are pivotal in driving long-run economic growth; Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share this posture by highlighting the role of useful knowledge. Economic growth is fundamentally propelled by technological progress. This process is complex; it requires institutional adjustments to facilitate the efficient use of innovations. The relationship between technology and institutions is dynamic, with technological advancements necessitating corresponding changes in societal structures and ideologies. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows that epochal innovations, those that drive sustained growth over long periods, demand significant institutional and ideological adjustments to integrate into the economy effectively. This interplay can cause conflicts within societies as traditional modes of life and family patterns may clash with modern technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.6) adds that modern economic growth is possible if these conflicts are resolved peacefully. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates on this by recognizing the possibility of coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them. Without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. This highlights the importance of technological progress as well as the role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining long-run economic growth.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) provide an example of how detrimental institutional legacies can impede economic progress. This impact extends through historical periods, shaping long-term economic trajectories. One of the key mechanisms through which the slave trade affected economic development was by fostering a culture of mistrust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that this mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, thereby significantly influencing intergroup relationships and social cohesion. This cultural shift is crucial because it hinders the development of strong institutions necessary for economic growth, such as those that support rule of law and market efficiency.

For instance, the centralised government and well-developed markets in regions like the Kongo kingdom facilitated trade with Europeans. The slave trade often undermined these structures, leading to fragmented societies less capable of supporting sustained economic development. This institutional weakness persists even after accounting for other factors such as colonial rule and more recent events. Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates on this by highlighting how the lack of differentiated social and economic institutions hindered Africa's ability to engage in foreign trade, thereby limiting its economic potential.

Simson (2019, p.11) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this by examining specific aspects of African economies such as per capita income levels and post-independence budget policies. They highlight that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting a more nuanced economic landscape than previously thought. This perspective is echoed in Simson's (2019, p.11) work, which examines East Africa's post-independence budget policies and finds that these were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly believed. This focus on specific economic indicators and budgetary shifts provides valuable insights into the economic performance of African countries without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, leaving room for further exploration of institutional factors that might have influenced these economic outcomes. Simson (2019, p.11) also notes that Kenya experienced high subsidies and interest payments during this period, which displaced both capital and recurrent spending. This budgetary shift highlights the complex interplay between economic policies and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these dynamics could offer critical insights into Africa's economic development trajectory.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Austin (2008, p.2) both show that colonial rule in Africa was an intricate process of negotiation and imposition, leading to institutional changes driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors. This perspective is shared by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2). The dynamics of colonial rule varied significantly across different regions; where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to adjust their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation highlights how local initiatives could influence colonial policies, challenging a monolithic view of colonial rule. In some instances, colonial administrators opted for higher production equilibriums rather than lower ones, contrary to hypotheses that predict otherwise. The new economic history of Africa thus reveals that institutions were not solely imposed but also negotiated, with local dynamics significantly influencing their evolution. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that understanding this historical context is essential for informing contemporary institutional reforms and addressing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-established, with credible commitment to property rights standing as a key factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, leading to varied economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists and not promote long-term growth, highlight the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Austin argues that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions and political dynamics. This indicates that local agency played a crucial role in shaping institutional outcomes during the colonial period, contra views that institutions were solely imposed from above (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's analysis reveals that understanding this historical context is essential for contemporary institutional reforms. Hopkins builds on this by showing that the new economic history of Africa can inform these reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (North 1989, p.6). North emphasizes that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are critical for long-term economic growth. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The new economic history of Africa thus reveals that institutions were not solely imposed but also negotiated, with local dynamics significantly influencing their evolution. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This historical evidence shows that colonial institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists and not promote long-term growth had a lasting impact on economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
