The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is an understanding of how societies can foster sustained prosperity. There are two main positions on the topic: one that asserts institutions' centrality and another that highlights rival causal factors. Proponents of institutional primacy argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce contracts, directly influences economic outcomes by incentivizing investment and innovation (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18). In contrast, other scholars emphasize geographic factors, cultural traits, or exogenous shocks as primary drivers. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy, shaping how governments and international organizations approach development strategies. The question of whether institutions are the linchpin of economic growth remains unresolved, with ongoing research continually refining our understanding of this complex relationship.

The first stream addresses the direct role of institutions in fostering economic growth by examining historical case studies and theoretical frameworks that underscore their importance. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19) . These differences, in turn, significantly influenced current income levels. The historical evidence from Europe also shows that the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This stream highlights how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic progress, depending on their design and enforcement.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) assert that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Europeans adopted varied colonization strategies, leading to divergent institutional outcomes. In settler colonies like the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment. In regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences persisted over time, shaping long-run economic trajectories. This extractive approach led to significant disparities in economic development between colonies with different institutional frameworks, illustrating the profound impact of colonial institutions on subsequent economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies further underscores this influence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that these extractive institutions were equilibrium responses to political and economic conditions. This nuanced understanding highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies, institutional design, and long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) highlight that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs. This was achieved through the development of new financial instruments, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. The political context of these developments is crucial: the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). This historical backdrop highlights the significance of institutional changes that were both economic and deeply political. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that these institutions governed how rules are enforced and changed, reflecting a credible commitment to property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, this perspective is qualified by the evidence from capital markets, which suggests that institutional changes alone were not revolutionary; instead they must be understood in relation to broader economic conditions. This nuanced view highlights the intricate relationship between financial institutions, transaction costs, and long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. This perspective is echoed by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who notes that epochal innovations have been dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These technological advancements are not isolated events; they require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that the pace at which technology and economic growth advance differs across centuries and regions, indicating a complex interplay between technological change and societal adaptations (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The role of institutions in this context is to facilitate the absorption and diffusion of new technologies. While Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges the necessity of institutional progress to complement technological advancements, he also points out that this process is not linear or irreversible (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This qualification suggests that while institutions are crucial for sustaining economic growth driven by technology, their effectiveness can be limited by various factors, including coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors. Therefore, the relationship between technological progress and institutional frameworks is dynamic and multifaceted, with each influencing the other in a continuous feedback loop.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. This perspective is supported by Frankema (2012, p.15), who notes that British rule fostered formal education, altering perceived benefits and cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders on the macro level (Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) also recognizes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor public goods provision or institutional quality. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further emphasize the complexity of this relationship by showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies these interactions by noting that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This underscores the importance of understanding how colonial legacies and local initiatives can both support and constrain institutional effectiveness in Africa.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that in New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that favored elites. This pattern is evident in the policies that restricted competition and maintained elite dominance over time. The extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped the institutional environments of these colonies. This initial inequality was driven by differences in factor endowments, with land and natural resources being abundant relative to labor. In some regions, this abundance led to high living standards on average but also to extreme disparities among the population. These inequalities were reinforced by institutions that protected elite interests, such as those governing property rights, financial structures, and public infrastructure investment. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) demonstrate that this pattern of inequality was not unique to the New World; it also characterized colonial Africa, where institutional frameworks similarly reflected and reinforced power dynamics. This dynamic highlights a key insight: institutions in colonized societies often evolved to preserve existing inequalities rather than promote broad-based economic growth. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by initial conditions of inequality, had lasting effects on development trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

The slave trade's impact on Africa was profound and persistent, shaping institutions that continue to influence economic development today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade had a lasting effect by fostering a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust has been identified as a critical factor in Africa's reduced trust levels, which in turn hinders economic development. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates is multifaceted, involving both internal norms and external environmental factors. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the internal channel of mistrust, shaped by historical experiences of exploitation, is at least twice as large as the external channel influenced by the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Austin (2008, p.15) elaborates on this dynamic, noting how colonial administrations often tailored their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation suggests that while mistrust is a significant barrier, it is not insurmountable; institutions can evolve in ways that mitigate some of its effects. However, the persistence of mistrust underscores the deep-seated nature of the problem, which requires comprehensive approaches to address both internal norms and external conditions. The historical legacy of the slave trade continues to influence economic outcomes by shaping institutional frameworks that perpetuate mistrust and impede development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by defining the rules that govern economic interactions and property rights. This is demonstrated by North's argument that institutions reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. extend this idea by showing how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional frameworks that significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). European settlers established extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, while in low-mortality areas they set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional design highlights how historical contexts shaped long-term economic outcomes.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.2). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and provided the necessary incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The historical legacy of the slave trade continues to influence economic outcomes by shaping institutional frameworks that perpetuate mistrust and impede development. As Hopkins points out, the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic conditions.

The role of mistrust in shaping institutions is a shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of mistrust highlights the deep-seated nature of the problem, which requires comprehensive approaches to address both internal norms and external conditions. This is evident in Austin's analysis of how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2). The remaining disagreement is about whether the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are due to geographic factors or institutional ones. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions matter for long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This disagreement can be settled with a measurement type used in both papers: the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, which was related to industrialization and provides evidence of how institutional changes played a central role.
