The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and sustain prosperity over extended periods. A central debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are crucial for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). Some scholars suggest that institutions' impacts depend on historical contexts and paths, indicating that institutional change results from complex interactions between various factors (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate underscores the need to examine how different types of institutions influence economic growth in diverse settings. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are universally beneficial for economic growth or if their effects vary based on specific historical and contextual factors, with some scholars emphasizing the importance of institutional quality over other determinants such as geography (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering secure property rights. North (1989, p.9) highlights the significance of institutional changes such as the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This development was crucial for establishing a long-term capital market and driving economic growth. The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is central to this process. North (1989, p.9) further explains that as economies grow, the increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic underscores how institutional frameworks facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the success of these institutions hinges on credible commitment by the regime to enforce rules governing property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They argue that political factors driving economic growth encompass the rules governing economic exchange as well as the institutions enforcing and altering these rules. This perspective suggests that while economic performance can be influenced by various factors, the role of institutions in securing property rights is a critical determinant of long-run economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets both assert that technological advancements and knowledge growth are fundamental to long-run economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The expansion of useful knowledge serves as the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of innovations in technology, which are not merely tools for enhancing productivity but also catalysts for broader institutional and ideological changes that facilitate sustained economic improvement.

The integration of new technologies into society necessitates adjustments to existing institutions and ideologies. Kuznets notes that technological progress often conflicts with traditional rural lifestyles and familial structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This tension can be resolved through institutional adaptations, which are crucial for maintaining social cohesion during periods of rapid change. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is often irreversible, economic improvements require complementary institutional progress to be sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of "economic reasonableness" must accompany technological advances to prevent the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential for long-run economic growth, they must be supported by robust institutions that can manage societal conflicts and ensure sustained development. Mokyr's emphasis on institutional progress aligns with Kuznets' observation that modern economic growth is only possible if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs to the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This dual focus highlights a necessary condition: for technological advancements to drive long-run economic growth, they must be paired with institutions capable of managing societal changes and preventing rent-seeking behaviors.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth was profound and varied depending on the colonization strategies employed by European powers. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions were not conducive to investment or economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas with lower mortality rates such as the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.

The persistence of these colonial institutions has had lasting effects on economic growth. This is supported by the finding that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, leading to different institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. However, the persistence of extractive institutions has hindered economic growth in many regions, as they were not designed to foster development but rather to maximize short-term resource extraction. This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on long-run economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights that can guide institutional reforms and alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This perspective highlights a crucial mechanism by which colonial-era institutions shaped long-run economic growth: the development of formal education systems. British rule, for instance, was more conducive to fostering formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, as it introduced new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework influenced individual cost-benefit calculations and created broader incentives for investment, which are essential for development. This process varied across all regions; local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played significant roles in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores how institutional legacies from colonial rule influenced long-run economic growth, highlighting the enduring impact of these policies on Africa's development trajectory.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation through compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This critique is important because it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time, emphasizing the importance of contextual factors in shaping long-run economic growth. One specific mechanism that both Austin and Nunn identify is the role of colonial governance structures in influencing indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15; Nunn 2008, p.19). For example, where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which aligned with their fiscal interests. This dynamic led to higher production equilibria in some regions. Frankema and van Waijenburg note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about long-term economic development unanswered (Austin 2008, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the complexity of assessing the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, as it requires a deeper understanding of historical wage levels and trends.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. They assert that these conflicts led to institutional arrangements that favored elites over broader economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the conventional view of serfdom, guilds and other institutions as efficient responses to economic conditions. Instead, these institutions were often designed to maintain power structures by restricting competition and maintaining wealth disparities. For instance, in New World societies, greater inequality led to government policies that protected elite interests through various mechanisms. These included restrictions on land ownership, monopolies on trade, and the suppression of labor mobility (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie notes that while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor, they interact with broader cultural and political contexts to shape institutional outcomes. She argues that institutions should be seen as an indispensable tool for economic historians, underscoring the need to incorporate them into long-term research programs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is particularly relevant when examining the persistence of inequality in New World societies, where initial conditions of extreme wealth disparities led to enduring institutional frameworks that reinforced these inequalities.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamental to understanding the divergence in wealth among nations. Institutions are critical in determining how resources are allocated and managed over time; they shape the incentives for investment and innovation (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). For instance, Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to different institutional frameworks across colonies: some were established with extractive institutions designed to transfer resources back to Europe, while others adopted more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). This variation in institutional design highlights the profound impact of historical context on long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of inequality in New World societies can be traced back to these initial institutional frameworks, which reinforced pre-existing wealth disparities through mechanisms like legal systems and property rights enforcement (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The creation and evolution of institutions are not merely about efficiency; they are deeply rooted in the political dynamics that govern societal power structures. North argues that transaction costs play a crucial role in economic growth, as efficient property rights and their effective enforcement reduce these costs, thereby fostering market exchanges and specialization (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is further supported by Austin’s assertion that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating how local contexts shaped broader institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15).

These insights are particularly relevant when examining the persistence of inequality. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that institutions are not neutral; they reflect the interests of those in power and can perpetuate economic disparities over generations. The historical evidence from former colonies demonstrates how institutional frameworks, once established, can have lasting effects on a nation's economic trajectory.

Ultimately, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is indisputable (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). There remains disagreement about whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns or distributional conflicts. North and Austin agree that secure property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth, but they differ on how these institutions come to be established: North emphasizes the role of transaction costs in shaping efficient property rights (North 1989, p.6), while Austin highlights the importance of local political dynamics in determining institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is found in the varying economic trajectories of former European colonies, where initial conditions and colonial strategies led to different institutional frameworks with enduring effects on wealth disparities.
