The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within the field of economics and economic history. At stake is understanding how societies develop and sustain prosperity over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around whether extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources from colonies to European powers, are central to explaining current income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Another debate centers on the extent to which institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, drove the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This disagreement underscores the broader question of whether institutions matter for economic growth and, if so, how they interact with other determinants such as population density or prosperity (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15). The debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies in regions that have historically struggled with poverty and underdevelopment. It is therefore crucial to examine the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, and how these mechanisms interact with other factors over time. The evidence suggests that the evolution of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms plays a pivotal role in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were crucial for long-run economic growth. These developments were pivotal for fostering a long-term capital market essential for sustained economic growth. This perspective challenges neoclassical theories that primarily emphasize population and savings as key drivers (North 1989, p.9). They note that the success of these institutional changes is evident from capital markets, which show a remarkable transformation during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). However, North (1989, p.9) qualifies this by noting that while institutions are critical, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced; for instance, the bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV in France was not as significant a hindrance to economic growth as previously thought. This suggests that institutional effectiveness is context-dependent and relies on how these rules are enforced and adapted over time (North 1989, p.6).

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established varying institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns, which directly impacted the economic trajectories of these regions. This colonial legacy persisted over time, influencing current income per capita through the enduring effects of these institutional frameworks.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that the long-term economic outcomes in former colonies were shaped by the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting a direct link between colonial policies and economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that equilibrium institutions could be extractive, emphasizing the political dynamics at play (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that while institutional frameworks were critical, their effectiveness was contingent on the political power dynamics within the colonies.

The implications of these findings are profound: the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of historical policies on contemporary economic development, illustrating how initial conditions set by European colonizers can have long-lasting effects on institutional quality and economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the institutional impacts of slavery have shaped long-term economic development by perpetuating historical inequalities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This dynamic is evident in how colonial-era institutions favored elite groups, thereby restricting competition and reinforcing inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman detail how New World societies with greater inequality implemented government policies that advantaged elites, leading to a self-reinforcing cycle of wealth concentration (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon find that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust contributes significantly to reduced economic development by undermining social capital and trustworthiness (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The mechanism by which slavery impacts long-term growth involves both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors, such as rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Nunn and Wantchekon note that while both channels are important, the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). However, Nunn cautions against overemphasizing mistrust as a determinant of Africa's underdevelopment by highlighting how the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, contrasting with colonialism's much shorter duration. This underscores that while slavery is a critical factor, it operates in conjunction with other historical and contemporary processes (Nunn 2008, p.2).

Institutional change driven by African initiatives and exogenous causes significantly impacts the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins argues that while ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily hinder the provision of public goods, it is the institutional framework that determines whether a society can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This perspective challenges the notion that ethnic heterogeneity inherently leads to poor governance by highlighting cases where ethnically heterogeneous societies have successfully established strong institutions. Frankema's work further illustrates this point by examining how the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by new social and political institutions implemented during British rule, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional change had a downstream effect on African families' cost-benefit calculations regarding education. However, Akyeampong and Fofack qualify this impact by noting that colonial rule also reinforced patriarchal alliances through customary law, which codified the subordinate status of women in access to land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This qualification underscores that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, they must be considered within the broader context of social dynamics.

Simson (2019, p.11) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) reassess post-colonial economic policies in Africa, focusing on the nuances of budget policies and economic performance. They argue that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive. Simson shows that these policies had to navigate significant challenges, such as high debt burdens and escalating military spending (Simson 2019, p.11). Subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in Kenya during the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. This shift was not unique to Kenya; Tanzania also experienced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18 per cent of expenditure (Simson 2019, p.11). These financial pressures were compounded by the growth in military spending as domestic defence budgets expanded in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This process was particularly marked in Kenya, where subsidies and interest peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s. This shift in budget priorities had significant implications for economic growth, as resources that could have been allocated to productive investments were instead directed towards consumption and military expenditures (Simson 2019, p.11). However Broadberry and Gardner's finding is qualified by Simson (2019, p.2), who notes that the post-colonial period saw a complex interplay of factors influencing budget policies. This variation highlights that the impact of budget policies on economic growth was not uniform across East Africa, suggesting that local contexts and historical contingencies played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The thesis has been used to understand African poverty as a persistent phenomenon, but Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that this perspective overlooks nuanced institutional dynamics. He argues that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). This encouragement was driven by fiscal security and the desire to create material incentives for subjects. Colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, demonstrating a level of institutional responsiveness not captured by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further this argument by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). They reveal that poverty was not an immutable condition but rather a product of specific historical and geographical conditions, which could potentially be altered.

(2001, p.27) show that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were not static but adapted over time, as seen in the real wage trends across colonies, indicating that local contexts and historical factors are crucial in determining economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The role of institutions in fostering economic growth is further underscored by North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term development (North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.9) demonstrates that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term capital markets, which are essential for sustained economic development. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that regimes or sovereigns are bound by rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The variability in institutional impacts on growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, which Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) attribute to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting how political power dynamics shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.18) further supports this view, noting that poor institutions are linked to Africa's dismal economic record and that good institutions provide incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus grounded in the premise that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms foster sustained development. North (1989, p.9) shows that efficient property rights and their enforcement reduce transaction costs, which are critical for market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). However, the remaining disagreement lies in whether institutions are always designed to maximize long-run growth or if they can be extractive, maximizing rents for politically powerful groups. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to benefit European colonists rather than promote economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of real wage trends across colonies and the interaction between institutions and industrialization during the nineteenth century clarify this disagreement by showing that local contexts and political power dynamics significantly influence institutional impacts on growth.
