The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within economic scholarship. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods and why some regions prosper while others stagnate. The debate centers around the causal mechanisms that drive institutional development and their impact on economic outcomes. One position holds that institutions are paramount, shaping economic trajectories through their influence on property rights, investment incentives, and governance structures. These scholars emphasize that robust institutions foster growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity. Another perspective suggests that institutions merely mirror deeper underlying factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographical endowments, which ultimately determine economic performance. The core disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely epiphenomenal to other forces. While institutions may be instrumental in facilitating economic activity and investment, they can also become extractive, perpetuating inequality and hindering development. This tension highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions function within different contexts and over varying time scales.

A key point of contention is the relationship between institutional quality and long-term economic growth. North argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for fostering market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which in turn drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective underscores the importance of political institutions in specifying and enforcing property rights, thereby creating a stable environment conducive to investment and economic development. Ogilvie, however, challenges this view by suggesting that distributional conflicts play a more significant role in shaping core economic institutions than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This highlights the complexity of institutional dynamics, where power struggles and political interests can influence the design and implementation of institutions, potentially leading to outcomes that are not necessarily efficient or growth-promoting. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for comprehending how institutions shape economic trajectories over the long run.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic development has been a significant area of study. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers had profound and lasting effects on the economic outcomes of their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were more likely to be implemented in regions with high mortality rates among Europeans. This perspective underscores how initial institutional choices can have enduring consequences, shaping economic trajectories over centuries. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize the importance of population density and prosperity in influencing the policies pursued by European colonizers, which in turn affected the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin offers a complementary view, suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that colonial governments often responded to indigenous economic and political action, adjusting their institutional prescriptions in ways that were not always extractive or detrimental to local development (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the importance of considering the dynamic interplay between colonizers and indigenous populations in shaping institutional outcomes.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is further complicated by the role of cultural beliefs and shared values. Ogilvie notes that institutions are not merely rational responses to efficiency concerns but are also shaped by deeply held beliefs about political legitimacy, individual rights, and social norms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how cultural factors influence institutional development and economic outcomes. North similarly emphasizes the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutions, arguing that they can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on their alignment with efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). These insights highlight the need for a more nuanced approach to studying institutions, one that acknowledges the complex interplay between cultural, political, and economic factors.

This interplay is evident in the evolution of financial institutions, which played a pivotal role by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North argues that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a significant milestone, leading to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution was crucial for fostering secure property rights over time, which are essential for both capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast further emphasize that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for securing property rights and promoting economic growth. They note that these political factors underpin economic growth by ensuring that regimes are committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange, which is a key determinant of market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment to secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms created an environment conducive to long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth. Recent research has challenged the notion that these institutional changes were revolutionary by highlighting France's comparable economic performance before the French Revolution. Nonetheless, the role of financial institutions in reducing transaction costs remains a significant factor in understanding long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, had lasting effects on economic development. This institutional design, aimed at maximizing rents for European colonists, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts played the primary role in shaping pre-industrial Europe's economic structures. These authors contend that institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by underlying power struggles between different social groups. For instance, Ogilvie posits that guilds emerged from the need of craft workers to protect their interests against powerful merchants. For example, the Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium in response to indigenous economic activity. This suggests that institutional changes were often driven by the need to maintain social order and manage conflicts. Consequently, institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by immediate economic needs as well as the complex interplay of power dynamics and social order. These findings highlight how the role of institutions extends beyond mere efficiency considerations to encompass broader social and political factors that influence long-run economic growth.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). These effects persist through cultural and institutional channels. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to impact economic outcomes by reducing intergroup trust, an essential component for economic development. This internalization of mistrust is at least twice as significant as external environmental factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical context reveals that centralized governments and well-developed markets were crucial for sustaining trade, suggesting that the disruption caused by slave trades impeded institutional development necessary for long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn clarifies that the adverse effects of the slave trades include direct economic disruptions. This historical perspective underscores how institutions shaped by such profound disruptions can have enduring effects on long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that women played pivotal roles in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa. Women's contributions were significant through their engagement in food farming and cash crop production, which was crucial for the economic revolution in regions like the Gold Coast. The introduction of a dual legal system under British colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances, further codifying the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights how British rule facilitated the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa by altering the perceived benefits of education through new social and political institutions. This educational development was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by these institutional changes. This suggests that while women's economic contributions were substantial in Africa, the institutional framework under colonial rule often hindered their participation and access to productive resources.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) share a focus on institutional reforms in Africa, highlighting specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty but do not directly address long-run economic growth. The literature shows that the quality of public goods provision is not inherently linked to ethnic fragmentation; rather, it depends on the development of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). This institutional framework can be shaped by various factors, including post-independence budget policies and governance structures. For instance, in Kenya, subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). These budgetary shifts illustrate how institutional reforms can redirect resources away from productive investments, potentially hindering long-run economic growth. Furthermore, the growth in military spending during this period underscores the complex interplay between institutional changes and resource allocation, which can have profound implications for a country's economic trajectory.

This dynamic is further illustrated by North's argument that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). For example, Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further underscored by the historical evidence from Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were designed purely for economic efficiency and instead suggests they were influenced by power dynamics within society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Additionally, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The new economic history of Africa provides additional insights into the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Hopkins argues that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The shared finding across these sources is that institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth by determining resource allocation and governance structures. The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate. While Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), Austin highlights the adaptive nature of institutional changes in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from colonial administrations settling in Africa and establishing financial institutions demonstrates that colonial powers often implemented extractive systems to maximise short-term gains. This settles the disagreement by showing that the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate are context-dependent, influenced by both power dynamics and adaptive responses to local economic conditions.
