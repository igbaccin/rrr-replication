The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions directly influence economic outcomes by shaping property rights, enforcing contracts, and fostering investment. Others contend that institutions alone cannot account for economic growth and that external factors, such as the actions of colonial powers, must be considered. Additionally, some research highlights the importance of specific historical contexts and the interplay between different institutional frameworks.

The debate is further complicated by the varying interpretations of what constitutes an institution. Some scholars focus on formal rules and enforcement mechanisms, while others emphasize informal norms and cultural beliefs. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive economic growth over the long term. The question remains: How do institutions shape long-run economic growth? It is crucial to explore the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and how these mechanisms differ across historical contexts.

North's seminal work posits that institutions are pivotal in economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North argues that effective political institutions specify efficient property rights, thereby facilitating market exchanges. This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who highlight the role of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The development of financial instruments, such as those during England's financial revolution, illustrates how institutional changes can lead to falling transaction costs and enhanced economic performance (North 1989, p.9).

Critics challenge this view. Some argue that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is more nuanced than simply reducing transaction costs. For instance, Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that power dynamics and social structures shape institutions alongside purely economic considerations.

A related stream of scholarship examines the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights how colonial powers shaped institutional frameworks that had lasting effects on economic development.

Austin offers a counterpoint by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. He suggests that different historical periods and paths must be considered to understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work underscores the importance of contextualizing institutional changes within broader historical frameworks, rather than viewing them as isolated factors.

The influence of colonial institutions is further complicated by the actions of colonial powers and their impact on local economies. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified customary law, reinforcing patriarchal alliances and marginalizing women's economic contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The complex interplay between colonial institutions, local power dynamics, and gender roles illustrates the multifaceted nature of institutional influence on long-run economic growth. These studies underscore the importance of considering how external factors, such as colonial rule, shape institutional frameworks and their subsequent impact on economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that the institutional framework is fundamental to economic growth. North's work highlights how institutions reduce transaction costs by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms, which are critical for a functioning capital market and long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic is evident in the English financial revolution, where new political and economic institutions significantly lowered transaction costs, facilitating increased market exchanges and specialization. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this perspective by showing how colonial institutions shaped the long-run development of former colonies. They demonstrate that European colonists established extractive institutions designed to maximize their rents rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions, characterized by weak property rights and high transaction costs, hindered economic development in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

This institutional divergence had profound effects on current income per capita, illustrating how historical factors influence long-term economic outcomes through their impact on institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al (2001) found that different measures of institutions consistently show similar results, underscoring the robustness of this relationship (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional development is exemplified by the English Glorious Revolution of 1688, which established a constitutional monarchy with clear limits on executive power. The credibility of these institutions was further reinforced through mechanisms that ensured rulers could not easily expropriate wealth from their subjects. North and Weingast's work highlights how this commitment to secure property rights fostered a stable economic environment conducive to sustained growth.

This finding underscores the critical role that political institutions play in shaping economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies based on local economic and political conditions. For example, Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium contrary to initial hypotheses, illustrating how institutional prescriptions could adapt to indigenous actions. This dynamic response shows that while secure property rights are vital for growth, institutions must also be flexible enough to accommodate local contexts and incentives. Thus, the interplay between stable property rights and adaptive governance structures is essential for sustained economic development.

Frankema corroborates this by highlighting how demand for education among African families was influenced by the new social and political institutions introduced under colonial rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The codification of customary law during colonial rule frequently marginalized women's access to land and resources. This process was reinforced by patriarchal alliances that shaped customary law, thereby limiting women's economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth; technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that innovations, particularly those that can be diffused widely across an economy, are key to long-term growth. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustaining economic development, as institutions facilitate the efficient use of new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological changes to integrate into traditional societies effectively. For instance, the rural mode of life and extended family patterns may not be compatible with advanced technologies, necessitating adjustments in societal structures to accommodate these innovations. The sovereign state plays a crucial role here by resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, ensuring that technological progress does not weaken the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr also acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are vital for economic growth, they must be supported by robust institutions to ensure long-term sustainability. These claims together indicate that technological and knowledge-driven growth can propel economies forward only if accompanied by appropriate institutional frameworks.

The slave trades in Africa had long-term adverse effects on economic development by eroding social capital and trust within African societies (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the slave trade caused lasting damage to Africa's economic institutions, a view shared by Nunn and Wantchekon in 2011. They show that one of the main mechanisms through which this occurred was a culture of mistrust within African societies; it persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is not solely explained by the impact of the slave trade on the perceived trustworthiness of others, but rather by an internalization of mistrust norms. These findings complement recent studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn also notes that Africa's pre-colonial societies did not develop strong institutions capable of facilitating trade until they reached a certain level of centralization and market sophistication; this observation suggests that the slave trades disrupted the processes necessary to build such institutions. The legacy of mistrust and weakened economic institutions together constitute a significant barrier to economic growth in Africa, making it difficult for these economies to develop robust frameworks that support long-term sustainability.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They propose that these conflicts arise from the distribution of political power, which influences how institutions develop. This is evident in pre-industrial Europe where guilds were established to protect the interests of elite groups rather than for efficiency gains. The craft and merchant guilds, for instance, restricted competition to maintain their monopolies, limiting economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Similarly, in New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This mechanism of institutional development, driven by distributional conflicts, highlights how power dynamics can override efficiency considerations.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding historical development trajectories. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These rights are not just economic rules but also involve political factors that enforce and change these rules, highlighting the interconnected nature of political and economic institutions. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that extractive states, which prioritize resource transfer to the metropole over local development, can persist over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This persistence underscores how initial institutional frameworks can lock in certain economic trajectories, even when they are not conducive to long-term growth.

The persistence of these institutions is also linked to distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. explain that politically powerful groups may resist institutional changes that could threaten their rents or political power (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This resistance can lead to the maintenance of extractive institutions, even when more efficient ones are available. Ogilvie adds that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutional development is also influenced by broader historical contexts and power dynamics. North highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.6). This institutional evolution shows how political crises can drive changes that facilitate economic growth. Austin notes that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating the dynamic nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement lies in the specific case studies presented by Acemoglu et al., who show how European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes, which persistently affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is directly tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Austin's work on colonial administrations adapting their policies further supports this view by showing that indigenous economic and political actions influenced these adaptations. This evidence underscores how initial institutional frameworks, shaped by distributional conflicts and power dynamics, can have long-lasting effects on economic growth trajectories.
