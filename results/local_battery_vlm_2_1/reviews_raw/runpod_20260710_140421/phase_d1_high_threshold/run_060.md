The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate through other variables such as geography, culture, or technological advancements. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are essential for fostering an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Others posit that while institutions matter, their impact is mediated by broader historical and cultural contexts, making them less decisive in isolation. The interplay between these perspectives shapes our comprehension of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. North argues that the creation of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective underscores the significance of institutional frameworks in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, thereby promoting long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The type of institutions established depended on the mortality rates experienced by European settlers. In locations where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, often at the expense of local development. This pattern is evident in the stark differences between colonies like the United States and Australia, where settler institutions promoted economic development, and those like the Congo or Gold Coast, where extractive institutions hindered it. These colonial institutional legacies persist, affecting current income per capita across former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The persistence of these institutions is fundamentally tied to political and economic structures imposed during colonization as well as geographic factors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document how population density and prosperity influenced European policies, shaping the institutional frameworks that either facilitated or impeded industrialization and long-term development in former colonies. This interplay between colonial institutions and economic opportunities underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) jointly establish that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England was pivotal in reducing transaction costs. This institutional innovation fostered secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting enhanced security of property rights over time. This dynamic is crucial for both capital markets and overall economic growth.

The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms further supports this perspective. This pattern underscores the role of institutional changes in economic development, highlighting that secure property rights are a key factor in fostering growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) provide evidence from capital markets to support this claim. They argue that the success of these institutions is remarkable, indicating that the political factors underpinning economic growth are as important as the rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors, emphasizing instead the critical role of credible commitments and secure property rights in driving long-term economic growth. The implication is that institutional frameworks that ensure these conditions can lead to sustained economic performance over time.

Mokyr and others (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1) argue that technological advancements are pivotal in driving economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, suggesting that new technologies need compatible social structures to sustain long-term development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress often necessitates significant societal changes, including shifts in lifestyle and family patterns. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, highlighting how societies that successfully integrate new technologies tend to experience sustained growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) notes that resolving conflicts arising from technological changes is crucial for maintaining political stability and ensuring economic growth. This implies that while technological advancements are essential, their successful integration into society depends on effective institutional responses to mitigate potential disruptions. Institutions play a critical role in managing the societal impacts of technological progress, thereby supporting long-term economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the key drivers behind the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie posits that core institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, merchant guilds, and communities were shaped by struggles over resource distribution. This perspective challenges conventional views that attribute institutional development primarily to efficiency-seeking behaviors. Austin further illustrates this dynamic through colonial contexts where European settlers' presence influenced labor policies and commodity production. He shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic activities and political actions, emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. This adaptive behavior contrasts with the rigid application of efficiency-driven models. The implication is that the success of long-term economic growth depends on the responsiveness of institutions to shifting power dynamics and resource distribution struggles. Austin's analysis qualifies this by demonstrating how colonial administrators adjusted their policies based on local incentives, highlighting the importance of context-specific adaptations in institutional development.

The adverse effects of slave trades on economic development highlight a distinct pathway to underdevelopment that does not directly engage with institutional frameworks (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). The impact of these trades can be traced through the disruptions they caused in African societies. For instance, the transatlantic slave trade led to a significant loss of human capital, as millions of Africans were forcibly removed from their communities and transported across the Atlantic, disrupting social structures and economic activities (Nunn 2008, p.2). This disruption was compounded by the fact that the societies left behind often lacked the institutional capacity to recover quickly. For example, Nunn (2008, p.2) discusses how many African societies were unable to support trade with Europeans due to their small size and undifferentiated social institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). The lack of centralized governance and developed markets meant these societies could not easily adapt to the economic demands imposed by external forces. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how these extreme inequalities in wealth and power led to societies where elites could maintain their status through government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies, in turn, perpetuated inequality and hindered economic growth. However, it is important to note that the impact of slave trades on African development was not uniform; some regions were able to adapt and develop despite these challenges. This variability underscores the complex interplay between historical events, institutional frameworks, and long-term economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) assert that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. To achieve this, it is essential to understand how institutions influence economic growth over the long term. Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethnic fragmentation but rather by the capacity to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic development, as "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) explains that by the 1990s, the focus on governance allowed institutions and organizations to come to the fore (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shift enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves key questions unanswered. This gap highlights the need for more comprehensive data to fully grasp the long-term economic development trends in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident from the divergence in European colonization strategies and their enduring impacts on current income levels. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers adopted varying approaches based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional frameworks significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes, with extractive states hindering growth by transferring resources rapidly back to the metropole rather than fostering local investment and development. Similarly, North argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic expansion. This underscores how institutional frameworks can either support or impede economic progress over extended periods.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a pivotal role in shaping the relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. assert that these changes were driven by population density and prosperity, which influenced the policies pursued by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of these colonies. This perspective is bolstered by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). By examining colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous economic action, Austin shows how institutional prescriptions were adapted in response to local incentives, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. This dynamic is further illustrated by Hopkins's assertion that good institutions are crucial for providing investment incentives essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The historical evidence and empirical comparisons show that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is profound. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as demonstrated by North, directly influenced transaction costs and facilitated market exchanges, which were critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. provide further evidence through the divergent paths taken by European colonies, where extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical evidence settles the debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by illustrating how institutional frameworks can either support or impede economic progress.
