The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and whether this evolution can be attributed to the development of specific institutional frameworks. The live disagreement centres around two primary positions: one that emphasizes the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth, and another that argues for the importance of other factors such as geographical conditions or cultural beliefs. These competing views highlight the complexity of economic development and the multifaceted nature of the factors influencing it.

Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. North similarly emphasizes the critical role of institutions in economic growth, noting that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which reflects increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This shared finding suggests that secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings collectively point to the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic development over the long term.

Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors over time. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the motivations behind institutional development may be more complex than simply maximizing economic efficiency. These arguments underscore the importance of considering multiple factors when analyzing the role of institutions in economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the potential for institutions to hinder economic growth if they are designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-term development. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as evidenced by the success of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This finding suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their ability to provide a stable framework for long-term development. These insights collectively point to the importance of considering both the design and the enforcement mechanisms of institutions when analyzing their role in shaping economic growth.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, contra geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). These findings suggest that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be influenced by a variety of factors, including colonial policies and indigenous actions. This interaction highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of how institutions shape economic growth over extended periods.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. It encompasses both the creation of institutional frameworks and their enforcement mechanisms, as well as their interactions with other factors such as geographical conditions and cultural beliefs, along with the motivations behind their development. Understanding these dynamics requires a nuanced approach that considers multiple perspectives and factors, ultimately providing valuable insights into the long-term economic development of societies.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutions established by European colonizers were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promoting long-term economic development. These extractive institutions persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions by inhibiting investment and growth.

These changes were influenced by factors such as population density and prosperity, which determined the policies pursued by Europeans. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization played a crucial role in the long-term development of the former colonies.

This perspective highlights the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. The persistence of these extractive institutions can lead to a self-reinforcing cycle where initial institutional design continues to shape long-term economic performance, hindering development in regions that were once colonized.

This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and overall economic growth by enhancing the efficiency of market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The role of these institutions in fostering economic growth is further illuminated by the contrast with France. This highlights that it was not just the rules themselves but the credibility of their enforcement that drove economic growth. Therefore, the evolution of these institutions in England created a self-reinforcing cycle where secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms fostered long-term economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both highlight that technological progress is a key factor in long-term economic development. Kuznets points out that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which he terms "epochal innovations, " are crucial for sustained growth over extended periods. These innovations spread across various sectors and regions, driving economic advancement. Moreover, Mokyr emphasizes that the accumulation of useful knowledge is essential for economic growth, suggesting that technological progress is more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets notes that these technological changes require institutional adjustments to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies a necessary interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations for modern economic growth to occur. Mokyr further elaborates on this by acknowledging the role of institutions in reducing access costs and preventing coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54), which underscores that while technology is pivotal, the institutional environment must support its application. Kuznets also stresses the importance of resolving conflicts arising from technological changes without weakening societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that although institutions are not the primary drivers, they play a critical role in facilitating and sustaining economic growth driven by technological progress.

The slave trades in Africa have been shown to have had profound and lasting detrimental effects on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to those who attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, which is a critical factor in economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). The authors show that both internal and external channels are important in transmitting this effect; however, the internal channel, the norms of mistrust, is at least twice as large as the external one. This finding underscores how deeply ingrained these cultural legacies are.

By examining the period from 1400 to 1900, during which Africa experienced four simultaneous slave trades, Nunn highlights that these trades spanned nearly five centuries, significantly longer than the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule. This historical perspective underscores the enduring impact of the slave trades on African economic development. For instance, local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade until they encountered centralized governments like the Kongo kingdom with developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that even when favorable conditions exist, the legacy of the slave trades can impede progress by creating a persistent environment of mistrust and institutional weakness.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This elite capture was facilitated by the high concentration of wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized these unequal societies from their inception. The extreme inequality meant that a small group could easily dominate decision-making processes, allowing them to implement institutions that protected their interests. For example, they could secure property rights in ways that favored large landowners, create financial systems that benefited existing elites, and limit public infrastructure investments that might have encouraged broader economic growth. As Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) emphasize, this institutional framework reinforced the economic disparities by limiting opportunities for competition and innovation, thereby entrenching inequality over time. Elite capture varied across regions. In some areas, the presence of a more diverse set of interests could moderate the influence of elites. For instance, in regions where land distribution was less concentrated or where there were strong countervailing forces such as powerful merchant classes, institutions might develop in ways that promoted broader economic growth. This variation highlights how specific historical and social conditions could alter the trajectory of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2) demonstrate that colonial education policies in sub-Saharan Africa were shaped by distinct historical factors rather than being uniformly detrimental to long-run economic growth. Frankema shows how British rule facilitated the development of formal education through a series of social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of education for African communities (Frankema 2012, p.15). This development was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by local leaders and families, highlighting that colonial policies were not monolithic in their impact. Simson extends this analysis to post-independence budget policies, arguing that they were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed. Simson also notes the significant shifts in expenditure patterns towards military spending and subsidies, which could have diverted resources from productive investments (Simson 2019, p.11). This suggests that while colonial education policies might have had positive effects on human capital development, post-independence policies introduced new challenges that could affect long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. They show that historical national accounting data for Sub-Saharan Africa reveals significant economic activity during colonial rule. This finding challenges previous perspectives that often underestimate the region's economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner emphasize that this growth was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) support this view by reconstructing real wage levels in British African colonies. Their analysis indicates that economic development was not uniformly hindered by structural impediments but rather varied across different regions and periods. While export production played a crucial role, the variability in growth patterns suggests that local conditions significantly shaped economic outcomes. This insight is important because it highlights the need for nuanced approaches to understanding African economic history. The diversity in growth experiences underscores that long-run economic development in Africa was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including but not limited to colonial institutions and policies.

Austin’s work highlights the complexity of historical paths and institutional evolution. He argues that European colonial administrations responded to local economic and political actions, which led to changes in their institutional prescriptions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium contradicts Nunn's hypothesis about lower production equilibriums (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie further elaborates on this complexity by asserting that the core institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. The persistence of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be attributed to these conflicts, which institutional theories often overlook. This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve in response to local conditions and power dynamics. This insight is crucial because it suggests that long-run economic growth is not solely determined by the presence or absence of certain institutions but also by the broader socio-economic context in which these institutions operate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally linked to their ability to foster secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North shows that the development of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, which are critical for both capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This aligns with North and Weingast's argument that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that rules governing economic exchange are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon processes (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutional frameworks provide the stability necessary for long-term investment and economic development. The evolution of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, thereby driving economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The persistence of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be attributed to distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie argues that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who may have obtained fewer rents with institutions of private property or feared losing their political power if there was institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is also supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

This perspective underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to local conditions. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter significantly in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly through their role in enforcing property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). There remains disagreement over whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), whereas Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence of colonial administrations adapting to local conditions settles this disagreement.
