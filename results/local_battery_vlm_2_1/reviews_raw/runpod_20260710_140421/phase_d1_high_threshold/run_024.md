The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. At stake is our understanding of the fundamental drivers behind sustained economic development and the persistence of poverty across regions. One position holds that institutions are pivotal in determining economic outcomes by creating stable frameworks for investment, property rights, and market exchanges. These scholars argue that effective institutions foster growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6) and encouraging long-term planning (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Some scholars assert that geographical factors or cultural beliefs are more influential than institutions in shaping economic trajectories (Author Year p.N). Certain scholars argue that distributional conflicts play a key role in understanding core economic institutions in pre-industrial societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The debate extends beyond whether institutions matter; it also questions the specific mechanisms through which they influence growth. For instance, some argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can persist and hinder long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Others emphasize the importance of credible commitments from political regimes in establishing secure property rights and promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of colonialism further complicates this debate. Some scholars argue that the institutions introduced by European colonizers significantly affected the long-term economic trajectories of their colonies. For example, high mortality rates in certain regions led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn impacted current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic outcomes.

The interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography and culture is complex. Some scholars suggest that while geographical conditions may influence initial economic development, it is the quality of institutions that determines long-run growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Others posit that cultural beliefs shape institutional frameworks and thus influence economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The study of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how these factors interact over time. For example, the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights can foster economic development by providing incentives for investment and market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North's work emphasizes that specific institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North argues that the creation of financial institutions such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. The political model described by North highlights that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was instrumental during fiscal crises, illustrating how institutions can evolve to support economic growth. The significance of these institutional developments is further nuanced when considering the broader context of European economic history. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role, their impact on long-run economic growth can be context-dependent and influenced by various historical factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress is crucial for long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). They propose that the growth of useful knowledge serves as a primary driver of economic development. These findings suggest that the interplay between technology and institutions is complex: while institutions may facilitate or hinder economic growth, it is the technological advancements that fundamentally drive this process. Mokyr further elaborates on the role of coordination failures in economic progress, emphasizing that without institutional adjustments to support technological improvements, long-term economic gains could be jeopardized (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets cautions that these adjustments must occur without excessive costs to society, highlighting the delicate balance required for sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This nuance underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and political implications of technological change.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) establish that British colonial rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers. This effect was driven by a shift in the perceived benefits of formal education, which were tied to new social and political institutions introduced during British rule. These institutions altered cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). African political leaders and families reassessed the value of education in light of these institutional changes, leading to increased demand for missionary education. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes by influencing educational attainment. British rule did facilitate formal education; however, this process was not uniform across all regions or time periods. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly between countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions, local endowments, transport costs, and political factors in shaping economic performance.

Their shared finding is that extractive institutions established during colonial periods persisted and had lasting impacts on economic development, with European settlers often implementing institutions designed to maximize their own rents rather than fostering long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) detail how the political power of colonists led to the establishment of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over sustainable economic development. This institutional legacy is evident in regions where European settlers imposed extractive states, which hindered investment and constrained economic growth. Colonies where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law institutions experienced more favorable economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutional framework set up during colonial times had a profound impact on subsequent economic performance, with extractive states leading to lower income per capita in the long run.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that population density and prosperity influenced European policies, which in turn shaped institutional development. This interaction highlights how geographic factors played a role in determining the type of institutions implemented during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to industrialization opportunities and the institutional frameworks established during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while geographic factors were important, the long-term economic development of these regions was ultimately determined by the institutions put in place by colonial powers.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty, but does not directly engage with the broader causal mechanisms of long-run economic growth through institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that new economic history can inform these reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach underscores how understanding institutional development is crucial for fostering sustainable economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this perspective by emphasizing the importance of a deeper historical understanding in assessing future growth prospects. They argue that reconstructing real wage levels and trends can help identify structural effects on African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Englebert's conclusion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments suggests that societal heterogeneity does not necessarily hinder institutional progress.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is evident in how factor endowments shaped institutional development. Specifically, the abundance of land relative to labor led to high living standards on average but also resulted in extreme wealth disparities from the outset. These initial inequalities were reinforced through institutions that favored elites, such as policies limiting competition and restricting access to resources. The persistence of inequality was not merely a result of economic factors but was deeply entrenched through institutional frameworks that perpetuated elite dominance (Sokoloff&Engerman 2000 (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4)). This institutional entrenchment of inequality had profound implications. For example, in Latin America, the persistence of elite-dominated institutions hindered the development of inclusive economic policies, thereby stifling growth and innovation. The resulting institutional framework was marked by a lack of property rights security and pervasive corruption.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and enforcement of property rights, which are crucial for fostering investment and economic activity (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. This reduction in transaction costs is directly linked to the increased security of property rights over time. Ogilvie highlights that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can either facilitate or hinder the establishment of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. The historical context shows that institutional frameworks that prioritize secure property rights tend to foster economic development, while those that do not can stifle growth.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the divergent paths taken by different colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in their colonies. Inclusive institutions, as seen in the United States and Australia, promoted investment and economic development. Extractive institutions, prevalent in regions like the Congo and Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, impeding long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The persistence of these colonial-era institutions continues to influence current income levels, with former colonies that inherited inclusive institutions generally faring better economically. This historical evidence underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories.

The role of credible commitment in fostering long-run economic growth is crucial. North and Weingast show that England's evolution towards secure property rights was underpinned by political factors ensuring a credible commitment to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credible commitment fostered the development of markets and provided the necessary incentives for investment, which are essential for economic growth. Hopkins argues that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in securing property rights and promoting economic activity. The historical evidence from capital markets indicates that institutional changes in England significantly enhanced its economic performance relative to France.

Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional changes, whether inclusive or extractive, had profound and lasting effects on the economic development of these regions. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their critical role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The fundamental point of agreement among scholars is that institutions matter for long-term economic growth because they shape property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). There remains disagreement about the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise historical evidence of colonial institutions and their enduring impact on current income levels settles this debate, showing that inclusive institutions foster economic development while extractive ones hinder it.
