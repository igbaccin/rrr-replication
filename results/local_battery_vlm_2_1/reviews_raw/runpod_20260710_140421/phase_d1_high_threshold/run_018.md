The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake is understanding whether and how the rules and structures that govern societies can either propel or hinder sustained prosperity over generations. The debate hinges on two competing positions: one emphasizes the primacy of institutional factors, while the other highlights rival causal variables such as geography, culture, or economic policies. Institutions are seen as both a cause and an effect in economic development, with some scholars arguing that they are the primary drivers of growth, while others see them as secondary to other factors. The interaction between institutions and economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted, involving formal rules as well as informal norms and beliefs that shape behavior. This interplay underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve and adapt over time, as well as their impact on long-term economic performance. The central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. North argues that transaction costs in a growing economy reflect the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The nature of these institutions was largely determined by the European mortality rates in those regions. In areas where Europeans faced high mortality risks, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This strategy contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutional frameworks has had lasting effects on economic development. This institutional legacy continues to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies today.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is further illuminated by the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These factors influenced the types of institutions implemented, which in turn affected industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) reinforce this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, are consistently correlated with economic outcomes. The interaction between these institutional frameworks and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This highlights how colonial institutions shaped immediate economic conditions and had enduring effects on growth trajectories by influencing key economic activities such as industrialization.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework provided the foundation for sustained economic growth. The development of financial instruments such as those introduced during the English financial revolution further solidified this trend. The impact of these institutions on economic performance has many facets. North and Weingast observe that while England's institutional changes were significant, they were not revolutionary in isolation; rather, their effectiveness stemmed from the political commitment to enforce these rules consistently (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underscores how credible commitments within a stable institutional framework are essential for long-term economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors. The quality of public goods and institutions is not solely determined by ethnic homogeneity but rather by the capacity to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, as Hopkins argues (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This capacity can be hindered or enhanced by historical and geographical conditions, which have influenced long-term African welfare development in both temporary and structural ways, according to Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2).

One key mechanism through which these factors operate is the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment. Hopkins observes that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, highlighting how "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The extent to which historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary or structural effect on long-term African welfare development remains debated.

The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends in colonial Africa, as undertaken by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), reveals a more nuanced picture than one of persistent poverty. This historical perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that can adapt to changing economic conditions and provide sustained incentives for growth. The interplay between endogenous factors such as local governance and exogenous influences like colonial policies highlights the complexity of institutional change in Africa, where effective institutions are crucial for long-term economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that the great divergence in economic performance between Europe and Africa was already evident before 1800, a finding shared by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2). This divergence has deep historical roots. One specific mechanism underlying this divergence is the role of institutions in facilitating trade and finance. This institutional framework allowed for efficient market mechanisms that supported high productivity levels through services as well as production. The service sector's contribution to economic growth was significant, as it enabled the distribution of manufactured goods at lower relative prices, thereby increasing living standards unevenly across different labor groups.

This institutional advantage in Europe contrasts with Africa's experience, where local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions varied widely (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of economic growth in African countries were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets. The lack of robust institutional frameworks for trade and finance in many African regions hindered sustained economic development. Broadberry and Gardner's analysis underscores that while some Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, this growth was often tied to external factors rather than endogenous institutional strength (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This qualitative difference in institutional frameworks between Europe and Africa highlights how institutions shape long-run economic trajectories.

Van Zanden argues that institutions facilitating human capital investment are central to understanding long-term economic performance and the skill premium (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). In Western Europe, guilds acted as formal institutions safeguarding apprenticeship contracts, thereby ensuring skilled labor supply. The guilds' role in facilitating intergenerational skill transfer is crucial for interpreting the skill premium, a measure of returns on human capital investment. Van Zanden's analysis suggests that the skill premium reflects training costs, institutional barriers to entry, interest rates, and the duration of skilled work (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This framework implies that societies with lower training costs and fewer institutional barriers would see higher investments in human capital, driving long-term economic growth. Bolt and van Zanden highlight the challenges in measuring past economic performance due to data limitations and subjective estimates of margins of error (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2), suggesting that accurate measurement of skill premiums requires robust institutional frameworks for data collection and analysis. This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes as well as enabling their measurement, thereby influencing our understanding of long-term economic trajectories.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions during pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the design of these institutions was driven by the need to manage power struggles among various social groups as well as efficiency considerations. For instance, serfdom and guild structures can be understood as outcomes of elite efforts to maintain control over resources and labor. These institutions often restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality and hindering broader economic development. The persistence of such structures in societies with high initial inequality is further elucidated by Sokoloff and Engerman, who note that these differentials in factor endowments led to enduring disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). For example, the extreme inequality in New World societies resulted from policies that favored elites, limiting economic opportunities for broader segments of society. This dynamic underscores how distributional conflicts can lock in institutional arrangements that perpetuate inequality, thereby influencing long-term economic trajectories. The interplay between institutions and distributional conflicts thus reveals a critical mechanism through which historical inequalities shape contemporary economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight that technological advancements are fundamental to sustained economic improvement. This perspective suggests that the accumulation of knowledge drives productivity gains, which in turn fuel long-term economic growth. This process requires institutional adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this by emphasizing that modern technology necessitates significant institutional and ideological adjustments, such as the development of the sovereign national state, to resolve growth-induced conflicts peacefully. These adjustments are crucial for integrating new technologies into the economy without excessive social costs. Thus, while technological progress is a key driver, it must be complemented by institutional changes that support its integration and sustainability.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This perspective is grounded in the historical context of Africa’s long-term engagement with slavery. The transatlantic and Indian Ocean slave trades, along with internal African slave trades, spanned nearly five centuries, from 1400 to 1900, profoundly shaping economic structures (Nunn 2008, p.2). These trades disrupted social cohesion and economic stability, creating a legacy that persisted through colonial rule. The impact of slavery on Africa's institutions was multifaceted; it led to the dismantling of local governance systems in some regions while fostering centralized states in others (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional fragmentation hindered the development of cohesive economic policies and infrastructure, which are crucial for sustainable growth. Austin suggests that colonial administrations adapted their strategies based on indigenous economic actions, sometimes encouraging higher production equilibria contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). The British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more nuanced effect than is often acknowledged, influencing the development of formal education systems through missionary efforts (Nunn 2008, p.19; Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational impact was driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. While Nunn’s focus on slave trades highlights a significant factor in Africa's underdevelopment, the nuanced role of colonial institutions and their adaptations to local conditions underscores the complexity of economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

The interaction between slavery, colonial rule, and institutional development reveals that Africa's economic trajectory was shaped by multiple historical forces.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs. North demonstrates that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These institutions facilitate market exchanges, reducing costs per transaction and increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges, which in turn drives economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is complemented by Acemoglu et al. who argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies with distinct institutional outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled, they implemented institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment. In areas like the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This distinction highlights how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the persistence of these institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. show that the extractive institutions established in high-mortality colonies have had enduring effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This finding is supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, which influenced the long-term development trajectories of these regions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This adaptation underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth.

The interaction between slavery, colonial rule, and institutional development reveals that Africa's economic trajectory was shaped by multiple historical forces. Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by these conflicts, which influenced long-term economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The shared finding across these sources is that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. This shared finding shows that institutions are critical to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North 1989, p.6).

The remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of extractive versus inclusive institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to foster long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North highlights that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are critical for economic growth, suggesting an inclusive institutional framework (North 1989, p.6). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparison of extractive versus inclusive institutions in former European colonies. This comparison shows that inclusive institutions, which enforce property rights and encourage investment, lead to higher long-run economic growth compared to extractive institutions, which maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
