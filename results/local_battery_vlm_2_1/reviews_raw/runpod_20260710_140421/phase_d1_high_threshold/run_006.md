The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of prosperity across different societies over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinants of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, directly influences economic outcomes. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that historical contingencies, cultural beliefs, or geographical factors are more decisive. Additionally, there is debate on whether institutions evolve endogenously in response to economic needs or if exogenous shocks drive their development.

One stream of research highlights the direct causal relationship between institutional quality and long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different types of institutions in colonies, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. North (1989, p.6) posits that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. The development of these institutions facilitates market exchanges, reducing transaction costs and fostering a growing economy. Hopkins (2009, p.18) asserts that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is critical for development. This perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective governance in driving long-term economic prosperity.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is another key area of study. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth; their research shows that political factors, such as the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange, are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This stream of thought highlights the importance of institutional stability and the rule of law in creating an environment conducive to economic development.

The role of institutions is also examined through the lens of historical contingencies and cultural beliefs. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) challenges the efficiency-based explanations for core economic institutions, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for their development. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and the need to consider multiple factors, including cultural beliefs and historical contingencies.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of exogenous shocks and endogenous evolution. North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) notes that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. This stream of thought underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve over time and how they adapt to changing economic and political landscapes.

Institutions determine the security of property rights, which in turn affects long-term investment decisions and economic growth. This institutional innovation facilitated economic growth by promoting market exchanges and encouraging investments.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that institutions are not always designed to maximize long-run growth; instead, they often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups. In colonial contexts, European colonizers established extractive institutions that maximized their rents rather than promoting economic development in the colonies (North 1989, p.9). This persistence underscores how initial institutional choices can have long-lasting effects on economic growth. The implications of this mechanism suggest that understanding historical institutional trajectories is essential for comprehending contemporary economic disparities.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrate that historical institutional changes introduced by colonialism shaped economic outcomes in different contexts. The role of institutions in fostering secure property rights is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. This adaptation varied based on local conditions and incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, illustrating how institutions evolved in response to specific historical contexts. The evolution of these institutions was complex; colonial administrations responded to economic and political pressures in varying ways.

This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their impact on economic growth is mediated by a range of factors, including local conditions and historical trajectories. The nuanced relationship between institutions and economic outcomes underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of how institutional changes interact with other variables over time.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa by altering local perceptions of educational benefits. This occurred because demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These changes in perception were tied to broader economic developments, such as the growth of export production, which varied across countries due to factors like local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of this growth highlighted the intricate relationship between colonial policies and local development trajectories.

The interplay between colonial institutions and local conditions underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic development. The influence of British colonial rule on African economies was not uniform; rather, it interacted with pre-existing local structures and historical trajectories to produce diverse outcomes. This nuanced relationship suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping educational development, their effects were mediated by a range of factors, including local political calculations and economic dependencies.

The legacy of British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa reveals how institutional changes can both facilitate and constrain long-term economic growth.

The role of African women in economic growth has been significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Hopkins 2009, p.18) jointly establish. Women contributed to economic development through their agricultural labor, which was crucial for food production and cash crops like cocoa. The marginalization of women's land access during the colonial period reinforced patriarchal structures, yet women continued to drive economic growth despite these constraints. This dynamic highlights that even when institutional reforms under colonial rule codified customary laws that disadvantaged women, they still managed to contribute significantly to economic development.

The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often privileged men over women, limited access to land and resources for women. However, this did not entirely diminish their economic contributions; instead, it redirected them into different areas such as food farming and cash crops. This pattern is evident in the experiences of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast, who migrated in search of farmland for cocoa production. By 1911, these efforts had propelled the Gold Coast to become the world's leading producer of cocoa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Colonial rule reshaped women’s economic contributions within a new framework of constraints and opportunities.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality. This insight implies that even in ethnically heterogeneous societies, progressive institutions can still develop. The role of African women in economic growth underscores the potential for institutional reforms to support development, despite historical marginalization.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) assert that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which has persisted over time. This mistrust is an internal channel through which the slave trades have had long-lasting impacts on economic development. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade likely had profound and enduring effects on African economies.

Distributional conflicts between elites and non-elites shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe by reinforcing structures that maintained elite power (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates how guilds, for instance, served as instruments of elite control rather than purely efficient organizations. These institutions were designed to restrict competition and maintain the status quo, with rules and regulations that favored established elites at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This perspective is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show how inequality in New World societies led to institutional frameworks that protected elite interests through government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating a similar dynamic where institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations.

The implications of this institutional framework are profound, as they often perpetuated economic stagnation and limited growth opportunities for the broader population. This suggests that the distributional conflicts underlying institutional design can have lasting effects on economic trajectories, shaping societies in ways that favor elite interests over broader economic efficiency.

The focus on distributional conflicts offers a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve to serve specific interests within society, rather than being driven solely by considerations of economic efficiency.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate efficient market exchanges and secure property rights. North shows that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework fosters a growing economy by encouraging specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illustrate this point by explaining that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in some colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Distributional conflicts within society also drive the evolution of institutions. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by these conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who demonstrate how European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, setting up institutions that either enforced the rule of law or created extractive states depending on the interests of the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These findings underscore the importance of understanding how institutions evolve to serve specific interests within society.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further highlighted by their role in facilitating investment and providing incentives for development. Hopkins emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating efficient market exchanges and securing property rights (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The precise remaining disagreement lies in whether the evolution of these institutions is primarily driven by economic efficiency or by distributional conflicts within society. Ogilvie's work shows that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, contra the belief that these institutions were solely shaped by considerations of efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) analysis of European colonization strategies and their impact on current income per capita. This analysis demonstrates how institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, rather than long-run growth, shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
