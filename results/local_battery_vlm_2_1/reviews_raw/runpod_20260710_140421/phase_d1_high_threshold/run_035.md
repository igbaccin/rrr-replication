The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development literature, where scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth and prosperity. The live disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. One position posits that institutions directly shape economic growth through property rights enforcement, rule of law, and investment incentives (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.2). Another position contends that the impact of institutions on economic development is mediated by other factors such as population density, prosperity, and colonial policies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The first stream of literature posits that institutions are pivotal drivers of economic growth through the enforcement of property rights and rule of law. This perspective emphasizes the role of secure property rights in facilitating long-term capital markets and reducing transaction costs, which are essential for economic development. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks, with some colonies adopting institutions that enforced rule of law and encouraged investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This stream highlights that the nature of these institutions significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies.

A related stream further develops this causal mechanism by examining how different colonial strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes. This distinction underscores how colonial policies shaped institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the development of former colonies (Austin 2008, p.15).

The third stream addresses the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by considering their impact on investment incentives and governance structures. This perspective emphasizes how effective governance and institutional frameworks can foster an environment conducive to investment and economic growth. However, the role of institutions in economic development extends beyond direct effects on investment; they also influence broader socio-economic contexts. This stream underscores how institutional frameworks can facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing investment incentives and governance structures.

The fourth stream examines the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth through the lens of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights how institutional frameworks are shaped by power dynamics and conflicts among different groups, which can either promote or hinder economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth through various mechanisms, including the enforcement of property rights, rule of law, investment incentives, and governance structures. The causal relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving interactions with broader socio-economic contexts and power dynamics.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were not uniform but varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates; where Europeans faced high mortality, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional framework was detrimental to long-term economic development as it prioritized short-term extraction over sustainable growth. This resistance further entrenched extractive institutions, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. also highlight the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European colonial policies, which in turn influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) demonstrate that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs. These institutions fostered secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England was a landmark event that significantly reduced the cost of transactions, reflecting an increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution enabled a more efficient capital market and facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of long-term investments. The emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms was critical in this process. These institutions ensured that costs per transaction fell, even as the size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This pattern highlights how institutional changes can shape economic outcomes by reducing friction in market exchanges. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution against overstating the revolutionary nature of these institutions, noting that similar economic performance in France suggests that institutional changes alone do not guarantee superior growth.

The role of credible commitment in sustaining economic growth is also evident in this framework. The success of financial institutions like the Bank of England hinged on the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights and maintaining stable rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credible commitment underpins the long-term stability needed for sustained economic development. However, the degree to which a regime is bound by these rules can vary, affecting the extent to which institutions support growth. This underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping long-run economic growth.

The Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This demographic shock triggered a series of economic adjustments. Pamuk argues that the plague's impact on labor supply caused significant shifts in wages, with skilled laborers seeing larger surpluses over basic food needs than unskilled laborers. The service sector played a crucial role in this economic growth, as merchant distribution and finance facilitated high productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This structural change was not uniform across Europe; institutional flexibility allowed regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities, while others, such as Tuscany, struggled with stagnation due to strong monopolies. The differing trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe are evident in urbanization rates, which serve as key indicators of productivity and economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.16). However, Broadberry and Gupta note that high agricultural labor productivity in developing parts of north-western Europe was driven by institutional change, suggesting that while the Black Death set the stage for divergence, institutional factors were pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. The uneven spread of early modern development highlights how regional variations in institutional responses to structural changes influenced economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49); this claim is shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1) who sees technological progress as the dominant source of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Both Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) agree that institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that without such institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements would be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions play a crucial role in harnessing this potential by reducing access costs and mitigating coordination failures. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on the importance of the state in resolving conflicts arising from technological changes, highlighting how institutional frameworks can facilitate long-term economic stability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that technology alone is insufficient for sustained growth; institutions are essential to manage the social and political dynamics that accompany technological progress.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Nunn 2008, p.2), along with Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), argue that the institutions of New World societies were shaped by inequality-advantaging policies. These policies restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, creating a dynamic where elites maintained their dominance through various government interventions. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that differences in institutions can be traced back to the factor endowments of these societies, which led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality was reinforced by colonial policies that codified customary laws, often at the expense of marginalized groups like women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Akyeampong and Fofack show how African women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, further entrenching gender-based inequalities. The implications of these institutional frameworks are profound: they not only perpetuated economic disparities but also shaped long-term development trajectories in fundamentally unequal ways.

One downstream implication is the impact on economic growth and development. Nunn (2008, p.2; 2008, p.19) argues that Africa's slave trades had a lasting adverse effect on its economic development, highlighting how historical inequalities can have enduring consequences. This suggests that the institutional legacies of inequality-advantaging policies in New World societies may similarly hinder long-term economic growth by creating entrenched social and economic structures that favor elites over broader societal development.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This is because institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. In particular, the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). However, the impact of colonial rule on African economic development is not uniform across countries; British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonial rule can have varied effects on long-term economic growth, depending on the specific policies and practices implemented by different colonial powers. The post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive (Simson 2019, p.11).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the position that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon. The authors argue that the economic performance of Africa before 1950 was more robust than previously thought, with many Anglophone economies achieving per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges the notion that African economies were stagnant due to structural impediments. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) identify export production as a key driver of economic growth during colonial periods, noting that its timing and scale varied across countries owing to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The authors argue that the relationship between foreign trade and domestic economic activity was complex but generally positive for African economies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels in British African colonies to demonstrate that poverty was not a persistent condition. Their findings indicate that historical data can reveal periods of significant economic development, challenging the view that Africa's economic trajectory has been uniformly dire.

Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This simplification fails to capture the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over time. This is particularly evident in the divergent paths taken by different regions within Africa during colonial times; for instance, where European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial government interests. This emphasis on institutional adaptability and local context challenges the notion that institutions uniformly drive growth across different historical periods and regions. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also stresses the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions function over the long term, suggesting that they should be integrated into economic historiography as indispensable tools (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering both the historical specificity and the adaptive nature of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-established, with institutions providing the foundation for secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This view aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can stifle economic development by prioritizing short-term gains over long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific impact of institutions varies across different historical contexts and regions. For instance, North highlights how the English financial revolution and the creation of the Bank of England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth through increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Austin's observation that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that institutions are not static but evolve based on contextual factors (Austin 2008, p.15).

The adaptability of institutions is further underscored by Hopkins' emphasis on the role of good institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This aligns with Ogilvie's call to integrate institutions into economic historiography as indispensable tools (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), highlighting their crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. The shared finding across these sources is that institutions matter significantly in economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; North 1989, p.6). The disagreement lies in whether institutions uniformly drive growth or if their impact varies based on historical and regional contexts. This is shown by Acemoglu et al.'s argument that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while Austin argues that institutional prescriptions change in response to local actions, indicating a more nuanced role of institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the varying impact of colonialism on different regions, as shown by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of mortality rates and institutional outcomes in European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
