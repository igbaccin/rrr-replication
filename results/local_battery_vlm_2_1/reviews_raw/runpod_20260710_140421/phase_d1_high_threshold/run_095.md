The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake are fundamental understandings of why some nations thrive economically while others stagnate. A central disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions themselves drive economic outcomes, versus other factors such as geography or culture. Some scholars posit that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing their role in establishing property rights and reducing transaction costs. Others argue that institutional frameworks are merely one among several factors, including population density, geographical features, and cultural beliefs, that influence long-term economic performance. Thus, the debate hinges on whether institutions are autonomous determinants or secondary effects of broader socio-economic forces.

Some scholars highlight how European colonial strategies established different institutional frameworks in colonies, which in turn influenced long-run economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted varied colonization strategies based on local mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that fostered growth or extractive ones that hindered it (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutional frameworks specifying property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). The divergence in colonial strategies underscores how early institutional choices can have lasting impacts on a nation's economic trajectory.

Acemoglu et al. assert that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These scholars argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on local mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In high-mortality areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were designed to quickly transfer resources to the metropole, impeding economic development. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that European colonists implemented institutions maximizing their rents and promoting long-run growth was not a priority; this pattern is evident in many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is linked to the interaction between colonial institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century. This dynamic underscores how early institutional choices can have profound and enduring impacts on economic outcomes, shaping the development paths of nations over centuries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the emergence of financial institutions in England was pivotal for long-run economic growth. This development reduced transaction costs by creating secure property rights through new financial instruments, such as those introduced during the English financial revolution. This institutional change can be understood as a response to fiscal crises, such as those arising from the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded with Parliament for revenues. North and Weingast further emphasize that the success of these institutions was underpinned by their commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England's according to evidence from capital markets. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes.

Mokyr emphasizes that the proliferation of useful knowledge is essential for sustained growth, while Kuznets notes that technological changes require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Institutions play a crucial role in resolving conflicts generated by these technological shifts, thereby facilitating modern economic growth. Mokyr elaborates on this dynamic, asserting that institutional progress must complement technological advancements to ensure the sustainability of economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress is complex. Kuznets acknowledges that while technological changes are necessary for growth, they also introduce conflicts that need institutional resolution. He notes that "the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge" require significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be effectively integrated into the economy (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr's analysis underscores that without these complementary institutions, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, their effective utilization depends on the ability of institutions to manage the associated challenges. The interplay between technology and institutions is complex; it requires a nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks support and enhance the impact of technological innovations on long-term economic development.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap in Europe by 1450 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). These structural changes were driven by demographic shifts and labor market dynamics. The Black Death caused significant population decline, which in turn reduced the supply of labor. This scarcity of labor led to increased wages for surviving workers, particularly in regions like northwestern Europe where institutional flexibility allowed for rapid economic adaptation (Pamuk 2007, p.16; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Broadberry and Gupta note that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was facilitated through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This wage gap created a divergence between regions with flexible institutions that could adapt to new economic opportunities and those with rigid structures that hindered such adaptation. Pamuk highlights how Tuscany's economy stagnated due to strong monopolies, while Holland thrived under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The ability of regions to respond to these changes was thus contingent on their institutional frameworks, which determined the effectiveness of economic adaptation and growth. This suggests that while demographic shocks like the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, the institutional environment played a critical role in shaping how these changes manifested and influenced economic divergence.

The slave trade's enduring impact on African economic development challenges the primacy of institutions as the sole fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust impedes economic development by undermining social capital and hindering cooperation. The slave trade disrupted African societies' internal norms and external environments, creating lasting barriers to trust and economic exchange. Nunn observes that the slave trades lasted nearly five centuries, outlasting the period of colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This prolonged exposure to the slave trade left deep imprints on African economies, shaping development trajectories in ways that cannot be solely attributed to institutional frameworks established during and after colonialism. Therefore, understanding Africa's economic development requires acknowledging the multifaceted legacies of the slave trades.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the British colonial administration's policies created conditions that made formal education more appealing to African leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for employment and social mobility (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was facilitated by the demand for missionary education, which depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders at a macro level and African families at a micro level. The British colonial administration's policies created conditions that made formal education more appealing to African leaders and families. This does not mean all colonial impacts were uniformly beneficial; Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that in New World societies with greater inequality, elites used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the benefits of British rule in fostering education could be mitigated by underlying socioeconomic inequalities. The legacy of colonial education policies thus shaped long-run economic growth through both direct educational impacts and indirect effects on social mobility and inequality.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that African women made substantial contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods. This dynamic was a product of colonial policies and rooted in pre-existing patriarchal structures that were exacerbated by these interventions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) illustrates how the new economic history of Africa has emphasized the role of institutions in shaping long-term development, highlighting that good institutions provide crucial incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). While this institutional framework is important, it also underscores a critical gap: the marginalization of women's contributions. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how patriarchal alliances and customary law during colonial rule further entrenched gender inequalities in land access and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional bias has lasting implications for understanding African economic development, as it suggests that the benefits of good institutions are not evenly distributed. The recognition of women's historical contributions to economic growth is essential for a comprehensive understanding of Africa's economic trajectory.

The historical economic performance of Anglophone African economies has been misrepresented as persistently poor by previous scholarship (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges the prevailing narrative of persistent poverty in Africa. These factors collectively shaped the economic landscape during colonial rule, influencing both the timing and scale of growth in export production.

The role of political institutions is particularly noteworthy. Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight how real wage levels and trends in British African colonies varied significantly over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This variation suggests that the economic outcomes were not merely determined by structural impediments but were also influenced by specific historical or geographical conditions. The reconstruction of real wage levels provides a more nuanced understanding of African welfare development, indicating that poverty was not a persistent phenomenon but rather fluctuated based on these conditions. The lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates limits our ability to fully assess the long-term impact of these factors (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Further research is needed to explore how these determinants relate to other parts of colonial Africa and to understand the broader implications for future growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far more complex than simple models suggest. Austin's analysis of colonial Africa highlights the importance of indigenous economic and political action, showing how local dynamics influenced institutional prescriptions set by colonial administrations. For example, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium was a response to the incentives created by these actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and actors' behaviors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this perspective by arguing that distributional conflicts often drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She contends that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights significantly impact institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting long-run economic growth depends on how well they align with prevailing cultural values and social norms. Ogilvie's call to transform institutions from a "fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument" suggests that a deeper understanding of these dynamics is crucial for economic historians (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view challenges the notion that institutions solely focused on property rights and contract enforcement are sufficient for sustained growth, highlighting instead the importance of cultural and social contexts.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating stable frameworks that facilitate market exchanges and investment. According to North (1989, p.6), the development of political institutions that specify property rights and enforce them effectively leads to falling transaction costs and increasing specialization. This institutional framework encourages economic growth by enabling more efficient market transactions and fostering a growing proportion of aggregate exchanges in GNP. The critical role of secure property rights is further emphasized by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who argue that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century significantly influenced long-run development. These arguments underscore the importance of institutional quality in promoting economic growth, particularly through the enforcement of private property rights and the reduction of transaction costs.

The effectiveness of these institutions depends on their alignment with prevailing cultural values and social norms. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that shared beliefs form part of a society's institutional structure, influencing how institutions are adopted and enforced. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. These findings suggest that the success of institutions in promoting growth depends on their ability to integrate with local cultural contexts and respond to societal dynamics.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is also shaped by distributional conflicts, which can either hinder or facilitate institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This aligns with Hopkins' argument (Hopkins 2009, p.8) that good institutions provide incentives for investment and are crucial for development. These insights underscore the importance of understanding how distributional conflicts influence institutional design and implementation.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping economic growth reveals a shared understanding that institutions matter, but there is disagreement on whether extractive institutions can be transformed into growth-promoting ones (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. This point is settled by evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), which shows that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either extractive states or growth-promoting institutions based on local conditions and political dynamics. This historical evidence demonstrates that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting long-run economic growth depends on their ability to align with prevailing cultural values and social norms while addressing distributional conflicts.
