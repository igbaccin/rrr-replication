The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake intellectually are the fundamental drivers of prosperity, and how societies can foster enduring economic development. The debate centres on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, or if they operate through other factors such as geography or culture. One position argues that institutions directly influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives for investment and innovation. This view posits that secure property rights and effective governance are crucial for sustained growth. Another perspective suggests that institutions themselves are shaped by underlying geographical factors or cultural beliefs, which ultimately determine economic performance. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are autonomous forces driving growth or merely intermediaries of other determinants. This question is pivotal because it informs policy recommendations aimed at fostering development in regions lagging behind economically. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is exemplified by historical events such as the English financial revolution and the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) traces how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, which fostered economic growth. This institutional change reflected an increased security of property rights over time, a critical factor for both capital markets and economic development. The political factors underpinning economic growth included credible commitments to enforce these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is evident in how the English financial revolution facilitated market exchanges and lowered transaction costs, which in turn spurred economic activity. Acemoglu et al (2002) extend this argument by showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the design of institutions matters greatly: those that prioritize the extraction of immediate benefits may hinder sustained economic development. The historical record shows that institutions matter because they shape the incentives and constraints under which individuals operate, influencing their decisions and outcomes.

Acemoglu et al show that European colonizers established extractive institutions in areas with high mortality rates, prioritizing rapid resource extraction over long-term development. These institutions persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies and affecting current income per capita. Austin (2008, p.2) adds nuance to this picture by highlighting that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that while initial institutional frameworks were crucial, they were not immutable; local dynamics could influence institutional evolution.

The persistence of extractive institutions frequently overrode these adaptations. Acemoglu et al demonstrate that even when controlling for various measures of institutional quality, such as constraints on executive power and property rights, the impact of initial colonial institutions remains robust (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This indicates that the foundational nature of these institutions had a lasting effect on economic outcomes, regardless of subsequent adaptations. The historical record shows that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, were crucial in determining long-run economic growth trajectories.

Ogilvie's argument suggests that pre-industrial Europe’s key economic institutions were more driven by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). She contends that institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups, which sought to maintain or enhance their relative advantages. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial inequalities led to institutional structures that perpetuated elite dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and hindered economic growth by favoring the interests of elites over broader societal benefits. The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the entrenched power of elites who used their influence to shape institutions that maintained their privileged status.

The institutional arrangements that emerged from these distributional conflicts had profound implications for long-run economic performance. Ogilvie emphasizes that understanding these institutions requires moving beyond simplistic efficiency arguments and considering the underlying power struggles (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This approach aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's observation that factor endowments, such as land abundance relative to labor, created environments where extreme inequality was virtually inevitable from the outset. These inequalities were perpetuated through institutions designed to protect elite interests, which in turn shaped economic trajectories over centuries (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The institutional legacies of these early distributional conflicts continue to influence contemporary economic structures and outcomes.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasize that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that the accumulation of practical knowledge has been crucial in enabling societies to harness their resources more effectively. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that this growth is not merely technological but also requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). The interplay between technical progress and the development of institutions is pivotal, as both must evolve in tandem to sustain economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges that without such institutional progress to complement technological advances, economic gains could be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This dynamic is evident in historical cases where societies failed to align their institutions with new technologies, leading to stagnation or even regression. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This process is not straightforward; technological progress can exceed the pace of institutional adaptation, resulting in periods of instability and uncertainty. This tension highlights the need for continuous adjustment and reform to maintain economic momentum over the long run.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlight how mistrust and institutional breakdowns created by the slave trade have impeded long-run economic growth in Africa. The legacy of this mistrust has been compounded by institutional breakdowns, further hindering economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a lasting adverse effect on economic development, shaping the continent's trajectory in ways that extended far beyond colonial rule and other recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2). This institutional breakdown is evident in the lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets that could support trade. The Kongo kingdom, for example, was able to sustain trade with Europeans due to its centralized government and national currency, highlighting the critical role of institutions in facilitating economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional framework varied across Africa, leading to uneven economic development. This dynamic is further complicated by Pamuk (2007, p.23), who argues that long-term structural changes, such as those initiated by the Black Death, led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). While this example pertains to a different region, it underscores how historical events can shape institutional frameworks and economic trajectories over extended periods. This suggests that while mistrust and institutional breakdowns are significant barriers to long-run economic growth in Africa, the specific historical contexts and institutional responses play a crucial role in shaping these outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) explore how colonial rule shaped African economic institutions through gender dynamics. Colonial administration codified customary law, which often marginalized women by restricting their access to land for cash crops. These changes also had implications for pre-colonial institutions, where women played a significant role in food production on marginal lands. Colonial policies exacerbated gender inequalities by reinforcing patriarchal alliances through legal frameworks that privileged male ownership and control over land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that the new economic history of Africa highlights how institutional reforms can drive development, suggesting that understanding these historical changes is crucial for contemporary policy-making. This variability underscores the need for nuanced approaches to institutional reform in Africa, acknowledging both historical legacies and contemporary challenges.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that the economic performance of African countries under colonial rule is more nuanced than often portrayed. They contend that British rule in particular fostered formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa to a greater extent than other colonial powers, which had significant implications for long-run economic growth. These institutions altered the perceived advantages of education, linking it to broader social and political changes.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) show that this educational development did not uniformly result in economic growth across different regions. They argue that high productivity in certain sectors was influenced by merchant distribution and finance as much as production itself, highlighting the uneven spread of early modern development benefits. This unevenness suggests that while colonial institutions may have facilitated certain forms of economic activity, their impact on long-run growth varied significantly depending on local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema (2012, p.15) further qualifies this by noting the varying degrees of institutional change brought about by different colonial powers.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show how post-independence economic policies in Africa have been shaped by specific historical and political determinants that influenced poverty levels. One specific mechanism through which these policies manifested was the significant increase in military spending post-independence, driven by regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This shift in expenditure patterns, from colonial subsidies to domestically funded defense, highlights how political dynamics influenced economic priorities. This pattern of increased military spending is influenced by the varying debt burdens across different countries, such as Kenya and Tanzania, which had higher interest payments compared to Uganda due to differences in access to capital markets (Simson 2019, p.11). This variation underscores the complex interplay between historical legacies, political instability, and economic policy outcomes.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) indicate that the skill premium is a key indicator of long-term economic performance. van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that the determinants of this premium can be understood through a supply-and-demand framework. The cost of training, institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades, interest rates, and the expected duration of higher earnings all influence the skill premium (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This framework sheds light on how economic incentives shape human capital investment, which in turn drives long-term growth. This uncertainty underscores the challenges in accurately assessing historical economic data, which is essential for understanding long-term growth patterns.

Peters (2004, p.19) and North (Peters 2004, p.19) argue that the negotiation of land systems in Africa is often misconstrued as a sign of flexibility. This interpretation misses deeper political-economic processes at play, namely exclusion and class formation. The complexity of these dynamics is evident in how private commercial transactions have shifted reliance away from decaying state institutions toward foreign firms and intermediaries (Peters 2004, p.19). This shift signifies a departure from traditional patrimonial dependencies, illustrating how economic activities are intertwined with political resources. These processes are interconnected and part of broader historical contexts that shape long-term economic outcomes. For instance, the late colonial period saw significant changes in perceptions of African landholding practices, which were often viewed as communal and thus inhibiting individual investment (Peters 2004, p.5). This perception fueled efforts toward agricultural development, highlighting how institutional views on land can drive economic policies and ultimately impact growth trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely acknowledged, with multiple studies highlighting how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder development. Institutions matter because they define the rules that govern economic interactions and allocate resources (North 1989, p.6). For example, Acemoglu et al. show that European colonial strategies in high-mortality regions led to extractive institutions that persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores how initial institutional setups can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes.

The mechanisms through which institutions influence growth are complex and multifaceted. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective indicates that institutional changes varied based on local conditions and colonial policies. For instance, in regions where European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production was often encouraged by colonial governments, illustrating how institutional prescriptions adapted to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The debate over the role of institutions in long-run economic growth also involves the interplay between political and economic factors. North argues that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not promote long-run growth but rather maintained the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the variation in colonial strategies and their institutional legacies. The extractive nature of institutions in regions like the Congo versus the more inclusive institutions in settler colonies like the United States shows how different institutional frameworks led to divergent economic paths (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence underscores that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting impacts on economic development, illustrating the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
