The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue among economists and historians. The core disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes over the long term. On one side are scholars who argue that institutions are pivotal in fostering economic development by providing secure property rights, enforcing contracts, and ensuring rule of law. These scholars often emphasize how colonial legacies have shaped institutional frameworks, influencing contemporary economic performance. On the other hand, there are those who posit that while institutions matter, they are not the sole determinant; geographic factors, cultural differences, and exogenous shocks also play significant roles. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at promoting sustainable economic growth in developing countries. The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes can guide effective institutional reforms that may alleviate poverty and spur development.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, highlighting how different colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted varying approaches in their colonies, setting up either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, in settler colonies like the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, they created extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This divergence in institutional frameworks significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

Austin offers a nuanced perspective by critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions alone determine long-run economic outcomes. Austin argues that this thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This view suggests that while institutions matter, their impact is mediated by a complex interplay of factors including indigenous economic and political action as well as the specific incentives created by colonial administrations (Austin 2008, p.15). The interaction between these elements means that the institutional legacy of colonialism is not uniform across former colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Colonial strategies varied widely and the institutions they established were directly tied to mortality rates among Europeans. In regions where European settlers faced high mortality, such as tropical Africa, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This institutional framework persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of these colonies and their subsequent independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) found that different measures of institutions, such as constraints on executive power, property rights, rule of law, judicial efficiency, and their effects were consistent across various metrics. This consistency underscores the robustness of institutional quality in determining economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) suggest that population density and pre-colonial prosperity also influenced the policies Europeans implemented. These factors could have moderated the impact of colonial institutions on long-run development, indicating that institutional effects were not uniform across all former colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that financial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. North [1989] describes this period as the English financial revolution, highlighting how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms are critical in facilitating market exchanges. The reduction in transaction costs per exchange is indicative of a growing economy, with increasing specialization and division of labor multiplying the aggregate volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework not only supported economic growth but also ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing these rules, thereby underpinning credible commitments in the economy. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize the political factors governing how these rules are enforced and changed, suggesting that a sovereign's commitment to these rules is critical for sustained economic development. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as initially thought, given the similar economic performance of France before the French Revolution. This qualification underscores the importance of considering multiple factors beyond institutions alone in explaining long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and African economic history reveal how different historical periods shaped long-run economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' oversimplifies causation by compressing distinct historical paths. Austin further elaborates on how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic indicates that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played significant roles in shaping export production patterns within African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that while European settlers' presence influenced labor competition, it did not uniformly determine economic outcomes. These findings collectively suggest that the interplay between colonial policies, local conditions, and indigenous actions created a complex landscape for economic development in Africa.

The slave trade's impact on African economic development is profound and persistent. Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to undermine trust levels today. This mistrust is deeply rooted in historical experiences, where the slave trade not only depleted human capital but also eroded social cohesion and intergroup relations. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that the Kongo kingdom's centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated sustained trade with Europeans due to its strong institutions, suggesting that pre-colonial African societies had the potential for economic development, but the slave trade disrupted this process. The internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the slave trade, are at least twice as significant as external factors like the rule of law in reducing trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This persistent mistrust, cultivated over centuries, has profound implications for contemporary Africa's economic development and institutional efficacy.

Institutional reforms have been pinpointed as crucial to alleviating poverty among Africa's bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform these institutional reforms. The quality of public goods provision in African governments is not determined by ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity, but by the institutions they develop. However, these historical conditions are not immutable. The reconstruction of real wage levels in British Africa during colonial rule indicates that there were periods of economic growth and development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that with the right institutional reforms, African economies can overcome poverty and achieve sustained economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that colonial education policies varied considerably across sub-Saharan Africa, with British rule generally fostering more formal educational development than other colonial powers. This differential impact can be attributed to the specific institutional frameworks imposed by the British, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. However, the extent to which these educational advancements translated into long-term economic growth remains nuanced. Frankema (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) highlights that while British colonial policies might have promoted formal education, the broader institutional environment and factor endowments also played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes. The persistence of inequality and the specific institutional arrangements in African societies influenced by British rule meant that educational gains did not uniformly lead to sustained economic development. This suggests that while colonial education policies were a significant factor, they operated within a complex web of institutional and socio-economic conditions that ultimately determined long-term growth trajectories.

The centrality of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the shared finding that institutions influence both property rights and the cost of transactions (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth because it fosters secure property rights, a critical factor for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

However, while Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions in perpetuating economic stagnation, they acknowledge that these institutions are often designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights a key disagreement: whether institutions are inherently extractive or can be reformed to support development.

The historical evidence from colonial policies shows that the presence of European settlers significantly influenced institutional outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups, with some colonies benefiting from rule-of-law-enforcing institutions while others were subject to extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical comparison settles the disagreement by showing that the nature of institutions is shaped by specific historical contexts and colonial policies.
