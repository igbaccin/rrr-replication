The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, as it addresses why some societies have developed economically while others stagnate. The stakes are high because the answer determines whether policy should focus on building particular types of institutions to foster growth. One live disagreement centers around how colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes, with scholars debating whether these institutions were extractive or conducive to long-term development. Another debate revolves around the extent to which institutions matter for economic growth compared to other factors such as geography and culture. The question also extends to whether institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth can provide crucial insights into economic policy and development strategies, making it a critical area of study. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The creation of financial institutions and new financial instruments were pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). A critical example is the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, which significantly lowered the cost of transacting. This financial revolution reflected an enhanced security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up as falling costs per transaction; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This is because increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. Evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this, showing that institutional changes were not so revolutionary after all (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of these institutions was remarkable, as shown by the evidence from capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets-not simply the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that innovations and the expansion of useful knowledge are more fundamental than institutions in fostering long-term economic development. Technological progress requires institutional adjustments to efficiently use new ideas and innovations; it also necessitates ideological changes for its effective integration into society. For example, Kuznets highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This compatibility issue underscores the need for societal shifts in both institutions and ideologies to accommodate technological advancements.

Mokyr builds on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge; he notes that coordination failures can hinder economic improvement, but institutional progress complements technological progress to ensure sustainability. Without such complementary progress, Mokyr warns that rent-seeking behaviors could erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). McCloskey further elaborates on the ideological aspect, suggesting that changes in societal rhetoric and tolerance for experimentation are crucial for fostering an environment where new techniques and institutions can thrive; he argues that these changes are more important than institutional adjustments. For example, McCloskey points out that societies began to permit open-source discussions about experiments after 1700, which facilitated the uptake of new technologies (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This ideological shift enabled a more permissive environment for innovation and economic growth.

McCloskey also acknowledges that religious leaders who initially opposed certain changes eventually adapted their views. By the late nineteenth century, even the Pope favored "capitalism" over socialism (McCloskey 2015, p.4), illustrating how ideologies can evolve to support economic progress. This evolution highlights the dynamic nature of ideological shifts and their role in supporting long-term economic growth.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn and Austin both suggest that the legacy of these slave trades has been particularly detrimental to African economic development. This is due to their impact on institutional structures, which are critical for long-term growth. The slave trades led to a depletion of human capital, as well as the destruction of social and political institutions that could have supported economic progress (Nunn 2008, p.2). Austin further argues that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic dynamics, indicating that the institutional environment was not uniformly hostile to development (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of these slave trade impacts is also evident in the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates, which leaves significant gaps in understanding long-term economic trends and the structural effects on African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts between elites and non-elites shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is crucial for understanding the long-term impact of institutions on inequality and development. The mechanisms underlying these distributional conflicts reveal that institutions were often designed to maintain elite power structures by restricting competition, which in turn influenced economic growth trajectories. Ogilvie's focus on serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds illustrates how these institutions were shaped more by political struggles than by efficiency considerations. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, where institutions evolved differently due to varying degrees of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In societies with high initial levels of inequality, such as those in Latin America, institutions were structured to benefit elites at the expense of broader economic development. This dynamic highlights how institutional design can perpetuate existing inequalities and hinder growth over the long term by limiting opportunities for non-elite groups to participate fully in the economy. Ogilvie's work thus complements the broader understanding of institutions' role in shaping inequality, suggesting that the persistence of certain institutions may reflect a history of distributional conflicts rather than purely economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share that the literature on economic development in Africa under colonial rule explores various factors influencing public goods provision and institutional change. Missionaries played a significant role in establishing formal education systems in Africa during colonial rule, with British rule being particularly conducive to educational development. This was due to the altered benefits of formal education under new social and political institutions, which influenced both macro-level decisions by African political leaders and micro-level choices by families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The institutional framework set up by colonial powers thus had a lasting impact on human capital formation, which is a key driver of economic growth. However, the effectiveness of these institutions varied across different regions and periods, contingent upon local endowments, transport costs, and political dynamics (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), highlighting that while institutions are important, their impact is mediated by other contextual factors.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that women played crucial roles in African economic growth both before colonial rule and during it. Women's contributions were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, but their efforts propelled significant economic developments. For example, the migration of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in search of farmland for cocoa production transformed the Gold Coast into a leading cocoa producer by 1911 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This underscores that women's roles were pivotal to agricultural development during colonial periods. However, Nunn and Wantchekon note that the legacy of the slave trade has had enduring effects on trust within African societies, this mistrust persists today and is a significant factor in contemporary economic dynamics (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances, further marginalizing women's access to land for cash crops. This legal framework solidified the subordinate status of women, limiting their economic agency and contributions during colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The implications are profound: the mistrust engendered by the slave trade, combined with the institutional barriers faced by women, has created a complex web of challenges that continue to shape African development. This interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutions highlights how deeply rooted cultural factors can influence long-term economic trajectories.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) share a focus on specific institutional dynamics in Africa without directly addressing long-run economic growth (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). The two authors jointly identify budget policies as a key mechanism through which institutions shape development trajectories. Simson (2019, p.2) reveals that East African post-independence budgets were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, with spending patterns reflecting regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). However, these expenditures were often directed towards consumption rather than public goods. Peters (2004, p.19) shows that the apparent flexibility of land systems in Africa conceals processes of exclusion and class formation. This suggests that budget policies and land systems interact to shape economic outcomes. The implication is that while budgetary allocations may not be as inefficient as previously thought, the underlying dynamics of land distribution can exacerbate inequalities. This interplay between budget policies and land systems underscores how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonialism shaped long-run economic outcomes through the establishment of divergent institutional frameworks. These institutions varied based on European mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as tropical Africa, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic growth: the extractive institutions in high-mortality regions hindered investment and economic activity. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that these institutions persist today and significantly affect current income per capita.

These findings are supported by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) analysis of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. They argue that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than geographic factors. This suggests that the long-term economic impacts of colonial institutions are deeply rooted in their persistent effects on economic activity and investment patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between colonial policies, institutional frameworks, and economic outcomes.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) share the view that long-term economic performance is influenced by collaborative research efforts and historical events such as the Black Death. van Zanden's work highlights how institutions that guarantee fair execution of apprentice contracts, such as guilds in western Europe, played a crucial role in the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This institutional framework helped mitigate costs associated with training, thereby influencing the skill premium and long-term economic performance. Collaborative efforts have allowed scholars to build on Angus Maddison's work, improving estimates of national accounts and reducing margins of error in historical economic data. Pamuk (2007, p.23) provides a nuanced view by arguing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This perspective underscores how historical events can have enduring effects on economic structures, complementing van Zanden's focus on institutional impacts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented by several scholars who emphasize that institutions act as fundamental determinants of economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the development of institutions in colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, shaped the economic trajectories of these regions over centuries. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) asserts that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. This perspective underscores how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder long-term economic development.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had lasting effects on the relative incomes of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. This aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). These findings collectively illustrate how historical institutional arrangements have profound and enduring impacts on economic structures.

The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes is further elucidated by their influence on colonial policies and subsequent economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This perspective is complemented by North's argument that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These insights highlight how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder long-term economic development, depending on their design and implementation.

The agreement across these sources is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. This is evident in Acemoglu et al.'s argument that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which these institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) argues that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting a more dynamic interplay between local contexts and institutional changes than Acemoglu et al.'s focus on extractive institutions alone.

The historical evidence of varying urbanization rates and institutional flexibility in different regions settles this disagreement. These variations show that while institutions are pivotal, their impact is mediated by broader historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.15).
