The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is an understanding of the fundamental drivers of prosperity over centuries. One live disagreement concerns whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. On one side, some scholars argue that institutions emerge to facilitate efficient outcomes, maximizing productivity and growth. Others posit that institutional arrangements often reflect power dynamics and the interests of dominant groups, prioritizing wealth distribution and political control over economic efficiency. The debate is not merely academic: it has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies worldwide.

These divergent views are evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which in turn significantly influenced current income per capita. These institutions were designed to either enforce the rule of law and encourage investment or to extract resources rapidly for the metropole's benefit. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that institutional arrangements can be deeply rooted in power dynamics and social structures, as well as economic considerations.

The role of institutions in long-run growth is further complicated by the interplay between historical contexts and contemporary outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) highlight how European settlers' presence or absence influenced colonial policies, leading to divergent institutional paths that persist today.

Austin (2008, p.2) also addresses the complexity of historical periods and paths, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical experiences. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time, shaped by both internal and external factors. Austin's work complements Acemoglu et al.'s findings by emphasizing the importance of considering multiple historical periods and paths when analyzing institutional impacts on economic growth.

The discussion around institutions and long-run economic growth is also enriched by the insights from North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6). North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. He highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on institutional frameworks that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment.

The idea that institutions are central to long-run economic growth is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, helping to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This suggests that understanding and addressing institutional failures is crucial for development strategies in regions with historical institutional shortcomings.

The relationship between institutions and long-run growth extends beyond efficiency considerations to include the broader social and political contexts in which institutions operate. This theme carries forward into the next section, where we will explore how different theoretical frameworks can help understand the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic performance is crucial to understanding structural changes in Europe (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Formal institutions like guilds were pivotal in ensuring fair execution of apprentice contracts, which influenced economic outcomes. These institutions mitigated risks associated with training costs and barriers to entry into skilled trades. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) emphasizes that the skill premium, or the remuneration for investing in human capital, was determined by factors such as training costs, completion rates, interest rates, and the duration of skilled employment (van Zanden 2009, p.3). The guilds' role in managing these factors highlights how institutional frameworks can facilitate long-term economic growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies this perspective by noting that while these institutions were beneficial, they also had monopolistic tendencies that could restrict entry into trades and keep wages above market-clearing levels. This dual nature of institutional effects underscores the complexity of their role in economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.9).

This pivotal evolution was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth through the governance of public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). This institutional development is particularly evident through the creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments, which significantly reduced transaction costs. This evolution was driven by fiscal crises, notably the Hundred Years' War, which led to a trade-off where certain rights were ceded to Parliament in exchange for revenues. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement should manifest as falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector relative to GNP (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast qualify these implications by noting that while England's institutional changes were significant, they argue against overstating their revolutionary nature. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic development and highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how different factors interact to shape long-run economic growth.

This argument shifts focus from efficiency to distributional conflicts as a driving force behind economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Peters 2004, p.19). They suggest that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped more by struggles over resources and power than by any drive for economic efficiency. Peters builds on this idea by showing that reports of flexible land systems in Africa often mask underlying processes of exclusion and class formation, which are driven by distributional conflicts (Peters 2004, p.5). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are merely efficient responses to economic conditions; instead, it suggests that they are deeply rooted in social and political dynamics. This focus on distributional conflicts does not negate the importance of cultural factors. Ogilvie notes that while shared beliefs contribute to institutional structures, they do not fully account for the complexities of institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This nuanced view underscores how different factors, including both distributional struggles and cultural beliefs, interact to shape economic institutions over the long run.

This culture of mistrust, rooted in historical events like the slave trade, continues to impact trust levels and economic development today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon note that this mistrust is rooted deeply within African societies, influencing both internal norms and external environments. This internal channel, however, is at least twice as large as the external one. The implications are profound for economic development, given the established importance of trust in fostering growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).) However, the persistence of this mistrust is not uniform across all African societies. Nunn argues that the impact of slave trades on economic development varied significantly depending on the pre-existing political and social institutions of different regions. For instance, centralized governments with well-developed markets were more resilient to the adverse effects of the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while mistrust is a significant barrier, the institutional framework in place can mitigate some of its detrimental effects.

This shaping of economic outcomes through institutional frameworks highlights the enduring influence of colonial policies in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.2) show that colonial governments often adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political actions, indicating a degree of responsiveness to indigenous needs. This adaptability was not uniform; Frankema (2012, p.15) illustrates how British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The development of formal education under British rule altered the perceived benefits of education for African families and political leaders, creating new incentives for economic participation. This institutional framework laid the groundwork for post-colonial economic policies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Colonial institutions also created enduring challenges; Simson (2019, p.2) highlights how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were marked by significant military spending and debt burdens (Simson 2019, p.11), which diverted resources from public goods and services. This pattern underscores the complex legacy of colonial institutions on African economic development, where initial gains in education and infrastructure were offset by later fiscal strains and military expenditures.

This exploration reveals how different colonization strategies led to varying economic outcomes in former European colonies, influenced by the institutions they established (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established by colonial powers was heavily influenced by the mortality rates of Europeans in these regions. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term resource extraction, whereas low mortality rates facilitated the establishment of institutions that encouraged investment and economic development. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were established to efficiently exploit resources. In areas with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. For instance, Acemoglu et al. document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is attributable to these institutional changes rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Acemoglu et al. also highlight how population density and prosperity influenced European policies during colonization, suggesting that the interaction between institutional frameworks and industrial opportunities played a central role in shaping long-term development trajectories(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This nuanced view underscores how colonial institutions not only shaped immediate economic outcomes but also set the stage for future development paths, influencing the ability of colonies to industrialize and grow over time.

This literature underscores how specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty may not directly address the fundamental outcome of long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins observes that African governments can develop progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity, suggesting that the quality of public goods and rule of law are not necessarily determined by ethnolinguistic fragmentation but by governance structures. This is reinforced by Frankema and van Waijenburg's exploration of real wage trends in British African colonies, which indicates that poverty in Africa has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments alone (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Their work suggests that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across colonial Africa can provide insights into future growth possibilities. Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa has gained attention from development specialists by emphasizing the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This focus on poverty reduction may overlook broader structural changes needed for sustained long-term growth.

This investigation into government policies highlights how economic inequality is shaped by various factors, including the role of women in economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). They argue that institutions can perpetuate inequality through the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This institutional framework not only restricted economic opportunities for women but also shaped broader societal structures that perpetuated inequality. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in how they influence the distribution of resources and opportunities, which in turn affects economic outcomes over extended periods. These findings highlight that while institutions may facilitate growth, they can simultaneously reinforce existing inequalities, particularly along gender lines (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

This intertwined relationship underscores how technological advancements drive long-run economic growth by fostering useful knowledge that propels development (Mokyr 2005; Kuznets 1973). Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is corroborated by Kuznets, who asserts that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. The interplay between technology and institutions is crucial, as it allows societies to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes without weakening their political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Broadberry and Gupta further illustrate this dynamic by showing that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, reinforcing the idea that technological advancements are fundamental to long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Kuznets also notes that the pace at which technology and economic growth advanced differed over centuries and among regions of the world. This variability underscores the complex interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks, highlighting that while technological advancements are key drivers of economic growth, their impact is mediated by local conditions and institutional adaptations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intertwined with the pace and nature of technological advancements.

Bolt and van Zanden highlight the need for collaborative research to refine estimates of economic performance over time (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). Feinstein and Thomas also underscore this point. Their work has been foundational in estimating margins of error in economic data, a critical aspect of collaborative efforts to improve historical economic measurements (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This is particularly relevant for periods with sparse or incomplete data, where assumptions about the links between available data points and broader economic activities are necessary. This ongoing refinement is essential for understanding the complex interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks in shaping economic performance over centuries.

This pivotal role of ideas and ideologies highlights their significance in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.3) and Mokyr (2005, p.49). They assert that the ideological shift permitted experimentation and open discourse, fostering an environment conducive to technological advancements. This ideological transformation enabled societies to embrace new techniques and institutions, such as the electric motor or modern research universities. McCloskey (2015, p.3) contends that this change in rhetoric was crucial because it allowed people to experiment freely and share their findings openly, which was essential for economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This open-source approach to innovation contrasts with the secrecy and fear of disapproval that had previously hindered progress. The shift towards a more tolerant society where unconventional ideas could be proposed without violent repercussions facilitated intellectual innovation. Mokyr (2005, p.49) supports this view by highlighting that economic change depends significantly on what people believe, emphasizing the role of tolerant societies in allowing for the free exchange of ideas. This ideological environment was instrumental in the Industrial Enlightenment, which McCloskey (2015, p.3) describes as a period where new ideas could flourish without fear of theological or political disapproval (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This open discourse was not universally accepted; Pope Pius IX's condemnation of progress and liberalism in 1864 illustrates the resistance to these ideological changes. Despite such resistance, by the late nineteenth century, even religious leaders began to favor capitalism over socialism, indicating a broader acceptance of the new economic ideologies (McCloskey 2015, p.4). This acceptance underscores how deeply rooted ideas can shape long-term economic growth, even in the face of initial resistance.

The enduring debate about institutions' role in long-run economic growth finds common ground in their fundamental importance to development. Institutions are critical because they shape property rights and enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This is evident in the historical context of the Bank of England's establishment, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain contested. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and efficient enforcement as key drivers of long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). Others argue that distributional conflicts play a more significant role in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical evidence from the colonial period provides a compelling case study. The divergent institutional paths of European colonies, particularly those with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates, illustrate how initial conditions can lead to extractive institutions that persist over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These contrasting outcomes underscore the enduring impact of early institutional choices on long-term economic trajectories.

The key evidence that settles this debate is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This phenomenon shows that institutions introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors alone, were pivotal in shaping economic development paths (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
