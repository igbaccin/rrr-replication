The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. One live disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms linking institutions to growth. Some scholars argue that institutions shape incentives for investment, which are essential for development. Others propose that institutions themselves are shaped by broader cultural and historical forces, making them less deterministic in their impact on growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one factor among many. This question is important because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development. Understanding the causal role of institutions can guide efforts to create effective governance structures that support long-term prosperity.

The first stream of scholarship focuses on how different institutional frameworks have historically influenced economic outcomes in various regions. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions ranged from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that the institutionalist approach emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth but struggles with quantifying this relationship. This stream underscores how the legacies of colonial institutions can persist and shape economic trajectories over centuries.

The second stream examines the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, particularly in the context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. Hopkins contends that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He emphasizes that 'good' institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

The third stream explores the role of trust and cultural factors in mediating the relationship between institutions and economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This perspective suggests that cultural factors can significantly influence the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize the importance of credible commitment underpinning the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This stream underscores how cultural and historical factors can shape the effectiveness of institutions in promoting long-term economic development.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with high-mortality areas receiving extractive institutions designed to exploit resources rapidly (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework persisted and significantly influenced current income per capita by creating an environment unfavorable for investment and economic development.

One specific mechanism through which these colonial institutions shaped long-term economic outcomes is the establishment of property rights. North highlights that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This suggests that while secure property rights are beneficial, the specific design and enforcement of these institutions can significantly alter their impact on economic development.

Austin further nuances this perspective by arguing that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability shows that colonial institutions had varied impacts based on local conditions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies. These institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by concentrating wealth and power among a small group, which in turn shaped economic development. The legacy of inequality and mistrust continues to influence economic development by undermining social cohesion and hindering collective action. The impact is multifaceted: it not only affects individual behavior but also influences broader societal norms, which are crucial for economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This suggests that while addressing external institutions is important, changing deeply ingrained cultural norms may be a more significant challenge in fostering economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced. This commitment to institutional enforcement is a key mechanism through which economic development unfolds: credible commitments to enforce property rights create a stable environment for investment and economic activity. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutions, noting that these beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She argues that such shared beliefs can foster or hinder economic growth depending on their alignment with productive economic institutions. The implication is that simply having shared beliefs about property rights or other economic principles may not be sufficient; these beliefs need to translate into effective institutional frameworks that can enforce and protect those rights. This suggests that the interplay between cultural norms and institutional design is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of how these elements interact to drive long-run economic growth.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment. This dynamic interplay between exogenous factors like colonial policies and endogenous responses from African societies shaped institutional frameworks. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) caution that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies was tied to foreign trade and export markets, indicating that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played significant roles in determining growth patterns (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The implication is that while colonial rule introduced certain institutional frameworks, local adaptations and endogenous factors were crucial in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was evident well before 1800. This view challenges the notion of a late 18th-century 'great divergence' by highlighting early differences in productivity and development patterns. This productivity gain did not uniformly benefit all segments of society; instead, it created uneven economic growth where skilled labourers saw greater surpluses over basic food needs compared to unskilled labourers. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) reinforces this pattern by noting that economic development in Europe during the 16th and 17th centuries was concentrated around the North Sea countries, with England emerging as a high-cost economy after 1740 due to rising grain prices. This concentration of economic activity suggests that institutional changes, such as those facilitating merchant distribution and finance, were pivotal in driving early modern economic growth. However, van Zanden (1999, p.9) also notes that differences between English and Continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760, indicating that while institutional factors were important, they did not immediately translate into stark price differentials across regions. This suggests a nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth, where the impact of institutional changes may be gradual and subject to various mediating factors.

Institutional adjustments and technological advancements are crucial for efficient use of innovations in long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is pivotal for sustained growth. Technological progress alone does not guarantee economic development; it must be complemented by institutional changes, such as the modernization of state structures and societal ideologies, to resolve conflicts arising from growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This argument complements Mokyr's (2005, p.49) contention that useful knowledge drives economic growth. By focusing on the role of knowledge in shaping innovations and the importance of institutions in managing these changes, Mokyr suggests that institutional adjustments are essential for coordinating technological progress. He notes that without such coordination, rent-seeking behaviors could undermine economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights a nuanced relationship between technological advancements and institutional frameworks, where the latter facilitates the efficient use of innovations, thereby driving long-run economic growth. The role of institutions in managing conflicts induced by technological changes is particularly significant, as it ensures that societies can adapt to new technologies without excessive costs or political destabilization.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their capacity to facilitate technological progress and mitigate conflicts arising from such advancements. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development, depending on whether they are designed to maximize long-run growth or to extract resources for short-term gains. North's work on transaction costs and property rights further illustrates this point; he shows that institutions which lower the cost of transactions and secure property rights are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Institutions that provide a stable framework for market exchanges enable increasing specialization and division of labor, thereby promoting long-run economic development.

The historical context reveals that institutional frameworks can either support or undermine technological progress. Acemoglu et al. note that in some colonies, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional setup facilitated the efficient use of innovations, driving long-run economic growth. Similarly, North discusses how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The role of institutions in managing conflicts induced by technological changes is particularly significant. They ensure that societies can adapt to new technologies without excessive costs or political destabilization. Austin shows how colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic and political action, opting for higher production equilibria when it was in their interest (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive capacity of institutions is crucial for sustaining long-run economic growth.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions shape long-run economic growth are still debated. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize economic efficiency; instead, they often reflect power dynamics and rent-seeking behaviors. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the importance of understanding the political economy behind institutional design.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by empirical evidence on colonial outcomes and institutional reforms. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European colonization strategies led to vastly different institutional setups, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence from former European colonies and the empirical comparison of institutional setups in different regions settle the debate by demonstrating that institutions designed to enforce property rights and lower transaction costs are crucial for long-run economic growth.
