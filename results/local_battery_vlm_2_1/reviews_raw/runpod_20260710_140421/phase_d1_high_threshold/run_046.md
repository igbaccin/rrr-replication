The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with different camps advocating for various causal mechanisms. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, play a more significant role. One position posits that institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and ensure secure contracts, are crucial for fostering economic growth. This view is often juxtaposed with arguments that emphasize the importance of cultural beliefs, geographical endowments, or historical legacies in determining economic outcomes.

The disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions can be considered endogenous or exogenous to the economic development process. Some scholars argue that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural values and beliefs, while others contend that institutional frameworks themselves create the conditions for economic growth. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of why some countries thrive economically while others stagnate. Understanding the causal role of institutions in economic growth can inform strategies for development and poverty reduction.

The central theme of this review is to explore how different streams of scholarship have approached the question of institutional causality in long-run economic growth, specifically focusing on the mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. The first stream examines the direct impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and shaped current income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19), (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies; these changes were not always beneficial for the colonized populations.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that long-run economic growth is driven by specific institutional developments. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 was a pivotal moment that marked the "English financial revolution" because it significantly reduced transaction costs, which are critical to fostering both capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This transformation reflects an increased security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for long-term economic stability. The fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War prompted this institutional change by necessitating a trade-off where certain rights were ceded to Parliament in exchange for revenues, highlighting the political underpinnings of such developments (North 1989, p.9). This historical context underscores that institutions are dynamic entities shaped by and responsive to economic and political pressures. The evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England further illustrates this point; these institutions were crucial for establishing secure property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of institutional changes on economic performance can be nuanced. Recent research has shown that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was almost at parity with Britain's, suggesting that institutional differences alone did not determine economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This implies that while institutions play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political regimes also contribute to this complex interplay.

McCloskey and Mokyr share that ideas and ideologies drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.3). Their shared finding is that the Industrial Enlightenment was catalyzed by societies embracing open discourse and experimentation. This ideological shift allowed for the uptake of new techniques and institutions, as people were finally permitted to experiment and discuss their findings without fear of disapproval. McCloskey elaborates that this change in societal rhetoric enabled individuals to engage in open-source fashion about experiments (McCloskey 2015, p.3). Mokyr's work shows that intellectual innovation could only occur in tolerant societies where unconventional ideas were not met with violent responses against "heresy" and "apostasy" (McCloskey 2015, p.4). This ideological environment fostered economic growth by encouraging innovation and the adoption of new technologies. These ideological shifts were crucial; their impact varied across all regions. For instance, McCloskey shows that even as late as 1864, Pope Pius IX condemned progress, liberalism, and modern civilization in his Syllabus of Errors (McCloskey 2015, p.4). This demonstrates that the acceptance of new ideas varied significantly by region and religious influence. The implications of this are clear: while ideological shifts were pivotal for economic growth, their effects were contingent on local contexts and prevailing power structures.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr emphasizes the role of technological progress, while Kuznets highlights the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. The mechanism for technological progress is clear: new technologies improve productivity and spur growth by enabling more efficient production methods. This process is not automatic; it requires institutions that facilitate the adoption and diffusion of these technologies. Kuznets notes that "modern technology" demands significant societal shifts, including changes in family structure and attitudes toward nature (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Institutions play a crucial role in managing these transitions, as they can help resolve growth-induced conflicts without undermining societal cohesion.

Mokyr cautions that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic improvement. He points out that "without institutional progress to complement the technological progress," the benefits of new technologies may be limited or even reversed (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutions in supporting and sustaining technological advancements. Kuznets echoes this sentiment, noting that modern economic growth is possible only if societal conflicts are resolved peacefully, a task that falls to strong national states (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The implication is clear: while technological progress drives economic growth, it must be supported by robust institutions capable of managing the social and political challenges that arise from rapid change.

Nunn and Wantchekon establish that slave trades had an adverse effect on Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trade created an environment of mistrust within African societies, which continues to affect socio-economic outcomes. For instance, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). This mistrust has had long-lasting effects on economic development. The slave trade's impact on trust is significant: it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on intergroup trust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors play crucial roles in this dynamic, with internal norms being at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). The importance of trust for economic development is well-documented. For example, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) cite studies that highlight the role of trust in facilitating economic growth, international trade, political stability, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This underscores how the legacy of mistrust from the slave trade continues to shape Africa's economic landscape.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and their long-term effects on growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds is better explained by distributional struggles than efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) similarly argue that New World societies with greater inequality saw institutions evolve to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality through government policies. These institutional arrangements reflect power dynamics and optimal economic design. For instance, the persistence of guilds in Europe can be linked to the political influence of artisans who benefited from restrictive practices (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The same logic applies to serfdom, where landlords' interests were protected at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This institutional framework had long-term implications for economic development, as it constrained innovation and hindered the emergence of more productive economic activities. This perspective on distributional conflicts has qualifiers. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges that while institutions may arise from power struggles, their impact on growth can be nuanced. For instance, guilds might have stifled competition but also provided training and quality control mechanisms that benefited certain sectors. This highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, where distributional conflicts shape institutions that in turn influence long-term growth trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern resource allocation and market interactions. North argues that institutions reduce transaction costs, facilitating economic exchanges and fostering growth (North 1989, p.6). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups, with some promoting investment and rule of law while others focused on extractive practices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The impact of institutions on long-run growth is further illuminated by the role of credible commitments in economic development. North and Weingast show that secure property rights, underpinned by credible commitments, are crucial for sustained economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which highlight how institutions designed to maximize rents for colonizers often hinder long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The complexity of institutional design is also evident in the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie notes that while institutions may arise from power struggles, their impact on growth can be nuanced, with some providing benefits such as training and quality control despite stifling competition (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s observation that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development but rather serve the interests of politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence from colonial experiences shows that institutions shaped by European colonizers had lasting effects on economic outcomes. For instance, the establishment of extractive states in colonies like the Congo differed from the more inclusive institutions set up in places like the United States and Australia (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon's work complements this by demonstrating how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting economic development through both external and internal channels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The precise historical evidence settles remaining disagreements regarding institutions' role in shaping long-run economic growth. The measurement of income per capita across former European colonies reveals that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in determining relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This empirical comparison underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic trajectories, highlighting how initial conditions and institutional setups can have lasting effects on development outcomes.
