The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. A key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes versus other factors such as geography, culture, or initial endowments. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, with geographical factors like climate and disease playing a more significant role in shaping economic development. The debate also extends to whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, influencing how policy interventions can effectively promote long-term prosperity. This discussion is pivotal for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the efficacy of contemporary development strategies.

The reduction of transaction costs and the establishment of secure property rights through financial innovations were pivotal in Europe's historical economic trajectory. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29). One critical mechanism through which these institutions operated was the reduction of transaction costs. This reduction facilitated market exchanges by enhancing efficiency, security, and predictability in economic interactions. The development of institutions like the Bank of England exemplifies this process; its establishment led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was crucial for fostering long-term capital markets and overall economic growth.

They argue that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by developments in capital markets. The political factors underpinning economic growth encompassed both the rules governing economic exchange and how these rules were enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This highlights the importance of credible commitment to property rights and institutional stability.

The implications of this perspective are substantial for understanding long-run economic growth in Europe. This nuanced view suggests that while financial institutions and secure property rights were critical drivers of economic growth, the broader institutional environment played a complex role in shaping these outcomes.

Some scholars argue that technological progress and intellectual developments are significant drivers of long-run growth, more so than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that the interplay between technology and institutional frameworks significantly influences economic outcomes. The introduction of new technologies necessitates adjustments in societal structures, including family patterns and ideological shifts. For instance, Kuznets notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large extended families, requiring substantial changes for effective integration (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This dynamic indicates that institutions are crucial and must adapt to technological progress, driving it in tandem.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes the growth of useful knowledge as a critical factor in economic development. He argues that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements alone cannot sustain long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors that Mokyr discusses underscore the necessity for institutions to support technological progress effectively. This interplay between technology and institutions highlights that while technological innovations are essential, their impact on economic growth is contingent upon an adaptive institutional environment.

This perspective implies that countries with robust frameworks for knowledge diffusion and technological adoption may experience more sustained economic growth, even if their initial institutional settings are not optimal. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting the crucial role of the modern national state in resolving conflicts arising from technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are important, their effectiveness is contingent upon their ability to adapt and support technological progress.

The implications of these findings underscore the importance of both technological advancements and adaptive institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) present a contrasting view, emphasizing distributional conflicts as the primary drivers behind core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates this by examining pre-industrial Europe's institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds, attributing them to struggles over wealth and power. This perspective is bolstered by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that inequality in New World societies led to institutional structures that favoured elites, thereby shaping economic development through policies restricting competition. The downstream implications of these distributional conflicts are significant: the persistence of inequality can impede long-term economic growth by locking in institutions that benefit a few at the expense of broader societal progress. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also observes that these institutions' impact is not uniform; their effects vary based on interactions with other socio-economic factors and beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Austin challenges this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by conflating different historical periods and trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This approach overlooks the complex interactions between institutions, economic policies, and local responses over time. Austin notes that colonial institutions did not always follow a uniform path; instead, they adapted in response to indigenous economic actions and political incentives. For example, colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent from African labour markets (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn highlights the importance of pre-colonial factors, such as the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of official colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). The sustained trade in regions with centralized governments and developed markets, like the Kongo kingdom, illustrates how pre-existing institutions could facilitate economic engagement. Nunn also recognizes that local societies' small size and undifferentiated institutions initially impeded foreign trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This nuanced view suggests that African development was shaped by a complex interplay of long-term historical factors, institutional adaptations, and indigenous responses to colonial policies.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa underscores how targeted changes can alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people by improving governance frameworks (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) illustrates this by noting that while some African societies are ethnically homogeneous but dysfunctional, others are heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This finding underscores that institutional quality is not predetermined by ethnic composition; instead, it is shaped by governance structures that can be improved through targeted reforms. They argue that a better grasp of these historical determinants can inform policy recommendations aimed at alleviating poverty. Hopkins acknowledges that while institutions matter for providing incentives for investment and development, the new economic history of Africa must also consider the broader context of governance (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is crucial because it highlights that institutional reforms are not just about economic policies but also about creating a governance framework that supports long-term development.

Acemoglu et al. and others (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) examine how colonial strategies shaped institutions in former European colonies, impacting long-run economic growth. They find that Europeans adopted diverse colonization approaches which led to different institutional outcomes. In areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole rather than invest in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where institutional frameworks were not conducive to long-term economic growth. These variations in colonial strategies had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these regions, with extractive institutions hindering growth while inclusive ones fostered development.

The persistence of these institutional legacies is crucial for understanding current economic disparities. This dynamic is particularly relevant in former colonies, where institutions were designed to maximize benefits for European colonists, not promote sustainable growth. The interaction between these institutional frameworks and the opportunity to industrialize during the 19th century played a central role in shaping the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical perspective underscores that while colonial strategies initially determined institutional outcomes, the enduring impact of these institutions continues to influence economic growth patterns today.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North shows that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework supports individual investments and fosters a broader economic environment conducive to growth, as seen through the development of financial instruments like those introduced by the Bank of England (North 1989, p.9).

The interaction between institutions and economic opportunities is particularly evident in colonial contexts. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, leading to extractive states in regions with high mortality and more inclusive institutions where Europeans settled permanently (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by colonial strategies, continues to influence current income levels and growth patterns.

The historical evidence from former colonies underscores that while initial institutional outcomes were determined by colonial policies, the enduring impact of these institutions shapes contemporary economic realities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He emphasizes the need to consider the complex interplay between institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over extended periods (Austin 2008, p.2).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of former colonies with high mortality rates versus those where Europeans settled permanently. This comparison shows that extractive institutions in regions with high mortality led to persistent economic underdevelopment, whereas inclusive institutions fostered growth and development. The measurement of current income per capita across these regions provides empirical support for the enduring impact of colonial-era institutional frameworks on long-run economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15).
