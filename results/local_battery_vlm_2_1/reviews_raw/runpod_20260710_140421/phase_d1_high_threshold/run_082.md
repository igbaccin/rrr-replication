The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense debate among economists and historians alike. The stakes are high because identifying the causal mechanisms underlying sustained economic development can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering growth in both developed and developing economies. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations, such as geographic factors or cultural beliefs. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, while others point to distributional conflicts and the influence of colonial policies. The interplay between these factors and their relative significance in determining long-run economic outcomes remains a contentious issue.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, had significant impacts on current income per capita by influencing investment and economic development trajectories. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutional designs can either facilitate or hinder economic development.

North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, which are critical for a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. He notes that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast further emphasise the significance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for this purpose, highlighting how political factors underpinning economic growth include both the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They identify the creation of the Bank of England as an institution that significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional development was not merely a response to economic needs but also a product of political exigencies, notably the fiscal crises stemming from the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by evidence from capital markets, which shows that institutional changes in England were critical for securing property rights and fostering economic growth. This suggests that while specific institutions like the Bank of England played a pivotal role, the broader institutional framework governing public choice was equally important in shaping long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. Mokyr's argument aligns with Kuznets' assertion that technological progress has been a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods, spreading to substantial parts of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This shared finding underscores the role of technological innovation in driving economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) points out that technological advancements require complementary institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without such coordination, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited and could ultimately be undermined by rent-seeking behavior.

Mokyr's insights align with Kuznets' observation that modern technology requires significant institutional adjustments. These changes are essential for efficiently utilizing innovations within societies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that the resolution of conflicts arising from technological progress is crucial for maintaining political stability, which in turn supports economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements drive economic growth, the institutional framework must adapt to support these changes. The ability of a society to resolve growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs is vital for sustaining long-term economic development.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments highlights the complex dynamics at work in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) identify a more positive impact of British colonial rule on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. They find that the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education at both macro and micro levels, which drove demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework facilitated growth in export production, which was tied closely to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This growth pattern varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This indicates that while British colonial rule had a more positive impact on education in sub-Saharan Africa, the effectiveness of these institutions depended on local conditions and adaptations. The interplay between colonial institutions and local factors shaped the trajectory of economic growth in the region, underscoring the role of institutional adaptation in driving long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates was a critical factor in shaping long-run economic development. This variation in institutional design had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al.'s work underscores how the persistence of these colonial-era institutions continues to influence current economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic performance.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies African economic development by compressing different historical periods into a single narrative. This simplification obscures how colonial institutions interacted with local conditions to shape economic outcomes.

This interaction is evident in Austin's observation that colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on indigenous economic activities (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, in areas without European settlers competing for African labor, colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production to enhance fiscal security and political stability. This dynamic relationship suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was more nuanced than a straightforward extraction narrative would suggest.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further qualify this picture by highlighting the importance of pre-colonial economic conditions in shaping post-colonial outcomes, which can be seen as an indirect influence on institutional adaptation. They reconstruct real wage levels to show that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon but rather a product of specific historical and geographical conditions. This nuanced view challenges simplistic causal narratives by illustrating how local economic activities influenced colonial policies, thereby shaping long-term development paths in complex ways.

This perspective underscores the need for more granular analyses that consider the diverse trajectories of African economies under colonial rule.

Distributional conflicts are central to understanding economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the persistence of serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds can be explained by the power dynamics between different social groups rather than efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that these institutional structures, which emerged from the extreme inequality present at the outset of colonization, persisted over time. This persistence is critical because it shaped long-term development paths by influencing subsequent institutional evolution. However, this perspective does not negate the role of institutions in economic growth; rather, it highlights how institutions are themselves shaped by underlying conflicts and inequalities (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Institutions play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the evolution of property rights and transaction costs. North shows that secure property rights are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9). He argues that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that extractive institutions, which do not encourage economic development, can persist because they maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions are shaped to benefit the ruling elite rather than promote long-term growth.

The persistence of these institutional structures over time is a key factor in determining long-run development paths. North and Weingast highlight that credible commitment underpins economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England where political factors bound the regime to secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional evolution is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate how differences in European colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, with some colonies adopting extractive states that transferred resources rapidly to the metropole rather than investing in long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that institutions are themselves shaped by underlying conflicts and inequalities.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the historical context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions were not primarily driven by efficiency but rather by the need to address social tensions and maintain power structures. Austin supports this view by noting that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights how institutions evolve in response to local conditions and conflicts, further shaping long-run economic growth.

The shared finding across these sources is that institutions matter for economic growth and that they are shaped by underlying conflicts and inequalities (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which these institutions are driven by efficiency versus distributional conflicts. Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local conditions, indicating a more dynamic role for institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). This is settled by evidence from capital markets: North and Weingast show that the success of seventeenth-century England's economic growth was underpinned by political factors that bound the regime to secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), demonstrating how institutional evolution can foster long-run development.
