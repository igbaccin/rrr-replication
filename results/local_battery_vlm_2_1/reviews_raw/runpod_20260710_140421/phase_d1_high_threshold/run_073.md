The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, as it addresses whether and how political and social structures can foster prosperity over extended periods. At stake is the extent to which institutional frameworks can be designed or reformed to promote sustained economic advancement. One live disagreement centers around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions matter by providing secure property rights and enforceable contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Some scholars assert that distributional conflicts play a more significant role than efficiency considerations in molding core economic institutions such as serfdom or guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Still others propose a historical contingency: the specific institutional arrangements of colonial powers were key determinants of long-run growth. This debate reflects broader questions about whether institutions can be engineered to promote development or if they are path-dependent and resistant to change.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional frameworks established by European colonizers were pivotal in determining the economic trajectories of their colonies. These institutions ranged from inclusive, which encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, to extractive, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. One key mechanism through which these institutions operated was mortality rates among Europeans: high mortality led to extractive institutions, while low mortality fostered more inclusive ones. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the interaction between these colonial institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century was crucial in shaping the long-term development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings suggest that the initial conditions set by colonial powers, particularly their institutional choices, had enduring impacts on economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the profound and lasting influence of these institutions on economic growth, illustrating how historical institutional frameworks can shape contemporary economic performance.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and new financial instruments were crucial in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 was a pivotal moment in this regard, marking a significant decline in transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights over time. The rise of these financial institutions occurred within the context of a broader political and economic transformation that influenced England's path toward economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize the role of credible commitment in this process, highlighting how the regime's adherence to rules governing economic exchange fostered a stable environment for market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their analysis indicates that institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were less revolutionary than previously believed. This perspective challenges the notion that institutional development is a singular determinant of economic success and suggests that other factors, such as bureaucratic structures and political stability, also play significant roles. The nuanced view of financial institutions' role in economic growth underscores the complex interplay between political and economic factors.

Ogilvie and other scholars posit that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were influenced by distributional conflicts as well as efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The guilds of medieval Europe exemplify this dynamic. These organizations, which regulated craftsmanship and trade, emerged from the struggle between different social classes over economic resources and political power. Guilds restricted competition to protect the interests of their members, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman who demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These policies restricted competition and contributed to persistent inequality. Ogilvie advises against overstating the role of distributional conflicts. She notes that shared beliefs also play a crucial role in shaping institutions, although they are not institutions themselves (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they do not operate in isolation from other social and cultural influences.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that economic progress relies heavily on institutional adjustments, which facilitate the efficient use of innovations. This is complemented by Kuznets' (1973) observation that technological progress requires significant institutional and ideological changes to resolve conflicts arising from growth-induced disruptions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These changes are necessary for modern economic growth to be sustained over long periods. While Mokyr emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, Kuznets highlights how these adjustments help manage societal conflicts that emerge during technological transitions. This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for long-term economic development as it ensures that innovations are effectively integrated into the economy. For instance, he argues that without such institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements might be eroded by rent-seeking behavior and coordination failures. This underscores the necessity of robust institutions in harnessing technological progress for long-term economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African economic history is more complex than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Colonial administrations influenced commodity production by altering institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives. For example, Austin describes how colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent and when fiscal security was a priority (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic illustrates that institutions evolved in ways that supported economic activity in specific contexts and periods.

The role of colonial administrations in shaping long-run economic growth can be further understood by examining their impact on real wages. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct real wage levels across British African colonies from 1880 to 1965, revealing that poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon but rather varied significantly over time (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This finding underscores the importance of contextual factors in shaping economic outcomes. Austin's analysis complements this by showing how colonial institutions adapted to local conditions, highlighting the nuanced relationship between institutional change and economic growth.

The long-term effects of these adaptations remain debated. This responsiveness could have both positive and negative effects on long-term economic growth, depending on the specific historical and geographical conditions.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) argue that the institutional changes brought about by colonial rule in Africa had profound impacts on public goods provisioning. These changes were motivated by both exogenous factors and African initiatives, which influenced the development of formal education systems and altered the benefits associated with formal education. For example, Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights how British rule facilitated the growth of missionary education, which was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further elaborate on this dynamic by showing that the timing and scale of economic growth in export production were closely tied to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors collectively influenced the provision of public goods and the overall economic performance of African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Hopkins (2009, p.18) also observes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments was influenced by institutional functionality as well as ethnic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18), indicating that even ethnically homogeneous societies could develop progressive institutions. This points to a nuanced understanding of how institutional changes under colonial rule shaped long-run economic growth in Africa, where both exogenous and endogenous factors played critical roles. The dynamic interplay between these factors highlights the complexity of African economic development during the colonial period.

These persistent effects highlight the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) identify that the legacy of slavery includes an internalised mistrust among individuals, which is at least twice as large as external factors such as rule of law or trustworthiness of others. The persistence of this mistrust into contemporary African societies highlights its role in shaping economic development, as it impacts trust-based interactions essential for economic growth. This internalised mistrust can impede the formation of social capital and cooperation necessary for collective action and economic progress (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). The historical context provided by Nunn (2008, p.2) illustrates that centralized governance and well-developed markets, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, were instrumental in facilitating trade. This suggests that the absence of such institutions due to the disruptions caused by slave trades may have hindered Africa's economic development. The internalized mistrust resulting from the slave trade is a contemporary obstacle to African economic growth, affecting individuals' willingness to engage in trust-based transactions and collaborative efforts essential for modern economic activity.

Institutions have played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating trade and investment. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that secure property rights are central to economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Austin suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific impact of institutions on economic growth varies based on their nature. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage long-run growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further highlighted by their impact on trust and cooperation, which are essential for modern economic activity. The internalised mistrust stemming from the slave trade continues to affect individuals' willingness to engage in trust-based transactions and collaborative efforts essential for modern economic activity (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This institutional legacy of mistrust has contemporary implications for African economic growth. Hopkins argues that poor institutions are linked to Africa's dismal economic record, highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The historical evidence from centralized governance and well-developed markets in the Kongo kingdom illustrates that such institutions were instrumental in facilitating trade. This suggests that the absence of such institutions due to the disruptions caused by slave trades may have hindered Africa's economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15). The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints under which individuals and firms operate (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence of extractive versus inclusive institutions in former colonies resolves the disagreement. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage long-run growth in former colonies but instead maximized the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
