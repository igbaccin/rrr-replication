The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is understanding the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty over centuries. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological change play more decisive roles. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for sustained economic development. Others posit that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, where geographic endowments, cultural beliefs, or external shocks significantly influence long-term growth trajectories. The debate is further complicated by the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic processes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies and set up very different institutions in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment; in others, they created extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The debate on the role of institutions is also influenced by the broader question of whether institutions are shaped by economic factors or if they shape economic outcomes. North argues that institutions matter for economic growth and that transaction costs are crucial in this relationship (North 1989, p.6). He posits that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling costs per transaction, thereby fostering economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions in determining long-term development outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, do not encourage economic development and can hinder long-run growth. The centrality of institutions in shaping economic trajectories is further underscored by Hopkins, who highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Despite these arguments, the debate continues over whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors play more significant roles.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and overall economic growth by providing a stable environment for market exchanges and investment.

The implications of secure property rights extended beyond immediate financial gains. North and Weingast emphasize that institutional changes in England involved both economic rules and their enforcement and adaptability, which supported economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, indicating a broader commitment to property rights and market development. This commitment was instrumental in fostering an environment conducive to long-term investment and economic expansion.

The degree to which the regime is committed to these rules plays a critical role in ensuring their effectiveness. Credible commitment underpins economic stability and encourages investment, thereby driving long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping their economic and political environments (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The variation in European colonization strategies resulted in diverse institutional outcomes; where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law institutions, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, long-term economic growth was fostered. This institutional divergence had profound effects on current income per capita in former colonies.

The persistence of these institutions is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. The political power dynamics within these colonies often led to the entrenchment of extractive institutions, as local elites benefited from maintaining the status quo. This institutional design had lasting impacts on economic development, as it discouraged investment and hindered the creation of a productive environment.

The influence of colonial institutions reaches beyond direct economic effects; they also molded social structures and cultural norms (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This interplay highlights how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or impede economic growth by influencing factors such as technological adoption and human capital formation. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that population density and prosperity influenced European policies, which in turn affected industrialization and long-run development trajectories. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is a testament to the enduring impact of these institutional legacies on economic growth.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty. In other words, even when African societies are heterogeneous, they may still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions that can influence economic growth. This perspective aligns with Frankema's (2012, p.15) analysis, which posits that British colonial rule was more conducive to formal education development in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, affecting long-run economic growth. This highlights how institutional frameworks can influence factors such as human capital formation, which is crucial for economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further qualify this by showing that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). They argue that patriarchy and customary law codified under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops, which influenced their economic contributions. This finding shows that institutional frameworks can both foster long-run economic growth and create barriers to it by limiting opportunities for certain groups such as women.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both argue that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. The mechanism by which this occurs involves the interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments, where institutions adapt to efficiently use innovations. This process involves more than adopting new technologies; it also requires resolving conflicts stemming from these advancements. Kuznets highlights that economic growth requires the resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs, emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in peacefully managing such challenges (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates on this by recognizing coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them, underscoring the necessity of institutional progress to complement technological advancements. This interplay is crucial for maintaining economic improvement and preventing its erosion due to rent-seeking behaviors. While Mokyr acknowledges that economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54), this does not diminish the fundamental role of knowledge growth in driving long-run economic growth, as it remains a critical factor shaping both institutional adjustments and overall economic development.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades have had an enduring adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective highlights the profound and lasting impact of historical events such as slavery on contemporary economic conditions. The mechanism through which this influence operates is complex, involving both internal and external channels. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that a culture of mistrust developed within Africa due to the slave trades, and this mistrust persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust significantly impedes economic development by reducing social cohesion and hindering cooperative endeavors essential for growth. Additionally, Nunn shows that the adverse effects of slavery on economic development are not solely attributable to recent events or colonialism but rather stem from long-term historical factors (Nunn 2008, p.19). Sokoloff and Engerman underscore this point by illustrating how institutional arrangements in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic indicates that the legacy of slavery involves more than just economic deprivation; it also includes entrenched social structures that limit opportunities for broad-based development. This nuanced understanding underscores the importance of recognizing the multifaceted impacts of historical injustices on contemporary economic conditions.

Austin's critique of institutional theories (Austin 2008, p.2) and Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) both show that economic growth is shaped by historical complexities as well as institutions. Both scholars contend that oversimplifying causation can obscure the broader dynamics at play. Austin argues that  the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, which simplifies the process in a way that does not account for all variables. Ogilvie suggests that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions, such as serfdom, craft guilds, or merchant guilds. For example, Austin shows how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding local responses to external interventions, which can alter institutional outcomes in unpredictable ways.

Ogilvie acknowledges that institutions are essential for understanding long-term research programs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), suggesting a nuanced approach where institutions are re-evaluated within a broader context. This perspective aligns with the idea that historical legacies and local adaptations significantly influence institutional outcomes.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for economic activities to flourish. North argues that institutions are indispensable for economic growth because they specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further illustrate this point by showing how European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, which significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These differing institutions were not merely a response to geographical conditions but were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, highlighting the critical role of institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes. Austin argues that local adaptations and historical legacies are crucial factors influencing institutional outcomes, which in turn affect economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie's nuanced approach suggests that institutions should be re-evaluated within a broader context to understand their impact on long-term research programs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the evidence from Acemoglu et al. who show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run growth but rather perpetuated poverty and underdevelopment in many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with North's argument that efficient property rights and effective enforcement are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The historical evidence from Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes, as well as the need for empirical comparisons to settle disagreements about their impact.
