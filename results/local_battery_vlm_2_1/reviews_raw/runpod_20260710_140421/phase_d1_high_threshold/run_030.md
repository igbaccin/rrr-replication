The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is how to understand the persistent disparities in wealth between nations, with some arguing that institutional frameworks are pivotal drivers of prosperity while others dispute this primacy. The debate centres on whether institutions, defined as formal rules, informal constraints, and enforcement mechanisms, are the primary determinants of economic growth or whether other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. One key position is that institutions foster stable environments conducive to investment and innovation, thereby catalysing economic development over extended periods. This view contends that well-designed institutions, which ensure secure property rights and enforce contracts, are crucial for sustained growth. A rival perspective posits that the impact of institutions on economic growth has been exaggerated; proponents argue instead that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks like colonial policies, have a more profound influence on long-term economic outcomes. This debate underscores a fundamental question: to what extent do institutions shape economic trajectories over centuries? The answer lies in understanding how different institutional frameworks facilitate or hinder economic activities and how these dynamics unfold over time.

A stream of scholarship highlights the varied strategies Europeans employed during colonial expansion, which resulted in distinct institutional legacies that persist today. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonies with high European mortality rates were more likely to develop extractive institutions, which hindered long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These colonies were governed by regimes focused on rapid resource extraction, leading to enduring negative impacts on income per capita. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by collapsing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political actions, challenging the notion that European settlers uniformly imposed extractive institutions. This interplay between local dynamics and colonial policies reveals how diverse institutional outcomes emerged from similar imperial contexts.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) share the view that institutions are pivotal for economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as primary drivers of development. This institutional framework is critical in facilitating market exchanges, which are essential for a growing economy. By providing security to property rights and reducing transaction costs, these institutions create an environment conducive to long-term capital markets and overall economic development (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.9). The implications of these institutional frameworks are profound; they influenced immediate economic conditions and had enduring effects on the development trajectories of these regions.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), along with Ogilvie (2007, p.3), argue. This institutional framework enabled economic growth by providing a stable environment where property rights were protected and contracts enforced, which were vital for investment and market development. North and Weingast's analysis highlights how credible commitments from political regimes to uphold these rules significantly influenced economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie cautions that the role of institutions should be carefully analyzed in historical contexts as other factors such as distributional conflicts may also have shaped economic structures. This nuanced view suggests that while institutional frameworks are pivotal, they operate within a complex web of social and political dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

This perspective emphasizes that technological innovations foster long-term economic improvements. Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires concomitant institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of new technologies. These changes are necessary to resolve conflicts arising from modernization, ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. For example, the transition from rural modes of life to industrialization necessitated significant shifts in societal structures and values. Mokyr (2005, p.54) extends this idea by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the benefits of innovation can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. McCloskey (2015, p.3; 2015, p.4) further elaborates on the role of ideas and ideologies in driving economic change. He argues that the Industrial Enlightenment was a period when societies became more tolerant of new ideas, enabling greater experimentation and open communication about innovations. This ideological shift created an environment conducive to technological progress and economic growth. McCloskey acknowledges that this process is not linear or irreversible; societal attitudes towards innovation can fluctuate over time.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Pamuk 2007, p.23) examine how inequality and structural changes influenced economic development, without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. They argue that societies with greater inequality tended to develop institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This is evident in New World societies where factor endowments led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). While Sokoloff and Engerman focus on the persistence of inequality through institutional design favoring elites, Pamuk's analysis suggests that institutional adaptability can mitigate long-term economic stagnation by fostering growth. Pamuk notes that the political authorities in Tuscany worked to strengthen existing monopolies, indicating that institutional rigidity could hinder economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between inequality and institutions in shaping economic trajectories, where structural changes can either reinforce or challenge existing power structures depending on institutional responses.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon shaped by structural impediments. This position challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis which suggests that Africa's current underdevelopment is due to institutional legacies from colonial rule, such as extractive institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, indicating a degree of institutional flexibility (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that African economies may have had more growth potential during the colonial period than is often acknowledged.

Their findings indicate that colonial Africa experienced periods of economic development that were influenced by local conditions and policies rather than being uniformly constrained by extractive institutions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced view of colonial impacts suggests that institutional changes, both positive and negative, played a significant role in shaping African economic trajectories.

Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2), along with Nunn (2008, p.2), show that the slave trade in Africa instilled a culture of mistrust. This legacy persists today and hinders contemporary economic development by reducing trust levels. The internalisation of mistrust among individuals is at least twice as significant as external factors such as rule of law, which further exacerbates this issue (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that the slave trade was a long-standing institution in Africa, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. This duration is far longer than the period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to about 1960 (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context underscores the deep-rooted impact of the slave trade on African societies, shaping their economic trajectories in profound ways. The presence of mistrust within these communities is a direct result of this enduring legacy and continues to influence contemporary development outcomes by impacting trust levels and impeding economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding historical and contemporary development trajectories. Institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the distribution of power, property rights, and incentives for investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can persist and hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms foster economic development by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

The historical context of colonial rule further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic growth. The legacy of extractive institutions established during colonial periods continues to influence contemporary development outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth, as seen in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is supported by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

The specific case of Africa highlights how long-standing institutions, such as the slave trade, have shaped economic trajectories. The deep-rooted impact of the slave trade on African societies is evident in the enduring legacy of mistrust within these communities, which continues to influence contemporary development outcomes by impacting trust levels and impeding economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This historical context underscores the profound and lasting effects of institutions on long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth by fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence of capital markets in seventeenth-century England and France settles this disagreement by showing that institutions per se, and in particular, the institutional changes described, were revolutionary for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
