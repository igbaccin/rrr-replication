The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages scholars across multiple disciplines. The stakes are high because understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth can inform policy and theory alike. At its core, this debate centers on whether institutions are primary drivers of growth, or if they merely facilitate other underlying factors. A key position in this discussion is that institutions shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and innovation. This view posits that secure property rights and effective governance structures foster an environment conducive to long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). Some argue that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper historical and geographical factors, which influence growth trajectories (Author, Year). The debate is further complicated by the role of colonial legacies, which have left enduring imprints on institutional frameworks worldwide. Austin argues that simplifying causal relationships into a singular narrative overlooks the complexities of historical paths and institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). Meanwhile, Acemoglu et al. emphasize how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, with extractive institutions in some regions hindering long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This complex interplay between institutions and economic growth underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical contexts shape contemporary development trajectories.

North and Weingast argue that financial institutions played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a significant milestone as it led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for long-term economic growth. This financial revolution facilitated market exchanges by developing new instruments and specifying efficient property rights. The evolution of these institutions reduced the cost per transaction while increasing the overall volume of exchanges, which reflects the growing specialization and division of labor in the economy. This institutional development was pivotal for fostering economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. North and Weingast note that the success of these institutions depended on the regime's commitment to upholding the rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They argue that credible commitments from political authorities were essential in enforcing these rules, which is a critical factor in sustaining long-term growth. This institutional framework contrasts with neoclassical theories by emphasizing the role of property rights and transaction costs over population and savings as principal determinants of economic development. North's work underscores that the impact of financial institutions on long-run economic growth was about creating new markets and ensuring their stability through credible commitments from political authorities. This nuanced view highlights how institutional changes can drive economic growth by addressing fundamental transaction costs.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by highlighting how epochal innovations have been dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges that technological progress is crucial; it must be complemented by institutional advancements to ensure sustainable economic improvement. This recognition underscores the importance of coordinating technological and institutional changes to maximize economic benefits.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this coordination by noting that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that modern technology requires specific institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, they need to be integrated into a supportive institutional framework to fully realize their economic potential. Institutions play a role in facilitating the adoption of new technologies by reducing access costs and resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Therefore, the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial for long-term economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Austin 2008, p.2) extend this argument by noting that institutions in societies characterized by greater inequality tend to favor elites. This dynamic is evident in New World societies, where institutional structures often restricted competition and reinforced existing power imbalances. The persistence of inequality is closely linked to the policies and regulations implemented by governing bodies. These policies, influenced by elite interests, create barriers to economic mobility for lower classes while preserving the status quo for those in power. Austin (2008, p.15) provides a specific example: colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often adjusted their institutional frameworks based on local economic and political actions. This adaptive behavior highlights how institutions can evolve in response to internal pressures, even if they initially favor elite interests. This evolution may perpetuate existing inequalities by maintaining policies that benefit those already in power.

This dynamic is vividly illustrated by the slave trade, which played a pivotal role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory by fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27)(Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of its reduced-form effect. This mistrust, they argue, operates through both external environmental factors and internal norms, with the latter being at least twice as influential. The enduring nature of this mistrust can be seen in how it affects contemporary economic development, as documented by Nunn (2008, p.2). He shows that Africa's underdevelopment is attributable to colonialism or recent events and deeply rooted in the legacy of slave trades spanning nearly 500 years. The historical context provided by Nunn (2008, p.2) reveals how centralised governments and well-developed markets, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, facilitated trade with Europeans. This suggests that the institutional frameworks present during the slave trade era could have mitigated some of its adverse effects, highlighting the conditional nature of institutional impact on economic growth.

This historical context sets the stage for examining how colonialism introduced institutional changes that led to varying economic outcomes in former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize that the mortality rates of Europeans were pivotal in determining the type of institutions established in their colonies, with high-mortality areas tending towards extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This institutional divergence was a direct result of colonial policies, not merely a function of geographic factors or resource endowments. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrate that these institutions persisted over time, significantly impacting current income per capita and economic development pathways. The persistence of extractive institutions is crucial as it implies that the economic legacies of colonialism are deeply rooted in institutional frameworks rather than transient factors such as temporary shocks or immediate policy changes. Acemoglu et al (2002) expand on this by highlighting how extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not promote long-term growth, underscoring the enduring impact of these colonial legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The downstream implication is that contemporary economic disparities among former colonies can be traced back to these historical institutional differences.

The complexity of this interaction means that while institutional changes due to colonialism play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, their effects are not deterministic.

Colonial institutions in Africa were instrumental in shaping both the provision of public goods and economic development trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share a common finding that institutional changes driven by African initiatives as well as exogenous causes influenced public goods provision (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). For instance, colonial rule introduced new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift was not uniform across Africa; British rule, in particular, played a significant role in developing formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15), illustrating how different colonial powers had varying impacts on institutional development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this by noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The interaction between these factors highlights that while colonial institutions laid a foundation for public goods provision, their impact on economic development was contingent on a range of additional variables.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and incentivize investment. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over economic development, can significantly impede growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by North's assertion that secure property rights are critical for fostering economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage long-term capital investments (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

Moreover, the evolution of political institutions that enforce these property rights is crucial. North and Weingast demonstrate how the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term investments, highlighting the interconnectedness of political stability and economic prosperity. Austin further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, illustrating how institutions can evolve to support growth even under varying conditions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The impact of institutions on economic development varies across different contexts. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Hopkins' observation that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments, indicating that institutional effectiveness can vary independently of ethnic diversity (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus settled by empirical comparisons across colonies with different colonial powers. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established extractive institutions where mortality rates were high, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence underscores the enduring impact of initial institutional frameworks on long-term economic outcomes, highlighting that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on the specific historical and political contexts in which they operate.
