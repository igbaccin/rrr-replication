The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic among economists and historians alike. The live disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development. On one side, some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures directly fosters economic growth. These scholars assert that institutions create the necessary framework for investment and innovation, thereby driving long-term prosperity. Some scholars argue that while institutions play a role in economic growth, cultural beliefs, geographic factors, and actions of specific groups within society are also important determinants (Author Year p.N). The debate is not merely academic: understanding the role of institutions has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies, influencing how resources are allocated and reforms are implemented. Institutions are often seen as a critical factor in determining economic outcomes, but the extent to which they shape long-run growth remains unclear. One key area of contention revolves around the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic performance. For instance, some scholars focus on the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions, while others emphasize the importance of cultural beliefs and historical legacies. This debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how different factors interact to shape economic growth over the long term. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this through the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects an increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this mechanism by noting that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups as well as economic efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These groups may resist institutional changes if they fear losing political power or do not directly benefit from the resulting economic gains, which can hinder long-term growth. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this implication by highlighting that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that while institutions are crucial, they are also dynamic and responsive to local contexts, influencing the path of economic development over time.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. The Glorious Revolution in 1688 marked a turning point where the English government transformed into one that was constrained by rules and committed to protecting property rights. This shift significantly influenced economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment, as the new institutions provided reliable expectations about future returns. Hopkins notes that the quality of public goods provision does not necessarily correlate with ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity; rather, it is the institutional framework that determines a society's ability to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight underscores the dynamic nature of institutions, which can adapt to local contexts and influence economic development over time. Hopkins also highlights that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment and thus are essential for development; however, East Asia's experience suggests that institutional reforms must be tailored to specific regional contexts (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This qualification implies that although secure property rights fostered by effective institutions are vital for economic growth, the path to achieving this can vary significantly across different regions.

Technological advancements are central to long-run economic growth as they drive productivity gains and facilitate the efficient use of resources (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological progress is fueled by the accumulation of useful knowledge, which in turn spurs economic development. Similarly, Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that epochal innovations have been dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and across many regions (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These technological breakthroughs necessitate institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from rapid economic changes. Such adjustments are essential for integrating new technologies into the economy without undermining social stability. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in managing growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that economic progress does not weaken societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by pointing out that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustainable economic improvement; it must be complemented by institutional progress to mitigate coordination failures and prevent rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where institutions facilitate the integration of new technologies into the economy.

The role of women in economic development during colonial periods highlights how gender dynamics influenced agricultural practices and land access, which were shaped by customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document the de-privileging of women in accessing land for cash crops. This was reinforced through colonial rule's codification of customary law, which marginalized women to less fertile lands for food farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). The patriarchal alliances and customary law systems that emerged during this era further entrenched these gender inequalities. These legal frameworks limited women’s economic opportunities and shaped broader social structures that hindered overall economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). However, Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the demand for missionary education, driven by both political leaders and families, altered the perceived benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This shift suggests that despite the institutional barriers faced by women, new educational opportunities could potentially mitigate some of the economic disadvantages imposed by colonial legal systems.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, demonstrate that Africa’s slave trade has had profound and enduring effects on the continent's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). This trade's legacy is not merely historical; it continues to shape contemporary social structures through a deeply ingrained culture of mistrust. This mistrust persists across generations, as it was fostered by the slave trade and has become embedded in societal norms and intergroup relationships, significantly impacting trust levels (Nunn 2008, p.2). The mistrust engendered by the transatlantic slave trade is so pervasive that it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on contemporary trust levels. This impact stems from internalized norms of mistrust as well as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The cultural legacy of mistrust affects economic development by undermining social cohesion and reducing cooperation, which are essential for productive economic activity. Despite this, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) acknowledge that the external environment, such as the rule of law and trustworthiness of others, plays a role in shaping contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal norms of mistrust, however, are at least twice as impactful. This cultural legacy of mistrust presents a significant barrier to economic growth, as it hinders the development of social capital and cooperative behaviors that are crucial for sustained economic progress.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They posit that power struggles among different social groups drove the evolution of these institutions. For instance, serfdom and craft guilds were maintained because they served the interests of elites who benefited from the status quo. The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiencies, can be attributed to the political influence of those who stood to gain from them. This dynamic is particularly evident in pre-industrial Europe, where institutional arrangements were often shaped by the need to maintain social order and control rather than to maximize economic efficiency. The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding the long-term effects of these institutions on economic development. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that the persistence of inefficient institutions can hinder long-run growth by limiting innovation and reducing productivity. This point is particularly relevant in the context of Latin America, where Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate how institutions that favored elites contributed to persistent inequality. The legacy of these distributional conflicts continues to shape economic outcomes today, as societies grapple with the consequences of historical institutional arrangements.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster stable and predictable environments conducive to investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in settlement patterns led to the creation of either extractive or inclusive institutions, which significantly influenced current economic outcomes. North argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These institutions reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and encouraging long-term investments. The historical evolution of institutions, as highlighted by Austin, demonstrates how different paths of institutional development can lead to varied economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2).

The persistence of inefficient institutions can hinder long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions in ways that prioritize the interests of powerful groups over broader economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where institutions like serfdom and guilds were maintained despite their inefficiencies. Acemoglu et al. also point out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional persistence can limit innovation and reduce productivity, as seen in the legacy of historical arrangements in Latin America.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can eliminate poverty. Hopkins emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The experience of East Asia, where institutional changes have been pivotal, offers a relevant model for African countries seeking to improve their economic trajectories. Englebert's findings on ethno-linguistic fragmentation show that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding underscores the importance of institutional quality in driving economic growth, regardless of ethnic composition.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth. North shows that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement concerns whether the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Austin's point is that European settlers' presence was not necessary for indigenous commodity production expansion, suggesting that colonial institutions were adapted based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the comparative analysis of institutional changes and their impact on economic outcomes in former colonies. This comparison shows that inclusive institutions are more conducive to long-run economic growth.
