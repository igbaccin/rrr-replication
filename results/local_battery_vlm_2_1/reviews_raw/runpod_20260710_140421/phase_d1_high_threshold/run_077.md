Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect investment and productivity. This question is central to understanding why some nations prosper while others stagnate, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors such as geography or culture are more influential. One key position highlights that extractive institutions, designed to benefit a ruling elite and promote broad-based growth, can hinder long-term economic development. This view is challenged by those who argue that institutional change is not always deterministic and can be influenced by a variety of historical and contextual factors. The debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and impact economic outcomes over time; this evolution is shaped by both endogenous and exogenous forces, making it crucial to explore the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, with some promoting economic development while others were extractive and hindered growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) propose that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These studies underscore the significance of institutions in determining long-term economic trajectories, showing how initial conditions and historical events can shape institutional outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions, particularly those governing property rights and political factors, are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. They posit that the creation of secure property rights through institutional changes fosters a conducive environment for economic development. This is evident in North's analysis of the English financial revolution, where the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported long-term capital markets, which are critical for economic growth. The political model outlined by North highlights that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises was instrumental in this process. Such institutional changes were both economic and political transformations. This institutional development underpinned the remarkable success observed in capital markets, indicating a direct link between institutional changes and economic growth. This perspective is nuanced by evidence suggesting that while institutions are important, they are not the sole determinant of economic performance.

Mokyr and Kuznets argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). The rise in useful knowledge enables innovations that push the boundaries of what is economically possible. This process requires institutional adjustments to facilitate efficient use of new technologies. For instance, Kuznets notes that modern technology demands significant shifts in societal structures, such as urbanization and changes in family patterns. Mokyr further elaborates on this by highlighting the role of ideological adjustments, suggesting that societies must be open to new ideas and innovations without excessive resistance or rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological advancements and societal adaptations creates a dynamic environment where economic growth can flourish. However, McCloskey qualifies this by emphasizing the importance of ideologies in facilitating the uptake of new techniques and institutions, suggesting that open discourse and tolerance for new ideas are crucial for sustaining long-term economic improvement (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This implies that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by a societal environment that fosters innovation and adaptation. The growth of useful knowledge is about technological advancements and the ideological framework that supports them.

Acemoglu et al. show that the nature of these institutions was determined by European mortality rates and settlement patterns; where Europeans faced high mortality, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe rather than fostering local economic development.

Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on this mechanism by highlighting that these extractive institutions were equilibrium outcomes shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize their rents and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, with those subjected to extractive institutions experiencing lower levels of development and income per capita. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that these institutional differences persist even when controlling for other factors such as geography or initial endowments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), underscoring the independent impact of institutions on economic outcomes.

The long-run effects of colonial institutions are not uniform across all former colonies. This suggests that while extractive institutions have generally hindered economic development, the specific impact can vary based on initial conditions and subsequent policy choices.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Pamuk, explore how institutions shaped by inequality influenced economic outcomes in historical contexts (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.23). They argue that differences in wealth and political power across societies can lead to divergent institutional paths. This is because the endowments of land and natural resources relative to labor led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset, which then affected institutions through their impact on various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. Broadberry and Gupta, however, add that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, suggesting an earlier start for institutional differences than some other accounts might indicate (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The implication is that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth may be more complex and multifaceted than often acknowledged. This perspective underscores the need to consider how initial conditions and subsequent policy choices can shape institutional trajectories over time, with significant implications for economic development.

Hopkins's exploration of African economic development highlights that colonial legacies and structural impediments have significantly shaped Africa’s economic trajectory (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Colonial institutions often imposed administrative frameworks that did not align with local socio-economic conditions. This misalignment led to inefficiencies in resource allocation and governance, which stunted long-term economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner show that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). These advances were often tied to foreign trade and export markets, making them vulnerable to global economic fluctuations. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This perspective suggests that while colonial legacies created significant challenges, they did not necessarily condemn Africa to perpetual underdevelopment. Hopkins highlights the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner point out that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), suggesting that institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic development depending on their alignment with local conditions. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing effective policies to foster long-term economic growth in Africa.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The extensive period of the slave trade, nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, significantly impacted African economies by depleting human capital and disrupting social structures. This historical trauma created a culture of mistrust that persists today, with Nunn and Wantchekon estimating that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust within Africa (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The internalization of mistrust norms is particularly detrimental as it affects individuals' behavior and attitudes towards economic activity. This finding underscores the enduring legacy of historical events such as the slave trades, which have shaped contemporary economic outcomes by fostering a climate of distrust that hinders cooperation and investment. In regions where European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production was encouraged by colonial governments seeking fiscal security (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is undeniable. In particular, the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-term growth, hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions persisted over time and continue to impact current income levels.

The legacy of colonial institutions is evident in the economic outcomes of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks, which in turn shaped economic development paths (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). In regions where European settlers were present, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were established, fostering economic growth. In areas without significant settler presence, extractive states were formed, leading to stagnation.

The impact of colonial institutions extends beyond economic outcomes to social and political structures. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (North 1989, p.6). This perspective underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, highlighting that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in determining long-run development trajectories.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is supported by the historical evidence from capital markets, which shows that institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were instrumental in promoting economic development.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins argues that 'good' institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (North 1989, p.9; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth lies in the contrast between regions with extractive institutions and those with inclusive ones. The measurement of income per capita across former colonies shows a clear reversal in relative incomes, directly attributable to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15). This empirical evidence underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.
