The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. Institutions are widely recognized as critical factors influencing economic development. The debate centres on how and why certain institutional frameworks foster prosperity while others impede it. One position underscores the primacy of institutions that enforce property rights and the rule of law, arguing these provide the stability necessary for long-term investment and growth (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). A rival position posits that distributional conflicts are more significant than efficiency in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The stakes are high. The debate over institutions' role in economic growth has implications for policy-making, as understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence development can guide efforts to promote sustainable prosperity. If institutions are indeed pivotal, then crafting effective policies requires a nuanced grasp of how institutional frameworks evolve and interact with other economic factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Distributional conflicts primarily shaping institutions suggests that addressing inequality may be critical when focusing on institutional design.

The debate is not merely academic; it has real-world consequences. For instance, the persistence of poverty in many regions can be linked to the quality of institutions, highlighting the urgent need for effective policies that foster economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes and points towards a future where well-designed institutional frameworks could drive sustained prosperity.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. This institutional framework ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by highlighting how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This security was crucial for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution against overestimating the revolutionary impact of these institutional changes by noting France's pre-revolutionary economic performance was not vastly inferior to England's. This suggests that while secure property rights were important, other factors also played a significant role in economic development. Thus, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was a critical factor in fostering secure property rights and driving economic growth, but it is essential to recognize the multifaceted nature of economic development.

Building on this, colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the nature of these institutions was shaped by European mortality rates and settlement patterns, which led to divergent colonization strategies. In areas with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences had profound effects on economic development, as evidenced by current income per capita levels in these regions. The persistence of extractive institutions in high-mortality areas hindered long-run growth, while the establishment of inclusive institutions in low-mortality regions fostered economic prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize these institutional differences are directly tied to the policies pursued by European colonizers, which were influenced by population density and local prosperity. This does not mean all colonies with extractive institutions remained stagnant; some experienced industrialization during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in their long-run development. This interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and economic outcomes, illustrating how institutional legacies continue to shape economic trajectories today.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie shows how these institutions reflected power dynamics among different social groups. She notes that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights significantly influenced institutional structures, which in turn affected property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this perspective by demonstrating how the unequal distribution of wealth and power in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and reinforcing inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic highlights a specific mechanism: when distributional conflicts are resolved in favor of powerful groups, they create institutional frameworks that perpetuate their advantages. For instance, the persistence of serfdom in Europe or the establishment of craft guilds can be seen as outcomes of these power struggles, where institutions were designed to secure the interests of dominant classes rather than to maximize overall economic efficiency. The downstream implication is that such institutions can stifle long-term growth by limiting innovation and competition. Ogilvie warns against over-simplifying this perspective, emphasizing that institutions are complex and multifaceted (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This complexity suggests distributional conflicts play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions; they interact with other factors such as cultural beliefs and exogenous shocks.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.2), along with Hopkins (2009, p.18), challenge the conventional wisdom that colonial administrations uniformly stifled African economic development. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which can obscure the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). One specific mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped African economies was fiscal policy. Colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, aligning with local economic interests rather than merely extracting resources (Austin 2008, p.15). This pattern suggests that colonial policies could be responsive to local incentives and actions. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.18) shows that the presence of poor institutions is not universally linked to ethnic fragmentation but can also result from broader governance failures (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Post-independence budget policies in East Africa further illustrate this complexity: Simson (2019, p.2) demonstrates that these policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, contrary to prevailing narratives. Instead, they reflected responses to regional instability and domestic dissent, with military spending increasing significantly during the late 1960s and 1970s (Simson 2019, p.11). This nuanced view of colonial and post-colonial institutions underscores their impact on economic development was varied based on local contexts and policy responses.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that African economic history has been marked by periods of growth in per capita income. This perspective challenges the idea that Africa's poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments; instead, it highlights that economic development in colonial Africa was dynamic and responsive to various factors including endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that export production patterns were closely tied to foreign trade markets, which influenced the timing and scale of growth across different regions. African economies experienced fluctuations driven by external demand for exports. These insights are crucial as they reveal that local conditions played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, rather than being solely determined by colonial institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) note the absence of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates complicates efforts to fully understand these dynamics.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that Africa's slave trades had direct adverse effects on long-run economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trade disrupted social structures and fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines cooperation and hampers the establishment of effective institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon found that the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalization of mistrust creates a self-reinforcing cycle where low levels of trust perpetuate underdevelopment. This historical context is crucial for understanding how these deep-seated issues continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. He notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This claim is bolstered by Kuznets' (1973) observation that institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations has varied over centuries and regions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Institutions facilitate the coordination needed to overcome coordination failures, thereby enabling the sustained growth driven by useful knowledge. Mokyr cautions this growth is not irreversible; rent-seeking behavior can erode economic improvements if institutions fail to support technological progress effectively. This underscores the importance of robust institutions in harnessing the full potential of technological advancements for long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their ability to facilitate the coordination needed for sustained technological progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth by enabling long-term contracts and investment. This is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, with some promoting rule of law and investment while others resulted in extractive states (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions shaped the economic trajectories of colonies by either encouraging or hindering technological progress. Moreover, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic development over time (Austin 2008, p.2). This is supported by Acemoglu et al., who document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and show that it was related to industrialization, providing evidence of how institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Institutional effectiveness is contingent on their ability to adapt to changing economic conditions. North shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This is supported by Austin's finding that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that effective institutions can adapt to local contexts and incentives (Austin 2008, p.15; North 1989, p.6). These adaptive capabilities are crucial for sustaining long-run growth, as they allow institutions to support technological progress even as economic conditions evolve. However, Ogilvie cautions that distributional conflicts often drive institutional design rather than efficiency considerations, highlighting the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional creation and maintenance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Institutions can either facilitate or hinder technological progress, depending on their design and enforcement. North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of falling transaction costs per transaction and increasing specialization and division of labor (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.6). This is supported by Ogilvie's assertion that institutions are an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, emphasizing their role in shaping long-term economic outcomes (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.15). However, Hopkins shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, indicating that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This underscores the importance of robust institutions in harnessing the full potential of technological advancements for long-run economic growth.
