The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because the answer determines how societies can foster sustainable development and prosperity through institutional design. Institutions matter to the extent that they underpin property rights, contract enforcement, and incentives for investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). However, there exists a disagreement over the mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth: some argue that efficient institutions are key to growth while others contend that distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions, with efficiency being a secondary factor. The debate hinges on whether institutions are primarily designed to enhance economic outcomes or if they arise from political and social dynamics that prioritize other objectives such as power distribution and cultural norms.

Institutions can be either extractive or inclusive; this distinction is central to understanding their impact on growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive institutions, which concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, tend to stifle economic development by discouraging investment and innovation. Inclusive institutions provide secure property rights and enforce contracts fostering an environment conducive to long-term economic growth. This perspective is supported by evidence from colonial settings where different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, colonies with settler populations often developed more inclusive institutions, whereas those without settlers were subjected to extractive states that prioritized resource extraction for the benefit of the metropole (Austin 2008, p.2).

The evolution of financial institutions and property rights is a critical aspect of economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights, thereby facilitating economic development (North 1989, p.9). This historical example underscores the broader point that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes by influencing the cost and efficiency of transactions. The development of new financial instruments during this period reflects increased security in property rights, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. The English financial revolution illustrates how institutional changes can lead to sustained economic prosperity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted. Institutions facilitate market exchanges and shape the incentives driving investment and innovation. For example, the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms results in falling transaction costs over time. This, in turn, fosters a growing proportion of the economy dedicated to market exchanges, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can support long-term economic growth by creating an environment where efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are the norm.

While institutions are crucial for economic growth, they also reflect broader social and political dynamics. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is a significant factor that cannot be overlooked (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by conflicts over the distribution of power and resources rather than purely by efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely designed to enhance economic outcomes, suggesting instead that they arise from complex social and political interactions.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can inform development policies aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). By understanding the historical context and the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, policymakers can design more effective strategies for fostering growth and development. This stream of research highlights the importance of understanding institutional dynamics in order to develop effective policies that can drive long-term economic prosperity.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled, but it is clear that institutions play a crucial role in determining economic outcomes. Whether through facilitating efficient market exchanges or reflecting broader social and political dynamics, institutions are central to understanding how societies achieve sustained economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are pivotal in long-term economic growth, highlighting how they facilitate efficient market exchanges and shape societal outcomes. This perspective is complemented by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law institutions, economic development was encouraged; conversely, extractive states in regions with high mortality rates impeded growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is evident in the divergence of income levels among former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a pivotal role (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Acemoglu et al. also note that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), suggesting that the benefits of institutional development can be unevenly distributed and may not always align with broader economic prosperity. This highlights the complexity of institutional dynamics in shaping economic outcomes, showing how initial conditions and colonial policies can have enduring effects on a region's economic trajectory.

This is particularly evident in Africa, where institutional reforms are crucial for alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that understanding institutional dynamics is essential for addressing long-term economic development. Specifically, Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the significance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, a critical driver of development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores that effective institutions can mitigate ethnic fragmentation and foster progressive governance, which are vital for sustainable growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg note that African poverty is not solely due to persistent historical impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This nuanced view suggests that while institutions play a pivotal role, their effectiveness can be context-dependent and influenced by various factors beyond immediate institutional quality. For instance, the experience of British African colonies shows that real wage levels and trends during colonial rule varied significantly across different regions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2), indicating that historical and geographical conditions also shape long-term economic outcomes. Therefore, a comprehensive approach to institutional reform must consider these contextual factors to ensure sustained economic growth in Africa.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment to rules underpinning economic growth. This commitment is critical as it ensures that the regime or sovereign is bound by these rules, thereby creating a stable environment for market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of England's economic policies can be attributed to their ability to enforce and adapt these rules to changing circumstances.

This institutional framework allowed for significant economic growth in England. This does not imply that other societies with different institutional frameworks were destined for failure. Sokoloff and Engerman note that the differentials in development paths across New World societies can be traced back to varying degrees of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The environments in which Europeans established their colonies significantly influenced the evolution of institutions, leading to societies with extreme differences in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. This initial inequality had lasting effects on institutional development and economic growth, as it shaped policies that favored elites and restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these inequalities suggests that while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, the specific context and historical factors also play a significant role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

This perspective challenges the notion that institutions evolve primarily to enhance economic efficiency. Ogilvie specifically focuses on the role of serfdom, the community, and guilds in shaping European economies. She contends that these institutions emerged as a result of power struggles among different social groups rather than as optimal responses to economic incentives. Austin, meanwhile, highlights the importance of colonial governments' institutional prescriptions, which were often shaped by local economic and political dynamics. He shows how these administrations adjusted their policies in response to indigenous actions, illustrating that institutional change can be driven by distributional conflicts. In this view, institutions are not merely tools for achieving efficiency but are deeply embedded in social and political power structures. The interplay between colonial governments and local populations reveals that institutions can be shaped by the need to maintain stability and control rather than purely economic considerations. This dynamic suggests that institutional development is influenced by a complex web of factors, including historical legacies and power relations.

This historical legacy is evident in how the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that this effect is not merely a result of external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. This suggests that historical experiences of exploitation and violence have deeply ingrained distrust within African societies. Nunn further supports this by noting that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a duration significantly longer than the period of colonial rule. However, Nunn (2008, p.2) also highlights that centralized governments and well-developed trading networks, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, were able to support external trade despite the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19), suggesting that institutional structures can mitigate some of the negative effects. This underscores a complex relationship between historical events, such as the slave trade, and contemporary levels of trust within African societies.

Frankema's work on British colonial rule shows that formal education in sub-Saharan Africa thrived under British administration more than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding challenges the notion that all colonial regimes had uniformly negative impacts on African education systems. Broadberry and Gardner note that the timing and scale of educational development varied significantly across regions due to a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These variations underscore the importance of specific institutional conditions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasise that technological advancements are driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This process requires institutional adjustments to efficiently utilise innovations. The interplay between technological changes and societal adaptations shapes long-run economic growth. Kuznets highlights that resolving conflicts arising from technological change is crucial for sustaining modern economic growth without weakening political stability. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in managing the societal impacts of technological advancements, ensuring that they do not hinder long-term growth (Kuznets_1973). Mokyr argues that while institutional progress complements technological advances, it is not always as irreversible and monotonic as technological progress itself. This suggests that the stability of economic improvements hinges on the effectiveness of institutions in mitigating rent-seeking behaviors (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). North argues that the development of political institutions specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs per unit. This dynamic supports a growing economy through increased specialization and division of labor, which multiplies aggregate exchanges. Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on how extractive institutions can persist and affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional frameworks in colonies. In some cases, Europeans set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment; in others, they established extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole.

This variation in colonial institutions highlights how initial conditions can shape long-term economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe shows that distributional conflicts often drive core economic institutions. These institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize rent extraction over long-run growth. This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on the role of political institutions in specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms.

The interaction between institutions and economic development is further illustrated by Austin’s critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that this thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This argument underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-term economic growth, as institutions adapt to changing incentives created by indigenous economic and political action.

Institutional reforms can play a crucial role in eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins shows that 'good' institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is vital for development. This view aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). They document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and show that it was related to industrialization during the nineteenth century.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between North's emphasis on transaction costs and Acemoglu et al.'s focus on extractive institutions lies in the measurement of institutional quality. This is exemplified by the varied outcomes in former European colonies, where different colonial strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. provides a clear empirical basis for understanding how initial conditions and institutional changes introduced by colonialism shape long-run economic growth. This historical evidence underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic development.
