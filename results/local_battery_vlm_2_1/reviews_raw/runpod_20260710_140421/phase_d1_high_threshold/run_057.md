The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it addresses how societies can achieve sustained prosperity through the effective structuring of social arrangements. At stake intellectually is whether institutional frameworks are indeed the primary drivers of economic development, or if they merely facilitate other determinants such as geography or culture. The live disagreement centres around the relative importance of institutions versus alternative factors in long-run growth. One position emphasizes that institutional quality and stability fundamentally determine a nation's ability to foster innovation, investment, and overall economic dynamism over extended periods. This perspective often highlights institutions like secure property rights and effective governance as crucial for sustainable development. The rival view posits that institutions are secondary to other determinants such as geographic factors or cultural traits, which can independently influence long-term economic outcomes. This debate is critical because it shapes policy recommendations and theoretical frameworks in economics, influencing how governments and international organizations approach development strategies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the trajectory of economic development in colonies. They posit that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, with some colonies receiving extractive institutions that hindered growth while others developed inclusive institutions that fostered prosperity. Austin (2008, p.15) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) builds on this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation and overlooks how indigenous economic actions influenced colonial institutional prescriptions, which in turn affected long-term development.

North (1989, p.6) (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) further underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth by highlighting how political structures that enforce secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges reduce transaction costs. This reduction is critical for fostering a growing economy where increased specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges, leading to sustained economic development.

The relationship between institutional frameworks and long-term economic outcomes is further illuminated by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) emphasis on how colonial institutions shaped the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. They argue that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of these regions, indicating that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were more influential than geographic factors.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) provide additional evidence supporting the role of institutions in economic growth. They demonstrate that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn underpinned economic development. The successful enforcement of these institutions is evident in capital markets, where credible commitment to rules and their enforcement facilitated economic growth.

This suggests that institutional structures are shaped by efficiency considerations as well as power dynamics and societal beliefs, which can significantly influence long-term economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.3) (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) also highlights the importance of institutions in economic growth by emphasizing how shared beliefs within a society form part of its institutional structure. This perspective underscores that institutions are deeply intertwined with cultural and social norms, which collectively shape long-term economic development.

The discussion around institutions and long-run economic growth is crucial because it highlights the multifaceted role of institutions in fostering prosperity. Institutions are influenced by a complex interplay of historical, cultural, and political factors that collectively determine their effectiveness in promoting economic development.

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They claim the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institution-building process was crucial for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the commitment of the regime to these rules is a critical political factor. The development of markets hinges on how these rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Other evidence challenges the implication that institutions per se were not revolutionary after all. This nuanced view underscores that while specific institutions governing public choice are vital for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, their impact can be contextualized by broader historical and comparative perspectives.

Technological advancements and knowledge growth are fundamental drivers of economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that technological progress is the primary source of sustained growth over long periods. This technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, suggesting that while institutions may facilitate growth, they are not its sole driver. He argues that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets (1973, p.1) identifies a crucial role for the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, highlighting how institutions can mitigate the costs associated with technological change and ensure long-term economic stability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that institutional progress is not monotonic or irreversible like technological progress. Thus, while institutions play a significant role in supporting growth, they are not the primary cause of it; rather, they facilitate the efficient use of technological advancements and knowledge growth.

The slave trade significantly impeded African economic development by fostering mistrust and weakening institutions (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the legacy of the slave trade persists in contemporary Africa, shaping societal norms and institutional frameworks in ways that hinder growth. This mistrust operates through both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law, and internal norms within individuals (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), with the latter being at least twice as influential (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade's enduring effects on economic development are a function of contemporary institutions and reflect deep-seated cultural shifts that have persisted over centuries. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights how Africa’s slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on the continent's economic trajectory, with colonialism being just one part of this complex history (Nunn 2008, p.19). This perspective underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic growth, where historical events like the slave trade can have enduring institutional and cultural effects that shape development trajectories.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in colonies. This pattern underscores how colonial policies shaped institutional frameworks that persistently influenced economic development. The legacy of these institutions is evident in the current income per capita of former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in this long-run development trajectory, illustrating the profound and lasting effects of colonial institutions on economic growth. Thus, understanding the historical context of institutional development is crucial for comprehending current economic disparities among nations.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Austin's critique is shared by Broadberry and Gardner, who argue that focusing on institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth overlooks specific instances of commodity production and colonial governance. This approach highlights the importance of examining local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Broadberry and Gardner's work on historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa demonstrates the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, which challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This data reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema builds on this by showing how British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, altering the perceived benefits of formal education for African communities. He argues that demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the impact of colonial rule varied significantly depending on the specific policies implemented and the local context in which they were applied.

These findings underscore the complexity of colonial impacts on economic development, highlighting how local factors and specific policy decisions shaped long-run economic outcomes. The historical record shows that the effects of colonialism depended on a range of contextual factors including commodity production, governance structures, and educational policies.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both demonstrate. They show that the persistence of core economic structures such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe and the New World can be attributed to the influence of elites who benefited from maintaining these institutions, which restricted competition and consolidated their power. This elite-driven institutional design is evident in how colonial policies were implemented across different regions. The New World societies with greater inequality saw policies that advantaged elites through various government measures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These measures included restricting competition and limiting access to resources, which in turn fostered the persistence of inequality. This suggests that institutional design is about power dynamics and distributional conflicts as well as efficiency. Understanding how these cultural factors interact with distributional conflicts provides a more comprehensive view of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally about how they structure incentives and distribute power. Institutions are critical for securing property rights and fostering market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This is shown by the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security in property rights. The development of new financial instruments reflects an institutional evolution that underpins economic growth. This perspective aligns with North and Weingast's argument that credible commitment by regimes is essential for enforcing rules governing public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their analysis of seventeenth-century England demonstrates how political factors, including the enforcement of property rights, were crucial in fostering economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in securing these rights and ensuring that they were enforced effectively.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is about power dynamics and distributional conflicts in addition to efficiency. Acemoglu et al. highlight how extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over broader economic development. This perspective is reinforced by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions served to maintain power structures and redistribute resources in ways that favored certain groups. Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights also played a role in shaping these institutions.

The interaction between cultural beliefs and institutional outcomes further complicates the picture of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Ogilvie acknowledges the influence of shared beliefs on political legitimacy and individual rights, which can affect the design and enforcement of institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is supported by North's argument that institutions are part of a broader system of shared beliefs and values that influence economic outcomes (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Understanding how cultural factors interact with distributional conflicts provides a more comprehensive view of institutional impact on long-run growth.

The remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of cultural beliefs versus power dynamics in shaping institutions. The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this debate is found in Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of European colonization strategies and their long-term economic impacts (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Their work shows that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. This empirical comparison demonstrates how both cultural beliefs and power dynamics interact to shape institutional outcomes, providing a more nuanced understanding of their role in long-run economic growth.
