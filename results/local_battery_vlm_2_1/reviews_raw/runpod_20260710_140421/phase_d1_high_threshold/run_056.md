The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. Institutions are defined as the rules that structure social interactions and can be formal (e.g. laws) or informal (e.g. norms). At stake intellectually is understanding whether institutions are merely passive reflections of economic conditions, or active drivers of economic outcomes. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions matter in their own right, or if they are merely proxies for other underlying factors such as geography, culture, or initial endowments. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions, specifically those that protect property rights and enforce contracts, directly influences economic performance by reducing transaction costs and fostering investment (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Others contend that institutions are shaped by underlying distributional conflicts, which means they may not necessarily promote efficient economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic conditions. Some scholars emphasize the persistence of colonial institutions as a key determinant of current economic performance. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights how historical institutional choices can have lasting effects on economic development.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of institutions on economic growth through the reduction of transaction costs. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that differences in institutional quality explain why formerly poor regions are now rich and vice versa. Austin suggests that this thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the complexity of institutional change and its interaction with other factors, such as indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).

A rival explanation for long-run economic growth emphasizes distributional conflicts and efficiency. This perspective underscores how institutional outcomes can be shaped by power dynamics and political interests as well as purely economic considerations.

The role of credible commitment in shaping long-run economic growth is another crucial aspect highlighted by North and Weingast. They argue that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of political institutions in ensuring that economic rules are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon procedures.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled. While some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on reducing transaction costs and fostering investment, others highlight the influence of historical legacies and distributional conflicts. The interplay between these factors suggests that institutions are not merely passive reflections of economic conditions but active drivers of economic outcomes. Understanding this complex relationship is crucial for designing effective policies to promote long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. describe how Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. This institutional framework was maintained by politically powerful groups who benefited from the status quo and were reluctant to initiate changes that might reduce their rents.

Acemoglu et al. further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors. This reversal was closely linked to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, highlighting how institutions shaped by colonial policies influenced long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in determining the economic trajectories of former colonies.

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and subsequent financial innovations resulted from political developments that secured long-term property rights, which were essential for both a robust capital market and sustained economic expansion. The implications of this institutional evolution are profound: the emergence of effective political institutions that specify and enforce property rights led to falling per-transaction costs. This dynamic is crucial because it enabled greater market participation through increased transactions volume as part of GNP, thereby stimulating economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight how institutions in New World societies were shaped by initial endowments, which influenced the distribution of wealth and power. This inequality was a driver of institutional arrangements that favored elites. The process began with extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. These inequalities persisted over time, shaping institutions such as property rights, financial structures, and public infrastructure (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Broadberry and Gardner corroborate this dynamic by showing how local endowments and political institutions influenced the timing and scale of economic growth in African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This institutional path dependence had profound implications for development trajectories. The persistence of inequality meant that institutions did not evolve to support broad-based economic growth but rather to maintain elite dominance, restricting competition and innovation.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more than other colonial powers did. They note that the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered African perceptions of the benefits of formal education, thus encouraging its development. Frankema (2012, p.15) underscores this dynamic by highlighting how demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by both African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework under colonialism created a foundation for educational advancement that differed from other colonial experiences, contributing to the region's development trajectory. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further reveal that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about economic growth unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This gap in data underscores the need for more comprehensive historical analysis, which could illuminate how colonial institutions shaped long-term economic outcomes. The specific impacts of British rule on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa highlight a mechanism through which institutional changes influenced broader societal developments, even if they did not directly address long-run growth.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Their interpretation focuses on how economic growth during this period was driven by factors including merchant distribution and finance. This perspective highlights that service sectors played a crucial role in early modern economic development, much like they do in the modern era. Van Zanden corroborates this view, noting that the shift of economic development to North Sea countries such as England and Holland after 1700 led to changes in relative price levels (van Zanden 1999, p.9). The implication is that these shifts were more about regional economic dynamics than broad institutional transformations. Van Zanden's findings also suggest that economic development did not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all, indicating uneven distribution of the benefits of growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This nuances the idea that early modern economic growth was universally beneficial, showing instead that its impacts varied widely across different regions and social groups.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Austin 2008, p.2) both examine specific institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena. Austin's work critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by demonstrating that European settlers' presence influenced colonial governments to encourage indigenous commodity production and adapt their policies based on local economic incentives. This dynamic highlights how institutions shaped by settler influence could foster economic development in colonies where Africans were not displaced from the labor market. These institutions arose from power struggles over resources and control, shaping the economic landscape of pre-industrial Europe. Austin's analysis of colonial policies illustrates how institutional prescriptions could be flexible and responsive to local conditions, challenging the notion that institutional frameworks were uniformly rigid or inefficient. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) underscores that these institutions often served to entrench power imbalances rather than promote economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The implication is that while institutions in pre-industrial Europe could adapt and evolve based on local pressures, they tended to reinforce existing social hierarchies.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development. The slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. This mistrust has been shown to be at least half responsible for the negative impact of the slave trade on trust, and this mistrust is a key factor in inhibiting economic growth. Nunn highlights that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period significantly longer than the roughly 75 years of official colonial rule. This extensive duration underscores the profound and lasting impact of the slave trade on African societies, shaping subsequent economic development in ways that go beyond more recent historical events. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that while the slave trade had a detrimental effect, pre-existing institutional structures could mitigate some of its adverse impacts on economic development.

This process involves more than just innovation; it requires institutional adjustments to facilitate the efficient use of new technologies. This perspective highlights how institutions can either foster or hinder technological adoption and diffusion, thereby influencing long-term economic outcomes. For example, Mokyr (2005, p.49) describes how the growth of useful knowledge was supported by formal institutions that reduced access costs to new information, thereby accelerating technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) cautions that economic growth can be derailed if institutional changes fail to resolve conflicts arising from technological advancements. The resolution of such conflicts is crucial for maintaining the political stability needed for sustained economic growth. This suggests that while technology drives long-term economic progress, institutions play a pivotal role in ensuring that this progress is sustainable and beneficial to society as a whole.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster stability and security, which are critical for sustained development. North shows that the establishment of secure property rights through political institutions significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework not only encouraged investment but also ensured that economic growth was sustainable over the long term. Similarly, North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth by enforcing rules that govern economic exchange and ensuring that these rules are binding on the regime or sovereign (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings highlight that institutions play a pivotal role in creating an environment conducive to economic progress.

Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonization had lasting effects on economic development. They show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers has enduring consequences for a region's economic trajectory.

The historical evidence from various regions shows that the interaction between institutions and technological advancements is crucial for sustained economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, emphasizing that different historical periods and paths must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional changes and their impact on long-run economic development. Similarly, Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions in ways that prioritize the interests of powerful groups over efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Institutions matter because they provide the framework within which technological advancements can be effectively utilized. North and Weingast show that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for fostering a stable environment where technological progress can thrive (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that institutions designed to maximize rents rather than promote growth can hinder long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence of colonial institutions and their enduring impact on current income levels provides a clear measurement of this dynamic.
