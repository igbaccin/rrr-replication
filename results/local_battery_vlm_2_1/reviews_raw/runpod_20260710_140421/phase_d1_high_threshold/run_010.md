The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing investment decisions, property rights, and market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Others propose that the impact of institutions is overstated, suggesting that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs are more critical in determining institutional structures and their effectiveness (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2).

A key point of contention is whether institutions evolved to enhance economic efficiency or were shaped by underlying power dynamics and distributive struggles. This debate extends to the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, such as the enforcement of property rights and the reduction of transaction costs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The disagreement underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and long-term economic performance.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. This process is driven by increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by noting that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced long-term economic performance, with settler colonies that enforced rule of law and encouraged investment achieving higher income per capita than extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this perspective by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if they maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), highlighting the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes. The specific historical context of colonization thus shapes both the type of institutions established and their impact on economic development, illustrating how power dynamics can influence institutional frameworks and their effectiveness in promoting growth.

This historical context is further illuminated by the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, which fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrated that the development of credible commitment mechanisms enabled stable political structures, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework enhanced market efficiency and encouraged investment by ensuring that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced consistently. The success of these institutions was evident in the thriving capital markets, which reflected the confidence of investors in the stability and security provided by the new political regime (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic growth varies across different historical contexts. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlighted that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This differential in institutional evolution suggests that the initial conditions, such as factor endowments and colonial policies, played a significant role in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of these societies. These findings underscore that while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, the specific historical context and initial conditions can significantly influence institutional outcomes and their effectiveness in promoting long-term development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) advance the idea that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They argue that these conflicts, driven by power struggles between different social groups, played a more significant role than efficiency considerations in institutional development. For instance, serfdom and guilds can be seen as outcomes of negotiations between landlords and peasants, or between masters and journeymen, rather than efficient responses to economic conditions. Ogilvie highlights that the persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the balance of power among these groups, which often led to suboptimal outcomes from an efficiency perspective but were optimal for maintaining social order and controlling resources. This perspective is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack's examination of African women’s economic contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They show how patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule marginalized women, reinforcing the idea that distributional conflicts shaped institutional outcomes. These institutions did not always hinder productivity; they could also provide stability and security, which were crucial for long-term economic growth. The implication is that institutional arrangements resulting from distributional conflicts can have complex effects on economic development, sometimes fostering growth despite apparent inefficiencies.

This complexity is further compounded by the role of technological advancements in driving economic growth through the accumulation of useful knowledge and improved productivity (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize that technological progress is a primary driver of long-term economic growth. While institutions are necessary for harnessing these technologies effectively, the underlying engine of growth remains the generation and application of new knowledge. This perspective highlights that technological innovations create new opportunities for economic expansion but also necessitate institutional adjustments to integrate them efficiently into society.

The interplay between technology and institutions is dynamic; technological progress often outpaces institutional adaptation, leading to periods of strain. Kuznets notes that modern technology requires significant shifts in societal structures and ideologies (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements spur growth, the pace at which institutions adapt to these changes can influence the sustainability of economic improvement. The role of institutions becomes crucial in managing the conflicts arising from technological progress, ensuring that these innovations are integrated without excessive social costs.

Mokyr elaborates on this by pointing out that institutional progress is essential for sustaining economic gains driven by technology (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without complementary institutional advancements, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. This implies that while institutions may not be the primary drivers of growth, they are indispensable in maintaining the long-term benefits of technological innovations. The effective management of technological advancement and institutional adaptation is key to achieving sustained economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that Africa's slave trades had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). They present evidence of the slave trade's role in shaping African institutions, suggesting that this legacy is not just an artifact of colonialism or more recent events but a significant force in its own right. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa; this mistrust is rooted deeply enough to persist today and contributes significantly to the reduced trust levels observed in contemporary African societies (Nunn 2008, p.2). Trust, as established by other studies, is crucial for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) (see: Knack & Keefer 1997, Fafchamps 2006). Nunn and Wantchekon show that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the slave trade's negative impact on intergroup trust. The legacy of institutional mistrust is not merely a consequence of external factors such as rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, but also stems from internal norms of mistrust cultivated during the slave trades (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This finding suggests that the historical experience of the slave trade has left an enduring mark on African institutions. The implication here is that addressing contemporary economic challenges in Africa may require not just institutional reforms but also efforts to rebuild trust at a societal level, given the deep-seated nature of this legacy.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) show that colonial rule in Africa shaped economic trajectories differently across continents by introducing varied institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.2) posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, as it compresses different historical periods and paths. Colonial administrations influenced indigenous commodity production and adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). These adjustments often favoured higher production equilibria, demonstrating that colonial institutions were not monolithic but adaptable to local conditions. This variability underscores the importance of understanding specific historical contexts when assessing the impact of colonial rule on African economies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). They assert that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. This complexity suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, their impact varied based on specific historical and geographical contexts.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' strategies, have significantly influenced Africa's long-term economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. show that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), leading to varied institutional setups across colonies. In some cases, they established extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole, while in others, they set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment. This variation highlights how specific historical conditions influenced economic policies and local responses (Austin 2008, p.15). These findings underscore that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping Africa's long-term welfare development.

The impact of colonial institutions on African economies was complex and varied, as seen through the interplay between institutional setups and local responses. Austin notes that where European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial governments' interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that while colonial institutions were pivotal, their effects were shaped by specific historical and geographical contexts. For instance, colonial administrations seeking fiscal security adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth extends beyond colonial legacies. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing economic growth by increasing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported a growing economy. Similarly, Ogilvie emphasizes the importance of understanding how institutions function within specific historical contexts to grasp their impact on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The precise role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term African welfare development remains debated. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Austin suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), indicating a need for more nuanced analyses. The specific historical or geographical conditions underpinning these debates include varying colonial strategies based on mortality rates and local responses to institutional setups, as well as the evolving nature of institutions over time.
