The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it addresses how societies can foster sustained prosperity. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the importance of institutions in driving economic development and those who propose alternative factors. Some argue that institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Others contend that colonial policies and their legacy significantly impact long-run economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). There is also a debate about the extent to which institutions matter in different historical contexts. Some scholars posit that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; North 1989, p.9). By exploring these perspectives, one can gain a deeper understanding of how institutional frameworks influence long-run economic outcomes. The question remains whether the impact of institutions is primarily through their role in shaping property rights and governance structures, or if alternative factors such as colonial policies play a more significant role.

The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were crucial in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering secure property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) underscored that the success of these institutions was underpinned by credible commitments from the regime to enforce property rights, which in turn drove economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional changes in England during this period encompassed both economic exchange and political factors governing rule enforcement and change. While the development of financial instruments and institutions was pivotal, it is important to note that the specific historical context and political commitments played a significant role in their effectiveness. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) observed that the institutional changes in England were part of a broader historical process including the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This nuanced understanding suggests that while financial institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by the political and historical context in which they operate.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The centrality of technological progress in driving long-run economic growth, as highlighted by Mokyr, underscores the importance of institutional adjustments to facilitate this process. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, necessitating significant institutional changes for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). As a result, technological progress requires complementary institutional developments to ensure sustained growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) reinforces this idea by noting that without institutional progress, economic improvements may be limited or reversed due to rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for long-term economic sustainability.

The historical context of technological progress reveals that societal conflicts can arise from the challenges posed by new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in resolving these growth-induced conflicts peacefully, thereby ensuring the continuity of modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of political stability and effective governance in supporting technological advancements.

Thus, while institutions play a role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader historical and political context.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism profoundly shaped long-run economic growth in former European colonies. The specific colonization strategies employed by Europeans led to divergent institutional frameworks in their colonies. In settler colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development: settler colonies experienced significant long-term growth due to their institutions that fostered investment and innovation; colonies with extractive institutions, however, struggled economically as these institutions maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.19) also emphasize the role of political power in shaping these institutions: powerful groups often resisted institutional changes that might threaten their political dominance, even if such changes could spur economic development. This dynamic highlights how colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as extractive institutions persist and hinder growth in many former colonies.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. The institutional framework introduced by the British created an environment where formal education was seen as a valuable asset, unlike other colonial powers whose institutions often did not prioritize educational development. This institutional support for education laid the groundwork for long-term economic benefits, as educated populations are better equipped to drive innovation and productivity. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely within and between countries, influenced by factors such as local endowments and transport costs, suggesting that while British institutions fostered education, other contextual factors also played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that this persistent mistrust is a direct legacy of the slave trade, which had profound effects on African economic development. This mistrust has significant implications for economic growth, as trust is a crucial factor in facilitating cooperation and reducing transaction costs (Nunn 2008, p.19) . The mechanism through which the slave trade affects trust involves both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the internal channel of mistrust, how individuals perceive and interact with others within their community, is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) . While this internal mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, it also highlights the importance of addressing these deep-seated social issues in order to foster growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and institutional reforms in Africa are seen as important for understanding economic growth but have been critiqued for oversimplifying causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, which can obscure the complex interplay between institutions and long-term economic development. This critique is important because it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. The effectiveness of these reforms depends on the quality of institutions and their ability to provide incentives for investment. Englebert's findings indicate that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments, implying that institutional quality can vary independently of ethnic diversity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight underscores the importance of focusing on institutional reforms that foster economic growth, regardless of ethnic composition. Therefore, while historical context is vital, the success of institutional reforms in Africa ultimately hinges on creating robust institutions that support long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster secure property rights and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling costs per transaction. This development facilitates market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth.

North and Weingast further emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), as seen in seventeenth-century England where institutional changes were crucial for fostering secure property rights. The success of these institutions is evident in the remarkable evidence from capital markets. Austin agrees with this emphasis on institutions, noting that economic historians see behavior today in the context of institutional evolution and investment experience over centuries (Austin 2008, p.2). This historical perspective highlights how robust institutions support long-term economic development.

Acemoglu et al. add that extractive states designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), showing that equilibrium institutions may be designed for short-term gains rather than long-term prosperity. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further supports this view by noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation shows that institutional changes can be responsive to economic and political action, underscoring the dynamic nature of institutions in shaping long-term growth.

Hopkins argues that ethnically homogeneous societies may still have poor institutions, while heterogeneous ones might develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18), highlighting that ethnic diversity does not determine institutional quality. This insight aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that extractive institutions do not necessarily lead to long-term growth, emphasizing the importance of focusing on institutional reforms that foster economic development regardless of ethnic composition.

The agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Disagreement remains over whether extractive institutions can ever support sustainable growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize short-term rents rather than promote long-term prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence from the former colonies shows that institutions designed for extraction did not lead to sustained economic development, settling this disagreement by demonstrating that extractive institutions hinder long-run growth.
