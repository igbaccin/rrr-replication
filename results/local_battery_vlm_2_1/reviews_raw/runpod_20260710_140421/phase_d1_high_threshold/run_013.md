Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. This issue is central to debates about why some countries develop rapidly while others stagnate. The crux of this debate lies between two main positions. One position argues that institutions are paramount, emphasizing how different institutional setups can either foster or hinder economic development. A rival position focuses on geographic factors and population density as the primary drivers of growth, downplaying the role of institutions in favor of these natural determinants.

The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to other factors such as geography and population density, or if they are exogenous variables that independently shape economic outcomes. This debate is critical because it determines how policymakers should approach economic development: by focusing on institutional reforms or by addressing geographic and demographic challenges. Institutions can be seen as either the product of underlying conditions or as the architects of long-term growth. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for understanding the historical paths of development and for designing effective policies to promote economic prosperity.

North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is evident in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which were crucial for fostering secure property rights and thereby promoting economic growth. This perspective aligns with North's broader argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth (North 1989, p.6), highlighting how political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement can drive economic development. The emergence of these institutions reflects a shift towards increased security of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, thereby encouraging investment and specialization.

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He contends that this thesis overlooks the complex interplay between institutions and economic development over time. Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth. The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial because it reduces costs per transaction, thereby encouraging investment and specialization.

Austin also highlights the role of colonial administrations in shaping institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). He argues that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This dynamic influenced the institutional prescriptions adopted by these administrations, which in turn affected economic development. Austin's analysis shows how institutions can be shaped by both exogenous factors and endogenous processes, underscoring the complexity of institutional change.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that the differing mortality rates of Europeans across colonies led to distinct colonization strategies; where settlers faced high mortality, they established extractive states designed for rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with lower mortality rates, such as North America and Australia, European settlers established institutions that safeguarded property rights and fostered investment. The extractive institutions in high-mortality areas were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to foster long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic underscores how colonial policies shaped by mortality rates resulted in enduring economic disparities. The robustness of this finding is further supported by the consistent results across various measures of institutions, including constraints on executive power and property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Building upon this framework, North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly argue that specific institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth. This institutional evolution was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North underscores that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) advise that these institutional shifts were important but did not universally transform societies. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic growth, where context and specific historical circumstances play a pivotal role.

The slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27)(Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. The internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This finding complements broader research documenting the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.2) builds on this by arguing that Africa's slave trades had a long-lasting impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to factors like colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2). The duration and extent of the slave trade in Africa were substantial. For nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, Africa experienced four simultaneous slave trades, a period significantly longer than that of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context underscores the profound impact of the slave trade on African economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) also observes that Africa's sustained trade began when the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom, which featured a centralized government and well-established markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This qualification suggests that while the slave trades had a detrimental effect, the presence of certain institutional factors could mitigate some of the adverse impacts.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) establish that African poverty was not an immutable historical condition but instead varied over time. The presence of colonial institutions significantly shaped the economic landscape. For instance, British Africa's real wages were influenced by a combination of endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). These factors drove patterns of growth and contraction in export production, which were pivotal to the economies of many African countries during this period. The timing and scale of these economic shifts varied widely due to local conditions, such as natural resource availability and infrastructure development. Colonial institutions actively molded these factors. Broadberry and Gardner show that despite these variations, many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This indicates a more complex economic history than previously acknowledged, where institutional frameworks played a crucial role in determining economic outcomes. The degree to which particular historical or geographical conditions have produced temporary versus enduring impacts on Africa's long-term welfare development is still debated.

The persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutions that favored elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) illustrate how unequal distribution of land and natural resources led to the emergence of a powerful elite class, which then shaped institutions to maintain their dominance. This elite control was exercised through policies that restricted competition and access to education for the broader population (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4), as formal education became a tool for perpetuating social hierarchies and limiting upward mobility among lower classes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights how colonial powers played pivotal roles in shaping educational institutions, with British rule being more conducive to developing formal education systems than other colonial regimes. This differential impact on education underscores the broader institutional framework that either facilitated or hindered long-term economic growth. The persistence of inequality thus became intertwined with the evolution of institutions that both reflected and amplified pre-existing social disparities.

By shaping educational policies to favor elites, these colonial institutions ensured that economic growth was managed by a select few.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and new economic history of Africa jointly critique over-simplified causation in explaining long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique is significant because it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution, which can vary widely across regions and time periods. This perspective underscores the importance of contextualizing economic growth within specific historical and institutional frameworks. Colonial administrations' reactions to indigenous economic and political initiatives present a more intricate scenario. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that in some cases, colonial governments encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent, and adjusted their policies based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This pattern suggests that institutions can adapt to local conditions, potentially fostering growth even within constrained environments. Yet Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to provide incentives for investment, which is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This qualification implies that while institutional adaptation is possible, it must be directed towards creating robust incentives for economic activity. Therefore, understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth requires a detailed examination of both historical contexts and the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate in different settings.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework within which economic activities occur. North and Weingast show that secure property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is evident in historical contexts where institutions have evolved to enforce rules governing economic exchanges, thereby creating an environment conducive to investment and development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the long-run economic trajectories of colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Specifically, extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-term growth in many regions.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the historical evolution of financial systems. North highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects an increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. Similarly, Austin argues that the institutional evolution in Europe and its overseas 'off-shoots' provides valuable insights into how institutions can drive economic growth over extended periods (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding shows that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by their ability to adapt and respond to changing economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive but are shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This implies that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to provide incentives for investment, which is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Therefore, understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth requires a detailed examination of both historical contexts and the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate in different settings. The empirical evidence from capital markets shows that institutional changes can significantly impact economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The precise remaining disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is whether these institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or by endogenous political and economic processes. North argues for the importance of institutions per se, showing that the development of secure property rights was a critical factor in economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. stress that the kind of institutions implemented during colonization greatly affected long-run economic paths, underscoring endogenous political processes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This disagreement is settled by comparing the institutional changes and their impacts on capital markets in England and France, where the evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
