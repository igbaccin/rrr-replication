Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but scholars disagree on how and why. The stakes are high because the answer determines whether economic development is an endogenous process that can be engineered through institutional design or an exogenous one driven by factors beyond human control. One key disagreement revolves around the nature of institutions themselves: do they reflect underlying cultural beliefs, or do they emerge as strategic responses to economic opportunities? Another dispute centers on the role of colonialism in shaping institutions and, consequently, long-run growth trajectories. Some researchers argue that extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers have had lasting negative effects on economic development in former colonies, while others contend that these institutions were not solely responsible for contemporary outcomes. The debate is further complicated by the question of whether institutional changes can be engineered to promote economic growth or if they are path-dependent and resistant to change. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) assert that European policies shaped colonial institutions in ways that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This was because the Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies depending on mortality rates and other factors. This divergence in institutional design had profound effects on economic development. These institutions were designed to benefit politically powerful groups and did not necessarily encourage economic development. This institutional legacy has persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies even today. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions affected growth varied by region, but the overarching pattern is clear: colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Institutional reforms are pivotal for African economic development as they address governance issues and policy recommendations that can eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa is crucial for informing these institutional reforms, which will help alleviate poverty. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that colonial institutions were not uniformly extractive; instead, their impact varied based on local conditions and responses. This variation underscores how indigenous economic and political actions influenced colonial administrations' decisions, leading to different institutional outcomes. For example, in regions where European settlers did not compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could foster economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). This aligns with Hopkins's (2009) emphasis on the importance of "good" institutions that provide incentives for investment and are essential for development. However, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the institutionalist approach faces challenges in explaining why some countries adopted growth-favorable institutions while others did not, highlighting the need for further research into the specific conditions that drive institutional change. This complexity is important to consider when designing reforms aimed at fostering long-term economic growth in Africa. This shift allowed the new economic history of Africa to gain traction among development specialists, who recognized that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment and thus essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that England's seventeenth-century institutional evolution was critical for securing property rights and spurring economic growth. They highlight the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 as a pivotal moment, which led to a significant reduction in transaction costs and facilitated long-term capital markets. The emergence of political institutions that defined and enforced efficient property rights resulted in falling transaction costs, fostering market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast's analysis underscores the importance of credible commitment by regimes to enforce rules governing economic exchange and facilitate institutional change. This commitment was essential for ensuring that political institutions supported economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, recent research has challenged traditional views on British economic history, suggesting that England and France were nearly at parity in economic performance before the French Revolution.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that these factors have significantly shaped economic development in different regions by influencing the institutional frameworks. Their work highlights how the slave trade led to institutional changes that entrenched inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, developed institutions that favored elites, limiting competition and perpetuating disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.19) further underscores the adverse effects of the slave trade on Africa's economic development by noting that sustained trade required centralized governance and market structures. This centralization was often disrupted by the slave trade, which fragmented societies and weakened institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the legacy of mistrust stemming from the slave trade persists to this day, significantly impeding economic development in Africa (Nunn 2008; Nunn&Wantchekon_2011) (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). Consequently, historical factors like inequality and the slave trade have not only shaped institutions but also left enduring impacts on trust and social cohesion, which are crucial for economic growth.

Women were central to African economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Ogilvie's work on how institutions shape economic outcomes posits that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure of society. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that women significantly contributed to this growth, particularly through their roles in agriculture and trade. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, which underscores the impact of gender dynamics on resource allocation and economic productivity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The role of colonial education systems also influenced African development by altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). British rule fostered a more conducive environment for developing formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, highlighting how institutional frameworks shaped educational outcomes. This does not negate the impact of patriarchal alliances and customary law, which reinforced gender disparities during the colonial era (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Frankema 2012, p.15).

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) share the view that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. The development of modern technology requires significant adjustments in societal norms and institutions to ensure efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets highlights how epochal innovations, such as those in the Industrial Revolution, have been pivotal in driving long-term economic growth across regions (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

Without these complementary developments, Mokyr argues, economic gains might be undermined by rent-seeking behavior, thereby limiting the sustainability of economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) also points out that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs is necessary for modern economic growth to be possible. The role of the sovereign state in resolving these conflicts peacefully underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This highlights how institutional progress, while not the primary driver, plays a critical role in complementing and sustaining technological improvements.

The interplay between technology and institutions is complex. While technological advancements drive economic growth, the effectiveness of these innovations depends on the adaptability and robustness of existing institutions.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how technological advancements are adopted and sustained. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is because the effectiveness of technological innovations depends on institutional adaptability and robustness, which in turn influences long-term economic growth. North shows that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

The historical context of institutional development reveals how specific policies and institutions can either foster or hinder economic growth. Hopkins argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but rather by the presence of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This highlights how institutional reforms can lead to significant economic improvements, even in regions with complex socio-political dynamics.

The role of colonialism in shaping institutions and economic outcomes is particularly noteworthy. Austin argues that European settlers' presence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production and the institutional prescriptions adopted by colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic shows how the interaction between colonial policies and local economic actions can shape long-term development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional choices (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The interplay between technology and institutions underscores the importance of supportive institutional environments for realizing the potential of useful knowledge. North emphasizes that the creation of financial instruments and secure property rights led to a decline in transaction costs, which is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutional designs are often shaped by power dynamics rather than purely economic considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is clear: they facilitate technological advancements and sustain long-term development. All sources agree on this underlying point (Austin 2008, p.15). The remaining disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Austin argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic actions, suggesting a dynamic interplay between external policies and indigenous responses (Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al. show that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists led to extractive states that hindered long-run growth in former colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their impact on current income per capita settles this disagreement, as shown by the interaction between population density, prosperity, and the policies pursued by Europeans during colonial periods.
