The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars who study economic development. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and prosperity, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. Some argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Others contend that institutions alone cannot explain long-term economic development without considering distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), and that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development, particularly in regions where historical institutions may have hindered progress. A key disagreement lies between those who view institutions as the primary cause of economic outcomes and those who see them as one among many factors, or even as a secondary factor. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth involves identifying causal mechanisms and understanding their relative importance in different contexts (Author Year, p.N). A stream of scholarship explores the specific ways in which colonial institutions have shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, focusing on the enduring effects of extractive versus inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.9) describes this financial revolution as involving both financial innovation and increased security of property rights. This institutional framework provided a stable environment for long-term capital markets to flourish, directly impacting economic development. However, North (1989, p.9) also notes that the emergence of these political institutions led to an increase in the size of the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP, reflecting the growing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This implies that while secure property rights were crucial, the broader economic landscape was also transforming in ways that amplified the benefits of institutional changes.

Colonial institutions, through their influence on economic policies and resource extraction strategies, significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. explain that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on the mortality rates they faced in colonized regions: extractive institutions were implemented where high mortality rates deterred settlement and encouraged resource extraction, while more inclusive institutions were set up where Europeans could settle permanently (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of these divergent institutions was not merely a response to geographic conditions but also a strategic choice that maximized the rents for European colonists. This pattern is evident in Acemoglu et al.'s analysis, which shows how extractive institutions were designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole rather than fostering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutional frameworks has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive ones promoting it. This suggests that while colonial institutions set initial conditions for economic development, subsequent internal political dynamics can modify or reinforce these effects.

Shifting focus to pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that these conflicts shaped institutions such as serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. The mechanism through which distributional conflicts influence economic institutions is rooted in the political power dynamics of pre-industrial societies; elites often controlled institutions to maintain their dominance, even if this impeded overall economic efficiency. This dynamic is evident in the persistent inequality observed in New World societies with extractive institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In England, for example, the evolution of institutions was influenced by the struggle between different social groups over resources and political power.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not account for the fact that colonial institutions were adopted in some countries but not in others. Hopkins (2009, p.18) supports this critique by emphasizing that African poverty is not solely attributable to poor institutions, as societies can be ethnically homogeneous or heterogeneous and still develop progressive institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interest of colonial governments when European settlers were absent. This dynamic highlights how institutional adoption varied based on local economic and political actions, rather than a uniform institutional prescription. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this perspective by suggesting that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments but rather a result of specific historical conditions that can be temporary or structural. This nuanced view challenges the notion that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, indicating that historical context and local dynamics play crucial roles in shaping institutional outcomes.

In contrast to this, the slave trade in Africa had a profound impact on its long-term economic development, creating a culture of mistrust that persists to this day and significantly affecting institutional structures (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights the enduring effects of the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. This period is markedly longer than the roughly 75 years of official colonial rule, suggesting that the institutional legacies of slavery are more deeply entrenched than those of colonialism. The centrality of mistrust in this dynamic is further underscored by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who find that a substantial portion of the slave trade's effect on trust operates through internal norms rather than external environmental factors such as rule of law or the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, which is at least twice as large as the external channel, has significant implications for economic development. Nunn also provides a counterpoint to those who might attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events, emphasizing that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context challenges the notion that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Kuznets's analysis supports this view, noting that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective suggests that the accumulation of useful knowledge can lead to technological progress and economic improvements, which in turn require institutional adjustments for efficient use.

This implies that while technology is crucial, institutions play a supplementary role in sustaining economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further qualifies the downstream implications of these adjustments: modern economic growth requires resolving conflicts without excessive costs or long-term weakening of political stability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by institutional changes to ensure sustainable development. Kuznets's and Mokyr's works share a common emphasis on technology as the primary driver of economic growth: technological advancements not only drive growth but also necessitate institutional adjustments for sustained prosperity.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding sustained prosperity and development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. They argue that extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources back to the metropole, hindered long-term economic growth in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast. This aligns with North's argument that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2) underscores the importance of institutional evolution, noting that while institutions matter for economic growth, their adoption varies across countries due to different historical paths and political contexts. The shared finding is that long-run economic growth depends on the establishment of institutions that encourage investment and secure property rights.

The historical evidence from Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2) shows that in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production to ensure fiscal security. This institutional adaptation reflects a broader pattern where colonial policies responded to local economic and political actions, shaping the long-term development paths of these regions. Austin's work highlights how Governor-General Clifford opted for higher production equilibria in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.3) further emphasizes that institutions are not merely efficiency-driven but also shaped by distributional conflicts and political power dynamics. This perspective is crucial for understanding the persistence of extractive institutions in some regions, as these institutions often maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The interaction between colonial policies and local institutional adaptations is a key factor in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, which had lasting effects on their development trajectories . This aligns with North's argument that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He underscores that 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment, are crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise measurement of income per capita across former colonies and the historical analysis of colonial policies settle the disagreement on the role of institutions versus geographic factors. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-term development. This is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
