The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic that has sparked significant debate among economists and historians alike. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder sustained economic development over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography, culture, or endogenous processes driven by political and economic actors. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key to unlocking growth, while others contend that they are merely a reflection of underlying economic conditions. This debate is crucial because it influences policy recommendations aimed at promoting economic development in both developed and developing countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of vastly different institutions across colonies, with some fostering growth through secure property rights and others stifling it through extractive practices. This perspective highlights the critical role of institutions in determining economic outcomes over the long term. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) emphasizes that efficient property rights and their enforcement are pivotal for economic development. He posits that political institutions specifying these rights can reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and promoting growth. The shared finding is that institutions matter significantly in fostering long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) share the stance that institutions are critical for economic growth. They argue that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights can reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and promoting growth. North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England during the English financial revolution led to a significant decline in transaction costs due to increased security of time dimension property rights (North 1989, p.9). This variation in institutions has been shown to significantly impact current income per capita levels. The implications extend beyond the past; Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents and long-run growth is not a priority (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They suggest that colonial powers introduced institutions based on their immediate interests, which often did not align with fostering sustainable economic development in the colonies. This historical perspective underscores the enduring effects of institutional frameworks established during colonization and their lasting impact on economic trajectories.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for establishing secure property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the commitment to rules by the regime underpins economic development. The success of England's institutions can be seen in capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated investment and growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate how institutional frameworks shaped by inequality in New World societies restricted competition and maintained elite advantages, contributing to persistent inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional evolution underscores the role of public choice institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. In New World societies, initial conditions of inequality influenced institutional development, leading to varying degrees of economic success. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between historical contexts and institutional frameworks in determining long-run economic trajectories.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.2) share a finding that Africa's economic development has been significantly impeded by historical factors beyond recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). Nunn argues that the legacy of slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, has had lasting adverse effects on economic institutions and growth. This historical burden is compounded by the enduring influence of patrimonial governance structures, where political power is used to distribute resources among supporters and investing in broad-based public goods is not prioritized (Nunn 2008, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). Simson illustrates this dynamic through post-independence expenditure trends, noting that military spending and debt service payments often displaced investments in capital and recurrent spending. This pattern was particularly pronounced in Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson also notes that Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier receipt of debt relief. This variation suggests that while patrimonial tendencies are pervasive, the specific outcomes depend on the interplay between historical contexts and contemporary policy choices.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting trust levels within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate this by showing how the slave trade significantly reduced intergroup trust. This enduring mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines social cohesion and hampers collective action. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further illustrate how patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced gender inequalities during colonial rule, with women often relegated to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered impact of the slave trade's legacy is particularly relevant because it intersects with broader patterns of mistrust. The codification of customary law under colonial rule marginalized women and entrenched patriarchal structures that continue to shape economic and social dynamics in Africa today (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). One downstream implication is that this historical mistrust can influence contemporary policy choices, as seen in the variation between Kenya and Uganda's debt payments. This suggests that historical legacies of mistrust and inequality continue to shape economic outcomes, even as contemporary policies seek to address them.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms by examining how ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This perspective is echoed in Frankema's (2012, p.15) analysis, which shows that demand for missionary education was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). The institutional changes introduced during colonialism altered the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby influencing local initiatives and economic development. This interplay between exogenous factors and local responses is crucial because it shaped the incentives for investment, which are essential for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting how different historical periods and paths complicate causal explanations for economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic outcomes, suggesting that a more nuanced understanding is necessary. For instance, Austin points out that colonial administrations sometimes adjusted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating how local responses can influence institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between external institutions and local adaptations suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is influenced by a variety of factors including local initiatives. This perspective complements Austin's critique by showing how shared beliefs and cultural values influence the adoption and effectiveness of institutions, further complicating the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to provide secure property rights and enforce contracts. North shows that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement can reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth by fostering a more dynamic transactions sector within the economy (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. complement this view by demonstrating that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2). The extractive institutions imposed in high-mortality colonies, for example, persisted and negatively impacted current income per capita. This underscores how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic development.

Institutional effectiveness is also contingent upon the broader cultural and political context. Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions more than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights influence which institutions are adopted and how effectively they function, as these beliefs can either support or hinder institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin further elaborates on this by showing that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15), indicating a dynamic interplay between external impositions and indigenous initiatives. This interplay suggests that institutions evolve in response to the complex interactions of various factors, including local beliefs and actions.

The historical evidence from former European colonies provides clear insights into how institutional legacies shape long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is directly linked to the institutions introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-term growth was not a priority, had lasting impacts on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Hopkins's work on Africa further illustrates this point by emphasizing the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by ethnic fragmentation and the effectiveness of their institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise historical evidence that settles institutional disagreements about long-run economic growth comes from comparative analyses of former European colonies, particularly those with high mortality rates. These cases show how extractive institutions imposed during colonial periods persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison highlights the enduring impact of initial institutional frameworks on long-term economic outcomes, underscoring the critical role that institutions play in shaping economic growth.
