The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and history, with significant implications for understanding development trajectories across different regions and time periods; the stakes are high because this question bears on how to understand why some societies have achieved sustained economic progress while others have not. The debate hinges on whether institutions fundamentally determine economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. One position emphasizes that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are the primary drivers of long-term growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Another view contends that the influence of institutions is often overstated, and that other factors such as colonialism and its legacies have a more direct impact on economic development. There is also disagreement about whether efficiency or distributional conflicts are the core drivers of institutional change; some argue that institutions evolve to maximize efficiency while others contend they are shaped by power dynamics and the interests of dominant groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The resolution of these debates has profound implications for policy, particularly in understanding how to foster economic growth in developing regions. Institutions can be a powerful lens through which to view economic development, but their precise role remains contested.

North argues that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the evolution of new financial tools were pivotal in enhancing secure property rights. These changes fostered long-run economic growth by lowering the cost of transacting, which is critical for both a robust capital market and overall economic development. This argument aligns with North and Weingast's analysis that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The emergence of these institutions facilitated market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs per exchange while increasing the aggregate volume of transactions. This dynamic is evident in the growing proportion of GDP allocated to the transactions sector as specialization and division of labor intensified. North (1989, p.6) observes that the effectiveness of these institutions depends on the regime’s commitment to enforcing these rules. North and Weingast emphasize this point by highlighting how the credibility of the state in upholding property rights is a key determinant of economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credibility underpins the effective functioning of capital markets, which in turn supports sustained economic development.

Colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions Europeans set up depended on their mortality rates and settlement patterns. This institutional framework persisted over time, shaping long-term economic trajectories. The persistence of extractive institutions hindered economic growth by failing to encourage investment or enforce property rights effectively, which are crucial for sustainable development. A specific mechanism through which these institutions operated was their impact on capital markets. Extractive states often imposed heavy taxation and regulations that discouraged private investment, thereby stifling the growth of capital-intensive industries (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) observe that extractive institutions hindered long-run economic growth but did not block all development; in some cases, they facilitated limited industrialization and modernization efforts with potential longer-term implications for economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner assert that African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding is shared with Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), who argue that colonial rule in British Africa saw real wage levels increase over time. The economic performance of these regions was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, which drove growth patterns within countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This highlights the significant role institutions played in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating or hindering participation in global trade networks. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the long-term picture of African welfare development was shaped by colonial institutions as well as specific historical or geographical conditions. This suggests that while institutions were crucial, they interacted with other factors to determine economic outcomes over time.

The slave trade's legacy of mistrust in Africa offers an alternative explanation for the region's economic stagnation, challenging the primacy of institutions as the sole determinant of long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon posit that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day. This mistrust is internalised through norms that are at least twice as large in impact than external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic: they evolved in response to indigenous economic and political actions, meaning that their effects varied significantly across Africa. This variability implies that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust may have interacted with diverse institutional contexts, leading to complex outcomes that go beyond simple institutional determinants (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The interaction between this historical legacy and varying colonial institutions may have produced a mosaic of economic outcomes across Africa.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets jointly establish that technological advancement requires institutional adjustments for efficient use. Institutions can facilitate the diffusion of new technologies through the creation of formal structures such as scholarly societies, which reduce access costs to knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This process involves coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors that institutions must address to sustain economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of useful knowledge is not a linear process; it requires institutional progress to complement technological advancements.

This interplay between technology and institutions highlights that while technological progress is essential for economic growth, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that can adapt to new knowledge. The process of integrating technological innovations into the economy involves resolving conflicts and coordinating efforts across society. Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrate that the relationship between technology, institutions, and economic growth is complex and multifaceted (). This complexity suggests that while technological progress is a crucial driver of long-run economic growth, it must be supported by robust institutional frameworks that can adapt to new knowledge and manage the societal changes that accompany technological advancements.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that these conflicts are pivotal in determining the structure of economic institutions alongside efficiency concerns. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be explained by the power struggles between landowners and peasants, where landowners used their political influence to maintain a system that benefited them economically (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is mirrored in New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites. For example, policies restricting competition and maintaining elite control were implemented through government actions that reinforced existing power structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The impact of these institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth is complex. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggest that while initial inequality may have driven certain institutional developments, the persistence of these institutions could hinder growth over time by limiting competition and innovation. This highlights a complex interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional development, and economic outcomes, where the long-term effects of institutions shaped by conflict can be both enabling and constraining for economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the nature of property rights and their enforcement. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) demonstrates that secure property rights are crucial for economic development, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that extractive institutions, which do not enforce property rights effectively, can hinder long-run growth by failing to incentivize investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is thus critical for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show how credible commitment underpins growth through the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange, while Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the importance of institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political action.

The interaction between institutions and economic outcomes is further complicated by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations, suggesting that these institutions often served the interests of politically powerful groups rather than promoting broad-based economic development. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s observation that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for certain groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of such institutions over time can limit competition and innovation, thereby constraining long-run growth.

The historical evidence from European colonies provides a clear example. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were shaped by population density and prosperity. This finding is supported by Austin's analysis of colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous economic action in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). The empirical comparison between colonies with extractive institutions and those with more inclusive ones shows that the former experienced slower economic growth due to limited investment and innovation.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement lies in the measurement of long-run economic outcomes in relation to institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, experienced slower growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions. This is consistent with North's argument about the critical role of secure property rights and efficient enforcement in fostering economic development (North 1989, p.9).
