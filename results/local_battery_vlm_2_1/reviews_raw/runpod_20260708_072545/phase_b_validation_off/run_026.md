The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, as it addresses whether and how socio-political structures determine the trajectory of development over centuries. At stake are our understanding of prosperity differences between countries today and our ability to design policies that foster sustained economic advancement. A central disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms linking institutions to economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on growth, while another highlights the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions. Yet another perspective focuses on the historical contingencies that lead to the adoption of different institutional frameworks. These views collectively underscore the complexity of institution-driven growth and the need for a nuanced understanding of how political and social structures interact with economic development over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies resulted in diverse institutional setups, ranging from those that enforced rule of law to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. These institutions significantly influenced current income per capita levels, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize that institutional changes introduced during colonialism drove the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This perspective highlights how some institutions' extractive nature can stifle long-term growth as they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) posit that credible commitment underpins growth by fostering secure property rights and market development. This view aligns with North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast's focus on the political factors governing how rules are enforced and changed complements Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on the extractive nature of certain institutions. Together, these perspectives illustrate how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by shaping property rights and market exchanges.

Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, arguing instead for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution. This view is supported by Ogilvie's (2007, p.3) call to transform institutions from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in economic historians' toolkits. Both authors emphasize the need for a comprehensive approach that considers the complexities of historical and institutional contexts, rather than relying on simplified causal narratives.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) establish that the development of financial instruments and political institutions in 17th-century England was pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution fostered a robust capital market essential for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The development of efficient property rights and their enforcement was a response to specific historical pressures. North (1989, p.9) notes that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, laying the groundwork for future economic stability (North 1989, p.9). This political context highlights how institutional changes were driven by immediate needs but had long-term implications for economic growth. This qualification underscores the importance of contextual factors in understanding institutional impacts on economic development. The historical conditions under which institutions evolve are crucial for determining their effectiveness in fostering long-run economic growth, and these conditions can vary significantly across different societies.

Colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institution implemented was contingent on the mortality rates of Europeans and that extractive institutions were more likely to be set up when mortality was high, leading to lower income per capita today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persist through their design: they maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions are shaped by powerful groups who resist changes that would diminish their political power or fail to directly benefit them. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varies depending on historical and contextual factors such as the presence or absence of opportunities for industrialisation.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficient use of innovations, driving long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that modern technology necessitates significant adjustments in institutions and ideologies to facilitate its integration into society. This process is not uniform across regions or time periods; rather, it involves complex interactions between technological changes and the societal structures they encounter. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further highlights the role of the sovereign state in resolving conflicts that arise from economic growth, underscoring the importance of political stability and effective governance. This implies that the success of technological progress in driving economic growth is contingent on the ability of institutions to manage societal changes and maintain social cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These insights suggest that the interplay between technology and institutions is dynamic and multifaceted, with both factors playing critical roles in shaping long-run economic growth.

Slavery and inequality have had profound impacts on institutions, shaping societal outcomes in enduring ways (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). The institutional frameworks forged by historical inequalities can persistently affect economic development. This dynamic is evident in New World societies where initial conditions led to extreme wealth disparities. These differences in factor endowments, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, fostered societies with stark inequality from their inception (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These inequalities were reinforced through institutions that favored elites, limiting competition and perpetuating economic disparities over time. The resulting institutional structures often restricted opportunities for broader segments of the population, hindering long-term growth by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern is not confined to the New World; similar dynamics are evident in Africa, where the slave trades had far-reaching effects on economic development. The adverse impact of slavery on institutions extended beyond immediate disruptions, creating lasting legacies that continue to shape contemporary socioeconomic conditions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn's work underscores how these historical factors have left deep imprints on African societies, influencing their trajectories in ways that go beyond recent events or colonialism (Nunn 2008, p.19). The precise mechanisms through which these historical inequalities affect modern institutions remain a subject of ongoing research.

To understand these dynamics better, Broadberry and Gardner challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Broadberry and Gardner's analysis of colonial-era African economies reveals that many Anglophone countries had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding is significant because it contradicts earlier characterizations of Africa as a region of persistent poverty and low economic development, offering a more nuanced understanding of historical economic performance in Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg further support this perspective by showing that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The authors reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing empirical evidence that challenges the notion of enduring economic stagnation in Africa. Broadberry and Gardner extend this argument by linking these income levels to foreign trade and export markets, which were influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that while these findings suggest a more dynamic economic history for Africa, the long-term effects of specific historical or geographical conditions on African welfare development remain complex. Their work underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. This nuanced view of African economic history highlights the importance of considering multiple factors in assessing long-run economic growth, including the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

In response to these findings, Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that distributional conflicts are crucial in shaping core economic institutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Distributional conflicts can be understood as power struggles between different groups over resources, which often result in institutional arrangements that reflect these power dynamics. These conflicts create a complex interplay of incentives and constraints that influence how institutions evolve over time. This perspective offers a nuanced view of economic history by emphasizing the role of conflict and negotiation in shaping institutions. One mechanism through which distributional conflicts shape institutions is through the political process, where different groups lobby for policies that benefit them. For instance, colonial administrations might change their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores how institutions are continually shaped by ongoing power struggles. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions against overemphasizing efficiency as a driving force behind institutional changes. Instead, she argues that shared beliefs within a society can significantly influence the formation of institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced understanding of institutional evolution suggests that future research should consider both power dynamics and cultural factors when examining long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of secure property rights, as exemplified by the English financial revolution, is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights and credible commitment foster economic growth, as shown by North and Weingast. This underscores the importance of political factors in underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The dynamic nature of institutional change is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al., who show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates in colonies, leading to varying levels of economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that the transaction costs associated with property rights significantly impact economic growth. As economies grow and specialize, the volume of exchanges increases, making efficient enforcement of property rights even more critical (North 1989, p.6).

Instead, she argues that shared beliefs within a society can significantly influence the formation of institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by enforcing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. This is shown by North's discussion of the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). There remains disagreement over whether efficiency or shared beliefs drive institutional changes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency does (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from the varying approaches to commodity production and fiscal security in colonial administrations, as shown by Austin (2008, p.15). This empirical comparison highlights how institutional changes were influenced by both power dynamics and cultural factors.
