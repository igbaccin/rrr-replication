The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. Institutions are defined as the rules of the game in a society, which shape human interactions and influence economic outcomes. The stakes revolve around understanding whether institutional quality is the primary driver of economic development, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technology play more significant roles. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are endogenous to economic growth, meaning they evolve in response to economic conditions, or exogenous, implying that they independently determine economic trajectories. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they provide the framework for secure property rights and enforceable contracts, which are crucial for fostering investment and innovation (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Some scholars assert that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs may be more influential in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

One side of this debate posits that the quality of institutions is paramount. This perspective emphasizes that institutions which enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are essential for long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks, with some colonies establishing extractive states while others developed more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences have had lasting effects on current income per capita levels.

This focus on institutional quality is further supported by evidence from colonial histories. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonists established different types of institutions based on mortality rates and the ease of settlement, which in turn shaped the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This perspective emphasizes that institutions can independently drive economic outcomes as well as reflect them.

The debate extends to whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. Some scholars suggest that institutions evolve in response to economic needs and technological changes, implying an endogenous relationship. Others maintain that institutions are more stable and exert an independent influence on economic development. This perspective is supported by evidence from colonial histories, where institutional legacies have had enduring effects on economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these institutional effects highlights the exogenous nature of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs. This perspective indicates that institutional development is influenced by power dynamics and social norms in addition to purely economic factors.

The relationship between institutions and long-term economic growth is complex. While some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and enforceable contracts, others highlight the role of distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs in shaping institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive economic development. The persistence of these institutional effects highlights their enduring influence on long-term economic growth.

The development of financial institutions and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) posits that the creation of the Bank of England and other financial innovations led to a marked decline in transaction costs. This reduction was critical because it enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War drove the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, illustrating how political instability can catalyze institutional change (North 1989, p.9). The commitment of regimes to enforce these rules and the degree to which they are bound by them are critical factors in this process (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also suggest that while institutional changes were significant, they did not revolutionize outcomes independently. This qualification underscores that although institutions play a crucial role, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and economic performance must be considered in the broader context of economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show how European colonization strategies shaped institutions in colonies, ranging from settler societies with rule-of-law-enforcing institutions to extractive states designed for rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This variation in institutional design had profound economic implications. Colonies that were settled by Europeans and established strong institutions experienced higher levels of investment and economic growth. Colonies where Europeans implemented extractive institutions experienced significant economic underdevelopment. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies on economic development pathways, shaping contemporary economic outcomes in profound ways.

Broadberry and Gupta and van Zanden share that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9)(van Zanden 1999, p.1). This divergence was not primarily driven by institutional factors but rather by differences in productivity and development patterns. This suggests that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers experiencing a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers. Van Zanden's analysis complements this by showing that economic development in Europe did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living (van Zanden 1999, p.9). For instance, after 1740 England became the most expensive country for basic foodstuffs, indicating a rise in relative prices due to economic development. Broadberry and Gupta challenge the notion that high productivity in southern Indian agriculture during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was solely due to geographical factors or investment levels (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). They argue that the institutional changes in Europe led to increased agricultural labour productivity, suggesting that while productivity differences were significant, the role of institutions in shaping these differences remains complex.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa generated a pervasive culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The transatlantic slave trade's impact on African economic development is not limited to the immediate loss of human capital. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the legacy of the slave trade persists through its effect on social trust, which they find accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn's research complements this by highlighting that it was not just the loss of people but also the disruption of institutions and social networks that hindered Africa's ability to develop. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experience of slave trades, which involved large-scale violence and exploitation. Such a legacy has made it difficult for African societies to build strong institutions and foster cooperative behavior necessary for economic growth. The persistence of this mistrust is evident in contemporary Africa, where low levels of social trust continue to hinder development efforts (Nunn 2008, p.2).

The growth of useful knowledge is critical for sustained economic development, as Mokyr and Kuznets argue that technological advancements drive economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that the accumulation of knowledge fosters innovation, which in turn propels economic progress. The role of institutions becomes evident when considering the adjustments needed to efficiently use innovations: a society must adapt its institutional and ideological frameworks to accommodate technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These adaptations are crucial for resolving conflicts arising from technological advancements without weakening the political fabric of society. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for modern economic growth, as Kuznets notes that the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully managing these transitions (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr further elaborates on this by highlighting coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them. While technological progress can be relatively smooth, institutional adjustments are often more complex and less irreversible. This complexity underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth, as they must evolve to support new technologies and mitigate potential disruptions (Mokyr 2005, p.49).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) jointly find that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations do. This explanation is about both economic factors and how inequality shaped institutions in New World societies. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can be understood through their impact on competition and inequality: elites in societies with greater inequality used government policies to restrict competition, thereby maintaining their advantages. For instance, European colonizers established institutions that reinforced existing inequalities, which had lasting effects on the development paths of these societies. This is seen in the persistence of inequality and the evolution of institutions that favored elite interests over broader economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design underscores how institutions can both facilitate and hinder long-run economic growth depending on the underlying power dynamics.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing and enforcing property rights, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9). For instance, the creation of the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated market exchanges, directly contributing to economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The evolution of institutions that secure property rights is a key factor in fostering long-term capital markets and overall economic development. This underscores how institutional frameworks can drive economic progress by ensuring the security of investments and promoting efficient market transactions.

The impact of institutions on economic growth extends beyond securing property rights to include the broader political and social environment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The shared beliefs and cultural values within a society shape its institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. For example, European beliefs about human intervention and values such as diligence and thrift led to the development of institutions that supported capitalistic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions facilitated economic exchanges as well as encouraged investment and innovation, thereby driving long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is complex and can sometimes hinder progress. Institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups may not promote long-run growth; instead, they can perpetuate extractive practices that benefit a few at the expense of broader economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This dynamic is evident in colonial settings where European colonizers established institutions that reinforced existing inequalities, leading to persistent economic disparities. The persistence of these inequalities highlights how institutional design can both facilitate and impede long-run economic growth depending on the underlying power dynamics.

The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by distributional conflicts or by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth, highlighting the role of credible commitment underpinning growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is seen in the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England, which reduced transaction costs and facilitated market exchanges. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The historical evidence from the English financial revolution shows that institutional changes were driven by fiscal crises and the need to secure property rights, settling this disagreement (North 1989, p.9).
