The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations and regions over centuries. At stake is whether institutional frameworks can be seen as a primary determinant of economic outcomes, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more decisive roles. A live disagreement exists between those who emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth and those who argue for alternative explanations, such as the role of cultural beliefs or geographic conditions. One side posits that robust institutions facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges, thereby fostering long-term economic development (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The other side contends that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs are more critical in shaping core economic institutions, which in turn influence growth trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The primacy of institutions is evident in the argument that secure property rights and efficient market exchanges are crucial for long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. highlight how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with settler colonies establishing rule-of-law institutions that encouraged investment, while extractive states in other regions focused on rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast underscore the significance of political institutions that enforce property rights and provide a stable framework for economic activity. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that economic rules are enforced and can be relied upon by market participants (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated through the lens of colonial experiences, where differing institutional setups had lasting impacts on economic development. Austin challenges the notion that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. He argues that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this argument by highlighting that institutions are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which often leads to extractive institutions that hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific mechanism through which these institutions shape economic outcomes is the enforcement of property rights and the reduction of transaction costs. This institutional framework fosters an environment conducive to investment and economic development. The implication is that the type of institutions introduced during colonialism can have lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks.

Their work aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis, which highlights how institutional frameworks can significantly influence long-term economic trajectories by shaping property rights and political structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework allowed for a stable environment conducive to investment, economic development, and market growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) expand on this by exploring how different colonial environments led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved within these societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implications are profound: early institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic outcomes, as they influence the distribution of wealth and political power. This interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes underscores the complex relationship between historical contexts and long-term growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions evolve. The legacy of colonial rule in Africa further illustrates this point: Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British colonial policies were more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers, suggesting that the type of institutional framework imposed by colonial rulers could have long-lasting effects on economic development (Frankema 2012, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this argument with their findings on how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today. This mistrust has a profound impact on trust within societies, which is crucial for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Distributional conflicts, therefore, have played a significant role in shaping institutions that either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) contend that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, which in turn requires institutional adjustments. This perspective underscores that technological advancements are pivotal for sustained economic development. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is evident in how societies adapt to new innovations. Kuznets elaborates on this dynamic, noting that modern technology demands significant changes in societal structures and ideologies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This adjustment process is crucial because it enables the efficient use of innovations, thereby fostering long-term economic growth. Mokyr further emphasizes that without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvements may be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The implication here is that institutions play a supportive role in harnessing the benefits of technological progress, ensuring that economic gains are not eroded by inefficiencies or rent-seeking behaviors. However, Kuznets highlights that these adjustments must occur without excessive costs to the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6), suggesting that the institutional environment must be stable and adaptable to accommodate technological changes.

Broadberry and Gardner assert that Africa's economic performance has been misunderstood, often being underrepresented in comparative historical work on growth and development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta similarly show that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). These patterns of economic performance were tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The role of the service sector in early modern development also played a crucial part. Broadberry and Gupta suggest that institutional change is pivotal for investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), suggesting that institutions can drive economic growth through enhanced productivity. While this mechanism underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, it also implies that economic development is contingent on the distribution of gains across different segments of society. This highlights the complex interplay between institutional factors and economic outcomes, where the benefits of growth are not always evenly spread.

Van Zanden and Pamuk agree that between 1500 and 1800, economic development in Europe was marked by shifts in regional dynamics rather than uniform growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). The wage data and urbanization rates studied by Van Zanden illustrate the geographical patterns of silver and grain wages across European cities and regions. This period saw a shift in economic focus from southern Europe to countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England after 1600 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). In this context, Pamuk highlights that institutional flexibility played a critical role in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. The unusual degree of institutional flexibility in late medieval Holland allowed new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies, while regions with stronger existing monopolies, such as Tuscany, struggled to adapt (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This suggests that the ability of institutions to adapt to changing economic conditions is a key factor in driving long-run economic growth. Pamuk qualifies this by noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes which led to significant wage gaps between northwestern and southern Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This underscores the importance of considering both institutional flexibility and exogenous shocks when examining the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), challenge the notion that African poverty is primarily due to structural impediments. They argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack illustrate this through the example of women in Ghana's cocoa industry, where they were relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule through customary law, which codified the subordinate status of women. The implications are significant: the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast a leading cocoa producer in 1911 would not have been possible without the contributions of these marginalized women. Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). They reconstruct real wage levels to understand the long-term picture of economic development in colonial Africa, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these historical dynamics is necessary to assess future growth possibilities. This underscores the importance of considering both institutional and gendered factors when examining African economic history.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and its implications for African development are explored through historical and institutional lenses (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. This critique is particularly relevant because it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution in Africa. Austin notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that institutional changes in colonial Africa were responsive to local economic and political actions, rather than being solely imposed from the outside.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this perspective by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He emphasizes that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight underscores the complexity of African economic development, where institutional quality is not merely a function of ethnic fragmentation but also of broader socio-political dynamics. Hopkins further notes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

These observations collectively suggest that while colonial legacies and institutional frameworks play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, the specific mechanisms through which these factors operate are multifaceted and context-dependent. This complexity means that policy interventions aimed at fostering economic growth must be tailored to the unique historical and institutional contexts of different African countries.

The precise nature of this influence remains a subject of debate, with some scholars emphasizing the persistence of colonial legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) and others highlighting the adaptability of institutions over time. The historical evidence from Africa shows that while institutional frameworks are shaped by colonial histories, they can also evolve in response to local economic actions and incentives. The empirical comparison between former colonies with different institutional frameworks demonstrates how these differences have led to varied economic outcomes, with some colonies experiencing significant growth while others remain stagnant (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This evidence settles the debate by showing that the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by their adaptability and responsiveness to local conditions.
