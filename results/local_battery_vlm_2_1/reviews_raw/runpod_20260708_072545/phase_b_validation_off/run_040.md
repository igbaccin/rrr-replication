The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science that has sparked intense debate among scholars. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. The central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate through other mechanisms such as geographic factors, cultural beliefs, or fiscal policies. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on economic outcomes, arguing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering investment and development. Some scholars posit that institutions are shaped by wider historical contexts and distributional conflicts, indicating that institutional changes frequently stem from political and social dynamics alongside economic considerations. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the complexity of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic progress depending on their design and enforcement mechanisms.

A key stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of institutions on economic growth, highlighting how specific institutional frameworks can either promote or impede development. Acemoglu et al argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, with some promoting secure property rights and investment while others focused on resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that institutions matter significantly in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9). This perspective emphasizes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This stream underscores that effective institutional frameworks are essential for fostering economic growth by providing the necessary incentives for investment and development.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The English financial revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and promote long-term capital markets. This development is crucial because it illustrates how political institutions can create an environment conducive to economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights effectively. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) advise that these efficient institutions do not always focus on long-term growth; they can be structured to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the importance of considering the political dynamics and power structures within a society when analyzing the impact of institutions on economic growth. As such, while institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes, their specific effects depend heavily on who controls them.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth include not just the rules governing economic exchange but also the institutions enforcing these rules. This perspective emphasizes that credible commitments from regimes are essential for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights the importance of shared beliefs and cultural values in shaping economic institutions. She argues that these institutional structures can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth, depending on how they align with societal norms and values. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the transition from serfdom to free labor, for example, was driven by institutional changes that reflected evolving beliefs about property rights and individual freedoms. North and Weingast note that institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This qualification suggests that while institutions are pivotal in shaping economic outcomes, their impact depends on the political dynamics and power structures within a society.

Broadberry and Gupta note that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector during the early modern period was driven by merchant distribution and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.9). They point out that this service sector played a crucial role in economic growth. This mechanism of high productivity in the traded goods sector was reinforced by the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warm climates (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), which affected the relative prices of manufactured goods versus basic foodstuffs. Van Zanden also observes that shifts in economic development occurred during this period, with a focus on countries bordering the North Sea after 1700 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This shift confirms the view that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, reinforcing the idea of structural changes driven by external factors. Pamuk's analysis shows that institutional flexibility significantly contributed to regions like Holland quickly adapting to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This highlights the importance of institutional arrangements in facilitating economic growth and structural change, even if these institutions are not explicitly linked as fundamental causes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that the colonial impact on African economic development is complex. The legacy of colonial rule in Africa is often viewed through the lens of institutional decay, but this perspective overlooks the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This shift reinforced patriarchal alliances and marginalized women's access to land, particularly for cash crops like cocoa in the Gold Coast. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that the demand for missionary education under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education at both macro and micro levels. This institutional change had profound implications for economic development. For instance, the codification of customary law under colonial rule influenced land access and broader economic activities. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This relationship is intricate. Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly impact the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This suggests that effective governance can emerge despite ethnic heterogeneity and dysfunctional societies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18) is that institutional arrangements, though often viewed as detrimental, can also facilitate economic growth by providing the necessary incentives for investment. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted role of institutions in African economic development during colonial periods. The complex interplay between institutional changes and economic outcomes highlights the need to consider how these factors shape long-run growth trajectories in Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African economic history is rich with evidence of higher per capita incomes and real wages during the colonial period than previously acknowledged (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This view aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's work on British Africa, which similarly challenges persistent poverty narratives by examining long-term income trends (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These studies collectively reveal that African economies were more robust than often portrayed. The economic growth in many Anglophone African countries was tied to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This dynamic suggests a pattern of economic development driven by external demand, which can offer insights into the role of institutions in facilitating or hindering such growth. This availability of data underscores the potential for deeper institutional analysis.

The literature on East Africa's post-independence expenditure trends and land conflicts highlights the complexities of economic development in the region, focusing on factors such as industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, debt service payments, and land conflicts, which are all influenced by institutional frameworks. Simson  (2019, p.2) argues that government resources were often treated as private property by political patrons, who used them to buy loyalty rather than deliver broad-based public goods, while Peters (2004, p.19) shows that post-independence competition over land deepened social divisions and class formation in sub-Saharan Africa. A key mechanism driving these trends was the shift from colonial subsidies to domestic military spending as countries gained independence. This shift was marked by regional instability and growing domestic dissent  (Simson 2019, p.11). The increased focus on military expenditure, coupled with high debt burdens, often displaced capital investments and recurrent spending, which could have fostered long-term economic growth. The implications of these trends are significant: the diversion of resources towards military and debt service payments, at the expense of productive investments, likely hindered economic growth by limiting the development of infrastructure and human capital. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as the effective management of public resources is crucial for sustainable development. The role of institutions in directing expenditure patterns towards productive investments rather than consumption or military spending is critical for fostering economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets share that technological progress is crucial for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This process involves the accumulation of useful knowledge. The pace at which technology advances affects the rate of economic growth; likewise, the institutional adjustments needed to efficiently use innovations vary with time and place. For example, Kuznets notes that the introduction of modern technology necessitates significant ideological shifts, such as moving away from rural lifestyles and large family structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr elaborates on this by identifying coordination failures in technological progress; these failures can only be rectified through institutional adjustments that facilitate economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutional changes are essential for the sustainability of economic improvement, as they help mitigate rent-seeking behaviors that could otherwise erode gains from technological advancements. Kuznets emphasizes that without resolving conflicts between traditional societal structures and modern technology, long-term economic growth is impeded by the weakening of political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This highlights how institutions, even if not the primary driver, are integral to harnessing the benefits of technological progress for sustained economic development. The interplay between technological advancement and institutional evolution reveals a complex dynamic where both factors reinforce each other. Economic growth driven by technology requires institutional support to manage conflicts arising from societal adjustments; this ensures that technological progress is translated into sustainable economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) assert that the slave trade in Africa has had enduring effects on social trust, which have impeded economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust, engendered by centuries of exploitation, manifests as a pervasive cultural attitude that extends beyond immediate interpersonal interactions. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates is multifaceted: it affects both individuals' internal norms and their external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn supports this view by highlighting that the slave trades in Africa spanned nearly 500 years, a duration far exceeding the period of colonial rule, thereby embedding mistrust deeply within societal structures (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The long-term effects of these historical events are evident in contemporary economic outcomes, where trust remains a critical factor for development. This perspective is corroborated by Nunn and Wantchekon, who note that the internal channel of mistrust, shaped by individuals' norms, is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This underscores how deeply ingrained cultural attitudes can hinder economic progress.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that colonial institutions in the New World were shaped by inequality, which advantaged elites through various government policies. They posit that extreme initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power led to societies with institutions favouring elite interests, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to have advantaged elites and restricted competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective is supported by Austin's observation that colonial governments often tailored their policies in response to local economic conditions and political actions, rather than adhering strictly to a preconceived institutional framework. While colonial institutions frequently favoured elite interests, they were not static; instead, they evolved in response to indigenous economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's analysis highlights that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on the incentives created by local actions, challenging the notion that these institutions were uniformly detrimental to growth. This adaptive nature of colonial institutions suggests that while they often perpetuated inequality, there was potential for institutional evolution in response to local pressures.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic actors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions which enforce the rule of law and encourage investment foster economic development. North (1989, p.9) supports this view, highlighting that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. These perspectives underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in creating an environment conducive to sustained economic progress.

The adaptive nature of institutions further highlights their role in economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) shows how colonial administrations evolved their policies in response to local economic and political dynamics, demonstrating that even extractive institutions can adapt over time. This adaptation reflects the continuous interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, where changes in one can influence the other.

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document how institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to this reversal, emphasizing that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the enduring effects of institutional legacies on economic development trajectories.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another key aspect. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for institutions such as serfdom and guilds than efficiency considerations alone. Hopkins (2009, p.8) extends this by highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

Shared beliefs also play a significant role in shaping institutional structures and economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This highlights how cultural factors intertwine with institutional frameworks to influence long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the role of secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement is over whether extractive institutions can adapt and potentially foster growth. Austin (2008, p.15) shows how colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local pressures, challenging the notion that these institutions were uniformly detrimental to growth. This adaptation suggests potential for institutional evolution in response to local dynamics. Historical evidence from colonial administration policy changes settles this disagreement by showing that even extractive institutions can evolve and influence economic outcomes over time.
