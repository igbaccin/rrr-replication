The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions encompass the rules and norms that structure interactions among individuals, groups, and governments. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions primarily influence growth through their impact on property rights and market efficiency or if they operate more broadly by shaping political stability and societal beliefs.

One position argues that institutions matter because they define the rules governing property rights, contract enforcement, and market exchanges. This perspective highlights how secure property rights and efficient markets are necessary for economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Another view posits that institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing political stability and societal beliefs about legitimacy and individual rights. The debate hinges on whether the primary mechanism through which institutions operate is efficiency or distributional conflict.

The question of how extractive versus inclusive institutions affect economic outcomes illustrates this disagreement. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder long-run growth by failing to encourage investment and innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Inclusive institutions that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights foster an environment conducive to economic development. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). This highlights how institutions that ensure secure property rights can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment.

The decline in transaction costs facilitated more efficient market exchanges. As economies grow, the size of the transactions sector increases relative to GDP, reflecting greater specialization and division of labor within the economy (North 1989, p.6). This pattern suggests that institutions governing financial markets are essential for economic development by reducing barriers to trade and investment.

The effectiveness of these institutions depends on their ability to enforce property rights and manage public choices in a way that maintains long-term credibility. This commitment is essential because it ensures that institutions remain stable over time, providing a reliable framework for economic activity. As such, the creation of financial institutions alone is not sufficient; they must be backed by political structures that enforce property rights and maintain credible commitments to these rules. Without this backing, even well-designed financial institutions may fail to support long-term growth. The institutionalization of secure property rights and predictable public policy can thus drive economic development through reducing transaction costs and enhancing investment certainty.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. These scholars demonstrate how European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates in their colonies. In regions with high mortality, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole; this pattern persisted and shaped long-term economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further show that these institutional legacies are robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had profound effects on economic development in former colonies, highlighting how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring impacts on a region's economic path. As such, the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes by shaping the quality of governance and investment environments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective highlights how the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels innovation, which in turn propels long-term economic development. Technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to integrate these innovations efficiently into society. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress often clashes with traditional social structures, such as rural lifestyles and extended family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This tension necessitates significant institutional changes to accommodate new technologies without disrupting societal cohesion. Mokyr further emphasizes the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress, suggesting that coordination failures can hinder economic improvement if not addressed through institutional reforms (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by pointing out that these adjustments must be managed carefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. This underscores the delicate balance required between technological innovation and institutional adaptation for sustained economic growth. The success of modern economic growth hinges on resolving these conflicts effectively through robust institutional frameworks, ensuring that technological progress translates into meaningful economic development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate how the enduring legacy of the slave trade has fostered internal norms of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust operates through both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the perceived trustworthiness of others, and internal individual norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal channel, individuals' inherent mistrust, is particularly significant, being at least twice as large as the external channel. Nunn elaborates on this by highlighting how Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a duration much longer than the period of colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This prolonged exposure to the slave trade likely deepened the cultural roots of mistrust, creating a persistent barrier to economic development. Nunn also notes that the impact of the slave trade on economic development is not uniform across Africa; some regions show resilience due to pre-existing centralized governments and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This variability suggests that while the slave trade had a profound effect on trust levels, local institutional factors could mitigate its long-term economic consequences.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific changes aimed at improving governance and economic conditions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The new focus on 'governance' allowed institutions to come to the fore, making it possible for the new economic history of Africa to capture the attention of development specialists. "Good" institutions are crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that a better grasp of the long-term picture of economic development will help assess future growth possibilities. This includes understanding how specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. A key mechanism through which institutional reforms can impact economic growth is by enhancing the provision of public goods and services, thereby fostering an environment conducive to investment and economic activity. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also point out that poverty in Africa has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while institutional reforms are important, their impact on long-run economic growth may be contingent upon addressing these underlying structural issues.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts among elites and non-elites shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional efficiency-based explanations for institutional arrangements. Ogilvie demonstrates how institutions like serfdom and guilds were often designed to maintain elite power rather than to maximize economic efficiency. For instance, serfdom allowed landlords to extract labor from peasants while restricting their mobility, thus securing a stable workforce and maintaining social hierarchy. Craft and merchant guilds similarly served to limit competition by controlling entry into trades and regulating prices, thereby protecting the interests of established elites at the expense of broader economic efficiency. These institutional frameworks were actively shaped by power dynamics between different social groups; they were not merely the result of shared beliefs or cultural values. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this analysis to New World societies, showing how initial inequality in factor endowments led to persistent differences in institutions that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped institutional development. For example, policies restricting land ownership and labor mobility were designed to maintain elite control over resources and prevent social mobility among lower classes. This institutional framework perpetuated inequality by limiting access to economic opportunities for the majority of the population. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress can sometimes mitigate these institutional barriers to growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either facilitate or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) agree on the importance of institutions in fostering growth, but they focus on different aspects. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established distinct institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. North (1989, p.9), similarly, highlights the significance of financial innovations and secure property rights in driving economic growth. These shared findings (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(North 1989, p.9) emphasise that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, directly impacts long-term economic outcomes.

The evolution of institutions is closely tied to political dynamics and power structures. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping long-run development trajectories. The evidence from capital markets, as North and Weingast highlight (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), shows the profound impact of institutions on economic performance. This shared emphasis on political factors underpinning growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29)(Ogilvie 2007, p.12) underscores that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritise their own interests over long-run economic development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is another critical aspect. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective complements Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) view that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximise rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth. The shared finding is that institutions are not always efficient but often reflect the interests of those in power (Ogilvie 2007, p.3)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This highlights a key point of agreement: institutions can perpetuate inequality by limiting economic opportunities for the majority.

The remaining disagreement concerns how to understand the persistence of extractive institutions. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions can be extractive if they maximise rents for colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities. This empirical comparison underscores that the nature of institutions introduced during colonialism had lasting effects on long-run development trajectories.
