The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually are the reasons why some countries thrive economically while others stagnate, and how historical institutional frameworks have influenced contemporary outcomes. The live disagreement hinges on whether institutions themselves are the primary drivers of growth or if they operate through other factors such as geography or culture. One position argues that institutions directly influence economic performance by shaping incentives for investment and innovation. This view emphasizes the importance of secure property rights, effective governance, and the rule of law in fostering long-term economic development. One view holds that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and social factors such as population density or geographic conditions (Author Year p.N). The debate is further complicated by rival explanations that focus on cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts as key determinants of institutional structures.

The question persists because institutions, as defined by North (1989, p.6) as the rules of the game in a society, are not static; they evolve over time and are shaped by political and economic forces. These evolving institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on whether they provide secure property rights and effective governance. The historical evidence shows that institutions matter significantly in long-run economic development.

The debate over the role of institutions versus other factors such as geography or culture is central to understanding why some countries have achieved sustained economic growth while others have not. The legacy of these institutions persists, affecting current income levels and economic performance. This perspective highlights that institutional frameworks can be designed to maximize rents for specific groups, promoting long-run growth.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted. North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term economic growth. This financial revolution reflects increased security of property rights, which are critical for both capital markets and overall economic development. The interaction between political and economic institutions is crucial; political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can lead to falling transaction costs per exchange. As the size of the transactions sector grows due to increasing specialization and division of labor, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically declined transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was a response to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated trade-offs between certain rights and revenues (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.9) elaborates that as political institutions specified "efficient" property rights and provided effective enforcement, transaction costs per exchange fell while the aggregate volume of exchanges grew. This dynamic facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth by supporting a long-term capital market. The institutional framework in England involved rules and how these rules were enforced and changed (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) demonstrates that the size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange, thereby fostering a stable environment conducive to long-term economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping their institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.  demonstrate that European colonists established extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates, primarily to exploit resources rapidly and transfer them to the metropole. For example, the establishment of extractive states in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast differs from the more inclusive institutions set up in settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions continues to affect current income per capita in former colonies. In addition to mortality rates, the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity. Acemoglu et al. caution that while institutions are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth. The interaction between these institutions and other factors such as industrialization opportunities played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and guilds. These institutions were influenced by power dynamics between different social groups. Ogilvie highlights how shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights often underpinned these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where initial factor endowments and inequality led to the formation of institutions that advantaged elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). For instance, in Latin America, these institutional frameworks restricted competition and reinforced existing power structures. The persistence of distributional conflicts means that these institutions evolved over time in response to changing social dynamics. Ogilvie's emphasis on transforming institutions into indispensable instruments for economic historians underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how these institutions functioned within broader social contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This approach challenges conventional views by showing that efficiency was not the sole determinant of institutional design; rather, distributional conflicts played a critical role.

Nunn argues that Africa's underdevelopment is primarily attributable to its slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2), with Frankema and van Waijenburg noting that 'it is important to take into account the long-term processes of economic development that are at work' (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn posits that the extensive slave trade, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, played a pivotal role in shaping Africa's subsequent economic trajectory. This occurred through the disruption of social and political structures. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further emphasize that understanding these historical processes is crucial for assessing Africa's current economic conditions and future prospects (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The implication of this perspective is that the legacy of slave trades still impacts contemporary African economies, as it stunted the development of robust institutions capable of supporting sustained growth. Austin suggests that focusing solely on historical factors like slave trades may oversimplify Africa's economic trajectory; there are multiple paths and periods to consider when examining long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity means that while the legacy of slave trades is significant, it does not entirely explain Africa’s current economic conditions.

Frankema and other scholars (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.15) argue that British colonial rule had a positive impact on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This influence was notably different from the effects of other colonial powers, which often hindered educational progress. These changes were driven by cost-benefit calculations made at both macro and micro levels; African political leaders and families saw value in education as a means to navigate new institutional landscapes. In this context, British colonial rule fostered an environment where demand for missionary education was higher than under other colonial regimes. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who demonstrate that the economic growth patterns in many Anglophone African economies were tied closely to foreign trade and export markets, both of which required educated labor forces, further solidifying the British influence on educational development. This positive impact on formal education does not imply uniform economic success; Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) note that the legacy of colonial rule also includes a pervasive culture of mistrust within Africa, which still affects contemporary economic interactions and development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) share a view that institutional reforms are pivotal for economic development in Africa, particularly in alleviating poverty among the continent's most vulnerable populations. These scholars assert that 'good' institutions provide crucial incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins highlights that ethnically homogeneous societies can still suffer from poor governance and underdeveloped institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18), while ethnically diverse societies can develop progressive ones. This insight underscores the importance of institutional quality over ethnic composition in driving economic growth. Simson qualifies this perspective by noting that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were heavily influenced by factors such as industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments (Simson 2019, p.2). These expenditures often displaced both capital and recurrent spending, which could have otherwise fostered economic growth. The shift towards domestic military spending, driven by regional instability and growing domestic dissent, further illustrates the complex interplay between institutional reforms and economic trends in Africa (Simson 2019, p.11). This dynamic suggests that while institutional reforms are necessary for economic development, they must be complemented by prudent expenditure management to ensure sustained growth.

The divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the high productivity in the traded goods sector of northwest Europe can be attributed to efficient merchant distribution and financial systems. The service sector played a crucial role in this growth, facilitating the development of trade networks and financial systems that supported broader economic expansion (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden (1999, p.1) shows that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period. This suggests that while structural changes were occurring, they did not uniformly translate into improved living standards for all segments of society (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.16). The divergence in economic development across regions within Europe highlights the complex interplay between institutional reforms and broader economic trends.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by noting that technological changes require specific institutional adjustments for efficient use and diffusion. He emphasizes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns, necessitating significant societal shifts to accommodate economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These shifts involve resolving conflicts that arise from these changes. He describes a database of scholarly societies founded in Europe over two centuries, illustrating how these institutions facilitated the dissemination and application of new ideas (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This institutional support for technological progress ensures that economic growth is not limited by rent-seeking behaviors or coordination failures. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges that while technological progress is more irreversible than institutional changes, it still requires sustained efforts to maintain its benefits (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and sustainability of resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2002, p.33) show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-term growth, as these extractive institutions do not encourage development. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9), who argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies how new financial instruments and institutions reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and long-term capital markets.

Institutional frameworks facilitate technological progress by lowering access costs to knowledge. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that secure property rights are essential for economic growth, while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that institutions enforcing the rule of law encourage investment and development. This institutional support ensures that economic growth is not impeded by rent-seeking behaviors or coordination failures, as technological progress requires sustained efforts to maintain its benefits.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further illustrated through historical comparisons. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies and show that this reversal was related to industrialization during the nineteenth century. This interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, highlighting how institutional changes can lead to significant economic shifts.

The significance of institutions in shaping economic growth is also evident in the context of Africa's development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective aligns with Austin (2008, p.15), who notes that colonial administrations encouraged indigenous commodity production when it aligned with their fiscal interests, illustrating how institutional prescriptions can be responsive to economic and political action.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate among scholars. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium in response to indigenous economic action (Austin 2008, p.15). This example demonstrates how institutions can adapt to economic incentives, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.
