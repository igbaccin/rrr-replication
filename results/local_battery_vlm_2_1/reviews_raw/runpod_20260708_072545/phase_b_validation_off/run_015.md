The question of what role institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent paths of development observed across countries. The debate hinges on whether institutions are primarily the result of geographic and cultural factors, or if they are endogenous to political processes and power dynamics. One position argues that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and culture, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes. One perspective maintains that institutions evolve endogenously through political processes, where powerful groups shape them to maximize their own interests and those of broader society (Author Year p.N). This debate is crucial because it determines whether policy interventions can effectively alter institutional frameworks to promote growth or if such efforts are futile against the tide of exogenous forces.

A key point of contention involves the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) both address this issue, but they offer different perspectives on how these institutions were established and their long-term impacts. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted distinct colonization strategies depending on mortality rates in colonies; those with high mortality rates saw the establishment of extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin, however, contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, which influenced institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates a more nuanced view where local dynamics played a significant role in shaping institutions, and European powers exerted exogenous determination.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further explored through the lens of property rights and transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) posits that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic development. He argues that institutions like the Bank of England, which reduced transaction costs, were pivotal in fostering growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie (2007, p.12), on the other hand, challenges the efficiency-driven view of institutional development and suggests that distributional conflicts often play a more significant role. She argues that institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by power struggles and purely economic considerations.

The enduring impact of colonial institutions is further highlighted through the lens of long-term development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had lasting negative effects on economic growth in former colonies. They emphasize that these institutions did not foster long-run development, as they were tailored to serve the interests of colonizers and economic prosperity in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) offer a complementary perspective by focusing on how credible commitments underpin growth. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for securing property rights and fostering economic growth. Credible commitment underpins long-term economic development, as it provides a stable framework for investment and market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This pattern underscores how institutional changes can foster economic growth by creating a more efficient market environment. The success of these institutions in England highlights the broader implications for economic development, suggesting that similar institutional frameworks could yield comparable results in other contexts.

This approach was driven by European settlers' mortality rates, which determined whether they established settler colonies or extractive states. These institutions persisted over time and had lasting effects on economic development by discouraging investment and stifling growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutional differences led to varied outcomes in income per capita, highlighting how colonial policies created divergent economic trajectories. The persistence of these extractive institutions underscores the enduring impact of colonial legacies on contemporary economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the notion that African poverty has been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating periods of growth despite colonial rule. This finding is supported by recent work in African economic history which demonstrates the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries due to a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This insight is crucial as it helps assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has left many questions unanswered, but reconstructing and comparing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule can provide valuable insights into economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This historical perspective is essential for understanding the complex interplay between institutions, economic growth, and colonial legacies in shaping contemporary African economies.

The transatlantic slave trade, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) assert, had profound and lasting effects on Africa's economic development. This effect is an outcome of the slave trades themselves and a legacy of colonialism. Nunn emphasizes the long duration of these slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. This period is substantially longer than the approximately 75-year span of official colonial rule, indicating that the adverse effects on economic development are deeply rooted in this historical context (Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade's impact on trust is both external and internal, affecting individuals' norms and perceptions of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalization of mistrust creates a self-reinforcing cycle that hinders economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, supported trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that despite the overall adverse effects, specific institutional structures could mitigate some of the negative impacts of the slave trade on economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) agree that technological advancements are the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective challenges institutional theories by proposing that the expansion of useful knowledge propels long-term economic development, in addition to institutions alone. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that scientific and technical progress generates new ideas and innovations, which in turn stimulate economic activity. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this mechanism through epochal innovations: those technological breakthroughs that dominate as sources of sustained growth over extended periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These innovations necessitate institutional adjustments to integrate them efficiently into the economy. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further notes that these technological changes can induce social and political conflicts, which must be resolved to sustain long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that, although technology is the mainspring of growth, institutions play a crucial role in sustaining and amplifying its benefits (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

They challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting how historical interpretations often compress different periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This simplification overlooks key factors such as indigenous economic actions, which can influence colonial institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations altered their policies in response to local incentives, suggesting that institutions are shaped by the dynamics of local power and interests as well as imposed. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that economic historians should adopt a more nuanced approach to institutions, treating them as indispensable tools and mere fashion accessories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the need for a deeper understanding of how institutions evolve in response to distributional conflicts and local incentives.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent paths of development among nations. Institutions determine the framework within which economic activities occur, influencing everything from property rights to market regulations. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both underscore this point, with North emphasizing that institutions matter in economic growth because they define and enforce property rights (North 1989, p.6), while Acemoglu et al. show that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Institutions are not static; they evolve in response to political and economic pressures. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates this by showing how colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local incentives, indicating that institutions can be shaped by the dynamics of power and interests within a society (Austin 2008, p.15).

The evolution of institutions is deeply intertwined with distributional conflicts and local incentives. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, and these rights are underpinned by credible commitment from the regime (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups may not encourage long-run economic development. They show that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, shaped by the interests of those in power and efficiency considerations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is shared by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The specific historical evidence from colonial administrations adapting policies shows the importance of local incentives in shaping institutions. For instance, Austin's work on Governor-General Clifford opting for higher production equilibrium challenges hypotheses that assume uniform policy responses across different contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). This historical example underscores the need to consider the dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional design. Similarly, North's discussion of the English financial revolution illustrates how institutions can lower transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by defining property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which local incentives versus broader political and economic pressures shape institutional evolution. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates how colonial administrations adapted policies based on local conditions, showing that institutions are shaped by local power dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2). This historical evidence underscores the importance of considering local contexts in understanding institutional change.
