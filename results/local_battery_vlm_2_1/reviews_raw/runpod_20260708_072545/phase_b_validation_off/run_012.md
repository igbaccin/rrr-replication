The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the efficacy of contemporary policy interventions. Institutions are understood as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, and they influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, facilitating transactions, and allocating resources. The live disagreement revolves around two main positions: one argues that institutions matter fundamentally because they determine the quality of property rights and governance; another contends that institutions are merely one factor among many, such as geography or culture, in determining long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional outcomes, with extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies and more inclusive ones elsewhere. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors like distributional conflicts and shared beliefs. Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The interplay between these positions highlights the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, setting the stage for a deeper exploration into how institutions operate as causal mechanisms in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) focus on specific institutional developments and their impact on transaction costs, rather than directly addressing how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. They examine the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This financial revolution was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth by reducing the cost of transacting, thereby facilitating market exchanges and increasing specialization. This perspective is supported by recent research that has upgraded France's pre-revolutionary economic performance, suggesting that the institutional changes in England might not be the sole determinant of its economic growth. This focus on specific mechanisms like transaction costs and credible commitment within institutions provides a nuanced understanding of how institutional changes can influence long-run economic outcomes by facilitating market exchanges and ensuring secure property rights. The role of political factors in shaping these institutions is crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth, as it underscores the importance of both the rules governing economic exchange and the mechanisms that enforce them.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The differential mortality rates of European colonizers led to varied colonization strategies and institutions. In colonies with high mortality, Europeans established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Lower mortality rates in other regions facilitated the development of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence shaped long-term economic trajectories, as extractive institutions inhibited growth while inclusive ones fostered it.

The persistence of these colonial-era institutions is crucial for understanding current economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is tied to institutional changes rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between these institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development outcomes.

These findings align with the broader understanding of how political factors shape economic growth. This political dimension underscores the importance of both the rules governing economic exchange and the mechanisms that enforce them, as seen in the role of credible commitment in institutional development.

The implications of these findings extend beyond colonial contexts. The persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-run economic growth by limiting investment and innovation. In regions where colonial legacies have left deep institutional scars, addressing these structural issues is essential for fostering sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) (Austin 2008, p.2) re-evaluate the historical impact of colonial policies on African economic development, challenging conventional views that emphasize structural impediments as the root cause of persistent poverty. The key mechanism here involves the interaction between colonial administrations and indigenous economic actors: where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged commodity production to align with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutional prescriptions could evolve in response to local incentives, rather than being uniformly extractive as some theories suggest. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) point out that the lack of pre-1950 GDP estimates for Africa leaves many questions unanswered about the long-term effects of these policies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Thus, while colonial institutions did shape economic outcomes, their impact varied significantly depending on local conditions and the specific incentives at play.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) posits that the progress in technology necessitates institutional adjustments to harness innovations efficiently; institutions must facilitate the diffusion of new ideas. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this mechanism, observing that without resolving conflicts arising from technological changes through institutional means, growth may be impeded or even reversed. Such institutional adaptations are essential for integrating modern technologies into existing economic systems and societies. The sovereign state emerges as a pivotal entity in managing the societal tensions that can arise during these transitions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) also recognizes that this process is not linear; technological advancements do not guarantee sustained growth without complementary institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective underscores that while technology and knowledge are fundamental drivers of economic growth, their impact is contingent on the adaptive capacity of institutions.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate how the legacy of the slave trade has fostered an enduring atmosphere of distrust within African societies. This mistrust manifests both internally, shaping individual norms, and externally, influencing broader societal dynamics such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal channel, where individuals develop deep-seated norms of mistrust, is particularly impactful, being at least twice as significant as external factors. Nunn (2008, p.2) emphasizes that this mistrust is not merely a historical artifact but has tangible contemporary effects on economic development. The slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period far longer than the subsequent colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This prolonged exposure to exploitation and violence has left deep institutional scars, making it challenging for African societies to develop robust institutions that support long-run growth. Furthermore, the historical absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many regions hindered economic progress (Nunn 2008, p.2). The persistence of mistrust, therefore, is a critical factor in understanding Africa's economic trajectory, as it undermines the very foundations needed for sustainable development.

Distributional conflicts are key drivers of pre-industrial European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the core economic institutions in these societies were shaped by struggles over wealth distribution rather than efficiency. These distributional conflicts led to a variety of economic structures, such as serfdom and guilds. The persistence of inequality is central to understanding how these institutions developed: in New World societies with high initial levels of inequality, elites leveraged government policies to maintain their advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic restricted competition and perpetuated economic disparities. The role of inequality in shaping institutions is not straightforward. This nuanced view suggests that while inequality drives institutional development, it interacts with additional variables to produce the complex economic landscape of pre-industrial societies.

Institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can persist even when they do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions often arise from colonial strategies that prioritize resource extraction over long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin further elaborates on the complexities of institutional evolution, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; this highlights the necessity for nuanced analysis of how institutions evolve over time and their varying impacts on economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions is also crucial. Ogilvie argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, influencing core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is pivotal in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was a result of political factors underpinning economic growth, such as credible commitment to rules and enforcement mechanisms (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting economic development. Austin also highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, further emphasizing the dynamic nature of institutions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of the impact of institutions on economic growth. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was crucial for the long-term growth of the economy, as it facilitated market exchanges and encouraged investment.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are central to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that these changes were driven by population density and prosperity, which influenced the policies pursued by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a crucial role in the long-run development of these colonies.

The precise remaining disagreement lies in how much distributional conflicts versus cultural beliefs explain institutional variations. Ogilvie highlights that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they do not fully account for all institutional variations; other factors, such as cultural beliefs, also play a significant role (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view suggests that while inequality drives institutional development, it interacts with additional variables to produce the complex economic landscape of pre-industrial societies. The historical evidence from colonial strategies and their impact on current income per capita settles this disagreement by showing how extractive institutions can persist even when they do not encourage long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15).
