The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. The stakes lie in understanding whether the path to sustained prosperity is paved by robust institutional frameworks that encourage investment and innovation, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancement play more decisive roles. At the heart of this debate are competing views on how institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or inclusive, directly determines a nation's economic trajectory. Others contend that the impact of institutions is mediated by broader historical and cultural contexts, suggesting that institutional effects are not universal but contingent on specific conditions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that colonial experiences shaped the institutional landscapes of former colonies, with extractive institutions in high-mortality regions persisting to this day and significantly impacting current income per capita. This perspective aligns with North's assertion that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). In a similar vein, Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This view underscores the importance of considering the specific institutional contexts in which economic development unfolds.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends beyond colonial legacies to broader historical and political contexts (Acemoglu et al 2001). North (1989, p.9) highlights that institutions such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (Austin 2008, p.15) finding that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the importance of credible commitment mechanisms in ensuring that institutions are enforced and can be changed when necessary. Acemoglu et al.'s assertion supports this perspective that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes, but their effects are contingent on specific historical and political contexts. This understanding is essential for developing policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth.

The English financial revolution of the 17th century, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The development of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms was crucial in this transformation. This institutional evolution lowered the cost per transaction and facilitated market exchanges by enhancing property rights security over time (North 1981). The emergence of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during this period highlights how institutional changes can fundamentally alter economic outcomes. These institutions provided the necessary framework for long-term capital markets to flourish, thereby supporting sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that these transaction cost reductions were a direct result of increased security in property rights (North 1989, p.9). This emphasis on secure property rights as a driver of economic growth is corroborated by North and Weingast's (1989, p.29) analysis, which underscores the role of political institutions in enforcing these rights. The success of England’s financial revolution demonstrates that institutional changes can have profound and lasting effects on economic development.

Acemoglu et al.'s work (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) shows that the institutions established by European colonizers in their colonies were instrumental in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) explain that European settlers implemented different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In areas with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states focused on rapid resource extraction, while in regions with lower mortality, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by their analysis of various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19), which consistently show that better institutions correlate with higher income per capita. This pattern underscores how colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes in former colonies.

The equilibrium institutions designed during colonial rule were often extractive, prioritizing the interests of the colonizers over the economic development of the colonies. This perspective is crucial for understanding why some former colonies have struggled with economic growth while others have thrived.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This critique highlights that the economic trajectories of various continents cannot be reduced to a single narrative. Austin's analysis demonstrates how colonial institutions, shaped by settler presence or absence, influenced local economies in complex ways. This dynamic reveals that colonial institutions' impact on economic development varied based on specific historical and geographical contexts (Acemoglu et al 2001).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) extend this complexity by examining the cultural legacies of the slave trade, which fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. They find that this mistrust persists to this day and significantly affects contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The internal norms of mistrust, developed during the slave trade era, are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law or trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores how historical institutions and cultural practices can have enduring effects on economic development, even after the formal institutional structures have changed. The interplay between historical institutions and cultural legacies thus shapes long-run economic growth in profound ways, highlighting the need for nuanced analyses that consider multiple dimensions of institutional influence over time.

Frankema and van Waijenburg share a view that African poverty has not been an enduring historical phenomenon (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). They argue that Africa's economic history is marked by periods of growth and decline tied to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era has enabled recent work in African economic history to demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance, which often emphasized persistent poverty due to structural impediments. The reconstruction and comparison of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. The period from 1880 to 1965 provides a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This research aims to explore how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa, thereby offering insights into the long-term picture of economic development and the distribution of income. These findings suggest that specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development (Acemoglu et al 2001). This nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history highlights the need for future research to assess the possibilities of growth and the extent to which these conditions have influenced long-term welfare development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels innovation, which in turn propels economic development. Mokyr emphasizes that while institutions play a role, they are secondary to technological advancements. Kuznets elaborates on this by highlighting how technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Mokyr acknowledges that without complementary institutional progress to support technological advancements, economic improvement sustainability would be limited (Mokyr 2009).

Mokyr further illustrates this point by noting that coordination failures can hinder economic progress. Institutions must evolve to reduce access costs and facilitate the dissemination of knowledge. This is evident in the historical development of scholarly societies and journals, which played a pivotal role in reducing barriers to scientific advancement (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets complements this view by emphasizing the importance of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, is crucial for managing the societal tensions that arise from technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Mokyr qualifies this perspective by noting that economic reasonableness, or the rational use of resources, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2009). This suggests that while technology drives growth, the institutional environment must be conducive to sustaining these advancements. The growth of useful knowledge alone is not sufficient; it requires a supportive institutional framework to translate into long-term economic development.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the core economic institutions of serfdom, community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were driven by power struggles among social groups rather than efficiency considerations. These distributional conflicts are rooted in the unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality led to the establishment of institutions that privileged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating disparities. For instance, serfdom secured land for the nobility while craft guilds regulated labor markets to the benefit of skilled workers. However, Ogilvie cautions that this perspective does not negate the importance of institutions but rather shifts the focus from their efficiency to their role in mediating social conflicts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly influenced by their ability to mediate social conflicts and secure property rights. North shows that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth. Similarly, North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that regimes are bound by rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions provide the necessary stability for long-term capital markets to thrive.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex (Acemoglu et al 2001). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can be extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts often drive institutional formation, which may prioritize maintaining power structures over economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's critique further complicates this narrative by highlighting the oversimplification of causal relationships in historical contexts. He argues that different historical periods and paths must be considered to understand how institutions evolve and impact growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore nuanced. While North and Weingast show that credible commitment and secure property rights are essential for fostering economic development, Acemoglu et al. reveal that extractive institutions can persist and hinder growth. Austin's critique underscores the need to consider multiple historical periods and paths when examining institutional impacts. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the varying outcomes of European colonization strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These strategies resulted in different institutional frameworks, some of which were more conducive to long-run economic growth than others.
