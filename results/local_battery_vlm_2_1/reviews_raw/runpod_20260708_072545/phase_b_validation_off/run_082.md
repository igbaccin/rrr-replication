The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars of economic development. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure economic interactions, drive sustained economic performance over centuries. One live disagreement concerns whether institutional quality or other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits, are more determinative. Another debate hinges on the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth: some argue for direct effects via property rights and market efficiency, while others point to indirect pathways like political stability and social cohesion. A third controversy is about whether institutional change is endogenous to economic development or driven by exogenous shocks such as colonialism or technological innovation. The nature of these disagreements reflects the complexity of isolating institutions' causal role amidst a web of interdependent historical factors.

Acemoglu et al. and North both emphasize the primacy of institutions in long-run growth, but they differ in their explanations for why some societies develop growth-enhancing institutions while others do not. Acemoglu et al. argue that initial conditions, particularly European settlement patterns, shaped colonial institutions which persisted over time to influence current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). North's work highlights how the development of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms underpin economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6), but he does not specify exogenous triggers for institutional change. The two authors are aligned in their view that institutions, once established, have enduring effects on economic performance; however, they diverge on the origins of these institutions.

Austin challenges the simplicity of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions determine current economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that focusing solely on institutional legacies overlooks the dynamic nature of historical processes and the agency of local actors. He contends that European settlers were not always present to compete for African labor, allowing indigenous commodity production to thrive in some contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors, such as local economic conditions and political dynamics. This perspective complements North's emphasis on institutional change driven by transaction cost reductions, but it also suggests that endogenous processes within societies may be just as important.

Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on the mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced long-run development. They argue that European settlers established extractive states in colonies with high mortality rates to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Settler colonies like the United States and Australia developed more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The authors provide evidence that these institutional differences are closely linked to current income per capita levels. This work highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-run development trajectories by influencing both the initial conditions for economic growth and the subsequent path of institutional evolution.

Hopkins argues that understanding Africa's economic history can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He emphasizes that 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment, are crucial for development. Hopkins' perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s view that colonial institutions have enduring effects on economic outcomes, but it also suggests that institutional reforms can mitigate these historical legacies and promote growth in the present day. This approach underscores the potential for policy interventions to shape institutional trajectories and improve long-run economic performance.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They highlight how the English financial revolution of 1694, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. The evolution of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling transaction costs per exchange. Consequently, the transactions sector grew proportionally with GDP due to increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast further argue that these institutional changes were critical for economic growth. They show how this was evidenced in capital markets, where England’s performance stood out despite similar economic conditions in France. North and Weingast also note that elaborate bureaucratic structures like those inherited from Louis XIV did not necessarily hinder economic growth. The key factor was the regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. This political stability underpinned market development and economic growth, highlighting how credible commitments were essential for long-term economic success (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are central to understanding the development of economic institutions. The core economic structures of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by power dynamics between social groups rather than mere efficiency considerations. These institutions were not just passive responses to economic conditions; they were actively molded by elites who sought to maintain their privileges. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be attributed to landowners' desire to control labor, while craft guilds often restricted competition to protect the interests of established artisans (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic is evident in New World societies as well, where institutions were designed to entrench existing inequalities rather than promote broad-based economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight that extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped institutional development. These distributional conflicts do not operate in isolation; they interact with broader cultural and political factors to produce specific institutional outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasises the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions that goes beyond surface-level descriptions, advocating for a deeper analysis that considers power dynamics and historical contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. These scholars argue that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates and local conditions, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as those in Africa, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) also demonstrate that these institutions have persisted over time, shaping current economic outcomes and affecting income per capita in former colonies today. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on economic growth trajectories.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered the development of formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This was because the cost-benefit calculations for Africans shifted due to new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. The demand for missionary education was driven by these institutional changes, as African families and leaders adapted to the new conditions imposed by British rule.

This dynamic is evident in how different colonial powers influenced educational development differently. This shift was crucial in fostering the growth of educational infrastructure in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) complement this by noting that the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates for Africa leaves many questions unanswered about long-term economic development, including the role of education in shaping African welfare.

The persistence of poverty in sub-Saharan Africa cannot be solely attributed to structural impediments. This includes exploring how these factors relate to other parts of colonial Africa, which can provide insights into future growth possibilities and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show how the legacy of the slave trade has shaped African societies' internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law. This mistrust is deeply rooted in historical experiences that have left a lasting imprint on social interactions and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn demonstrates that the prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a profound adverse effect on Africa's economic development. The duration and intensity of these trades created conditions where local societies were unable to develop robust trading networks due to their small size and lack of differentiated institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context is crucial for understanding the enduring impact of mistrust on African economies today, as it highlights how past institutional weaknesses have hindered economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner's work on Anglophone African economies challenges conventional narratives by demonstrating that many of these regions achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding, shared with Broadberry and Gupta (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), indicates that historical patterns of growth in Africa were more complex than previously thought. The economic performance of these African economies was intricately tied to foreign trade and export markets. Factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions significantly influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These elements highlight how institutional frameworks shaped economic outcomes. For instance, merchant distribution and finance played a crucial role in driving productivity in traded goods sectors, underscoring the importance of service sectors in early modern economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This mechanism suggests that the benefits of economic development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers experiencing greater surpluses over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers. Broadberry and Gupta caution against simplistic interpretations of high productivity in agriculture, noting that geographical factors and investment levels also played significant roles (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This nuanced view underscores the complexity of economic development in historical contexts, where multiple factors interact to shape long-run growth trajectories. The varied impact of institutional frameworks on different sectors within an economy illustrates the multifaceted nature of economic performance over time.

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, focusing instead on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden's analysis of wage data across twenty European cities reveals that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living during this period. This finding challenges optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth. Pamuk highlights how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland allowed for rapid responses to new economic opportunities, enabling the development of advanced urban and commercial systems (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This suggests that while broad economic trends may not directly correlate with immediate improvements in living standards, institutional adaptability can facilitate long-term structural changes that support economic growth. The shift in economic development towards North Sea countries exemplifies how regional dynamics and institutional settings can influence economic trajectories over time.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently harnessing technological innovations to drive economic growth (Kuznets 1973; Mokyr 2005). Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows that epochal innovations require institutional and ideological changes to advance human knowledge and spread across the world. Mokyr (2005, p.49) explains that formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, are essential for reducing access costs and fostering growth in useful knowledge. These adjustments facilitate the adoption of new technologies by resolving conflicts between traditional and modern economic systems.

Kuznets (1973, p.6) also notes that these conflicts must be resolved peacefully to ensure long-term sustainability; without this, the political fabric of society could weaken. This implies that while institutional adjustments are necessary for technological progress, their effectiveness depends on maintaining social cohesion. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates that the growth of economic reasonableness complements technological progress and prevents rent-seeking behaviors that could erode gains from innovation. The sustainability of economic improvement thus hinges on both technological advances and complementary institutional progress.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis has been pivotal in shaping our understanding of Africa's economic trajectory, but it oversimplifies the complexities of institutional evolution and economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins argues that understanding Africa's new economic history can guide institutional reforms to alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This critique underscores the importance of a more granular analysis of institutional changes over time. The effectiveness of these institutions is contingent on their ability to adapt to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives, illustrating a dynamic interplay between external impositions and internal adaptations. This dynamic is crucial because it shows that institutional reforms must be contextually sensitive to be effective. The challenge remains in identifying which institutions are most conducive to economic growth in specific contexts, as well as how these institutions can be adapted to meet local needs while fostering long-term economic development.

This underscores the practical implications of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth, particularly in regions with complex historical legacies (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Institutions are not static; they evolve in response to political and economic pressures. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions are formed and how they evolve over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes the need for a nuanced approach to institutions, suggesting that they should be seen as an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth.

Institutional change can have profound effects on economic development. The historical evidence shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists significantly impacted the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is further supported by North's analysis of the English financial revolution, which highlights how institutional changes can lead to dramatic declines in transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles these disagreements lies in the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on long-run economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) use of alternative measures of institutions, such as constraints on the executive and indices of law and order tradition, provides a robust framework for understanding how different institutional arrangements affect economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This approach illuminates the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth and offers a clear path forward for future research.
