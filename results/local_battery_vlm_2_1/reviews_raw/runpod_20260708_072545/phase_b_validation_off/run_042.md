The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and political science, with significant implications for understanding the divergence between developed and developing economies. The stakes are high because the answer determines policy recommendations for fostering development. A key disagreement revolves around whether institutions that promote private property rights and secure contracts are universally beneficial or if their impact depends on historical, cultural, or geographical contexts. Some scholars argue that institutions shaped by colonial policies have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Others contend that the efficiency of institutions is secondary to distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies. Additionally, there is debate over whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed solely to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or if other factors, such as population density and prosperity, also play crucial roles.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been extensively studied, with some scholars emphasizing their foundational importance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income per capita levels. The authors point out that European settlers adopted different strategies in colonies, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions. These institutions, in turn, shaped economic outcomes by determining the extent to which investment and rule of law were enforced. Austin (2008, p.2) further supports this perspective by highlighting how the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that institutions influence long-run economic growth through complex interactions over time.

North (1989, p.9) (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) provides a complementary view, asserting that institutions matter in economic growth and development. He emphasizes that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to dramatic declines in transaction costs, which were essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) builds on this by suggesting that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. She argues that shared beliefs within societies can influence the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, which are crucial for economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) present a nuanced view by emphasizing the role of population density and prosperity in shaping colonial policies and their subsequent impact on long-run development. They argue that equilibrium institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and highlight the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further supports this perspective by noting that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. He suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

Efficiency considerations, distributional conflicts, and shared beliefs within societies all shape institutions. The role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of historical, cultural, and geographical contexts to fully appreciate their impact.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights. This institutional development was crucial for economic growth, as it facilitated market exchanges by reducing transaction costs. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments were instrumental in this process. These changes reflected an increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast's analysis highlights that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, setting the stage for institutional changes that would later support economic development. The success of these institutional changes underscores the importance of a regime committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and shaped long-run economic outcomes. This institutional framework was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The impact of these institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, highlighting that geographic factors alone do not determine economic development. This perspective underscores the lasting effects of colonial institutions on economic performance. Therefore, while colonial institutions had a profound impact on economic growth, their effects were mediated by subsequent economic opportunities and policies.

Colonial policies in Africa played a pivotal role in shaping institutional frameworks that influenced long-term development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) asserts that the new economic history of Africa underscores how colonial institutions have shaped African economies by altering incentives for investment. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby influencing economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further elaborate that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, highlighting the role of colonial policies in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This development was tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, with local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions all playing crucial roles (Frankema 2012, p.15). However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly between and within countries, indicating that while colonial policies set the stage for economic development, local factors also played a significant role (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Thus, the legacy of colonial rule in Africa is complex, with both institutional frameworks and local conditions shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are complementary drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets's work highlights that technological innovations necessitate corresponding institutional changes to be efficiently absorbed by economies. This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for resolving conflicts arising from economic growth, such as those between traditional rural lifestyles and industrialization. Mokyr complements Kuznets’s analysis by emphasizing that technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional changes (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in facilitating the efficient use of innovations is pivotal; this efficiency hinges on reducing access costs to new knowledge, which requires robust formal institutions. The coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors that Mokyr identifies as potential impediments to sustainable economic growth underscore the necessity for institutions to mitigate these obstacles (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress spurs initial growth, it is the institutional framework that ensures this growth is sustained over the long term. By fostering an environment where new knowledge can be effectively utilized, institutions play a critical role in translating technological advancements into economic gains.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 was driven by factors beyond institutions alone (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that merchant distribution and finance played crucial roles in north-western Europe's high productivity, highlighting the service sector as a key driver of early modern economic growth.

These factors suggest that institutional change, while important, was not the sole determinant of economic growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) finds no clear relationship between economic development and improvements in the standard of living during this period, suggesting that the 'industrious revolution' may have been more nuanced than previously thought. This observation qualifies the notion that institutional change directly led to widespread economic benefits, as it indicates that other factors, such as merchant distribution and finance, were instrumental in shaping regional economic trajectories.

Thus, while institutions are crucial for long-run economic growth, they operate within a broader framework of economic activities and environmental conditions that also significantly influence outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that New World societies' institutional structures often privileged elites at the expense of broader economic competition. Sokoloff and Engerman contend that this elite advantage was particularly pronounced in regions where factor endowments favored extreme inequality from the outset, such as those with abundant land relative to labor. Akyeampong and Fofack further illustrate how these dynamics played out in colonial Africa, where patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced the subordinate status of women, limiting their economic agency (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional framework restricted competition and shaped long-term development trajectories by influencing access to resources and opportunities. Nunn extends this perspective by highlighting how these institutional legacies can have enduring effects on economic growth, showing that Africa's slave trades created lasting impacts on its economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). However, the adverse effects of inequality are not uniform across all regions; in some cases, local conditions and institutions may mitigate or exacerbate these impacts. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that centralized governments with developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom, were better positioned to support trade and economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while institutional structures favoring elites can hinder long-run economic growth, local conditions and governance play a critical role in shaping outcomes.

Austin further illustrates this point with examples from colonial administrations that adjusted their policies in response to local economic conditions (Austin 2008, p.15), showing how institutions can be shaped by both exogenous and endogenous forces. This nuanced view suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their adaptability to local contexts, which may not always align with simplistic models of institutional change.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic interactions and incentivize investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonial powers established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to either extractive states or those enforcing the rule of law. This variation in institutional design directly influenced long-term economic outcomes by determining whether colonies developed towards industrialization and prosperity or remained stagnant. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) highlights how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth.

Economic conditions and local political actions shape institutions as well as being shaped by them. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic incentives, demonstrating the adaptive nature of institutions. This mutual influence between institutions and economic conditions suggests a dynamic process where institutions evolve to meet the changing needs of societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) further emphasizes the importance of understanding this evolutionary process by advocating for institutions as indispensable tools in economic historians' toolkits.

The role of institutions extends beyond mere efficiency considerations; they are also influenced by distributional conflicts and political power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective underscores the importance of considering political factors when analyzing institutional effects on economic growth. North (1989, p.6) adds that the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges is closely linked to the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement.

The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how institutional reforms can address poverty by fostering investment incentives through good governance. Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for development, highlighting that effective institutions are crucial for long-term economic growth. Shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)  shows that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to adapt to local contexts and address distributional conflicts.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions significantly influence long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks for economic interactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates or by endogenous factors like local political action and indigenous economic incentives. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to local economic conditions, indicating the importance of endogenous factors. This disagreement is settled by historical evidence from colonial administrations that demonstrate how institutions can be shaped by both exogenous and endogenous forces.
