The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake intellectually are competing explanations for why some economies thrive while others stagnate over centuries. One position asserts that the quality of institutions, especially those governing property rights and political stability, drives sustained economic growth; another view posits that geographical factors, such as climate or disease prevalence, determine long-run economic outcomes by influencing institutional development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to geography or if they operate independently to foster growth. The question of how institutions shape long-term economic trajectories remains unresolved.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are vital for fostering economic growth; they provide the framework within which economic activity occurs and influence long-term development trajectories. North (1989, p.6) points out that as economies grow, political institutions evolve to specify efficient property rights and enforce them effectively, reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional evolution is crucial because it creates an environment where economic growth can be sustained over the long run. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that colonial policies shaped by European settlers often led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists and promote long-term economic development. These institutions, which persist over time, significantly impact current income levels in former colonies. This institutional legacy highlights the enduring influence of historical policies on contemporary economic outcomes. Thus, understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of how historical contexts influence institutional development and their subsequent impact on economic performance.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis highlights how political factors underpinning economic growth are not limited to rules governing economic exchange but also encompass the institutions enforcing these rules and their potential changes (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is because a critical factor is the degree of commitment by the regime or sovereign to these rules, which in turn fosters secure property rights. The adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth varies across countries, highlighting the complexity of institutional change (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.15). This variability underscores the importance of historical contexts in shaping institutional development and their subsequent impact on economic performance. Understanding these dynamics is essential for appreciating how institutions influence long-run economic growth.

The evolution of property rights in colonial settings further illustrates this complexity. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could align with local political and economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction between institutional prescriptions and local incentives suggests that the effectiveness of property rights depends on contextual factors, including the presence or absence of European settlers and the responsiveness of colonial governance to local conditions. The implication is that while secure property rights are crucial for economic growth, their impact is mediated by the specific historical and political contexts in which they operate.

The historical and structural factors influencing poverty and growth in Africa are extensively explored by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They argue that the quality of public goods provision and institutional development in Africa is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. The presence of poor institutions, while significant, does not fully account for Africa's economic record. This perspective underscores the multifaceted nature of African poverty, which cannot be reduced to a single cause such as colonial legacies or structural impediments. Hopkins (2009, p.18) observes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation alone is insufficient to explain institutional dysfunction in Africa; even homogeneous societies can face governance challenges and fail to uphold the rule of law. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2; 2012, p.28) highlight the importance of understanding long-term economic development trends, including income distribution, to assess future growth prospects. They suggest that specific historical or geographical conditions may have had temporary effects on African welfare. This nuanced view implies that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, their impact is mediated by various factors and cannot be considered in isolation from broader historical and contextual elements. The complexity of African poverty necessitates comprehensive approaches that consider multiple determinants beyond institutional frameworks alone.

Technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both underscore that technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge are primary drivers of long-run economic growth. The technological changes introduced into and diffused through growing economies necessitate institutional adjustments to efficiently utilise innovations. This dynamic highlights a reciprocal relationship between technology and institutions: while technological advancements spur economic growth, they also demand complementary institutional developments to sustain that growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that modern technology requires substantial societal changes, including shifts in lifestyle, family patterns, and environmental attitudes, which are integral for accommodating new technologies. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for resolving conflicts arising from economic growth, thereby ensuring the sustainability of long-term development.

This suggests that although technological advancements are pivotal for economic growth, their benefits may not always translate into sustained development without robust institutional frameworks. Economic improvements must be complemented by institutional progress to mitigate rent-seeking behaviours that could erode gains from technological advancements. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in harnessing technological progress for long-term economic growth, highlighting that while technology is a key driver, its impact is contingent on supportive institutional environments.

Nunn and Wantchekon's (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) work demonstrates that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust, which has had enduring effects on African societies. This mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals' norms and behaviours. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) ) assert that this internalized mistrust plays at least twice the role in shaping intergroup trust compared to external environmental factors such as the rule of law or others' trustworthiness. This finding aligns with broader research on the importance of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn highlights that this mistrust is a persistent legacy of Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years. The implications of this enduring mistrust are significant, as it affects various aspects of social and economic life, including cooperation, investment in public goods, and overall economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers behind the formation of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that these distributional conflicts shaped core institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. The power dynamics between elites and commoners influenced the development of economic systems in ways that were reflective of underlying social tensions. For instance, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that societies with greater inequality in New World settlements had institutions that favored elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. These distributional conflicts have profound implications: they shaped economic institutions and influenced long-term growth trajectories by entrenching power imbalances, which affected investment patterns and technological adoption. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for understanding economic history, underscoring their role in mediating the effects of distributional conflicts on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective highlights how the struggle for resources and power among different social groups can lead to institutional arrangements that perpetuate inequality.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident from the historical perspective that shows how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. Instances where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were more likely to experience sustained economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole hindered long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

This perspective is supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development. He shows how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security was crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The political model outlined by North illustrates that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for facilitating market exchanges, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6).

The institutionalist approach, while emphasizing secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, also acknowledges the complexity of explaining why some countries adopted favorable institutions while others did not. Austin argues that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between local conditions and external forces highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional development.

The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional arrangements further underscores the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie argues that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for understanding economic history, particularly how they mediate the effects of distributional conflicts on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This highlights how initial conditions and subsequent institutional developments can have lasting effects on economic trajectories. The shared finding across these studies is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, with particular emphasis on the importance of property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions influence growth remain debated. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, leading to different institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), while Austin contends that colonial administrations adapted their prescriptions based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence of varying institutional responses to similar economic conditions in different colonies settles this disagreement.
