The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in development economics. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing behavior, are widely recognized as critical factors influencing economic performance. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions themselves drive growth or if they merely reflect underlying socioeconomic conditions. Some scholars argue that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by providing incentives for investment and ensuring secure property rights. Others contend that institutions are endogenous to economic development processes, evolving in response to changes in technology, demographics, or other exogenous factors. This debate is not just academic; it has profound implications for policy-making, as understanding the causal mechanisms at play can guide efforts to promote sustainable growth.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the pivotal role of secure property rights and effective enforcement institutions in fostering economic growth. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective highlights how institutional frameworks can facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting economic development. Similarly, North and Weingast focus on the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, arguing that these institutions were instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They note that credible commitments are essential for economic growth, as they ensure that rules governing economic exchange are enforced and can be changed only under specific conditions. This stream underscores the importance of institutional stability and predictability in promoting long-term economic development.

Another stream of scholarship emphasizes how colonial institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights how historical institutional legacies can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. Similarly, Hopkins notes that poor institutions are linked to Africa's dismal economic record and argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This stream underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic development.

Acemoglu et al. establish that the type of institutions imposed during colonial rule was determined by the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in these regions. This institutional framework persisted long after independence, shaping economic trajectories. For example, European settlers in regions with low mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, established robust property rights and rule of law. These inclusive institutions fostered economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. This institutional legacy has had enduring effects, with former colonies that adopted inclusive institutions experiencing higher levels of economic growth compared to those saddled with extractive ones. The persistence of these colonial institutions underscores their profound impact on contemporary economic outcomes, illustrating how historical institutional legacies can shape long-run economic development.

This institutional evolution underpinned secure property rights, which are essential for long-term capital market growth. The emergence of these political and economic institutions facilitated market exchanges and increased the aggregate volume of transactions in a growing economy.

This implies that while specific institutions like the Bank of England played a significant role, other factors such as bureaucratic structures may have also influenced economic growth. The evolution of these institutions in Europe highlights their pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering long-term economic growth.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficient use of innovations, driving long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological progress necessitates complementary institutional changes to resolve conflicts between traditional societal structures and modern economic practices. For example, the transition from a rural mode of life to industrialization requires significant adjustments in family patterns and societal values (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). Without such institutional support, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the importance of a robust framework that can absorb and integrate new innovations efficiently.

In contrast to Europe's experience, the impact of colonial rule on African economic development reveals how external governance structures influenced local institutional frameworks, shaping long-term socio-economic trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in determining the trajectory of economic growth by establishing administrative structures that either facilitated or hindered the development of formal education and public goods. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds to this by showing that British colonial rule was particularly conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that the type of colonial governance mattered significantly for educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.1).

The specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced economic growth involve the creation of administrative and educational infrastructures. This suggests that the type of colonial governance can have enduring effects on socio-economic development through its impact on institutional frameworks. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that while institutions matter, the specific outcomes depend on how these institutions interact with local contexts and ethnic dynamics (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The interaction between colonial institutions and local factors thus determines the long-term economic trajectory of African nations.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa persists through contemporary trust levels, which are influenced by historical mistrust and affect economic outcomes, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is not primarily explained by its impact on the trustworthiness of others. Instead, they find that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalization of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it shapes individuals' perceptions and behaviors in ways that hinder cooperation and collective action. Nunn's work underscores the enduring nature of this impact by noting that Africa experienced four distinct slave trades over nearly 500 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). This prolonged exposure to exploitative systems likely entrenched mistrust more deeply within societal norms, creating a persistent barrier to economic growth. This qualification suggests that while mistrust is a critical factor, it operates in conjunction with other historical and contemporary forces to shape African economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by concentrating wealth, human capital, and political power in the hands of a few. The unequal distribution of resources created an environment where institutional frameworks favored elite interests at the expense of broader economic development. This dynamic is evident in the way institutions evolved to protect the interests of the privileged classes, often through mechanisms such as land grants, monopolies, and restrictive labor policies. These measures not only perpetuated inequality but also hindered the emergence of a competitive market economy. The resulting institutional frameworks were less conducive to innovation and economic growth because they discouraged investment in human capital and infrastructure, which are crucial for long-term development. Broadberry and Gupta highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that while institutional factors were critical, the role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period was also significant. However, the persistence of inequality in New World societies meant that the benefits of economic development were unevenly distributed, with elites capturing a disproportionate share of the gains. This unequal distribution of wealth and power created a self-reinforcing cycle where institutional frameworks continued to favor elite interests, further entrenched inequality, and limited economic growth potential.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis in Africa has been re-evaluated by Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), challenging the notion that African poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They argue that European settlement patterns and colonial government incentives shaped indigenous commodity production, creating different paths for economic development. Austin shows that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged local commodity production, altering institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This finding highlights the importance of local agency and adaptive institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg note that pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates are lacking, making it difficult to fully assess the persistence of poverty and its structural determinants over time (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This gap underscores the need for more comprehensive historical data to understand the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as shown by the interplay between colonial strategies and institutional outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European settlers established extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These findings are supported by North (1989, p.9), who argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) thesis that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are central to the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. Austin (2008, p.15) also highlights how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous actions, indicating that local agency and adaptive institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic development.

The persistence of poverty and its structural determinants remain complex issues. While Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions for providing incentives for investment and development, Austin (2008, p.2) notes the difficulty of testing institutional theories quantitatively and the need to understand why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not others. This complexity is further underscored by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who argue that credible commitment underpins growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of income per capita and the analysis of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This shows that while institutions matter, their effects are contingent on historical context and local agency.
