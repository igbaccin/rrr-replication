The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development studies. At stake is understanding how different institutional frameworks can either foster sustained prosperity or hinder it over extended periods. The debate centres around whether institutions themselves are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they merely facilitate other underlying factors. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions, shaped by historical and geographical factors, have determined the trajectory of economic development in former colonies (Austin 2008, p.15). Another key point of contention is whether the efficiency of institutions, as measured by their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs, is the crucial factor in economic growth or if other political and social dynamics are at play. Additionally, there is a debate over whether institutional changes introduced during colonialism were more influential than geographic factors in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The central question remains: how do institutions shape long-run economic growth?

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework enabled a dramatic decline in transaction costs through developments such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, collectively known as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). These innovations reflected increased security of property rights over time, which was critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (Author Year p.N). North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that this institutional evolution led to the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, thereby reducing costs per transaction while increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges. This dynamic highlights how secure property rights facilitated market exchanges and fueled economic expansion by encouraging specialization and division of labor.

The implications of these institutional changes are profound; however, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) caution against overestimating their revolutionary impact when viewed in isolation. They argue that while the institutional framework was crucial, it is essential to consider broader economic contexts. For instance, recent research has upgraded France's pre-Revolutionary economic performance, suggesting that England and France were nearly at parity economically. This implies that the institutional changes alone did not create a stark divergence in economic growth between the two nations; rather, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political regimes also played significant roles. The nuanced view is that while institutions are critical, their impact must be evaluated within the broader socioeconomic landscape, where multiple factors interact to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize the varied colonization strategies Europeans employed, ranging from settler colonies like the United States to extractive states such as the Congo and Gold Coast. This institutional diversity was driven by mortality rates among European colonizers: high mortality led to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, characterized by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities for locals, persisted long after colonial rule ended. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illustrate that these institutional legacies were pivotal in shaping the long-run development trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering industrialization and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional impact interacted with local conditions such as population density and initial prosperity. This nuance underscores that although colonial institutions were crucial, their influence on long-run economic growth was mediated by local socioeconomic factors.

Ogilvie's research suggests that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were fundamentally shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). These institutions, including serfdom and guilds, were influenced by power struggles between different social groups. For instance, guilds served as mechanisms for skilled craftsmen to protect their interests against competition from unskilled workers or other guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with Austin's argument that the institutional evolution in Europe was shaped by complex interactions between various economic and political forces. These forces included colonial governments' need for fiscal security, which led them to adjust their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Such adjustments could result in higher production equilibriums, contrary to the hypothesis that colonial administrations uniformly imposed extractive institutions. This nuanced view of institutional development highlights how the pursuit of efficiency was often secondary to the immediate political and social needs of different groups.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had unique impacts on educational development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that the demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of these institutions changed the incentives for formal education, making it more attractive to African communities. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that factor endowments varied widely across New World societies, leading to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality persisted over time and affected development through its impact on evolving institutions. The implications of these findings suggest that while British colonial rule may have fostered some aspects of educational development, the broader historical context, including the impact of slavery, must be considered when evaluating long-term economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges previous narratives about Africa's economic performance during colonial rule. The export market played a pivotal role in this growth, as Broadberry and Gardner note that patterns of economic expansion were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These economies' reliance on exports meant that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions significantly influenced the timing and scale of growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight that understanding real wage levels across British Africa is crucial for assessing long-term welfare development and future economic possibilities. This perspective underscores the importance of historical data in evaluating the structural effects on African economies, suggesting a more nuanced view of colonial-era economic performance than previously acknowledged.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by structural changes that initiated different trajectories of economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that these divergent paths were shaped by differences in the traded goods sector, with north-western Europe benefiting from merchant distribution and finance. Regions with stronger monopolies, such as Tuscany, struggled to adapt to the same extent (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Pamuk also notes that urbanization rates provide another key indicator of productivity and economic development in this period, suggesting that institutional factors may have played a more nuanced role than initially apparent (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These findings collectively underscore how structural changes and institutional settings shaped the early modern economy, influencing long-term economic trajectories.

Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) both assert that technological progress is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes that the accumulation of useful knowledge underpins this process, while Kuznets highlights how technological advancements necessitate institutional adjustments for efficient use and widespread adoption. The interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is crucial in sustaining economic growth.

Mokyr's perspective (Mokyr 2005, p.49) underscores the importance of formal institutions such as scholarly societies that reduce access costs to knowledge. These institutions facilitate the dissemination and application of new technologies, thereby accelerating economic development. Kuznets extends this idea by noting how technological progress requires resolving conflicts arising from socio-economic transformations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). He stresses the role of the modern national state in peacefully managing these conflicts, ensuring that the political fabric remains strong enough to support sustained growth.

Mokyr also recognizes a potential downside to relying solely on technological advancements for economic growth. Without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements can be undermined by rent-seeking behavior and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the complex interplay between technology and institutions in driving long-term economic growth. Kuznets' insights into the role of the state in managing growth-induced conflicts further highlight the necessity of robust institutions to sustain the positive impacts of technological progress.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce efficient rules for economic exchange. This is evident as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European settlers established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, which significantly impacted current income per capita. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is shown as North and Weingast (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the institutions guaranteeing property rights and contract enforcement, while North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the importance of credible commitment underpinning long-term economic growth. The historical evidence from capital markets in seventeenth-century England supports this view.

The specific institutional arrangements and their effectiveness in promoting economic development vary across different contexts. Austin (2008, p.2) shows how European settlers' presence influenced the adoption of institutions, with colonial governments often encouraging indigenous commodity production when settlers were absent. This highlights that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may not prioritize long-run growth but rather maximize rents for themselves.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, showing that it was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This underscores how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on their design and enforcement.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions shape long-run economic growth remain a subject of debate. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) focus on the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies, North (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions guaranteeing property rights and contract enforcement. The historical evidence from capital markets in seventeenth-century England supports the view that credible commitment underpins long-term economic growth.

The shared finding is that institutions matter for economic growth by fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The disagreement persists over whether these institutions are shaped primarily by population density and prosperity or by shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights. The historical evidence from capital markets in seventeenth-century England settles this debate: credible commitment underpins long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), showing that secure property rights are crucial for sustained economic development.
