The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory and historical economics. At stake intellectually is the causal mechanism for sustained economic progress over centuries. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, or exogenous shocks play dominant roles. One position posits that institutions, particularly those ensuring secure property rights and effective governance, are indispensable for fostering economic development. These scholars argue that institutions shape the incentives and constraints under which individuals operate, thereby influencing investment, innovation, and overall economic performance. Another position emphasizes the role of geographic factors such as climate or natural resources, while a third focuses on cultural beliefs and values that influence economic behavior. A fourth stream suggests that distributional conflicts and power dynamics within societies are more critical than efficiency considerations in shaping core economic institutions.

The debate extends to how institutions evolve over time and the extent to which they can be changed through deliberate policy interventions. Some scholars highlight the historical path dependence of institutional development, where initial conditions and past events significantly influence current institutional arrangements. Some scholars highlight political factors and power dynamics in shaping institutions, contending that politically powerful groups often design institutions to maximize their rents while promoting long-run economic growth (Author Year p.N). The interplay between these competing explanations underscores the complexity of understanding the causal mechanisms behind economic development and the need for a multifaceted approach that considers various factors simultaneously.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the emergence of novel financial tools led to a substantial decrease in transaction costs. This reduction was pivotal for enhancing the security of long-term property rights, which are essential for both capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War drove the initial trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, marking a critical juncture in institutional development (North 1989, p.9). This political maneuver was part of a broader pattern where institutions evolve to facilitate market exchanges. The commitment of the regime to enforcing these rules was a critical factor in sustaining long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.9) also notes that as economies grow, the transaction sector becomes an increasingly significant part of GNP, reflecting the rising volume of exchanges due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This implies that while institutional changes reduced transaction costs, they also necessitated a growing administrative apparatus to manage the increasing complexity of economic interactions. The development of secure property rights through institutional innovations was about reducing immediate transaction costs and laying the groundwork for sustained economic growth over centuries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) maintain that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The nature of these institutions was deeply rooted in the colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. In areas with lower mortality rates, Europeans were more inclined to settle and develop institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. One specific mechanism through which these institutions operated was maximizing rents to European colonists while promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This extractive nature of colonial institutions meant that even when equilibrium was achieved, it did not necessarily foster economic development. Instead, it often resulted in institutional designs that prioritized short-term gains for the colonizers over sustainable economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner's work, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), challenges the prevailing notion of persistent poverty in Africa by highlighting periods of economic growth tied to foreign trade and export markets. These findings illustrate that African economies were dynamic and responded to external market forces, particularly through colonial export production. For example, Broadberry and Gardner show how export production varied significantly across countries due to factors such as local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the importance of specific conditions in shaping economic outcomes during this period. Frankema and van Waijenburg further emphasize that historical data on real wages can provide insights into long-term welfare development, revealing periods of growth that were previously overlooked (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). These patterns also highlight the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic performance, as different institutional frameworks could either facilitate or hinder growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a persistent culture of mistrust, which has had lasting economic effects (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that this mistrust arises from internal norms developed during the slave trade in addition to the trustworthiness of others. This culture of mistrust, in turn, affects economic development through various channels, including reduced cooperation and lower levels of social capital. This institutional framework must be understood within the context of historical legacies such as the slave trade, which created enduring norms of mistrust that continue to influence contemporary economic behavior and development. The persistence of these cultural effects underscores the complex interplay between historical events and long-term economic outcomes, highlighting how deep-seated social norms can shape economic performance over extended periods.

Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.54) propose that technological progress is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. They argue that this progress is fueled by the accumulation of useful knowledge, which in turn requires institutional adjustments to facilitate its efficient application. This perspective highlights how the interplay between technology and institutions enables sustained economic improvement. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes the role of the sovereign state in resolving conflicts arising from technological changes, underscoring that political stability is crucial for managing growth-induced disruptions. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates on this by noting the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements. He argues that without such coordination, economic improvements may be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors and other inefficiencies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by pointing out that technological changes are not uniformly distributed across regions or periods; some innovations have more significant long-term impacts than others. This variability suggests that the specific nature of technological progress and its institutional context can shape economic growth trajectories in diverse ways.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping key economic institutions, as argued by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). They posit that distributional struggles over wealth and power fundamentally drive the formation of institutions. This perspective is grounded in the idea that elites often manipulate institutions to preserve their privileges. This dynamic is evident in New World societies where elites leveraged institutional frameworks to sustain inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) illustrate how institutions in these societies were designed to advantage the ruling class through policies that restricted competition and maintained social hierarchies, thereby perpetuating economic disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional design, driven by distributional conflicts, had profound implications for long-term economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that while these institutions might seem inefficient from a modern perspective, they served to stabilize the social order and maintain elite dominance in their historical contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This highlights how institutional arrangements that appear suboptimal can persist due to the underlying power dynamics.

Institutional arrangements are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth as they determine how resources are allocated and power is distributed within societies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), highlighting how institutional design can perpetuate economic disparities over extended periods.

North (1989, p.9) underscores the role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth, noting that the English financial revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This institutional development was critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the importance of incorporating institutions into economic analysis, suggesting that they are indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian.

Austin (2008, p.2) notes that European settlers often adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions, challenging the notion that institutions were uniformly extractive. This adaptability shows how institutional arrangements can evolve in response to local conditions, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth by enforcing rules governing economic exchange and ensuring that these rules are binding on the regime or sovereign.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in historical cases where institutional changes led to significant economic transformations. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that extractive institutions designed by European colonizers had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies, which were directly related to industrialization and long-term income levels. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) present evidence from capital markets indicating that institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Institutional design and adaptation are key factors in determining the trajectory of long-run economic growth. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in shaping economic outcomes, as shown by North (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether extractive institutions are always detrimental to growth or can sometimes be adapted to local conditions. Austin (2008, p.15) shows how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This historical evidence of adaptability settles the debate by demonstrating that while extractive institutions may initially hinder growth, they can evolve over time to support long-term economic development when responding to local incentives.
