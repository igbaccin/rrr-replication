The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake is understanding whether and how political and social structures influence the trajectory of economies over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. Some scholars argue that institutions directly impact economic outcomes by determining the security of property rights and the efficiency of markets (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others contend that institutions are shaped by broader historical and cultural factors, such as beliefs and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The interplay between these perspectives highlights the need to delineate how specific institutional arrangements translate into sustained economic growth.

Building on this, North's work on economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) emphasizes how financial institutions can mitigate transaction costs and secure property rights. In particular, the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments marked a significant shift in the English economy by dramatically lowering transaction costs and enhancing long-term capital markets. The development of these financial institutions facilitated market exchanges, allowing for increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of transactions. This institutional framework fostered economic growth by ensuring that capital markets operated efficiently and reliably. These financial innovations have broader implications that merit attention. The emergence of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments occurred within a larger political and economic transformation. Recent research has challenged traditional views on the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes by highlighting that similar economic performance could be achieved under different institutional frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that while specific institutions like the Bank of England were crucial, the broader context of political stability and credible commitments was equally important in driving long-run economic growth.

Building on this, colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. establish that European colonization strategies varied widely based on mortality rates and led to the establishment of different institutions in colonies. In high-mortality regions, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas low-mortality areas saw the development of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. The extractive nature of these institutions in high-mortality colonies impeded long-term growth by maximizing rents for European colonists rather than fostering development.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is further qualified by the interaction between population density and prosperity. Acemoglu et al. argue that the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by local conditions, including population density and existing levels of prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction shows how colonial institutions adapted to local contexts, leading to varied economic outcomes across different regions. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to these institutional differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), which influenced the opportunity for industrialization and long-run development.

Distributional conflicts were central to shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies alike. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the economic institutions of these regions were fundamentally shaped by distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations alone. In pre-industrial Europe, key institutions such as serfdom, communal structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds emerged from struggles over wealth and power between different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions reflected the balance of power among elites, peasants, and artisans, who sought to protect their interests through institutional arrangements that could entrench inequality. In New World societies, initial differences in factor endowments created extreme inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These inequalities were entrenched by institutions that advantaged elites at the expense of broader economic development. For example, government policies restricted competition to maintain elite advantages, which contributed to the persistence of inequality over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

The downstream implications of these distributional conflicts are evident in the long-run economic trajectories of both Europe and the New World societies. Similarly, in the New World, the institutions that evolved to protect elite interests hindered broader economic development by limiting competition and restricting access to resources. However, Sokoloff and Engerman qualify this by pointing out that these institutions also created incentives for elites to invest in human capital and infrastructure, which could have positive effects on economic growth under certain conditions. This suggests that while distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping economic institutions, their impact on long-run economic growth was complex and context-dependent.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight that technological advancements drive economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores the critical role of innovation in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Mokyr emphasizes the importance of useful knowledge, suggesting that it is the primary driver of economic growth and that institutions must adjust to facilitate its efficient use. Kuznets supports this view by noting that technological progress requires institutional adjustments for modern economic growth. He identifies "epochal innovations" as pivotal drivers of sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These advancements necessitate significant changes in societal structures, including the adoption of new modes of life and production sectors. Kuznets also points out that these shifts can induce conflicts within societies, which must be resolved to ensure long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

This implies that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements could be eroded over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, while technological innovations are crucial for long-run economic growth, their impact is contingent on the ability of institutions to adapt and support these changes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) examine how historical factors have shaped economic development in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). One of the key mechanisms through which these historical influences operate is the legacy of colonialism, which has left enduring imprints on institutions and cultural norms. Austin highlights that while European settler presence often drove indigenous commodity production, colonial administrations also adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation could lead to higher production equilibria, countering the notion of a uniformly exploitative colonial system. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) extend this by showing that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms as well as being an external environmental factor. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonialism are complex and multifaceted, influencing both formal institutions and informal cultural norms. The enduring effects of these historical factors imply that any analysis of long-run economic growth must account for the deep-seated impacts of colonial rule and the slave trade, which continue to shape contemporary African economies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that poverty in Africa has not been an immutable feature of the continent's economic landscape. This is evident from their work on historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa which reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of persistent poverty. These findings are supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), who reconstruct real wage levels and trends in British African colonies to demonstrate variability over time. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further elucidate that economic growth patterns were intricately tied to foreign trade, export markets, local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors collectively shaped the diverse trajectories of economic development across Africa. However, despite these variations, the overall trend indicates that African economies were more dynamic than previously thought, with periods of significant growth interspersed with periods of stagnation or decline. This suggests that the continent's economic history is marked by a complex interplay of external and internal factors, which have shaped its long-run economic performance in nuanced ways.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the structure and efficiency of markets through property rights and transaction costs. North argues that institutions reduce these costs, facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al.'s work complements this perspective: their analysis shows how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with extractive states hindering growth while inclusive ones promoted it (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic performance is not uniform across regions. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores that historical paths and periods must be considered individually to understand institutional evolution and its effects on growth (Austin 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al.'s research supports this nuanced view, demonstrating how colonial institutions shaped long-run development trajectories in former colonies by influencing industrialization opportunities (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions further highlights the complexity of institutional impact on growth. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts drove the establishment of core pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The shared finding across these studies is the centrality of institutions in economic development: their evolution and enforcement mechanisms determine property rights security and transaction costs, which are critical for market functioning and growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The disagreement lies in the specific historical processes that shaped institutional trajectories. Austin's analysis shows that colonial administrations adapted to indigenous economic actions, challenging the notion of uniform extractive institutions across colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15). This is settled by comparative evidence from capital markets and long-run growth rates: England's financial revolution and secure property rights, driven by political commitments to institutional rules, fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), whereas extractive colonial institutions in regions like the Congo hindered development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2).
