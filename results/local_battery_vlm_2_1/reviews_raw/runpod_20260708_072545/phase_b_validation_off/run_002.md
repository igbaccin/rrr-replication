The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development studies. Institutions are understood as the formal and informal rules that structure social interactions and shape incentives, which can either facilitate or hinder economic progress. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize institutional factors versus those who attribute greater weight to other determinants such as geography, culture, or initial conditions. Some scholars argue for a direct causal link between institutions and growth, positing that inclusive institutions fostering secure property rights, political stability, and open markets are essential for sustained economic development. The rival camp argues that institutions alone do not determine economic outcomes; instead, geographic factors like climate and natural resources, as well as cultural beliefs, play significant roles in shaping economic trajectories. This debate underscores the complexity of economic growth, where multiple factors intertwine to influence long-term development.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the crucial role of institutions in fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges. North argues that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who highlight how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, which in turn significantly influenced current income per capita. The authors note that in colonies where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law institutions, economic development was more robust than in regions where extractive states were imposed (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional framework is seen as foundational for long-term economic growth because it creates an environment conducive to investment and innovation.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) shared this finding in their study of public choice institutions. The creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs is crucial because it reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This institutional framework facilitates investment and innovation by creating an environment where economic actors can confidently engage in long-term planning and risk-taking. However, North and Weingast argue that while these institutions are essential for growth, they also note that the evolution of these institutions is not straightforward and can be influenced by various political factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Acemoglu et al.'s work on colonialism highlights that institutions established during this period significantly shaped current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The authors demonstrate that European colonizers implemented different strategies based on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement. In colonies with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. The persistence of these institutions is crucial because they continue to influence economic outcomes by shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al.'s work is supported by empirical evidence showing that various measures of institutions correlate strongly with current economic outcomes. For instance, constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights protections, rule of law indices, and judicial efficiency all align with the institutional frameworks established during colonial times (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. observe that institutions are critical but their impact varies across colonies. The interaction between these institutions and local conditions, such as population density and initial prosperity, further complicates the development trajectory of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic growth, highlighting that the legacy of extractive institutions continues to shape contemporary economic performance in profound ways.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) along with Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2) highlight that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth. This mistrust manifests as both external environmental factors and internal norms within society. The internal channel, driven by deep-seated historical grievances and social fragmentation, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which encompasses factors such as the rule of law and the perceived trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This pervasive mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it erodes social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. The historical legacy of slavery has left deep scars on African societies, creating a climate where trust is scarce and relationships are strained. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the slave trades in Africa spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, dwarfing the period of official colonial rule which lasted approximately 75 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extended duration underscores the deep and enduring impact of slavery on African economic development. Slavery's adverse effects extend beyond economic stagnation to social and political instability, further hindering Africa's progress. It is crucial to recognize that while mistrust is a significant barrier to growth, addressing it requires a nuanced approach that acknowledges both the internal and external dimensions of this historical legacy.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective emphasizes technological advancements as the key factor in long-run economic development, suggesting that institutional adjustments are necessary but secondary to the role of technological progress. Institutions facilitate the efficient use and diffusion of new technologies, enabling sustained growth over extended periods.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress often conflicts with existing social structures, requiring institutional changes to resolve these tensions without undermining societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is crucial for maintaining long-term economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges that technological progress is more straightforwardly irreversible than institutional improvements which are complex and subject to setbacks (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This nuanced view suggests that institutions support economic growth but are not the primary drivers of long-term development.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) assert that the skill premium is a significant predictor of long-term economic performance, highlighting the role of institutions that guarantee fair execution of contracts in shaping economic outcomes. This investment is underpinned by institutional frameworks that facilitate the transfer of skills across generations. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) underscores how guilds in Western Europe played a pivotal role in managing apprentice contracts, thereby ensuring the efficient transmission of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions also faced criticism for potentially monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades, which could counteract their beneficial effects on skill development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further elaborate that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was driven by both merchant distribution and finance, as well as production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, reinforcing the idea that institutional support for skill development is integral to long-term economic performance. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that early modern development benefits were unevenly distributed (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The wages of skilled laborers yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than those of unskilled laborers, indicating that the economic gains from institutional support for skill development were concentrated among certain segments of society. This uneven distribution highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, where institutional frameworks can both facilitate and constrain long-term economic growth depending on how they are structured and implemented.

Inequality shaped economic performance in New World societies by influencing institutional arrangements that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the high inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset of European colonization led to institutions that concentrated resources among a privileged few. This concentration was maintained through policies that restricted competition in favor of elite interests. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that the growth of export production varied due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The interplay between inequality and institutions is evident in how these factors shaped economic development paths. The timing and scale of growth in export production were influenced by policies that favored elite interests, as seen in the variation across countries with different institutional frameworks (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Institutional reforms in Africa have targeted specific changes to improve governance and economic conditions, but these efforts do not directly address how these reforms relate to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that institutional quality is crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) argument that understanding the long-term picture of economic development in Africa can help assess future growth possibilities. The focus on institutions as a driver of economic growth is evident in Hopkins' discussion of how good institutions provide incentives for investment, a mechanism that underpins development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) suggest that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about poverty persistence unanswered. This gap in historical data complicates efforts to trace the long-term effects of institutional reforms on economic growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule offers a potential avenue for understanding how specific historical conditions have influenced long-term welfare development, but these findings are not directly linked to contemporary institutional reforms.

Women played a crucial role in shaping African economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a fact often overlooked by traditional narratives focusing on male-dominated activities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women were instrumental in agricultural production, particularly in food farming. They also highlight the economic revolution driven by cocoa production in the Gold Coast, where farmers migrated to secure land for cultivation. This institutional change had profound implications for gender dynamics in African societies, as it limited women's economic agency despite their significant contributions to agricultural production. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the demand for missionary education varied based on cost-benefit calculations by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education under new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of colonial education systems, therefore, had a complex impact on African societies, sometimes reinforcing existing inequalities while also creating new opportunities. This dynamic interplay between institutional changes and gender roles underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth in Africa during these periods, highlighting that women's contributions were both essential and often underappreciated.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional theories for oversimplifying causal mechanisms by compressing different historical periods and paths. Their shared finding is that distributional conflicts provide better explanations than efficiency for core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, leading to an inadequate analysis of institutional change. For example, colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to local incentives and actions, illustrating how institutions evolved differently depending on specific contexts (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's argument that pre-industrial European institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts. Specifically, serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds are better understood through the lens of power dynamics and social struggles (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique underscores the importance of recognizing the complexity of institutional change and the role of local contexts in shaping economic outcomes.

Van Zanden and Bolt and van Zanden's work shows that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not translate into clear improvements in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This challenges optimistic interpretations of early modern European economic growth. The authors argue that economic expansion during this period was not uniformly beneficial for all segments of society, and that measuring long-term economic development is fraught with uncertainty. They point out that estimates are often based on partial data and assumptions about the links between these data and economic activities, leading to margins of error that increase as one goes further back in time (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). The authors also note that Maddison's pioneering work on measuring economic performance has induced many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development. Bolt and van Zanden argue that collaborative research is essential for improving estimates of national accounts, particularly when data are scarce or incomplete (van Zanden 1999, p.9). However, they also note that the margins of error in such estimates are often subjective, reflecting underlying assumptions about the data rather than objective measurements. This suggests that while economic development may have occurred during this period, its impact on the standard of living was not uniform and remains difficult to quantify with precision.

Pamuk's work on European economic divergence (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Peters 2004, p.19) demonstrates that long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to significant differences in productivity and development between the northwest and southern regions of Europe. This divergence was further influenced by urbanization rates, a key indicator of productivity and development in the pre-modern period. Kuznets' emphasis on structural change highlights how shifts in economic growth were driven by institutional adaptations that facilitated or hindered regional responses to demographic shocks like the Black Death. The varying trajectories of northwest and southern Europe illustrate how institutional frameworks shaped long-term economic outcomes (Pamuk 2007, p.16). These findings are complemented by Peters' work, which reveals deepening social divisions in Africa stemming from competition over land and the exclusionary processes that accompanied colonial and post-colonial agricultural policies (Peters 2004, p.19; Peters 2004, p.5).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how regions adapt to demographic and economic changes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonial powers established varying institutions based on mortality rates; extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies constrained economic development, whereas inclusive institutions in low-mortality settlements fostered growth. This institutional divergence is further illustrated by the contrasting experiences of Holland and Tuscany, where institutional flexibility enabled regions to respond effectively to economic shocks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth, as seen in the English financial revolution. This historical evidence underscores how institutions can either facilitate or hinder long-run economic development.

Institutional adaptability is crucial for regions to navigate demographic shifts and economic changes effectively. Austin (2008, p.15) highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating the dynamic nature of institutional change. This adaptive capacity is essential for fostering growth, as rigid institutions can impede a region's ability to respond to new opportunities or challenges. North (1989, p.6) further supports this by showing that efficient property rights and effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby promoting economic exchanges and growth.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes affecting development trajectories of these regions with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today.

The institutional roots of Africa's current challenges are evident in the deepening social divisions stemming from colonial and post-colonial agricultural policies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.8) shows that good institutions, which provide incentives for investment, are crucial for development. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people by highlighting the importance of effective governance and property rights.

Institutional flexibility and adaptability thus play a crucial role in determining how regions respond to economic shocks. The divergence between Holland and Tuscany underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as seen in their ability to enable or constrain regional responses to demographic and economic changes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
