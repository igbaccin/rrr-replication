The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, with significant implications for understanding historical and contemporary development trajectories. Institutions are the rules of the game that structure political, social, and economic interactions, and their influence on economic performance has been extensively debated. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions cause long-term economic growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technology play a more fundamental role. One side argues that institutional frameworks determine the incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity, thereby driving economic growth over extended periods. Conversely, others contend that while institutions matter, they are not the primary drivers of economic development; instead, exogenous factors like geography and climate shape institutional outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic performance, with profound implications for policy-making and economic theory.

The causal role of institutions in long-term growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s (Austin 2008, p.2) argument that European settlers established different colonial institutions based on mortality rates. This institutional framework significantly influenced current income per capita. Similarly, North's work emphasizes the critical role of institutions in economic growth, highlighting how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). These findings suggest that institutions shape long-run economic performance by creating an environment conducive to investment and economic activity.

The debate extends to the specific types of institutions that foster economic growth. Acemoglu et al.'s work on colonialism illustrates how extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-term development in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is complemented by North's discussion on the English financial revolution, which highlights how institutional changes, such as the creation of the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs and supported economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These studies indicate that institutions promoting secure property rights and efficient market exchanges are crucial for sustained economic development.

The role of institutions is further complicated by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective suggests that the design and effectiveness of institutions are influenced by power dynamics and the interests of politically dominant groups. Hopkins' work on African economic history demonstrates that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not always hinder progressive institution development, suggesting that local contexts can influence institutional outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This variation underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on long-term economic growth.

The causal relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is further nuanced by the interplay between institutions and other factors. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests that institutional changes are part of a broader historical process influenced by multiple factors, including geography, culture, and technology. These studies highlight that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic growth, they operate within a complex web of factors that influence their design and effectiveness.

The development of secure property rights through new financial instruments and political institutions in 17th-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the emergence of novel financial tools dramatically lowered the cost of transactions. This financial revolution reflects an enhanced security in property rights over time, which is vital for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade-off where certain rights were ceded to Parliament in exchange for revenues, illustrating how political institutions can shape economic outcomes.

This is because increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions is evident in the remarkable performance of capital markets during this period.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also note that institutions are vital; their effectiveness relies on credible commitment by the regime or sovereign to economic exchange rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This underscores that the institutional framework alone is not sufficient; it must be backed by a political system committed to enforcing these rules. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in 17th-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the intricate relationship between political stability and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. European colonizers implemented diverse institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns, leading to varying economic outcomes. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans settled permanently, institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment were established. These institutional differences persisted and had profound effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that these institutions' strength is consistent across various measures, including constraints on the executive and property rights; this consistency highlights their critical role in shaping long-term economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This nuance suggests that the institutional legacies of colonialism are complex and multifaceted, with long-term effects that go beyond simple economic measures. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for grasping how historical institutions continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes in former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to protect the interests of elites. They argue this was achieved through various government policies that restricted competition, thereby preserving their economic dominance. For example, unequal access to education and political power reinforced existing disparities, as elites could maintain control over resources and opportunities. This institutional framework ensured that wealth remained concentrated among a small group, limiting broader economic growth and development. Pamuk (2007, p.16) highlights the importance of urbanization rates in understanding economic development trajectories during this period. He notes that regions with higher urbanization rates tended to experience greater economic dynamism, as cities often acted as hubs for trade, innovation, and cultural exchange. This suggests that the institutional structures which favored elites also hindered the growth of urban centers, further stifling economic progress in these societies.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered the development of formal education systems. Frankema emphasizes the role of new social and political institutions in altering African perceptions of educational benefits (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift, driven by missionary efforts, created a demand for education at both macro and micro levels.

The economic implications of British rule extended beyond education. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This economic growth was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

They argue that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies reveals significant economic development during the colonial period (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This finding indicates that specific historical and geographical conditions had temporary effects on long-term African welfare development.

The role of women in African economic growth and development was significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and institutional changes under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming. This de-privileging of women in access to land was further entrenched by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men's roles in cash crop production. This indicates that economic outcomes were influenced by more than just ethnic composition; institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping these outcomes.

The specific mechanism through which colonial rule impacted African economies was the introduction of a dual legal system that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary laws, further marginalizing women's access to land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This codification process under colonial rule systematically disadvantaged women, limiting their economic contributions. Hopkins suggests that new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that understanding historical institutional changes is essential for designing effective policies to address contemporary economic challenges.

The broader implication here is that while colonial institutions had profound effects on African economies, particularly through the marginalization of women, these effects were not immutable.

The slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust persists to this day, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This extensive period of exploitation had a profound impact on the continent's economic trajectory. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement these findings by showing the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). They argue that mistrust, which is a direct result of the slave trades, impedes economic growth. Moreover, this mistrust operates through both internal norms and external environments. Nunn (2008, p.2) points out that centralized governments and well-developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans in areas like the Kongo kingdom (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that even within Africa, regions with stronger institutions were better able to withstand the negative effects of the slave trades. These findings imply that addressing the historical legacy of mistrust is crucial for fostering economic development.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this position, noting that technological progress requires institutional adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technology and institutions facilitates sustained economic growth over long periods. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further explains that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth; he suggests that coordination failures can be rectified through policies designed to enhance this growth, highlighting the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advances (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns, necessitating significant social adjustments for economic growth. Thus, while institutions facilitate the efficient use of new technologies, it is the continuous generation and application of useful knowledge that underpins long-run economic growth.

van Zanden (2009, p.19); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the skill premium is critical for understanding long-term economic performance. Van Zanden points out that institutions shape the skill premium by facilitating apprenticeships, which manage training costs for skilled workers including both direct expenses and forgone income during learning periods (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This mechanism lowers the cost of producing skilled laborers, thereby influencing the level of the skill premium. The guilds' role in Europe exemplifies this: while they ensured fair execution of apprenticeship contracts and promoted intergenerational skill transfer, their monopolistic tendencies also restricted entry into trades, potentially inflating wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Broadberry and Gupta build on this by showing high productivity in traded goods sector is driven by production, merchant distribution, and finance; suggesting the service sector's role in economic growth is significant. The implication is that the skill premium, shaped by these factors, can drive long-term economic performance, but this relationship is contingent on the broader institutional and economic environment.

Austin and Ogilvie argue that distributional conflicts and historical paths significantly influence economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin asserts that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For example, where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production. This suggests that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie further develops this idea by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Austin's and Ogilvie's arguments collectively underscore the importance of considering a broader range of factors beyond institutional frameworks when analyzing economic development.

The literature on economic performance and institutional factors in Europe and Africa does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth but rather focuses on specific economic trends and developments (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Simson 2019, p.2). Van Zanden notes that the relationship between economic development and changes in living standards is complex, as there was no clear correlation between urbanisation ratios and wage data during early modern Europe. This perspective aligns with Simson's analysis of East Africa, which shows how expenditure trends post-independence were influenced by various factors such as industrial policy, capital investments, military spending, and debt service payments. The role of institutions can be seen in the shifting economic priorities that occurred after colonial rule ended. For example, domestic military expenditures grew significantly due to regional instability and internal dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This institutional shift had profound implications for resource allocation within these economies. This trend is clear; it reveals the specific economic consequences of institutional changes without directly explaining long-run growth trajectories. This nuanced view of institutional impacts on economic performance highlights how complex patterns can emerge from institutional shifts without necessarily driving long-term growth in a straightforward manner; instead they reveal that institutions influence resource allocation and expenditure priorities.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented by scholars who emphasize that different institutional frameworks lead to distinct economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. and North both highlight the significance of institutional design in economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6). They argue that extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over long-term growth, hinder economic performance. Inclusive institutions foster investment and innovation by protecting property rights and ensuring rule of law.

Acemoglu et al. show that European colonies with high mortality rates were more likely to develop extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. North further demonstrates how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Together, these findings underscore the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic trajectories.

Austin critiques oversimplification of causation in 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths. He argues that colonial institutions were not uniformly extractive but adapted to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.2). The role of indigenous economic action in shaping institutional responses is crucial. This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts are key drivers of core economic institutions; efficiency considerations alone do not drive them (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Acemoglu et al. and Hopkins provide complementary insights into the impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Meanwhile, Hopkins highlights how institutional reforms informed by economic history can eliminate poverty among Africa’s bottom billion (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which institutional design alone determines economic outcomes. Austin's work shows that institutions adapted to local conditions and indigenous action (Austin 2008, p.2), while Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that equilibrium institutions often prioritize short-term gains over long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial institutions in Africa, which Hopkins uses to argue for institutional reforms, settles this disagreement by showing how specific institutional designs have directly influenced economic performance.
