The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question for understanding development trajectories across nations. At stake is whether the institutional framework can be a driver of sustained economic growth or if it merely reflects other underlying factors such as geography and culture. The live disagreement centers around two primary positions: one emphasizes the causal role of institutions in fostering economic growth, while the other posits that institutions are secondary to other determinants like population density and savings rates. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering long-term economic development.

The first stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of institutional frameworks on economic growth. North contends that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are pivotal for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on this by showing how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups, which in turn affected long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England is cited as a critical factor in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security (North 1989, p.9), highlighting the importance of institutional design in fostering economic development. This stream argues that institutions matter fundamentally because they create the necessary conditions for sustained growth by providing stability, predictability, and incentives for investment.

This institutional framework was pivotal in reducing transaction costs and establishing secure property rights, which North argues were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 was pivotal in this transformation. It facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework fostered economic growth and increased overall market exchanges as specialization and division of labor expanded. The evolution of these institutions created a more stable and predictable environment for investment, which is crucial for long-run economic development.

The political factors underpinning this economic growth were equally significant. North and Weingast emphasize that the regime’s commitment to rules governing economic exchange was critical (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credible commitment ensured that property rights were secure and enforceable, fostering a robust capital market. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which shows remarkable growth during this period. This suggests that while institutional development was crucial, it was part of a broader context of political stability and credible commitment to property rights. The downstream implication is that the specific design of institutions matters less than their ability to provide secure property rights and enforce rules consistently.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was mediated through the type of institutions established by Europeans, which varied based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In areas with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established, focusing on rapid resource transfer to the metropole rather than fostering local economic development. In regions where Europeans settled permanently, institutions were designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment.

The institutional differences established during colonial rule persisted over time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions had profound effects on long-run economic growth by shaping the economic policies and governance structures that emerged post-colonialism. This pattern suggests that the initial institutional framework set by European colonizers had lasting implications for economic development.

The relationship between colonial institutions and long-run growth is not deterministic (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) acknowledge that while institutions are crucial, they interact with other factors such as population density and prosperity, which can influence policy outcomes and economic trajectories. This interaction highlights that although colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping long-run growth, their impact was contingent on local conditions and subsequent developments.

The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have persisted, influencing current economic policies and governance structures. This persistence underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks to address contemporary economic challenges.

This indicates that institutional effectiveness is contingent on broader governance structures and historical contexts, which can either enhance or hinder economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This reconstruction shows that the persistence of poverty is shaped by specific historical conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The implication here is that understanding these historical institutional frameworks can provide valuable lessons for contemporary policy-making. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that by studying the historical impact of colonial institutions on African economies, policymakers can identify effective strategies to promote long-run economic growth. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence is necessary to assess the possibilities of future growth accurately (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for long-term economic growth as they enable efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological progress requires significant institutional changes to be effective. For instance, he notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family patterns. This suggests that societies must undergo substantial adjustments in their social structures and ideologies to accommodate new technologies. Mokyr (2005, p.49) supports this view by emphasizing the role of institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, which is essential for technological progress. He argues that without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvements may be unsustainable. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also highlights another condition: resolving growth-induced conflicts. These conflicts can arise from the introduction of new technologies and must be managed to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. This implies that while institutional adjustments are necessary for technological progress, they must also address societal conflicts to ensure sustained economic growth. The sovereign state plays a crucial role in this process by resolving such conflicts peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the key to understanding economic institutions lies in distributional conflicts. This perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and inequality as well as efficiency alone. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe reveals that institutional structures such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were primarily shaped by the need for elites to maintain their dominance over subordinates. These institutions were fundamentally designed to preserve existing power relations in addition to being efficient responses to economic conditions. This insight is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's examination of New World societies, where they demonstrate that institutional differences arose from variations in inequality and factor endowments (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In these contexts, elites used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their privileged status, thus perpetuating inequality. The historical context of each society must nuance this perspective on distributional conflicts. Ogilvie's critique of cultural theories highlights that shared beliefs can influence institutional structures, but these beliefs themselves are often products of underlying power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that while economic efficiency may play a role in shaping institutions, it is secondary to the primary driver of distributional conflicts.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis offers an incomplete explanation for Africa's economic development by oversimplifying causation through compressed historical periods. This thesis often attributes African underdevelopment to colonial institutions, but Austin (2008, p.2) shows that when European settlers were absent, colonial governments could align with indigenous interests (Austin 2008, p.15). These administrations adjusted their policies in response to local incentives and actions, which contradicts the notion of a uniform, detrimental impact. This dynamic highlights the importance of local contexts in shaping institutional outcomes.

However, while Austin (2008, p.2) points out that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) identify another significant factor: the legacy of the slave trade. They find that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact is so profound that it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, which in turn affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The persistence of this mistrust suggests that even when institutions adapt to local conditions, underlying cultural factors can still hinder long-term growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Institutions are central to understanding the trajectory of economic development. North (1989, p.6) shows that efficient property rights and their enforcement reduce transaction costs, fostering a growing economy where increasing specialization and division of labor multiply aggregate exchanges. This institutional framework supports economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and lowering costs per transaction. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The paper highlights how institutions evolve in response to local conditions, demonstrating that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptability underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The legacy of colonialism further illustrates the enduring impact of institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, establishing extractive states in some colonies while setting up rule-of-law institutions in others. This variation in institutional frameworks persists today, significantly affecting current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions shows that even when local conditions adapt, underlying institutional structures can still hinder long-term growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective highlights how shared beliefs and political power dynamics shape institutions, which in turn influence economic development.
