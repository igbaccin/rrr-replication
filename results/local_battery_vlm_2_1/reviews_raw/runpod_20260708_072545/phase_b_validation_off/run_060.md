The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established in a society directly influences its economic trajectory, with secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms being crucial for sustained growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Others contend that institutions are shaped by broader social and political contexts, including distributional conflicts and shared beliefs, which can either facilitate or hinder economic development. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic reforms aimed at fostering growth in developing nations. The crux of the matter lies in determining whether institutional frameworks can be designed to promote long-term economic prosperity or if they are merely reflections of deeper socio-political dynamics.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is often examined through historical contexts, particularly colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutional frameworks in different colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The presence of settlers who enforced rule of law and encouraged investment resulted in more prosperous outcomes compared to areas where extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.9). This perspective highlights how early institutional choices can have enduring impacts on economic development, underscoring the importance of inclusive institutions that foster growth. The historical context is crucial for understanding modern economic disparities, as it reveals how past institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth involves both their efficiency and distributional conflicts as well as shared beliefs within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations. This perspective suggests that institutional design is influenced by power dynamics and shared beliefs about legitimacy and rights, which can either support or hinder economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.2). The interplay between institutions and broader socio-political factors is complex, as seen in the case of colonial administrations that adapted their policies based on indigenous economic actions. This adaptive behavior indicates that institutions evolve in response to changing circumstances and pressures from within societies.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have been a significant factor in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The institutional legacies of colonial rule, whether extractive or inclusive, continue to shape the economic trajectories of these regions. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development. It also highlights the importance of understanding how past institutional choices influence current economic outcomes and the potential for institutional reforms to foster growth in developing nations.

The establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional development was pivotal in creating a stable financial environment that encouraged investment and economic activity. This institutional change involved trading certain rights to Parliament for revenues during fiscal crises as well as an economic shift (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further underscore that the success of these institutions lay in their ability to enforce rules governing economic exchanges effectively. They argue that the degree to which the regime was committed to these rules was crucial for sustained growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment faced challenges. North and Weingast note that while England's institutional changes were significant, France's economic performance before the French Revolution suggests that similar outcomes could be achieved under different institutional frameworks. The implication is that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-run economic growth, but the precise institutional arrangements can vary across different historical contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The divergence in institutional frameworks established by Europeans in their colonies led to different economic trajectories over the long term. In some cases, such as the United States and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes in former colonies. This institutional legacy has profound implications for understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies.

The mechanisms through which these institutions influenced economic outcomes are multifaceted. This suggests that the initial conditions set by European colonization strategies had enduring effects on institutional development and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) clarify that the interaction between these colonial institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the importance of both the type of institution established and the historical context in which these institutions operated. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes, as evidenced by the persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies today.

The institutional impacts of slavery and inequality highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites. These institutions restricted competition, thereby preserving the status quo (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional bias towards elites was underpinned by a combination of high land abundance relative to labor and extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these institutional arrangements has had profound implications for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that Africa's slave trades significantly hindered economic progress over several centuries (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context is crucial because it shaped immediate economic outcomes as well as the long-term trajectory of development in these regions. The impact of slavery on trust within societies further underscores this dynamic: Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to affect social and economic interactions today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This enduring legacy of mistrust illustrates how historical inequalities can persist through institutional channels, shaping contemporary economic outcomes.

The expansion of useful knowledge is central to long-run economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) jointly assert. This perspective shifts focus from institutional frameworks to technological advancements, suggesting that the development of new ideas and technologies drives economic progress more fundamentally than any particular set of rules or structures. The growth of knowledge facilitates a process where technological breakthroughs lead to increased productivity, which in turn fuels economic expansion. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights how the proliferation of scientific journals and societies reduced access costs to knowledge, thereby accelerating technological progress. This mechanism underscores that intellectual advancements are essential for sustained growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) complements this by noting that economic growth requires technological changes as well as institutional adjustments to manage the social conflicts arising from these innovations. While Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize the primacy of knowledge, Kuznets' work underscores the need for political stability. This qualification is crucial because it shows that while institutions may not be the primary driver, they play a critical role in managing the societal disruptions caused by technological progress. Thus, the interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptability is vital for long-term economic growth.

Institutional reforms are crucial for Africa's economic development and poverty alleviation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa has gained traction among development specialists precisely because it emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This focus on institutional quality aligns with broader findings about the role of political stability in managing societal disruptions caused by technological progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that the benefits of formal education, a key component of human capital development, were influenced by new social and political institutions implemented during colonial rule. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that while institutions matter, the effectiveness of these reforms depends on local context and historical legacies (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The complex interplay between institutional quality and local conditions underscores the need for tailored approaches to economic development in Africa.

The early twentieth-century per capita income levels in Anglophone African economies and the pre-1800 divergence between Europe and Asia, both of which are central to understanding economic performance, share an emphasis on institutions as key drivers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that substantial economic data from Africa's colonial era reveals higher levels of per capita income than previously thought. The service sector played a pivotal role in early modern European growth, driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This points to an institutional mechanism where effective market integration and financial services facilitated economic development. The benefits of this development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers experiencing greater surpluses over basic food needs than unskilled laborers. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that patterns of growing and shrinking economies were tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This underscores the importance of considering multiple institutional dimensions when examining economic performance.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) examine the 'reversal of fortune' thesis in African poverty, focusing on economic contexts that often do not directly address institutions' role. Austin highlights that the thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The colonial administration's institutional prescriptions sometimes responded to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests a dynamic interplay between local agency and colonial governance, which could have shaped long-term economic outcomes in African colonies beyond structural impediments alone.

Their findings suggest that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This challenges the notion that African economies were inevitably constrained by long-term structural factors, suggesting instead that specific historical and geographical conditions may have had temporary effects on long-term welfare development. The dynamic interplay between local economic action and colonial governance, as well as the potential for temporary rather than structural impediments, implies that African economies might have experienced different growth trajectories under varied institutional arrangements.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the distributional conflicts that shaped pre-industrial European economies. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s assertion that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from both colonial and pre-industrial contexts shows that institutional arrangements are shaped by power dynamics and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes. The reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism as well as geographic factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
