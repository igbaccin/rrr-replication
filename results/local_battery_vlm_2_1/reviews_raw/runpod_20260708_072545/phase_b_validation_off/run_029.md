The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. The stakes are high intellectually because understanding this relationship can help explain why some societies prosper while others stagnate. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, and those who propose alternative explanations such as cultural factors or geographical determinants. While some scholars emphasize the causal role of extractive versus inclusive institutions in determining economic outcomes, others highlight the importance of historical paths and the complexity of institutional evolution. Furthermore, there is a debate about whether distributional conflicts or efficiency considerations are more crucial in shaping core economic institutions. This review synthesizes these perspectives to illuminate the multifaceted ways in which institutions influence long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argue that institutions play a crucial role in economic development. Acemoglu et al. assert that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates, which significantly impacted the long-term economic trajectories of their colonies. They identify two extremes: settler colonies like the United States, where rule-of-law-enforcing institutions encouraged investment, and extractive states like the Congo, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. North contends that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. He underscores that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for a thriving economy. These arguments highlight how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic progress, depending on their design and implementation.

The historical paths leading to these institutions matter. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin critiques the institutionalist approach for its limitations in testing quantitatively and explaining why favourable institutions were adopted in some countries but not others. He notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, challenging the notion that extractive institutions were uniformly imposed. Acemoglu et al. emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies. They argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was tied to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. These perspectives underscore the importance of historical context in shaping institutional outcomes, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of these paths is necessary for comprehensive analysis.

The role of distributional conflicts versus efficiency considerations in shaping core economic institutions is another critical area of debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) propose different explanations for the development of these institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. She suggests that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. Acemoglu et al. highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. They note that these institutions do not encourage economic development because they are shaped by the interests of those in power, who may fear losing their political influence with institutional change. This debate underscores the complexity of institutional design and the need to consider both distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations when analyzing economic outcomes.

The implications for policy and future research are significant. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), and North (1989, p.9) suggest that understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. Hopkins argues that ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, while heterogeneous ones can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. He emphasizes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. North highlights how political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms lead to falling transaction costs and economic growth. These arguments suggest that policymakers should focus on creating and maintaining institutions that support secure property rights and effective enforcement, as these are fundamental for long-term economic prosperity.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights. This institutional evolution involved economic rules as well as the political factors governing their enforcement and change (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by noting that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for lowering transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and economic growth. The emergence of these institutions underscores how political stability and credible commitments can drive long-term economic prosperity. This suggests that while secure property rights and effective enforcement are critical, their impact on economic growth may be contingent on other factors.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that differences in mortality rates led to varied colonization strategies. In regions with high mortality, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. For example, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the type of institution established during colonial rule had a lasting impact on economic outcomes, with extractive institutions hindering development.

The interaction between these factors and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores that the institutions established by colonial powers were not uniform but varied based on specific conditions, with significant implications for economic development. This implies that the persistence of extractive institutions can be attributed to the interests of those in power, who may resist institutional changes that could threaten their position.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trades in Africa directly hindered long-run economic development through their lasting cultural impacts. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists to this day and significantly impedes economic growth. This mistrust is not merely a product of external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others; instead, it is deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, far exceeds the period of colonial rule. Moreover, Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that the Kongo kingdom's centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans, illustrating how pre-colonial institutions could support economic activity. However, this also underscores that the disruptive impact of the slave trades was not uniform across Africa; some regions were better positioned to mitigate its effects than others. The adverse cultural legacies of the slave trades, therefore, present a significant barrier to economic development in many parts of Africa today.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that Africa's economic trajectory has been significantly shaped by its institutional landscape, which is a product of both internal dynamics and external influences. Frankema and van Waijenburg support this view by emphasizing the need to understand the long-term determinants of African economic development. They note that pre-1950 GDP estimates are lacking for most African countries, making it challenging to assess the persistence of poverty over time (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the importance of exploring both historical and contemporary factors in understanding Africa's economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates that institutional quality is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but rather by a complex interplay of factors, including governance structures and policy frameworks (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This highlights the need for a deeper understanding of how specific historical conditions have shaped Africa's long-term economic trajectory.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) show that colonial policies significantly influenced the development of formal education systems in Africa. British rule fostered more effective educational institutions than other colonial powers due to its emphasis on missionary activities and administrative structures, which created a demand for literacy among African populations as they navigated new economic opportunities. The implementation of these new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutional changes were not uniform across Africa; rather, they varied according to local cost-benefit analyses by both political leaders and families (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This variation suggests that while British rule generally promoted education more effectively than other colonial systems, its impact was contingent on specific historical conditions. While their focus is on Latin America, the underlying mechanisms they describe, such as how institutions evolve to advantage elites, offer a parallel for understanding African contexts where colonial policies exacerbated or mitigated existing inequalities. The nuanced relationship between colonial rule and educational development in Africa highlights the need for a more granular analysis of institutional legacies, which can inform contemporary policy efforts aimed at addressing long-standing economic disparities.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) together argue that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by complex interactions between political actors and economic structures. This dynamic suggests that institutions evolved not merely from top-down impositions but through a process of negotiation and adaptation. These institutions were not just efficient responses to economic needs but were also deeply influenced by power struggles and social dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe were more about managing conflicts than achieving efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions operate, as it reveals how institutional outcomes can be shaped by underlying power dynamics. The nuanced interaction between economic structures and political actors in shaping institutions highlights a critical aspect: while institutions are often seen as drivers of long-term growth, their evolution is deeply intertwined with historical and contextual factors.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by defining the rules that govern economic interactions and allocate resources (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is underscored by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how extractive institutions established during colonial times significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The influence of institutions extends beyond mere economic outcomes to encompass broader societal structures, as seen in the historical context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts were a primary driver behind core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This highlights how institutional arrangements are often shaped by underlying power dynamics and political contexts, which in turn influence long-term growth trajectories.

The role of institutions is further elucidated through the lens of colonial policies and their lasting effects. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers implemented different colonization strategies depending on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, have had enduring impacts on economic development in former colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This interaction between institutions and local contexts underscores how institutions are not static but evolve in response to historical and contextual factors.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth involve transaction costs. North argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on how extractive institutions can hinder long-term development by maximizing rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from colonial policies and the evolution of financial instruments in Europe illustrates that institutional arrangements directly affect economic outcomes by altering transaction costs and resource allocation mechanisms.

The debate on institutions and growth highlights a fundamental agreement: institutions matter. North shows how secure property rights and effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). Yet Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions often prevail due to the interests of politically powerful groups, which can impede economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from colonial policies and financial reforms in Europe shows that inclusive institutions promote growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing resource allocation efficiency, while extractive institutions hinder it by maximizing rents for powerful groups.
