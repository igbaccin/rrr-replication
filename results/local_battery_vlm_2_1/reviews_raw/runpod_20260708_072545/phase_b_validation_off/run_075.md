The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has spurred significant debate among economists and historians alike. The stakes are high because understanding the institutional determinants of economic growth could guide policymakers in fostering development and addressing persistent poverty. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography, or if they emerge endogenously through political processes driven by powerful groups. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over long-term growth, are a legacy of colonial policies influenced by mortality rates and settler motivations (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Others contend that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative. This debate highlights the importance of understanding how institutions evolve and their impact on economic outcomes.

The political economy perspective offers insights into how institutions are shaped by powerful groups seeking to maximize rents. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to benefit politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

The role of colonialism in shaping institutional outcomes is a critical aspect of this debate. Acemoglu et al. emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies, which in turn shaped the institutions of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective suggests that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions. Austin argues that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that local factors also influenced institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This interplay between exogenous and endogenous factors underscores the complexity of institutional evolution.

The debate extends to the specific mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth. This aligns with Ogilvie's call for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be transformed from mere fashion accessories into indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This historical context illustrates how the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework supported market exchanges and underpinned the development of a robust capital market in England. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering the broader political and economic context when evaluating the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The evolution of financial institutions in England thus serves as a critical case study in understanding how secure property rights, enforced through effective political institutions, can drive economic development.

In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole and foster local investment and growth. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted over time and had lasting impacts on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these extractive institutions is further explained by Acemoglu et al., who note that politically powerful groups often resist institutional changes that could threaten their control or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic highlights a specific mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth: the entrenchment of extractive practices that prioritized short-term resource extraction over sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. trace this pattern in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, linking it to industrialization opportunities and institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al.'s findings are nuanced by their acknowledgement that institutional changes alone do not fully capture the broader political and economic context, suggesting other factors may also play a role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions, as highlighted by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These authors argue that the persistence of pre-industrial Europe's core economic structures such as serfdom and guilds can be better understood through distributional conflicts and efficiency. The specific mechanism through which this occurs is the entrenchment of institutions that serve the interests of powerful groups, even if these institutions are not economically efficient.

This perspective challenges the traditional view that economic institutions are shaped solely by efficiency considerations. It suggests that distributional conflicts among different social groups create institutions that reflect power dynamics and purely economic logic.

Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, where greater inequality led to institutional frameworks that advantaged elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This process restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The downstream implication is that distributional conflicts can lead to institutions that perpetuate inequality by limiting economic opportunities for less powerful groups.

Ogilvie acknowledges that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they do not operate in isolation from other factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuance suggests that a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth requires considering multiple interacting factors.

In sub-Saharan Africa, British colonial rule was notably more conducive to developing formal education than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding is crucial for understanding how different colonial institutions shaped local development trajectories. African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that influenced demand for missionary education, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education as new social and political institutions were implemented. These new institutions changed the economic landscape by creating opportunities for export production tied to foreign trade (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores that while British colonial rule had a positive impact on formal education, it did not universally lead to economic development across all African colonies.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) identify a culture of mistrust within Africa fostered by the continent's slave trades. The slave trade extracted labor and engendered persistent social norms of mistrust among individuals and groups. This mistrust, in turn, has had profound implications for economic development by impeding cooperation and collective action. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that this mistrust is rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. This period of sustained exploitation far outlasted the roughly 75 years of official colonial rule, indicating a deep-seated impact on African societies. A specific mechanism through which this mistrust operates is by undermining the development of strong institutions. For instance, when communities lack trust in one another, they are less likely to invest in public goods or collaborate on large-scale projects, both of which are crucial for economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional weakness can be traced back to the slave trade's disruption of traditional social structures and governance systems. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that not all African societies were equally affected; those with centralized governments and well-developed markets, like the Kongo kingdom, were better positioned to engage in foreign trade despite the challenges posed by the slave trade. The legacy of mistrust continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run development.

Skill premiums serve as predictive indicators of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This is because the skill premium reflects the costs and risks associated with investing in human capital. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) identifies several factors that determine the skill premium, including training costs, institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades, interest rates, and the expected duration of higher earnings (van Zanden 2009, p.3). Institutions play a crucial role in mitigating some of these risks; for instance, guilds in western Europe helped facilitate the intergenerational transfer of skills by guaranteeing fair execution of apprentice contracts. Guilds have also been criticized for restricting entry into trades and maintaining above-market pay levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This tension highlights how institutions can both foster and hinder economic growth through their influence on skill premiums. These errors suggest a degree of objectivity but are based on subjective estimates of underlying data, complicating efforts to quantify long-term economic development. This underscores how the institutional context shapes not only the skill premium but also the very measurement of economic performance.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasize that technological advancements necessitate institutional adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets's work highlights the necessity of ideological adjustments alongside technological progress to sustain modern economic growth. He notes that epochal innovations, such as those in human knowledge, are pivotal for sustained growth over extended periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective underscores how institutions facilitate the absorption and application of new technologies, ensuring that economic gains are integrated into long-term development trajectories. Mokyr supplements this view by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in economic growth. He points out that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advancements; otherwise, economic improvements may be unsustainable due to coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating technological change and maintaining the stability and sustainability of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) acknowledges that these adjustments must resolve conflicts without excessive costs or long-term political weakening.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Austin (2008, p.2) (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Austin 2008, p.2) both argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach involves understanding how historical patterns of economic development have influenced current institutions, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment and essential for development. One specific mechanism through which this occurs is by examining the interplay between colonial policies and indigenous economic actions. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, reflecting a nuanced relationship between external policies and local economic interests.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates that the new economic history of Africa has gained prominence because it highlights the role of institutions in development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This emphasis on 'good' institutions is pivotal as they provide the necessary incentives for investment, which is fundamental for economic growth. Englebert's conclusion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments adds a layer of complexity to this narrative (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that while institutions are critical, other factors such as governance and policy implementation also play significant roles in shaping economic outcomes. The implication is that institutional reforms must be tailored to address specific contextual challenges, including the diversity within African societies, rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all approach.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities within societies. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to divergent economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North's argument that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England, for example, significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated long-term capital markets. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often drive the establishment of core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions, shaped by power dynamics, can either promote or hinder economic development depending on how they allocate resources and enforce property rights. Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity is evident in colonial administrations that adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic actions, showing that institutions evolve in response to local dynamics.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the interaction between colonial policies and local conditions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, highlighting how extractive institutions can persist and affect economic outcomes over centuries. Hopkins argues that Africa's economic record is linked to poor institutions, which hinder investment incentives essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments, suggesting that governance and policy implementation are also critical factors in shaping economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Ogilvie's argument that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts is supported by Austin’s analysis of colonial administrations that adjusted their policies based on local incentives. This shows that institutions evolve in response to local dynamics.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement comes from Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of European colonization strategies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These analyses show that institutional differences, particularly the establishment of extractive states versus inclusive institutions, are key determinants of long-run economic growth. Austin's work on colonial administrations and their responses to indigenous actions provides further evidence that institutions evolve in response to local dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This historical evidence underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over the long run, highlighting the need for tailored institutional reforms that address specific contextual challenges.
