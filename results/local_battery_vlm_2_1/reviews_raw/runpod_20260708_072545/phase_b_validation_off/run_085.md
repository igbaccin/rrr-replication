The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it challenges the primacy of traditional factors such as population and savings. At stake is whether institutional frameworks can transcend geographic determinants to drive sustained economic progress. The live disagreement centers on how institutions interact with other variables to influence growth: some argue that institutions are the primary drivers, while others posit that they operate through more indirect mechanisms or are themselves shaped by deeper antecedents.

One side of this debate emphasizes the direct causal role of institutions in economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) both show that institutions can significantly affect long-run economic growth by influencing transaction costs and the security of property rights. This perspective highlights how institutional frameworks can foster an environment conducive to investment and economic development by providing secure property rights.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional development was critical for both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. These institutional changes involved economic rules and their enforcement and adaptability, supporting a regime dedicated to maintaining them (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further argue that this commitment was evident in the remarkable success observed in capital markets, indicating the broader impact of institutional changes on economic development. This perspective underscores how secure property rights, facilitated by effective institutional frameworks, can drive long-term economic growth by fostering a stable environment for investment and market exchanges.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. posit that European colonists established different institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns; these extractive states were designed to maximize rents for the colonizers while fostering long-term economic development was not a priority (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, in colonies with high mortality rates like the Congo or Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive institutions to rapidly transfer resources back to their home countries. These extractive institutions persisted even after colonial rule ended, shaping the long-run economic trajectories of these regions by hindering institutional development and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The persistence of these institutions highlights a critical mechanism: the entrenchment of power structures that resist change. This dynamic is evident in the reluctance of colonial powers to implement institutions that would foster private property rights or broader economic development, as these changes could threaten their political dominance. The legacy of colonial rule involves both initial institutional frameworks and enduring power dynamics that influence economic policies and outcomes.

Distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe more than efficiency considerations did (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the power dynamics between different social groups. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of powerful groups, even if they were not efficient from a broader economic perspective. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the power dynamics between different social groups and the ability of elites to maintain their influence through institutional arrangements that favoured them. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, which were maintained despite their inefficiencies because they served the interests of powerful groups. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that the persistence of guilds can be attributed to the ability of guild members to maintain their monopoly power through institutional arrangements that restricted competition (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The unequal distribution of wealth and political power in New World societies led to the development of institutions that favoured elites and restricted competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the distributional conflicts that shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and their implications for long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden and Bolt and van Zanden (2009, p.19; 2014, p.3) examine economic performance in Europe without directly addressing institutions' role in long-term growth. These authors focus on the skill premium as a predictor of economic success and the importance of collaborative research to advance economic measurement, respectively. Van Zanden highlights that apprenticeship contracts, including those managed by guilds, played a critical role in developing human capital, but these institutions were not without controversy. Guilds were instrumental in transferring skills across generations, addressing issues like fair execution and training costs of the apprentice contract. They also faced criticism for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This duality suggests that while guilds facilitated skill development, their cartel-like behavior could stifle competition and economic dynamism. Bolt and van Zanden underscore the importance of collaborative research in refining economic performance metrics, which is essential for understanding long-term growth patterns. They note that Maddison's pioneering work, though subject to margins of error, stimulated further research and improved our understanding of historical economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The implication is that while institutions like guilds shaped skill development, their overall impact on economic performance was complex and multifaceted. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) adds another layer by showing that economic development did not necessarily translate into higher living standards in Europe between 1500 and 1800.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes in Europe that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of the continent by 1450. The institutional flexibility of regions like Holland enabled them to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, fostering urbanisation and commercialisation (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This phenomenon is mirrored in Kuznets' analysis of structural change, where shifts in productivity and economic development are marked by significant increases in urbanisation rates (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The institutional framework in Holland allowed for the flourishing of rural manufacturing under weak monopolies, contrasting with regions like Tuscany. This divergence highlights how institutional settings shaped economic trajectories following the Black Death. The interplay between these factors underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes post-Black Death.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades significantly impaired long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective diverges from institutionalist theories by emphasizing the adverse effects of these trades over other factors. The slave trades disrupted social and political structures, which in turn impeded the development of productive institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that the legacy of these disruptions can still be seen today (Austin 2008, p.2). These disruptions were so profound that they left a lasting imprint on African economies, making it difficult for them to develop robust economic institutions later on. The slave trades depleted human capital and undermined the potential for indigenous commodity production and trade; colonial governments often had little incentive to promote such activities when local labor was scarce (Austin 2008, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further suggest that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty in Africa, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of historical economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The long-term effects of these trades are evident in the current state of African economies.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education development more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This institutional framework influenced educational outcomes by altering the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule changed cost-benefit calculations, making formal education more attractive. This does not imply uniform benefits across all regions; Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that export production patterns varied widely due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while British rule may have been more conducive to education development, the actual outcomes were contingent on a multitude of factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is instrumental in fostering economic development. This perspective underscores the significance of technological advancements; it is not merely the accumulation of capital or labor, but rather the continuous generation of new ideas and innovations that propels economies forward. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights how institutional progress complements technological advancements, ensuring their sustainability and preventing rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that the success of modern technology is contingent upon its compatibility with prevailing social structures. Specifically, he points out that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to align with new production sectors and resolve growth-induced conflicts without undermining societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements are fundamental, their effective integration into society requires supportive institutions, which can mitigate potential disruptions and ensure long-term economic stability.

Institutions play an indispensable role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating and enforcing property rights that facilitate investment and market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonists established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with extractive states emerging where settlers faced high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework directly influenced current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by demonstrating how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduces transaction costs, fostering an environment conducive to long-term capital markets. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further highlights that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts as well as economic needs, which can lead to the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by their interaction with technological progress. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established by European colonists significantly affected the long-run development of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This interaction between institutions and technology is crucial because it shows how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or impede the integration of new technologies into society. Austin (2008, p.2) points out that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political actions, which could lead to higher production equilibriums (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability of institutions is essential for aligning technological advancements with societal needs, ensuring that new production sectors can emerge without disrupting social cohesion. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is vital for economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The quality of public goods provided by African governments, for instance, shows that effective institutions can overcome ethnic fragmentation and promote progressive policies. This shared finding underscores the critical role of institutions in fostering long-term economic stability and growth.

Institutional continuity is essential for understanding how historical legacies shape current economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies has significantly affected their income per capita, illustrating the long-term impact of institutional choices (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.9) shows that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. The emergence of these institutions reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges, which are fundamental for a growing economy (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the reversal of fortune thesis overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution, emphasizing the need to consider different historical periods and paths. This perspective is important because it shows how institutions can adapt over time in response to economic and political changes. The shared finding across these sources is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth by creating a stable framework for investment and market exchanges (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether the role of institutions should be evaluated through their efficiency or their distributional impact. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that these institutions are not merely efficient responses to economic needs (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by European colonists, showing how institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of income per capita across different regions, which shows the enduring impact of extractive institutions established during colonial times. This measurement type reveals that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists have had a lasting effect on economic outcomes while long-run growth was not a priority (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
