The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in the study of development economics, as it addresses whether and how formal rules and informal norms shape prosperity over time. At stake intellectually is understanding why some nations thrive while others stagnate. The live disagreement centers on the extent to which institutional frameworks drive economic outcomes. One position argues that institutions are paramount, shaping economic growth through their impact on property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability. Another position contends that institutions are secondary, with geographic factors or cultural beliefs being more decisive in determining long-run economic performance. A third perspective posits that distributional conflicts drive the establishment of core economic institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) both emphasize the critical role of secure property rights. Acemoglu et al. highlight how European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, with some creating extractive states that hindered long-term growth. North discusses the English financial revolution as a pivotal moment where institutions reduced transaction costs and secured property rights, thereby fostering economic growth. This shared focus on secure property rights underscores the idea that institutions matter significantly in shaping economic outcomes by providing the necessary framework for investment and development.

Acemoglu et al. (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) and Ogilvie (2007, p.3), however, both caution against overemphasizing efficiency in explaining institutional design. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize rent extraction over long-term growth, while Ogilvie emphasizes the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions. This perspective suggests that institutions can be influenced by power dynamics and political interests.

Acemoglu et al. (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.15) both explore the complexities of institutional change over time. Acemoglu et al. discuss how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions evolve based on various factors including local pressures. This dynamic view of institutions suggests that they can change over time, influenced by both external and internal forces.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and North (1989, p.6) both underscore the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins discusses how 'good' institutions are crucial for creating an environment conducive to investment, while North argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement lead to falling transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This shared emphasis on institutions as drivers of investment highlights their role in fostering long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, involving secure property rights, power dynamics, and evolutionary processes. Institutions matter significantly in providing the framework for investment and development, but their design and impact can be influenced by a range of factors beyond mere efficiency considerations.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges by providing a stable environment where property rights could be effectively enforced. The institutional changes in England were about establishing rules for economic exchange and creating a regime committed to enforcing these rules, ensuring that the sovereign was bound by them. This credible commitment underpins long-term economic stability and growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic development varies across different contexts. North and Weingast's analysis suggests that while institutional changes in England were revolutionary, similar bureaucratic structures in France did not hinder economic growth to the same extent. This indicates that the effectiveness of institutions depends on how they are enforced and adapted within specific historical and political contexts (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex; while secure property rights and credible commitments are crucial, their impact is mediated by broader socio-political factors.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established extractive states in regions with high mortality rates, focusing on rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al 2001). The legacy of these extractive institutions persists, as they continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial institutions have long-lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of historical policies on contemporary economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner argue that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg further show that real wage divergence in British Africa is tied to these economic conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), a pattern Broadberry and Gardner also observe as growth in export production varied between countries owing to factors like transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the economic performance of African economies was influenced by both local endowments and institutional frameworks. Simson's examination of post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa reveals that military spending and debt service payments significantly displaced capital investments (Simson 2019, p.2), highlighting how political factors could redirect resources away from productive uses. This pattern underscores the complex interplay between economic conditions and political institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in African economies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) jointly argue that the economic contributions of African women have been historically significant. Women were central to food farming in pre-colonial Africa, although they were often relegated to marginal lands under colonial rule. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) detail how women's access to land was restricted through customary law codified during the colonial period (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This limitation on land ownership hindered women’s ability to participate fully in cash crop farming, despite their crucial role in food production. The economic revolution propelled by cocoa farming in the Gold Coast illustrates that women played a pivotal part in agricultural development, even if this contribution was not always recognized. Hopkins notes that the quality of public goods and institutions is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that while colonial rule may have imposed constraints on women's economic contributions, the broader institutional framework also played a significant role in shaping these dynamics. The legacy of colonial governance thus influenced both the economic landscape and the gendered division of labor, with enduring implications for long-run economic growth in Africa.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that colonial powers influenced educational development in Africa differently, with British rule being more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers. This influence was not uniform across different colonies; instead it varied according to the political and social institutions implemented by the colonizers. For instance, African families' perceived benefits of formal education were tied to these new institutions, which altered their cost-benefit calculations (Frankema 2012, p.15). These differentials in educational development highlight how colonial rule shaped long-run economic growth through its impact on human capital formation. The presence or absence of secure property rights, the prevalence of corruption, and the structures of the financial sector were all influenced by these institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This variation in institutional frameworks across colonies underscores the enduring legacy of colonial governance on economic outcomes.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that Africa's slave trades have had a direct adverse effect on economic development. The slave trade in Africa led to the creation of a culture of mistrust within African societies, which continues to impact economic outcomes today. This mistrust is partly internalised by individuals and partly shaped by their external environment, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade's impact on trust is significant because trust is a crucial factor in economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. This mistrust has persisted over centuries, influencing Africa's economic trajectory long after the end of the slave trades. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates how pre-existing social structures and institutions, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, were more conducive to trade and economic development than smaller, less organised societies. The adverse effects of the slave trade often overshadowed these positive institutional factors, leading to a broader pattern of underdevelopment across Africa. The legacy of mistrust and weakened institutions has had enduring implications for Africa's economic growth, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to more recent events or colonialism.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was significant before 1800, driven by merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. This region's high productivity in the traded goods sector arose through efficient merchant networks and financial systems, which facilitated trade and investment. These factors contributed to the uneven spread of early modern development, where skilled labourers benefited more than unskilled ones. Pamuk (2007, p.16) complements this view by highlighting the role of structural changes initiated by events such as the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. These long-term structural changes, including institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, enabled rapid responses to new economic opportunities. Van Zanden qualifies this perspective by noting that economic development did not always translate into improved living standards across Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). The shift in economic focus to North Sea bordering countries like Holland and England after 1600 underscores the complex interplay between economic development, institutional changes, and living standards during this period.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue together that technological advancements are fundamental in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective posits that institutional frameworks are secondary to technological progress; they must adapt to facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that modern technology necessitates significant adjustments in societal institutions and ideologies, which can be achieved through epochal innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These breakthroughs alter production sectors and spread globally, driving sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) builds on this by highlighting the role of coordination failures in technological progress; without complementary institutional advancements, economic improvements become unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while institutions are crucial for managing the social impacts of technological progress, their role is supportive in driving economic development.

Austin and Ogilvie argue that institutional theories in economic history oversimplify causal relationships (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths. This compression can lead to an incomplete understanding of how institutions influence long-run economic growth, as it does not account for the varied responses of colonial administrations to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-based explanations for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation. For instance, serfdom and guilds are better understood through the lens of power dynamics rather than as efficient responses to economic conditions. Austin's critique underscores how simplistic institutional theories fail to capture the complexity of historical contexts, particularly when colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie extends this critique by highlighting the role of shared beliefs and cultural factors in shaping institutions, which are often overlooked in efficiency-focused explanations. This points to a broader implication: institutional theories must incorporate a more nuanced understanding of historical specificities and power dynamics to accurately explain long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is extensively documented by several scholars who emphasize different aspects of institutional influence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutions, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. These institutional differences significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies. This perspective is supported by North's work (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), which highlights how shared beliefs influence institutional structures and economic outcomes. The Bank of England's creation, for instance, led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights that are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical example underscores the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic development.

Austin (2008, p.15) extends this by noting how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local incentives, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. Austin's argument aligns with Ogilvie's critique that distributional conflicts often drive core economic institutions. This perspective is crucial for understanding why certain institutions persist despite their negative impact on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). For instance, colonial administrations might have encouraged indigenous commodity production when it aligned with their fiscal interests, even if these policies did not maximize long-term economic development.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further emphasized by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This aligns with North's view on the role of transaction costs, which are influenced by political institutions specifying efficient property rights and their enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). Hopkins' work supports this by demonstrating that good institutions provide incentives for investment, essential for development in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8), thus linking institutional quality to economic outcomes. The new economic history of Africa provides evidence that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor public goods provision or dysfunctional societies. This underscores the complex interplay between historical context and institutional effectiveness.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether distributional conflicts or efficiency considerations are the primary drivers of institutional design. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are more influential, as shown by the persistence of extractive institutions despite their inefficiency for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This view is countered by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate through comparative analysis of former colonies that differences in European colonization strategies and resulting institutional frameworks have significantly affected current income levels. The historical evidence of varying colonial policies and their long-term economic impacts settles this disagreement, showing that both efficiency considerations and distributional conflicts shape institutions but the specific outcomes depend on local incentives and historical contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15).
