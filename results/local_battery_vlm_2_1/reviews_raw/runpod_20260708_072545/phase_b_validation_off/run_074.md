The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history, as it addresses whether and how the rules, norms, and organizations that govern societies can drive sustained prosperity. At stake intellectually is understanding the mechanisms through which economies develop over centuries and what factors enable or hinder this process. Institutions matter because they shape incentives for investment, innovation, and productive activity. They are crucial to providing a stable framework within which economic actors can operate with confidence in the future. One live disagreement concerns whether institutions influence growth directly by fostering secure property rights and efficient markets, or indirectly through their impact on political stability and distributional conflicts. A related question is how institutions themselves evolve and change over time, driven by either exogenous factors like culture or endogenous processes such as political competition and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established in colonies during European settlement significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They contend that extractive institutions, which concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, hinder economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. In contrast, inclusive institutions, which distribute political power more broadly and protect property rights, foster economic growth by encouraging widespread participation in the economy. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He argues that the institutionalist approach emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, but it struggles to explain why some countries adopted favorable institutions while others did not. This highlights the complexity of institutional change and the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another critical factor. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions were shaped by struggles over wealth and power, rather than simply by considerations of economic efficiency. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how political and social dynamics influence institutional development.

North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). He contends that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering a dynamic economy. North and Weingast emphasize the role of credible commitments by political actors as essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They argue that when regimes or sovereigns are bound by rules governing economic exchange, it creates a stable environment conducive to investment and innovation. This perspective highlights how institutions can foster long-term economic development by providing a predictable framework within which economic actors can operate. Hopkins builds on this idea by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). He contends that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shape long-term economic outcomes.

This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, thereby fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this analysis by emphasizing how equilibrium institutions may be extractive rather than growth-promoting; these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that maximize their rents rather than long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic implies that the institutional legacy of colonialism has lasting effects on economic outcomes, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) qualify this by showing that these institutional impacts are robust across various measures, including constraints on the executive and property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The long-term economic effects of institutions are deeply rooted in the broader institutional framework they established.

This framework is further elucidated by North and Weingast (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), who propose that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the evolution of these institutions was crucial for establishing a framework where property rights could be securely enforced, which in turn facilitated long-term economic development. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors, such as technological advancements or resource endowments. The mechanism through which this institutional framework operates is by ensuring credible commitments from the state, which reduces uncertainty and encourages investment. This suggests that while secure property rights are important, the broader social and political context in which these institutions operate also plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) calls for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions can be incorporated into long-term research programs, emphasizing the need to move beyond superficial analyses and delve deeper into the complexities of institutional structures.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that colonial institutions in the New World shaped long-run economic growth by reinforcing inequality. They suggest that societies with greater initial inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and stifling economic dynamism. This dynamic is evident in how factor endowments influenced institutional development: regions with abundant land and natural resources relative to labor often saw the emergence of highly unequal wealth distributions from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These inequalities were perpetuated through institutions that favored elites, such as those governing land ownership and political power. This suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is complex and contingent on various factors, including local endowments and political conditions. The interplay between colonial institutions and inequality thus offers a nuanced perspective on how institutional frameworks can both constrain and facilitate economic development over extended periods.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight that the contributions of women to economic growth in Africa have been significant, yet often overlooked. The new economic history of Africa informs us that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, essential for development. This is evident in how patriarchal alliances and colonial legal systems reinforced gender inequalities, thereby constraining economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.18) points out that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but also by the effectiveness of institutions, which can uphold the rule of law despite heterogeneity. The role of women in African economic development thus offers a nuanced perspective on how institutional frameworks can both constrain and facilitate long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (alongside van Zanden (1999, p.1)) highlight that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800. The service sector's contribution to economic expansion during this era underscores a critical mechanism: the uneven distribution of benefits from economic growth. High productivity in traded goods sectors led to increased living standards for skilled labourers, while unskilled workers saw lesser gains, a pattern that persisted through changes in relative prices and wages (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This dynamic suggests that institutional frameworks supporting merchant activities and financial services were instrumental in shaping economic outcomes. Van Zanden's analysis (van Zanden 1999, p.1) shows that economic development did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living across Europe. This regional specificity highlights how institutions can both facilitate and constrain growth by shaping local economic conditions.

Technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the shift to modern technology demands significant changes in societal structures, such as family patterns and rural lifestyles. This transformation involves profound cultural shifts as well as economic ones. Institutions play a pivotal role in mediating these changes, ensuring that technological progress integrates smoothly into society without causing excessive social disruptions. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the primary driver of economic growth. He points out that institutional progress is essential to complement technological advancements. Without such coordination, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited and could even be reversed due to rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores how economic growth is not just a matter of innovation but also of effective institutional frameworks that can manage and sustain these innovations over time.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) both challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that African poverty is neither a persistent historical phenomenon nor solely attributable to structural impediments. Both scholars argue that this thesis oversimplifies causation, compressing different historical periods into one narrative. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) address this by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies from 1880 to 1965, revealing that economic development was influenced by a complex interplay of factors including indigenous economic action and colonial policy responses (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Austin further illustrates this complexity by noting that in the absence of European settlers competing for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged commodity production. Local conditions and political actions could shift institutional prescriptions towards higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). These insights suggest that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced approach to institutions and their interactions with historical context.

This interplay between historical periods, institutional evolution, and colonial policies underscores the need for a more dynamic view of African economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally linked to their ability to enforce property rights and create stable environments for investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). When secure private property rights are established, these institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. This dynamic is evident in the historical context of England's financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The development of such institutions not only supports economic growth but also ensures that the benefits of economic activity are distributed more equitably across society. This is particularly relevant for understanding African economic development, where colonial policies often influenced institutional frameworks in ways that either promoted or hindered long-term growth.

The interaction between historical periods and institutional evolution highlights the importance of considering the specific contexts in which institutions operate (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial governments frequently encouraged commodity production to maximize resource extraction, shaping local economic activities. This dynamic underscores how political actions can alter institutional prescriptions towards higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium illustrates that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political pressures, thereby influencing long-term economic outcomes. Institutions evolve in response to local conditions and historical contexts.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies further emphasizes the critical role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective highlights how the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, shapes long-term economic growth trajectories. The persistence of these institutions is a key factor in understanding why some regions have experienced sustained economic development while others have lagged behind.

The historical evidence and empirical comparisons from these studies collectively reveal that the specific institutional frameworks established during colonial periods continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.2). Institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists have had enduring effects on economic development. The case of African commodity production under colonial rule provides clear evidence of how institutional changes can shift economic trajectories, either towards higher production equilibria or stagnation (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
