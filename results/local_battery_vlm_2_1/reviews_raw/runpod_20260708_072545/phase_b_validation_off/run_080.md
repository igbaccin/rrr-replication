The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake is understanding how enduring patterns of prosperity and poverty emerge from the interplay between human agency and structural constraints. Scholars debate whether institutions are primary drivers that shape economic trajectories independently of other factors, or whether institutions' influence on growth is mediated by other variables, such as culture, geography, or political dynamics.

One key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions can be considered autonomous forces in economic development. Some scholars emphasize the independent power of institutions to foster or hinder growth through their effects on property rights, transaction costs, and market incentives (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others argue that institutions are embedded within broader social contexts that shape their form and function, suggesting that institutions' impact on economic growth is contingent upon cultural and political factors. This debate underscores the complexity of institutional effects on long-run economic growth.

The literature on institutions and economic growth has evolved significantly over time. These studies highlighted the role of political institutions in creating stable frameworks for market exchange, which encouraged investment and innovation. This perspective underscores how institutional changes can reduce the friction inherent in economic exchanges, thereby promoting growth.

More recent scholarship has nuanced this view by exploring how institutions interact with other factors, such as cultural beliefs and political dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that institutions are shaped by broader social and political struggles as well as being technical solutions to economic problems. Furthermore, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective highlights the need for more nuanced analyses that consider the complex interplay between institutions and other factors over time.

The evolution of institutional frameworks governing public choice has also been a focus of research. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective underscores how political institutions can create stable environments conducive to long-term investment and innovation.

Acemoglu et al (2001) highlight the role of European colonization strategies in shaping institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive states that transferred resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This suggests that initial institutional setups can have long-lasting effects on economic trajectories.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast highlight that the development of credible commitment mechanisms, such as those established through constitutional changes, were vital in securing property rights. This institutional framework enabled economic actors to engage in long-term investments with greater confidence, fostering capital markets and overall economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies further illustrates how institutions can shape economic trajectories by influencing the distribution of wealth and power. This underscores the importance of early institutional frameworks in determining long-term economic paths.

Ogilvie and Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges efficiency-based explanations by emphasizing how power struggles among different social groups influenced institutional development. Ogilvie's analysis highlights the role of distributional conflicts in shaping these institutions, suggesting that they emerged from struggles over wealth and resources. This dynamic influenced long-term economic outcomes by locking in institutional arrangements that favoured certain groups at the expense of others. Frankema and van Waijenburg extend this perspective to African colonial economies, demonstrating how similar power dynamics shaped wage levels and economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Their research shows that real wages in British African colonies varied significantly over time, reflecting underlying distributional conflicts and institutional changes. These findings underscore the importance of considering power dynamics when examining long-term economic trajectories; they also reveal that efficiency-based explanations for institutions often fail to capture the complexities of historical development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) estimate that the internal norms of mistrust account for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The mechanism underlying this persistent mistrust is rooted in the historical experience of exploitation and betrayal, which has fostered a deep-seated skepticism towards others (Nunn 2008, p.2). A critical implication of this enduring mistrust is its detrimental impact on economic development. This finding aligns with broader research documenting the importance of trust for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19), highlighting how historical institutions can shape long-term economic trajectories through their influence on social norms and interpersonal relationships. These findings underscore the complex interplay between historical institutions, social norms, and economic development in Africa.

Akyeampong and Fofack show that women played pivotal roles in African economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). Women's contributions were often overlooked by historical accounts that marginalized their role. This oversight is particularly notable in the context of agricultural activities, where women were frequently relegated to marginal lands for food farming under colonial rule. However, Frankema shows that formal education policies varied significantly among colonial powers, with British rule fostering more conducive environments for educational development in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial regimes (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.1).

The impact of these gender dynamics and educational policies on African economic activities was profound. This codification limited women's economic opportunities and shaped broader social norms around gender roles in agriculture. These educational policies had downstream implications for economic development, as they influenced the skill sets and occupational choices of future generations.

The interplay between gender dynamics, educational policies, and economic activities highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-term economic trajectories in Africa. Conversely, the development of formal education systems under British rule provided new opportunities for skill acquisition and occupational mobility, which could offset some of the negative effects of gender-based marginalization. This interplay underscores the complex ways in which colonial institutions influenced African economic development through both direct and indirect mechanisms.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that Africa's underdevelopment is rooted in colonial institutions, but it does not directly address how these institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin contends that the thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, focusing on the role of European settlers. This perspective highlights how colonial policies shaped African economies: where European settlers were not present to compete for labor, indigenous commodity production was often encouraged (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins adds that institutional reforms informed by the new economic history of Africa can help alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The presence of 'good' institutions is crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, Austin's analysis suggests that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, implying a more dynamic interplay between local agencies and colonial institutions than the reversal-of-fortune thesis acknowledges. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering local responses to colonial policies when evaluating long-run economic growth, suggesting that African societies were not merely passive recipients of external institutional frameworks.

This institutional divergence reflects the broader influence of colonial policies on economic development, as these institutions persisted long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The institutional legacy of colonialism remains a critical factor in understanding contemporary economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The colonial institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes today.
