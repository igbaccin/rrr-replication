The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in development economics and economic history. Institutions are broadly understood as the rules that govern behavior, encompassing both formal regulations and informal norms. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending why some regions have experienced sustained prosperity while others stagnate or decline. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are essential for fostering investment and innovation, thereby driving long-term economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is contingent on various historical and contextual factors, including the presence of European settlers and the nature of colonial policies.

A key point of contention revolves around the enduring effects of colonial institutions. Some scholars assert that these institutions have a lasting influence on contemporary economic outcomes, shaping patterns of growth and development over centuries. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional legacies to grasp current economic disparities. Conversely, others emphasize the need for nuanced analyses that consider multiple factors beyond institutions, including cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts. The live disagreement hinges on whether institutions are autonomous determinants of long-term economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other variables such as cultural values and historical contexts.

The debate around the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between institutional design and historical legacies. This interplay highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, affect economic outcomes over time. Such an understanding can inform policy recommendations aimed at fostering sustainable development in regions with historically disadvantaged institutional frameworks.

The establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution increased security of long-term property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth. The political model that North outlines suggests this was a product of fiscal crises from the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. Similarly, North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This evolution is evident in capital markets, which provide a striking indication of institutional effectiveness. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, leading to an overhaul of traditional interpretations of British and French economic history. If England and France were almost at parity in economic performance, it implies that institutions per se, and particularly those described by North and Weingast, were revolutionary. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth as traditionally thought.

However, the implications of these institutional changes extend beyond mere economic performance. The success of these institutions is remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast argue that this success hinges on political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, not simply the rules governing economic exchange but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. A critical factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the nature of these institutions was determined by European mortality rates and settlement patterns, which led to either inclusive or extractive institutional frameworks. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In contrast, in regions with lower mortality rates, like the United States and Australia, Europeans settled permanently and developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This framework explains why former colonies with extractive institutions have lagged behind those with more inclusive ones, as the institutional legacies of colonial rule continue to shape economic outcomes today. However, Acemoglu et al. caution that the persistence of these institutions is not solely due to their inherent stability but also because politically powerful groups may resist changes that could threaten their rents or political power (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that overcoming the institutional barriers to growth in former colonies requires addressing the interests of those who benefit from the status quo, a challenge that extends beyond mere institutional reform.

The new economic history of Africa reveals that institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins contends that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the effectiveness of institutions in providing public goods. This perspective challenges the notion that ethnic heterogeneity inherently leads to poor institutional performance. Englebert’s findings highlight that societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins notes that by the 1990s, the role of institutions had become central to development policy, with 'good' institutions being crucial for providing incentives for investment essential for development. This shift enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions could support economic growth under certain conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). However, Frankema argues that while British rule was more conducive to formal education development in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, it did not uniformly lead to better economic outcomes. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economies was complex and varied, influenced by both exogenous factors and local initiatives, with implications for contemporary institutional reforms aimed at fostering growth.

Historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time due to the enduring effects of slavery and inequality on socio-economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites through government policies, thereby restricting competition. Nunn's research supports this finding by showing that Africa’s slave trades had a profound adverse effect on subsequent economic development; this suggests that the legacy of slavery extends beyond immediate socio-economic disruptions and shapes long-term institutional structures. The persistence of inequality in Latin America and the enduring impact of slave trades in Africa highlight the deep-rooted nature of these historical institutions, which continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19). This phenomenon underscores how deeply ingrained institutional frameworks can perpetuate historical inequalities over generations.

Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is central to long-run economic growth. This perspective emphasizes technological and intellectual advancements as primary drivers of economic development. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological innovation and institutional change is crucial for sustaining long-term economic improvement. However, Mokyr cautions that without such institutional support, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in maintaining economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while knowledge growth is fundamental, it must be supported by adaptive institutions to ensure sustained economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts within societies shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They propose that the primary driver behind these institutions was not efficiency but rather the resolution of power struggles among different social groups, specifically landlords, peasants, and urban elites. This perspective highlights how the distribution of political power and wealth influenced institutional outcomes, such as the persistence of serfdom or the formation of guilds. Serfdom, for instance, can be seen as a mechanism to maintain control over agricultural labor by landowning elites, rather than an efficient allocation of resources. Similarly, craft and merchant guilds served to protect the interests of urban artisans and merchants against external competition, thereby reinforcing their economic power. Austin elaborates on this dynamic by showing how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation underscores the role of distributional conflicts not only within European societies but also in colonial contexts, where indigenous economic activities influenced institutional changes. However, Ogilvie cautions that while institutions are crucial for understanding long-term economic growth, they must be studied with a clear methodology to avoid becoming mere 'fashion accessories' (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This warning suggests that the study of institutions requires rigorous analysis and integration into broader research programs to truly understand their impact on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to influence and be influenced by distributional conflicts within societies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter for economic growth, as they specify property rights and enforce market exchanges, which are crucial for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and distributional conflicts is further highlighted by Ogilvie’s critique of the efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. She argues that these institutions were shaped more by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The impact of institutions on economic development is also evident in colonial contexts, where indigenous economic activities influenced institutional changes. Austin’s work shows that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation underscores the role of distributional conflicts not only within European societies but also in colonial contexts, where indigenous economic activities influenced institutional changes. However, Ogilvie cautions that while institutions are crucial for understanding long-term economic growth, they must be studied with a clear methodology to avoid becoming mere 'fashion accessories' (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This warning suggests that the study of institutions requires rigorous analysis and integration into broader research programs to truly understand their impact on economic development.

The historical evidence from colonial contexts provides specific cases where institutional changes due to distributional conflicts led to significant economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding is supported by North’s argument that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The precise measurement of these institutional impacts can be seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. document this reversal and provide evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The empirical comparison of colonial outcomes shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not long-run growth, were often the result of these interactions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
