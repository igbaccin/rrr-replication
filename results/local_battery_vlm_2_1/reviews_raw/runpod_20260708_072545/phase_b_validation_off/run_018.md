Institutions have long been recognized as pivotal in shaping economic growth over extended periods. The central question is how they exert this influence and whether their role can be isolated from other factors. This debate pits those who emphasize the primacy of institutions against those who argue for alternative explanations. Some scholars posit that institutions are the driving force behind economic development, while others contend that geographic or cultural factors play a more significant role in determining long-term economic outcomes.

The first major stream focuses on the direct impact of colonial institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers implemented vastly different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settler presence, with extractive institutions being established in colonies where settlers were absent or faced high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted over time and significantly influenced current income per capita. This perspective is complemented by North (1989, p.9), who highlights the importance of secure property rights as a critical factor in economic growth (North 1989, p.9), arguing that institutions which enforce these rights reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges.

The role of colonialism in shaping long-term economic outcomes is further elucidated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies during colonization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). They document how the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, underscoring the interaction between institutions and opportunities for economic development.

The second major stream addresses the role of trust in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting how historical events can leave enduring cultural imprints. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and significantly affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective underscores the importance of trust as a fundamental aspect of economic growth, complementing the institutional framework by showing how cultural norms can either support or hinder economic progress. The authors demonstrate that both internal and external factors contribute to mistrust, with internal norms playing a more significant role than external environmental conditions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), indicating that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth cannot be isolated from other historical factors. This stream highlights a nuanced understanding of how cultural and institutional factors interact to shape long-term economic outcomes, suggesting that trust is as important as property rights in fostering economic development.

The third major stream focuses on the political underpinnings of economic growth, emphasizing the role of credible commitments and secure property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The authors posit that the development of capital markets provides evidence of this, showing how political factors underpin economic growth by ensuring that rules are enforced and can be changed as needed. Credible commitments, such as those made by the English Parliament during fiscal crises, were instrumental in establishing a stable institutional framework that supported long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This stream underscores the importance of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting how credible commitments and secure property rights are essential for fostering economic development. North (1989, p.9) argues that neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants overlook the critical role of institutions (North 1989, p.6). This stream highlights the importance of political institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes, suggesting that credible commitments and secure property rights are essential for fostering economic development. Institutions shape economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges, reducing transaction costs, and ensuring that rules are enforced effectively.

Institutional frameworks established during colonial times have enduring effects on contemporary economic performance, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth. The interaction between cultural norms, such as trust, and institutional factors further underscores the complexity of economic development, suggesting that both internal and external factors play crucial roles in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Political institutions also contribute to economic growth by fostering secure property rights and ensuring credible commitments, highlighting how different aspects of institutional frameworks interact to support long-term economic development.

The enduring impact of these institutions is evident in how they shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. When Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe rather than fostering local economic development. This institutional framework persisted over time, locking former colonies into underdevelopment.

This institutional legacy has profound implications. It means that even when geographic factors might suggest potential for economic development, the institutional framework established during colonial rule can impede progress.

These extractive institutions not only failed to encourage investment and innovation but also created barriers to economic advancement. The persistence of these institutions over generations highlights how initial colonial policies can have enduring effects on a country's economic trajectory (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic underscores the critical role that institutional design plays in shaping long-term economic outcomes, illustrating how historical legacies can constrain contemporary development efforts.

In a similar vein, historical studies by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Pamuk 2007, p.23) provide insights into how institutions influenced economic outcomes in different contexts. In New World societies, greater inequality led to elite-advantaging government policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality over generations. These institutional structures were not merely passive reflections of underlying economic conditions but active forces that perpetuated disparities. Pamuk (2007, p.16) builds on this by showing how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes in Europe, leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This highlights how institutional responses to exogenous shocks can have lasting effects on economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illustrate that even when societies began with similar factor endowments, differences in initial inequality led to divergent paths of development through their impact on institutions. This dynamic underscores the critical role that institutional design plays in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Extending this idea, Frankema argues that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This is because British rule introduced new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that increased demand for missionary education, leading to a greater emphasis on formal schooling (Frankema 2012, p.15). The impact of these educational initiatives varied across colonies. Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The reconstruction and comparison of real wage levels in British African colonies reveal how economic development varied significantly over time, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of this divergence.

Building on this, Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by examining different historical periods and paths. These authors highlight that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production where European settlers were not present to compete for labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that institutional prescriptions could change in response to local economic and political incentives, suggesting a more dynamic relationship between institutions and economic growth than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this complexity by noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores how structural conditions can mediate institutional impacts on long-term economic performance. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with local conditions to drive economic growth over the long run.

This perspective is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) who propose that the slave trades in Africa had an adverse effect on economic development. They argue that this mistrust persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates suggests that the legacy of the slave trades continues to shape African economic development by undermining social capital. This internalization of mistrust is a critical factor in understanding the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes.

For instance, sustained trade with Europeans was only possible in regions like the Kongo kingdom, which had centralized governance and developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that pre-colonial institutional structures played a crucial role in facilitating economic interactions.

This political context underscores how institutions governing public choice can foster secure property rights and economic growth. However, North and Weingast note that the impact of these institutional changes is nuanced; they argue that while the development of capital markets was crucial, it did not solely determine economic performance, as evidenced by France's comparable economic standing before the French Revolution. This suggests that while institutions are critical, their effects can be contingent on broader political and economic contexts. The interplay between institutional developments and economic growth thus reveals a complex relationship where specific institutional changes can drive long-term economic prosperity but must be understood within the wider framework of historical and political conditions.

He shows that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood within patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe. This perspective underscores that institutions are not static but evolve over time in response to economic and political actions.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a matter of both their inherent characteristics and their interaction with broader historical contexts.
