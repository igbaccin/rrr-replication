The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and historical development studies. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around whether institutional frameworks are primary drivers of sustained economic progress or if they merely facilitate other underlying forces. Some scholars posit that institutions themselves, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial for long-term growth. Others argue that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights shape core economic institutions, which in turn affect economic performance. Additionally, the impact of colonial policies on institutional development and subsequent economic trajectories has been a subject of intense debate, with some scholars emphasizing the role of extractive institutions established during colonization periods. These debates underscore the complexity of understanding how institutions influence long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly lowered transaction costs by establishing secure property rights. This development was pivotal in fostering long-run economic growth as it facilitated market exchanges and capital accumulation. The emergence of these institutions can be traced back to political solutions devised during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded for revenues (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that this institutional change was a response to broader economic challenges. They further argue that these institutions had a profound impact on capital markets, enhancing the efficiency of property rights and securing long-term investments. This perspective is supported by evidence from capital markets, which shows that England's institutional changes were more effective in promoting economic growth than previously thought . This does not reduce the significance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita, as shown in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and shaped long-term economic outcomes. The design of these institutions aimed to maximize rents for colonizers. The extractive nature of these institutions was a deliberate strategy to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework had profound implications for property rights and investment incentives, leading to divergent economic trajectories among former colonies.

Acemoglu et al. further elaborate that the persistence of these extractive institutions can be attributed to the political power dynamics at play. Politically powerful groups often resist institutional changes that could threaten their control or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This resistance reinforces the extractive nature of the institutions, ensuring that they continue to serve the interests of the colonizers. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in shaping the long-term development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Colonial institutions influenced immediate economic outcomes and had lasting effects on structural conditions governing growth, such as property rights enforcement and investment incentives.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that societies characterized by high levels of inequality tend to develop institutions that favor elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating economic disparities. This dynamic is evident in the colonial contexts where European powers established settlements with vastly different factor endowments, leading to societies with extreme wealth, human capital, and political power inequalities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In such environments, institutions evolved to protect the interests of the elite, often at the expense of broader economic development. The institutional framework hindered competition and limited incentives for investment in public infrastructure and social capital, which are crucial for sustained growth.

In sub-Saharan Africa, the impact of colonial rule on education systems illustrates how institutional frameworks shaped by inequality can influence long-term economic outcomes . The demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift highlights how new social and political institutions can reshape educational priorities, which in turn affect human capital development. Nunn (2008, p.2) suggests that the legacy of slave trades also played a significant role in Africa's economic underdevelopment, indicating that adverse effects of inequality are compounded by historical factors such as slavery and colonialism. This underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional impact on economic growth, where both contemporary and historical factors interact to shape long-term development trajectories.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are pivotal for harnessing technological advancements to drive long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that technological progress necessitates corresponding institutional changes. He explains how the rural mode of life, large extended families, and traditional values can hinder technology adoption; thus, societies must adapt their institutions to integrate these innovations efficiently. This perspective aligns with Mokyr's (2005, p.49) emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge as a driver of economic progress. Mokyr suggests that technological advancements alone are insufficient without complementary institutional support. Institutional adjustments facilitate the diffusion and application of new technologies, ensuring sustained economic improvement over long periods. Mokyr also underscores the importance of addressing coordination failures and implementing policies to rectify them. This implies that while institutions play a crucial role in fostering growth, their effectiveness is contingent on resolving internal conflicts without weakening societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The integration of new technologies requires institutional changes and ideological shifts that support these advancements, ensuring long-term economic sustainability.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie specifically focuses on pre-industrial Europe, highlighting how the core institutions of serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by these conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. She challenges the prevailing view that these institutions were adopted for their economic benefits. Instead, she shows that they arose from power struggles between different social groups seeking to maximize their share of resources.

Ogilvie's analysis underscores how the distribution of political and economic power influenced institutional design. For example, serfdom was maintained because it served the interests of the landed elite. Similarly, craft guilds and merchant guilds were established to protect the economic privileges of their members against external competition. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who demonstrates that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). He argues that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This indicates that institutions can be shaped by the need to maintain social order and distribute resources equitably.

Moreover, Ogilvie’s emphasis on distributional conflicts suggests that institutional changes are not merely driven by technological advancements or exogenous shocks but are deeply rooted in societal power dynamics. This has important implications for understanding long-run economic growth, as it highlights the need to consider the political and social contexts in which institutions operate.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (van Zanden 1999, p.1), along with Pamuk (2007, p.16), find that economic divergence in Europe was already pronounced before 1800, driven by structural changes such as urbanisation rates. The Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, while the focus of economic development shifted to countries bordering the North Sea in the seventeenth century. This shift was marked by institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, where new towns could spring up and rural manufacturing could flourish under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The wages of skilled labourers yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than those of unskilled labourers, indicating that early modern development benefits were spread unevenly. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) qualifies these implications by noting that differences between English and Continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that African economies before 1950 were more economically robust than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner's work reveals the availability of extensive economic data from colonial Africa, challenging the notion that historical national accounting is scarce for this region (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg's analysis of real wage levels in British African colonies further supports this perspective by demonstrating that poverty was not a persistent feature of pre-1950 Africa. They highlight the importance of understanding long-term economic development patterns, including income distribution, to assess future growth prospects (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This approach underscores the significance of historical data in informing contemporary economic policies, suggesting that African economies were more dynamic and responsive to market forces than often portrayed.

Broadberry and Gardner show that export production varied significantly across and within countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the role of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage trends adds depth to this analysis by revealing that these institutional factors influenced not just aggregate economic performance but also the distribution of income within colonies. This qualification suggests that while institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, external market conditions also exerted significant influence. This interplay between institutional factors and market forces highlights the complexity of economic development in colonial Africa.

This nuanced view of African economic history challenges simplistic narratives of persistent poverty, instead revealing a region with dynamic economies shaped by both internal and external factors.

The role of African women in economic growth is significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The contributions of African women to economic development are often marginalised by views that focus on male-dominated sectors. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document that in the Gold Coast, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crops like cocoa. The implications of this institutional bias are profound: it limited women's access to productive resources and constrained their economic potential.

The role of institutions in shaping these dynamics is crucial. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms by highlighting how "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective suggests that understanding historical gender biases in land access and legal systems can guide contemporary efforts to create more equitable institutions. The complexity of African economies means that institutional reforms must consider multiple factors, including ethnic heterogeneity and the quality of public goods provided by governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view underscores the importance of comprehensive institutional reforms that address historical injustices and promote inclusive economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), along with other scholars (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), assert that the legacy of the slave trade has fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust is a persistent social norm that continues to influence contemporary African societies. The mechanism through which this mistrust endures involves both internal and external factors, with internal norms of mistrust being at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). For instance, the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is not primarily explained by changes in the trustworthiness of others but rather by deeply ingrained internal norms that have been shaped over centuries. This dynamic highlights how historical injustices can become embedded in social structures, affecting trust levels and economic interactions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The long-lasting impact of these norms underscores the importance of addressing both internal and external factors when designing policies aimed at fostering trust and cooperation within African societies.

Institutions play an instrumental role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and enforcement of property rights, market exchanges, and political stability. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied significantly based on mortality rates, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions. These institutions, in turn, influenced current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks.

The role of credible commitment is central to understanding how institutions facilitate economic growth. North (1989, p.6) argues that effective political institutions specify efficient property rights and enforce them, thereby reducing transaction costs and fostering a growing economy. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.19) findings, which show that different measures of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, and judicial efficiency, all correlate strongly with economic outcomes.

The long-term effects of institutions extend to social norms and trust levels in addition to economic metrics. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the behavior of older industrial economies can be understood through patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience spanning centuries. This historical continuity underscores how institutions shape economic trajectories over extended periods.

The distributional conflicts inherent in many societies provide additional insight into institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need to transform institutions from a fashion accessory into an indispensable tool for economic historians, suggesting that institutions are shaped by shared beliefs and political power dynamics. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive rather than growth-promoting, as they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups.

The historical evidence of colonialism reveals how institutional changes introduced by European powers have shaped the long-run development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) thesis highlights that population density and prosperity influenced European policies, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among these colonies. This perspective is supported by Hopkins' (Hopkins 2009, p.8) assertion that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the interaction between colonial policies and local conditions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Hopkins (2009, p.8). The measurement type used to settle this disagreement involves assessing the impact of institutional changes introduced by European powers on the long-term development of former colonies, revealing that good institutions provide incentives for investment and are crucial for economic growth.
