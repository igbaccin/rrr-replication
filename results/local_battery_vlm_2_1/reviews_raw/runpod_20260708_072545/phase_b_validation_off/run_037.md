Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how resources are allocated and productivity is enhanced. The central question remains: what specific institutional arrangements foster economic development? This debate has sparked a live disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions matter because they facilitate investment and secure property rights, versus those who contend that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Furthermore, some researchers posit that the impact of institutions on growth is contingent upon historical contexts and specific paths of institutional evolution, while others see them as a critical factor in overcoming poverty through targeted reforms.

The core of this scholarly debate lies in understanding how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, affect economic outcomes. Institutions can either promote long-term growth by encouraging investment and innovation or hinder it by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few, thereby creating barriers to development shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This transformation was an economic shift as well as a political one, driven by the need to address fiscal crises. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies this institutional change, which dramatically lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported a growing economy through increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.9). Political institutions played a pivotal role by specifying efficient property rights and ensuring their effective enforcement, leading to falling costs per transaction while expanding the transactions sector's proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6).

The political dimension of this institutional change was crucial. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth through credible commitments. This commitment underpinned the development of markets, ensuring that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could be changed only within a stable framework (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evidence from capital markets underscores this point, showing how political factors, such as the regime's commitment to rules, influenced economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s work shows how European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. The persistence of these extractive institutions over time has had lasting effects on the economic performance of these regions. Acemoglu et al.'s findings highlight that the nature of colonial institutions, shaped by European settlement patterns and mortality rates, directly influenced the economic trajectory of colonies. This institutional legacy continues to impact current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Moreover, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the 19th century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The implications of this institutional framework are far-reaching, as it suggests that the economic performance of many regions today is still influenced by the colonial institutions established centuries ago.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis is insufficient to explain Africa's economic trajectory because it does not account for varied colonial experiences and the nuanced roles played by European settlers and administrations (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin argues that the thesis oversimplifies causation. It compresses different historical periods and paths, leading to a reductionist view of Africa's economic history. This is because it overlooks the diverse institutional responses to local conditions in African colonies. For instance, colonial administrations often adjusted their policies based on indigenous economic actions and incentives. In such cases, European settlers' presence did not always lead to the suppression of local commodity production. Instead, colonial governments sometimes encouraged it when settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the relationship between settler presence and economic outcomes was more complex than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis allows.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this critique by arguing for a new economic history of Africa that can inform institutional reforms, thereby helping to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion. The new emphasis on governance and institutions in development policy has opened up space for this approach. This perspective is crucial because it highlights that 'good' institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is vital for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the idea of a singular African experience must be challenged by recognising the continent's diversity, including its varied colonial experiences.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by examining historical data on real wages and per capita income. These scholars argue that African economic performance was not as dire as previously thought, highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.2) work which demonstrates the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era in Africa. They show that patterns of growth were tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors drove both expansion and contraction of economies within different regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This pattern reveals that African economic performance was more dynamic than previously believed, reflecting a complex interplay between external market forces and internal institutional structures. The role of political institutions in shaping these outcomes remains an area for further exploration.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that Europe's economic divergence from Asia was already pronounced before 1800. They contend that north-western Europe’s high productivity in the traded goods sector arose through merchant distribution and finance, highlighting the service sector’s crucial role in early modern economic growth. van Zanden (1999, p.9) builds on this by noting that after 1740 England became the most expensive country for basic foodstuffs, confirming a rise in relative price levels due to economic development. This shift in economic focus to North Sea countries underscores how institutional changes and investment patterns shaped regional productivity. van Zanden (1999, p.9) qualifies this pattern by showing that differences between English and continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760, suggesting a nuanced interplay of economic factors beyond simple price shifts. This qualification highlights the complexity of early modern economic development and its reliance on specific institutional frameworks that facilitated trade and investment. The divergence in productivity between Europe and Asia during this period was driven by these institutional dynamics, which fostered regional specialization and economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) argue that distributional conflicts are the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Distributional conflicts among elites and commoners shaped the development of institutions such as serfdom, communal structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. In this context, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) contends that institutions are not merely fashion accessories but indispensable instruments in economic history. This perspective challenges efficiency-based explanations by suggesting these institutions were primarily designed to preserve the power of elites rather than to maximize overall productivity. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) expand this view, highlighting how extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutional structures that reinforced elite dominance. This dynamic is evident in New World societies where high levels of inequality resulted in institutions that restricted competition and maintained the status quo. This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor in shaping institutions, they operate within a complex web of social and political dynamics that influence long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that Africa's slave trades had profound impacts on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). They establish that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Their findings indicate that both external and internal channels are important in shaping this mistrust, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn traces the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades over nearly five centuries, from 1400 to 1900. This period is significantly longer than the duration of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). The longevity and scale of the slave trade suggest that its impact on African economic development was more profound than other factors such as colonialism or recent events. For instance, Nunn shows how the Kongo kingdom's centralized government, national currency, well-developed markets, and trading networks facilitated foreign trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This example highlights that the absence of such institutional structures in smaller societies hindered sustained trade. The enduring effects of the slave trades on trust and economic development underscore the need to consider these historical factors when examining Africa's contemporary economic challenges.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that economic growth is primarily driven by technological advancements and the expansion of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that institutional adjustments are necessary to facilitate the efficient use and diffusion of innovations. Mokyr elaborates on this idea, noting that institutions must reduce access costs to knowledge and coordinate efforts to prevent coordination failures. He highlights that without such institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvements would be unsustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets adds a nuanced condition: these adjustments must resolve growth-induced conflicts peacefully to maintain societal cohesion. This is crucial because if conflicts are not managed effectively, they can weaken the political fabric of society and impede modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to coordinate efforts and reduce access costs to knowledge. Institutions facilitate the reduction of transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is exemplified by the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and significantly contributed to economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The coordination failures that can impede long-run economic progress are thus mitigated through institutional arrangements that ensure efficient property rights enforcement. These institutions provide the necessary framework for market exchanges and foster an environment conducive to investment and development.

The historical context of colonialism further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic growth. European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to varied institutional outcomes in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive states set up by Europeans in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast differed from the rule-of-law-based systems in places like the United States and Australia. This divergence in institutional frameworks highlights how institutions can either promote or hinder economic development over the long term.

The persistence of these colonial-era institutions continues to influence current income per capita levels, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutional arrangements on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between population density, prosperity, and European policies also played a significant role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies. In regions where Europeans did not compete for local labor, indigenous commodity production was often encouraged, leading to different institutional prescriptions that influenced economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributable to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors alone. This underscores the importance of institutions in determining long-run economic performance. The equilibrium institutions established during colonial times were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical evidence shows that institutions matter profoundly in shaping economic trajectories.

The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether shared beliefs or distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation for institutions such as serfdom and guilds, challenging the belief that these institutions were efficient (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This debate is settled by empirical comparisons of historical evidence showing how institutional changes influenced economic outcomes in different regions. The measurement types used include constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights indices, rule-of-law measures, and judicial efficiency assessments, which collectively demonstrate the enduring impact of institutions on long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
