The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development research. At stake intellectually is an understanding of how institutional frameworks influence the trajectory of economies over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primary drivers of growth, with some scholars arguing that they are indispensable for fostering secure property rights and investment incentives (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6), while others suggest that economic development is primarily driven by factors such as population and savings (North 1989, p.9). Additionally, there are debates over the extent to which institutions are shaped by historical events like colonization or the slave trade versus endogenous institutional evolution. This disagreement underscores the complexity of understanding how institutions interact with other variables to influence long-run growth.

One stream addresses the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run growth. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For example, Austin notes that colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous economic activity to ensure fiscal security (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective indicates that institutional outcomes are influenced by initial colonial policies and subsequent adaptations to local conditions. The implications of these findings underscore the importance of understanding how historical institutions continue to influence contemporary economic development, even as they evolve over time.

North and Weingast show that political factors driving economic growth encompass rules governing exchange and institutions enforcing these rules along with their potential changes. This institutional framework was pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth. Hopkins suggests that understanding Africa’s dismal economic record requires recognizing the role of poor institutions in hindering investment incentives essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how institutional reforms can address poverty among the bottom billion people by providing a historical context to current challenges.

Institutions matter significantly because they provide the necessary incentives for investment, which is crucial for long-term economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize that trust, a critical component of institutional frameworks, has profound implications for economic development. Their findings suggest that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly affects current economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This perspective highlights how historical events can shape contemporary institutions by influencing social norms and behaviors. The role of trust in economic development underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks evolve over time to foster growth.

This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional frameworks across European colonies. These contrasting institutional outcomes underscore how initial conditions can shape long-term economic trajectories. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this perspective by emphasizing that institutions often evolve to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the beneficial effects of institutions on economic development are contingent on their design and the interests they serve, suggesting a more complex relationship between institutions and growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutional design (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show that the regime's commitment to rules governing political behavior, and its capacity to enforce these rules, were vital factors underpinning economic development. This institutional framework enabled a predictable environment for investment and exchange, which in turn facilitated capital market growth and economic progress. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight that factor endowments, such as land abundance relative to labor, influenced the development of institutional structures in New World societies; these institutions, characterized by extreme inequality, constrained competition and perpetuated disparities.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) establish that African poverty was not an enduring historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. The role of foreign trade and export markets is crucial in understanding this pattern. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) illustrate that growth in export production was tied to a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This mechanism underscores how external market forces and internal institutional frameworks interacted to shape economic outcomes. The timing and scale of this growth differed significantly across various countries and regions within Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has limited our understanding of long-term economic development in Africa. This gap underscores the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully grasp the determinants of real wage divergence and the broader economic landscape of colonial Africa.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are fundamental drivers of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) argue that sustained growth over long periods is primarily driven by epochal innovations in human knowledge, which shape technological changes and their diffusion through growing economies. The institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany these technological shifts are crucial for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets highlights that the pace of economic growth and technological progress varied across regions and centuries. For instance, early industrialization periods saw significant conflicts between traditional societal structures and modern technologies, necessitating institutional adaptations to resolve these tensions without undermining political stability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This process underscores how technological advancements require complementary institutional changes to ensure sustainable economic improvement. Mokyr notes that the growth of useful knowledge is not always linear or irreversible; it demands institutional progress to complement technological advances and prevent rent-seeking behaviors from eroding gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). These findings suggest that while institutions play a role in facilitating technological progress, the primary driver of long-run economic growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge through epochal innovations.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), suggesting the need for cultural shifts. Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) jointly establish that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust operates through both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internal norms of mistrust. This means that even if external conditions improve, the enduring internal norms of mistrust can continue to impede economic development. Nunn modifies this implication by pointing out that the slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development originates from deep historical roots of these internal norms (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that addressing the legacy of mistrust necessitates institutional reforms and cultural shifts fostering greater trust and cooperation.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) provides an alternative explanation for the economic trajectory of Africa by suggesting that institutional reforms can eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who contends that African countries have experienced varying degrees of institutional development due to differences in historical contexts and political dynamics. This suggests that the incentives created by local economic and political actions influenced colonial administrations' decisions. Therefore, institutional reforms must consider the complex interplay of historical factors and local dynamics to effectively address poverty in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with historical contexts, local dynamics, and the incentives created by political and economic actions. Austin argues that colonial administrations' decisions were influenced by the incentives generated by indigenous economic and political activities (Austin 2008, p.15). This influence extended to institutional prescriptions that promoted higher production equilibria when European settlers were absent. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The interplay between historical factors and local dynamics is crucial for understanding the role of institutions. Hopkins notes that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, highlighting the complexity of institutional reforms (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Acemoglu et al. further highlight this complexity by arguing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast illustrate this point through the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The specific historical evidence that settles these disagreements is the comparison between the institutional changes introduced by colonialism and their impact on long-run development. Acemoglu et al.'s documentation of the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies shows that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical comparison highlights the central role of institutional changes in shaping long-run economic growth, providing a clear measurement type that aligns with the historical evidence presented by Austin regarding colonial administrations' decisions.
