The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in the field of economics, as it fundamentally influences policy decisions and theoretical frameworks. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and ensure secure transactions facilitate economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3); others contend that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, are responsible for the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The stakes are high because understanding the causal mechanisms underlying economic growth can inform policies aimed at fostering development and reducing global income disparities. This debate is crucial for policy-making as it determines whether efforts should focus on institutional reform or other interventions.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the emergence of new financial instruments marked a significant decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution reflected an increased security of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War drove this institutional change, as certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further elaborate that the success of these institutions was evident in capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic growth. They underscore that political factors, particularly the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchanges, were pivotal (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional changes in England during this period involved both economic rules and the political framework that enforced and adapted these rules. This political dimension is crucial because it ensures that property rights are secure over time, which in turn fosters a stable environment for long-term investments and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast qualify this by noting that the bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not significantly hinder France's economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and resource extraction potential, leading to distinct institutional outcomes. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, highlighting a key mechanism by which colonial policies shaped economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. acknowledge the complexity of institutional change and note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive due to the political power dynamics at play (Acemoglu et al., 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19)). This suggests a nuanced relationship between colonial policies and economic growth, where the persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-term development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800. This mechanism is echoed by van Zanden, who identifies shifts in economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea during the seventeenth century. Van Zanden explains that these changes led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, confirming the view that economic development influenced living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Van Zanden also notes that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by examining historical economic data and colonial impacts. They reveal that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, thus providing a nuanced view of African economic history. This perspective contrasts with recent literature emphasizing structural impediments to growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that these patterns of economic growth were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly across different regions due to diverse local conditions and institutional frameworks. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about long-term economic development. This gap highlights the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and its relation to other parts of colonial Africa. This approach helps assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The nuanced view offered by these studies suggests that structural impediments are not the sole determinants of Africa's economic trajectory.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa. They demonstrate that this mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This finding is supported by Nunn's (2008) argument that the African continent experienced four simultaneous slave trades over nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, which had significant adverse effects on economic development. The duration and scale of these slave trades are striking when compared with official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to about 1960. Nunn highlights that the Kongo kingdom was a key exception in facilitating trade due to its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that even within Africa's slave trade history, there were specific institutional conditions that could mitigate some of the adverse effects on economic development.

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, as technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge underpins sustained economic growth. This process necessitates both technological innovations and complementary institutional developments to facilitate their adoption and diffusion. Kuznets elaborates on this, noting that modern technology is incompatible with traditional modes of life and family structures, requiring significant adjustments in societal institutions and ideologies. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional changes is crucial for economic growth. The sovereign state plays a pivotal role in resolving conflicts arising from these transitions, ensuring that economic growth remains sustainable. Kuznets also recognizes that this process can be fraught with challenges, as technological progress may not always align seamlessly with existing institutions. Mokyr further emphasizes the importance of institutional progress in sustaining economic improvements, suggesting that without such progress, gains from technological advancements might be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. This interplay between technology and institutions is essential for understanding long-run economic growth, as it highlights how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder the adoption of new technologies.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is overly simplistic, compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique highlights how the institutionalist approach, while emphasizing secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, struggles with quantifiable testing and explaining why certain countries adopted favorable institutions while others did not. Austin's perspective aligns with Ogilvie’s assertion that distributional conflicts offer a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic and political actions by altering their institutional prescriptions, demonstrating how local dynamics influenced broader colonial policies. This interaction between colonial powers and indigenous communities underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding historical and contemporary economic development. Institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic interactions, influencing incentives and constraints that affect productivity and growth (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast show how credible commitment underpins growth by enabling secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), while Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions can persist and significantly impact current income levels, highlighting the long-term effects of institutional choices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This interaction between institutions and economic outcomes is further illustrated by Austin's analysis of colonial policies, which shows how local dynamics influenced broader institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The evolution of institutions over time is crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs and increased market exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive but are shaped by politically powerful groups aiming to maximize rents rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where institutional changes introduced by European powers had lasting effects on the economic development of colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al., underscores the critical role of institutions in long-term economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Ongoing debates center around the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. North and Weingast focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth, highlighting the importance of credible commitment and secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes, showing how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks that persistently affected income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from capital markets and colonial policies provides a clear measurement type for assessing the impact of institutions on growth. Austin's analysis of indigenous commodity production and colonial administrations' responses to economic and political actions shows how local dynamics influenced broader institutional prescriptions, demonstrating the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). This historical evidence settles the debate by showing that the interaction between colonial powers and indigenous communities underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over time.
