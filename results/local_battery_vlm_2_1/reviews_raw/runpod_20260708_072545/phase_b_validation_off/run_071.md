The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societal structures influence economic outcomes over extended periods. One live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect growth. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly determines economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Others contend that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and political dynamics, which in turn influence economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This debate underscores the complexity of institutional impact on growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how different types of institutions interact with economic processes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for economic growth through the evolution of institutions governing public choice. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, reflecting a broader shift towards more efficient political and economic institutions. North and Weingast's analysis highlights that this institutional change was both an economic adjustment and a profound political transformation (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of their impact on economic growth. This evolution highlights the critical role of credible commitment underpinning long-run economic performance; regimes committed to enforceable rules fostered an environment conducive to market exchanges and economic specialization (North 1989, p.9). North's broader framework also emphasizes that as economies grow, transaction costs per unit decrease while the overall volume of transactions increases. This dynamic is driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which in turn requires robust institutions to manage and enforce property rights effectively (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional foundation had its complexities; North (1989, p.9) observes that the development of new financial instruments and institutions was a gradual process, reflecting the evolving political landscape. This suggests that while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, their establishment is contingent on broader political and institutional changes over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This institutional design was a direct consequence of colonization strategies, which varied based on European mortality rates and the resulting settlement patterns. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as parts of Africa and Latin America, they established extractive institutions focused on resource extraction with minimal investment in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions persisted over time, locking former colonies into a cycle of underdevelopment. Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and not geographic factors. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping long-run economic development. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on long-term economic trajectories, highlighting how historical institutional legacies can either facilitate or hinder growth over extended periods.

Building on this historical perspective, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) assert that the slave trade was instrumental in fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust is not merely an artifact of historical events but persists today, significantly impacting contemporary trust levels. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon involves both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates that the slave trade's impact on African economic development was profound; it disrupted social institutions and impeded the formation of stable trading networks. This disruption resulted from both population decline and the erosion of trust within communities, which hindered collective action and economic cooperation. Consequently, regions more heavily affected by the slave trade experienced diminished economic growth due to entrenched mistrust, which continues to obstruct development efforts. The legacy of this mistrust is evident in Africa's current economic underdevelopment, as it impedes the formation of robust institutions necessary for sustained growth.

Technological progress and intellectual advancements are fundamental drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is key to fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' argument that technological innovations, especially those that can be sustained over long periods and spread widely, are critical for economic growth. These epochal innovations necessitate institutional adjustments to ensure their efficient use, highlighting a symbiotic relationship between technology and institutions that fuels economic progress. The pace of technological change and its impact on different sectors vary widely across regions and time periods. The successful integration of new technologies requires institutional adaptations as well as ideological shifts within societies. Kuznets observes that modern technology is often incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, necessitating substantial societal changes to accommodate these advancements without causing undue strain. This dynamic interplay between technological progress and societal adjustments underscores the complexity of achieving sustained economic growth through innovation. Nevertheless, the role of institutions in facilitating this process remains crucial, as they must adapt to support new technologies while maintaining social cohesion.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) examine how inequality influenced economic performance in New World societies through the lens of institutions that advantaged elites. They argue that these institutional frameworks restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality by limiting opportunities for broader segments of society to thrive economically. This dynamic is evident in the policies and structures that emerged in response to colonial factor endowments, particularly the abundance of land relative to labor, which often resulted in extreme wealth disparities from the outset. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate this mechanism by highlighting how local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions shaped export production patterns, thereby influencing economic growth trajectories. These factors underscored the deep-seated inequality that characterized many New World societies, as elites leveraged their power to maintain control over resources and opportunities. While these institutional frameworks were instrumental in shaping long-run economic performance, it is important to note that they also reflected broader societal structures and historical contexts that influenced development paths in nuanced ways. This interplay between institutions and inequality highlights the complex nature of economic growth, where institutional arrangements can both facilitate and hinder progress depending on their alignment with broader social dynamics.

Colonial rule in Africa had multifaceted impacts on economic development that included both facilitating and constraining factors. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that colonial institutions influenced African economic growth by shaping access to resources and opportunities for different groups, including women. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) show that customary law under colonial rule often reinforced gender inequalities, limiting women's access to land and economic opportunities. The exclusion of women from key economic activities, such as cash crop farming, was a significant factor in shaping the economic landscape. This pattern had downstream implications for the broader economic development of African societies, as it limited the productive capacity of a large segment of the population. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this by noting that British colonial rule also fostered formal education, which could have mitigated some of these constraints by providing new opportunities for social mobility and skill development. The overall impact on economic growth remains complex, as the benefits of education had to be balanced against the persistent inequalities perpetuated by colonial institutions.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) both highlight specific issues in African economic policies and social divisions that do not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. Simson's analysis of post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa reveals that these trends were influenced by various factors including industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments (Simson 2019, p.2). This focus on consumption over public goods aligns with Peters' examination of land competition in sub-Saharan Africa, where conflicts over resources have deepened social divisions and hindered economic development. Peters (2004, p.19) argues that the image of negotiable and adaptive customary systems of landholding is misleading, as these systems are often characterized by pervasive competition and exclusion (Peters 2004, p.19). These findings suggest that the neopatrimonial tendencies identified by Simson, where government resources are treated as private property, have significant implications for economic policy-making. The high debt burden and military spending in countries like Kenya and Tanzania illustrate how these patterns can displace capital and recurrent spending, further complicating efforts to achieve long-run economic growth (Simson 2019, p.11). Peters (2004, p.19) notes that the perception of 'custom' as static can sometimes advantage groups seeking to evade official oversight, indicating that the dynamics of landholding are more complex than they initially appear. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes in Africa (Peters 2004, p.5).

van Zanden (2009, p.19); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) share a focus on long-term economic performance and structural changes, examining how skill premiums and historical economic divergences shape economic outcomes. Van Zanden highlights the role of institutions in facilitating the transfer of skills through apprenticeship contracts, noting that guilds in western Europe emerged to ensure fair execution of these contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions, however, have been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry, which could potentially counteract their beneficial effects on intergenerational skill transfer. Broadberry and Gupta complement this view by demonstrating how high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that economic growth during the early modern period was influenced by a broader range of factors, including institutional arrangements that supported trade and commerce.

Frankema and van Waijenburg build on this perspective by emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across colonial Africa. They argue that reconstructing and comparing real wage levels can provide insights into long-term economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of historical data in assessing the impact of structural changes on African welfare development, which is crucial for understanding future growth prospects. Broadberry and Gupta further qualify this by noting that Parthasarathi's explanation of high productivity in southern Indian agriculture raises logical difficulties, suggesting that institutional change is a key factor in driving investment and agricultural productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This implies that the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes extends beyond Europe and has broader implications for understanding global economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) both examine pre-industrial Europe's institutions to explain economic phenomena. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation, compressing historical periods into a single explanatory framework. This over-simplification leads to an inadequate understanding of the institutional evolution and policy experience in industrial economies.

Austin (2008, p.2) highlights how colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations seeking fiscal security adapted their policies based on local economic and political actions, in addition to preconceived notions. This dynamic response to local conditions reveals a nuanced institutional landscape in which policy changes were driven by the need for stability and economic growth.

These institutions evolved to balance power dynamics within societies as well as maximize efficiency. Ogilvie emphasizes that institutions must be understood as tools for managing social conflicts and maintaining stability.

A key implication of this perspective is that the effectiveness of economic policies often depends on their ability to address local distributional issues. This nuanced view challenges simplistic explanations of economic growth, suggesting that successful policies are those that can adapt to and mitigate existing power imbalances within societies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their capacity to manage social conflicts and maintain stability within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores that institutions are crucial for addressing distributional issues as well as enhancing efficiency. North emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). These institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering an environment conducive to long-term capital markets and economic development.

The historical evidence from colonial settings illustrates how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers in colonies like the United States implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states were established in regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Hopkins 2009, p.8). These divergent institutional paths significantly influenced current income per capita and underscored the enduring impact of historical institutional choices.

The institutional framework set by colonial powers had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin's analysis, which shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). These adaptations reflect the dynamic nature of institutions in response to local conditions and distributional conflicts. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes introduced during colonialism, highlighting the enduring effects of historical institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The specific historical evidence from colonial settings, such as the establishment of extractive states versus investment-promoting institutions, shows that long-run economic growth is shaped by the institutional frameworks set in place. These frameworks evolve in response to social conflicts and distributional issues within societies (Austin 2008, p.2). The empirical comparison between colonies with different institutional legacies settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, demonstrating that institutions designed to manage social conflicts and maintain stability are crucial for long-term economic development.
