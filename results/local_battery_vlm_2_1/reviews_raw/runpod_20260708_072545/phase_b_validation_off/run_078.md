The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it addresses how societies can achieve sustained prosperity. The debate centers around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that secure property rights and effective enforcement of rules are critical for fostering growth (North 1989, p.6). Others emphasize the impact of colonial institutions on current economic conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2).

One live disagreement revolves around whether colonial institutions were a primary driver of long-run economic outcomes. Some argue that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers persistently hindered growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective suggests that the nature of colonial rule, particularly the establishment of extractive states, significantly influenced current income per capita.

Another key disagreement concerns the interaction between population density and prosperity on institutional development during colonialism. Some research highlights how these factors shaped European policies and subsequent economic trajectories in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This view underscores that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to industrialization opportunities and the role of institutions in facilitating or hindering this process.

These debates are crucial because they shape our understanding of how institutional frameworks can either promote or impede long-run economic development. The stakes are high as they inform policies aimed at fostering growth in both developed and developing economies, particularly those grappling with legacies of colonial rule (North 1989, p.9).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both assert that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth; the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This development was critical because it fostered secure property rights and effective enforcement, which are essential for economic growth. The creation of such institutions reflects a broader trend where political changes led to institutional reforms that enhanced the security of property rights over time. These developments were part of a broader process where political stability and institutional frameworks evolved alongside economic needs.

This institution reduced transaction costs by providing new financial instruments, which facilitated market exchanges. North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutional changes hinged on the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights and maintaining stable institutions (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment was crucial for sustaining economic growth by ensuring that property rights were secure and predictable. The evolution of these institutions in England contrasts with other European countries, where similar economic performance did not necessarily translate into comparable institutional development.

The implications of these findings extend beyond the historical context of seventeenth-century Europe. They underscore the importance of stable political environments and credible commitments from regimes to enforce property rights. This stability is essential for long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth. The case of England highlights how institutions can adapt to changing economic conditions, providing a model for understanding institutional evolution in other contexts.

This influence is particularly evident in the divergent colonization strategies employed by Europeans. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In areas like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans settled permanently, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were put in place. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these extractive institutions, even after independence, highlights how initial institutional designs can have enduring impacts on a country's economic performance. This pattern is evident in the current income per capita of former colonies, which reflects the historical institutional frameworks imposed during colonial rule.

The long-term effects of these institutions are further underscored by their influence on industrialization and economic development. Variations in mortality rates and settlement patterns led to different institutional outcomes, which in turn affected economic growth trajectories. This nuanced view suggests that while colonial institutions were a significant factor, their effects were mediated by local conditions and historical contexts. The lasting influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which these institutions were established.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutions shaped by inequality and slavery influenced economic development in specific regions through the perpetuation of social disparities. The New World societies with greater inequality favoured elites by designing government policies that restricted competition, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism was evident in Latin American societies where elite interests dominated political structures, leading to policies that protected their wealth and power at the expense of broader economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutional frameworks actively perpetuated historical inequalities through legal and economic structures that favoured the elite class. As a result, the institutions in these societies became entrenched barriers to economic growth, limiting opportunities for social mobility and innovation. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, which was not solely attributable to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.19). This enduring impact of slavery on institutional structures highlights the deep-rooted nature of these inequalities, showing how historical injustices can have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that a historical perspective on national accounting reveals that many African economies performed better than previously thought. These economies were not only above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century but also exhibited patterns of growth tied to foreign trade and export markets, which varied according to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the institutional frameworks in place during colonial rule played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The demand for formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by new social and political institutions implemented under British rule, altering the perceived benefits of education at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) show that high productivity in the traded goods sector could be attributed to merchant distribution and finance as much as production. The service sector thus played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This indicates that while institutions may have facilitated certain economic activities, they also created disparities in wealth distribution, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled ones.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the role of colonial policies and labor dynamics rather than focusing on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. The authors' work underscores that African poverty is a result of complex interactions between colonial policies and local economic conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue for a deeper understanding of real wage divergence across British Africa as well as its relation to other parts of colonial Africa to assess the possibilities of future growth. This approach is supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the focus should be on indigenous economic and political action rather than just institutional frameworks. Colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local incentives and actions, which indicates a more dynamic relationship between colonial rule and economic outcomes. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the importance of understanding how colonial policies interacted with indigenous economic activities, rather than viewing institutions as static determinants of growth. The nuanced view of African poverty presented by these authors suggests that future research should consider the role of local agency and policy responses in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that advancing technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, and that epochal innovations have been dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies a complex interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks, where the latter plays a crucial role in ensuring that technological improvements are effectively harnessed for long-term economic growth.

One specific mechanism through which technological progress drives economic growth is the reduction of access costs to knowledge and innovations. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) also points out that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the development of institutions that support technological progress, has not been monotonic or irreversible (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial for long-term economic growth, the institutional frameworks supporting these advancements must be adaptable and resilient to ensure sustained economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that support and sustain technological progress and investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. This colonial institutional legacy significantly influenced current income per capita, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Concurrently, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and their varying impacts on economic development.

The creation of secure property rights is a critical institutional factor in long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) shows that the English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction was crucial for fostering economic growth by providing increased security to property rights over time. The importance of secure property rights is further underscored by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth. Their analysis of seventeenth-century England reveals how political institutions that ensure secure property rights are essential for sustained economic development.

The role of institutions in managing growth-induced tensions is also evident in the historical context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were central to the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, emphasizing the interaction between institutions and industrialization. This perspective aligns with North's view on transaction costs and property rights, showing how historical institutional legacies continue to shape economic outcomes in the long run.

Austin (2008, p.15) extends this analysis by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This adaptability underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in facilitating economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further illustrate the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes, showing how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists today and affects trust levels within Africa.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether institutional changes are primarily driven by exogenous factors such as population density and prosperity or by endogenous processes shaped by local political dynamics. Austin (2008, p.15) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9) show how colonial institutions were adapted in response to local economic conditions, suggesting a more endogenous process of institutional change. The historical evidence from the English financial revolution as well as the analysis of colonial administrative practices indicates that institutions evolve through a complex interplay of exogenous and endogenous factors.
