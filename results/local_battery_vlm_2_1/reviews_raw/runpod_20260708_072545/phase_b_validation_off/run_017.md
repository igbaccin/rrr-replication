The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play more significant roles. Institutions, in this context, refer to the rules, norms, and organizations that structure social interactions and govern behavior. Proponents of institutional primacy argue that well-functioning institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts, are essential for sustained economic development. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that countries with robust institutional frameworks tend to experience higher levels of growth and prosperity. Conversely, critics contend that institutions alone cannot account for economic outcomes, highlighting the importance of geographical factors or cultural beliefs in shaping economic trajectories.

The debate over the role of institutions in long-run economic growth intersects with discussions on the impact of colonialism and its legacy on contemporary economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persistently affected current income per capita. This perspective is complemented by Austin (2008, p.2), who posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Both scholars emphasize the enduring influence of colonial policies on institutional development, suggesting that the legacy of these policies continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies. The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by North (1989, p.9) who shows that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for fostering a thriving economy.

The debate over the role of institutions also extends to the importance of credible commitments in economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the success of capital markets is closely tied to political factors underpinning economic growth, particularly the degree to which regimes are committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) who emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and can be designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth. The interplay between political commitments, institutional design, and economic outcomes underscores the complexity of institutional influence on long-run economic growth, suggesting that credible commitments are essential for sustainable development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another critical aspect of this debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues for a deeper integration of institutions into economic historiography, emphasizing their role in structuring social interactions and governing behavior. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who contend that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth. The interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional design, and economic outcomes highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional influence on long-run economic growth, suggesting that distributional considerations are crucial for understanding institutional development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. In particular, they highlight how the creation of the Bank of England (1694) and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs; this institutional development reflected an increased security of property rights over time. This transformation facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges. The emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms was crucial in reducing per-transaction costs, which allowed the transactions sector to grow as a proportion of GNP. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this point, noting how increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth. This process was underpinned by political factors that ensured regime commitment to rules governing economic exchange; this commitment was essential for sustaining market development and economic progress. The evolution of these institutions in England highlights how credible commitments and secure property rights can drive long-term economic growth.

This influence was mediated through the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions. On the other hand, European settlers in regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand created inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, fostering long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional differences were not merely a function of geography or climate but were deeply rooted in the colonial policies implemented by European powers. This pattern underscores how institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have enduring effects on economic outcomes, shaping the trajectory of development for centuries.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that poverty in Africa was not an inevitable historical outcome but rather a contingent result of specific economic and institutional factors. This perspective challenges the notion of persistent African poverty by highlighting the significant levels of per capita income achieved by many Anglophone African economies during the colonial era. This variability suggests that the economic performance of African colonies was shaped by a complex interplay of endogenous and exogenous forces, rather than being determined solely by structural impediments.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that export production patterns played a crucial role in shaping these trajectories, with growth and decline in export markets directly impacting economic development. The timing and scale of this growth varied significantly across different regions within Africa, indicating that local conditions and institutional frameworks were pivotal in determining economic outcomes. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical and geographical contexts in which African economies operated during colonial rule. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of real wage levels and trends to assess long-term welfare development, suggesting that these metrics can provide valuable insights into the economic conditions and potential for future growth. This approach underscores the necessity of comprehensive historical analysis to disentangle the complex factors influencing African economic performance and to inform contemporary policies aimed at sustainable development.

The nuanced understanding provided by these studies indicates that African economies were capable of achieving significant levels of development under colonial rule, challenging the prevailing narrative of persistent poverty.

Ogilvie and other researchers (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts were the main drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges efficiency-based explanations by highlighting how power struggles between social groups shaped institutional frameworks. For example, craft guilds emerged not merely from a desire for efficient production but from the struggle of craftsmen to secure their economic position against competitors and patrons. The community's role was similarly rooted in distributional conflicts, as it served to allocate resources among members and protect them from external threats. These institutions were thus responses to political and social pressures rather than purely economic considerations. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates this dynamic by showing that colonial governments often adapted their policies based on local economic and political actions, even when these adaptations did not align with efficiency goals. This adaptability underscores the importance of understanding historical contexts in which institutions were formed.

Distributional conflicts also influenced the adoption of serfdom, where landlords' interests in maintaining labor control clashed with peasants' desires for autonomy. This conflict led to varied institutional outcomes across Europe. The merchant guilds, too, reflected power dynamics between merchants and other economic actors, shaping trade regulations that favored certain groups over others. Ogilvie's emphasis on distributional conflicts provides a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolved in pre-industrial Europe, offering insights into the complexities of long-run economic growth.

The slave trade's direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development offers an alternative explanation to those who attribute underdevelopment solely to institutions or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. This mistrust is not merely an artifact of external conditions but deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms, significantly impacting economic development. However, Nunn also notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom which had well-developed markets and trading networks. This indicates that pre-existing economic structures and institutions played a role in facilitating trade, suggesting that while the slave trade had adverse effects, it is not the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that technological progress has been a crucial driver in transforming economies and societies over time. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that this process requires institutional adjustments to efficiently use innovations, as technological advancements are not inherently compatible with traditional societal structures. This dynamic is evident in the historical transitions from rural modes of life to industrialized societies, where new technologies demanded significant shifts in social organization and governance (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The role of institutions becomes pivotal here: they must facilitate the integration of technological progress without causing excessive social disruption. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that institutional changes are necessary to complement technological advancements so that economic improvements can be sustained over the long term. The sovereign state plays a critical role in managing these transitions by resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, thereby ensuring that economic growth does not undermine the social fabric. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) cautions that if such conflicts are not resolved effectively, they can weaken the political structure and hinder sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The implication is that while technological advancements drive economic growth, their impact can be limited by institutional constraints or mismanagement. Therefore, the effectiveness of institutions in facilitating technological progress is a key determinant of long-run economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and managing societal conflicts. The establishment of effective institutions is crucial for fostering secure property rights and encouraging investment (North 1989, p.9). For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which reflects increased security of property rights and supports economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies with varying institutional outcomes, showing how institutions designed to enforce the rule of law can encourage investment and long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by their impact on technological progress. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with North and Weingast's (1989, p.29) argument that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity is evident in colonial contexts where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, leading to varied institutional outcomes based on local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are primarily shaped by political power dynamics or broader cultural beliefs. The measurement type or case that settles this disagreement is the historical evidence from colonial contexts where European settlers established different colonization strategies with varying institutional outcomes, showing how institutions designed to enforce the rule of law can encourage investment and long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
