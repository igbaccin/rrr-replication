The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. At stake is understanding why some nations prosper while others lag behind. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions drive economic development. One camp emphasizes the causal power of institutions in determining economic outcomes, arguing that secure property rights and effective governance are pivotal for sustained growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Another camp posits that institutional explanations oversimplify complex historical processes by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative. This disagreement underscores the need to unpack how institutions influence economic trajectories over time, especially in diverse historical contexts.

The causal power of institutions is evident in their role in shaping property rights and governance structures. North argues that institutions matter because they define and enforce property rights, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) . The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. extend this argument by showing how different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions were pivotal in shaping current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions highlights their enduring impact on economic development, even as other factors such as geography or culture may also play a role (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). He highlights the importance of property rights and their enforcement in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. This perspective underscores that institutions are pivotal for long-term capital markets and overall economic development.

Acemoglu et al. extend this argument by examining how different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional outcomes in colonies. Their work shows that Europeans established extractive institutions in high-mortality regions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The establishment of these institutions reflects the political and economic interests of European colonizers who sought to maximize their rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic illustrates how institutional legacies can shape economic outcomes over centuries.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological progress and innovation are fundamental to sustained economic development. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments in facilitating efficient use of innovations. Kuznets describes how epochal innovations, such as those in technology, can drive long-term growth but require significant societal changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments involve technological advancements as well as resolving conflicts arising from economic growth. For instance, Kuznets highlights that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs or weakening political cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that coordination failures can impede economic improvement, suggesting that institutional progress must complement technological advancements to ensure sustainable growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Economic reasonableness follows a path that is not straightforward like technological progress. This implies that while knowledge and technology are crucial, their effective utilization depends heavily on the adaptability and resilience of institutions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the conditions under which markets can flourish. They identify credible commitments as the key mechanism through which institutions foster economic development. Credible commitments ensure that political actors adhere to rules governing property rights and economic exchange. This adherence is crucial for fostering market stability and encouraging investment, both of which are essential for sustained economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis highlights how institutional changes in seventeenth-century England facilitated secure property rights, thereby driving economic progress. Austin (2008, p.15) builds on this by emphasizing that colonial institutions varied significantly across regions and periods, leading to divergent economic outcomes. This variation underscores the importance of contextual factors in determining the impact of institutions on economic development. For instance, colonial administrations that prioritized fiscal security often adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political dynamics. This adaptability allowed for more nuanced institutional responses that could better align with indigenous interests, thereby promoting economic growth. Austin also notes that the presence of European settlers could complicate these dynamics by introducing competing interests for labor and resources. This complexity suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on how they interact with local conditions and external influences.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and has significantly impacted trust levels within societies, which in turn potentially influences long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is not merely due to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust. This internalization of mistrust suggests a profound cultural shift, where the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape societal attitudes and behaviors long after its cessation. Nunn further elaborates that this culture of mistrust is intertwined with broader economic underdevelopment. The adverse effects on trust are compounded by the historical context in which African societies were unable to develop sustained trade networks due to their small size and undifferentiated institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). However, Nunn also notes that centralized governments and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, could facilitate foreign trade. This indicates that while the slave trade had a detrimental impact on trust and economic development, the presence of robust institutional frameworks could mitigate some of these negative effects. Therefore, the interaction between historical institutions and cultural legacies plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Colonial rule in Africa was not uniformly detrimental to institutional development; instead, African initiatives and exogenous factors shaped institutional change during this period (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa underscores how the role of institutions enabled policy recommendations to incorporate governance and allowed organizations to come to the fore. This shift in focus on institutions was crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This was due to a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Colonial impacts were not uniformly positive. The implementation of new social and political institutions during British rule had mixed effects on African societies' development trajectories. Thus, while colonial rule did facilitate some institutional developments, its overall impact on long-run economic growth remains complex and contingent on specific historical contexts.

The economic performance and divergence between regions reveals that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This finding challenges previous narratives about Africa's economic stagnation during this period. One mechanism driving this growth was the expansion of foreign trade and export markets, which fueled economic activity in these regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions also played crucial roles in shaping these trajectories. This suggests that institutional frameworks supporting trade and finance were instrumental in facilitating economic development. Moreover, the uneven distribution of these benefits across different labor groups indicates that the fruits of this growth were not uniformly shared, highlighting the complexities of economic divergence between regions.

The varying scale and timing of export production growth across different countries illustrate how local conditions and institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic development. This pattern highlights that while trade and market access were important, the effectiveness of institutions in managing these factors was critical for sustained economic growth.

Long-term structural changes in Europe significantly influenced economic development over centuries. van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) show how these shifts transformed the continent's economy without directly addressing institutional influences. The Black Death initiated a series of long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern and other European regions by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This divergence was reinforced as economic development shifted towards North Sea countries, notably Holland and England, which became leading economic powers in the 17th century (van Zanden 1999, p.9). The flexibility of institutions in Holland enabled it to quickly adapt to new economic opportunities by allowing new towns to flourish under weak monopolies. This institutional flexibility contrasts with Tuscany's rigid approach that strengthened existing monopolies and stifled growth (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The varying responses to these structural changes highlight how local conditions, including institutional frameworks and economic policies, shaped the trajectory of European economic development.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that these conflicts, driven by elites seeking to maintain their power and wealth, led to the formation of institutions like serfdom and guilds. These institutions were shaped by the desire to preserve existing power structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the traditional view that institutions are primarily formed for economic efficiency. In New World societies, for instance, institutions evolved in ways that maintained inequality because elites used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

Distributional conflicts are evident in how institutions were structured to favor those in power. For example, serfdom was a system of labor organization and also a means for elites to control the rural population and extract resources (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This institutional structure had long-lasting effects on economic development by limiting mobility and innovation among the lower classes. Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this dynamic in their analysis of New World societies, where extreme inequality led to institutions that perpetuated wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions often hindered growth by preventing the emergence of a competitive market environment.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions has nuances. While these conflicts certainly influenced institutional formation, the specific outcomes varied across different regions and historical contexts. For instance, the persistence of guilds in some European societies can be seen as a response to both elite control and broader social dynamics that valued craftsmanship and quality over efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This variation underscores that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they interact with other socio-economic factors. The downstream implications of these institutions on economic growth highlight the complexity of institutional formation and its impact on long-run development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they create the framework within which economic activities operate and evolve. Institutions matter because they are the underlying determinants of the political, social, and economic environments that facilitate or hinder economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They determine the incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity improvements by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Institutions can be either inclusive or extractive. Inclusive institutions foster economic growth by encouraging investment in physical capital, human capital, and technology, while extractive institutions concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, limiting broad-based prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, European colonization strategies varied widely: some colonies had institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to long-term economic growth. Other colonies had extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which stifled local economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence shows that colonial institutions significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies. This underscores the lasting impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes over centuries, illustrating how early institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). For example, Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions established by European colonists in high-mortality regions persist to this day and significantly affect current income per capita, highlighting the long-term impact of initial institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between inclusive and extractive institutions is the varying strategies of European colonization. The differing outcomes in regions like the United States versus the Congo demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has a profound impact on long-run economic growth, showing that inclusive institutions foster development while extractive ones impede it (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
