The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a crucial question that lies at the heart of debates between institutional economists and those who emphasize other factors such as geography and culture. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if they are merely one among many influential variables. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and ensure secure transactions, are essential for sustained growth. They contend that well-designed institutions can reduce transaction costs and foster an environment conducive to investment and innovation. The other side posits that while institutions do matter, their impact is often overstated. These scholars maintain that factors such as geography, culture, and historical legacies play equally significant roles in determining economic outcomes. Furthermore, some argue that the specific nature of institutional frameworks can vary widely depending on local contexts, making it difficult to generalize about their universal effects on growth. In essence, the debate hinges on whether institutions are the key determinant or simply one piece of a complex puzzle.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic development is a central theme in this discussion, highlighting how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted distinct approaches based on mortality rates and population density in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, whereas in regions with lower mortality rates, more inclusive institutions were set up to encourage investment and growth. This divergence in institutional frameworks has persisted over time, significantly influencing current income levels per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin elaborates on this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex historical paths taken by different economies, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolved over time. He points out that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction between local conditions and colonial policies underscores the dynamic nature of institutional development and its long-term effects on economic growth.

Institutions matter fundamentally because they shape the incentives for investment, which is crucial for economic development. North posits that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not prioritize long-run growth, leading to the establishment of extractive rather than inclusive institutions in many colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The enduring impact of these colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes highlights their lasting influence. However, Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing efficiency as a driving force behind institutional development. She suggests that distributional conflicts often play a more significant role in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective adds depth to the understanding of how institutions emerge and evolve, emphasizing the importance of power dynamics and social conflicts in institutional design.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the emergence of new financial instruments dramatically lowered the cost of transacting. North (1989, p.9) argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduce transaction costs as economies grow; thus, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies, leading to greater specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This institutional development is crucial for understanding how England's economic growth was fostered by secure property rights. North and Weingast further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in 17th-century England was pivotal, as evidenced by capital markets. Their findings suggest that the regime's commitment to rules underpins economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also caution against overemphasizing institutional changes alone; they note that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England's, suggesting that institutions were not the sole determinant of economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic development.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation, often cited as a determinant of poor institutional quality, does not significantly affect the provision of public goods in African countries. This insight suggests that even ethnically homogeneous societies can struggle with dysfunctional institutions, while heterogeneous ones may still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This nuanced view implies that while colonial institutions may have played a role, other factors such as real wage levels and trends during the colonial period are also crucial for understanding African economic development. This challenges the notion that ethnic diversity is inherently detrimental to economic growth. Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth; they ensure innovations are efficiently used (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets argues that technological advancements necessitate corresponding changes in institutions and ideologies to facilitate sustainable growth. Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, which is essential for economic progress. The interplay between technology and institutions involves resolving conflicts arising from modernization without compromising societal cohesion. Kuznets highlights that the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully managing these growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further underscores that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements alone cannot sustain long-term economic improvement; rent-seeking behaviors can erode gains if institutions fail to adapt. This suggests that while technology drives growth, the efficiency of its use is contingent on adaptive institutional frameworks (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping key economic institutions. This perspective is exemplified by the institutional arrangements of pre-industrial Europe, where serfdom, guilds, and community structures were not merely responses to efficiency concerns but were deeply rooted in the power dynamics between different social groups. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) asserts that these institutions were shaped by elites who used them to maintain their dominance over labor, capital, and political power (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) echo this sentiment, noting that in New World societies with high levels of inequality, institutions were tailored to benefit the elite class through policies that restricted competition and preserved social hierarchies. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of extreme wealth disparities, where institutional frameworks evolved to sustain these inequalities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by noting that while distributional conflicts are critical, they do not operate independently of broader cultural and historical contexts; rather, they interact with shared beliefs and values that also influence institutional outcomes. In this way, the interplay between power dynamics and cultural norms shapes institutions in complex and enduring ways that fundamentally impact economic growth over the long term.

This simplification overlooks distinct phases within the colonial period. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to hypotheses that predict lower production due to exploitative colonial institutions. This dynamic interplay between local actions and colonial responses highlights the complexity of institutional change during this period. This is also seen in how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of these historical dynamics continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as the effects of mistrust are at least twice as large through internal norms than external environments such as the rule of law. This suggests that the long-term impact of colonial institutions on African economies is not merely a product of initial conditions but also a result of evolving interactions and adaptations over time.
