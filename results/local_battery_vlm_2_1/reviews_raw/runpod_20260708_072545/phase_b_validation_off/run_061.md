The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has spurred extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures are essential for fostering economic development. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks alone do not determine economic outcomes; they must be considered alongside other variables. Additionally, there is a debate over whether institutions evolve endogenously in response to economic needs or if they are exogenously imposed by external forces. This disagreement highlights the complexity of understanding the multifaceted relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, where the nature of this relationship remains a subject of intense scholarly inquiry.

The centrality of institutions in economic development is evident in the work of North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29). North emphasises that institutions matter significantly in economic growth and development, challenging neoclassical theories which focus on population and savings as principal determinants. He argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, North and Weingast highlight the political factors underpinning economic growth, focusing on the degree to which regimes are committed to rules governing economic exchange. They argue that credible commitment is essential for fostering secure property rights and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This emphasis on institutions as crucial drivers of economic growth underscores their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth are further illuminated by Acemoglu et al (2001). They argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, significantly affecting current income per capita. In colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, economic outcomes were markedly different from those where extractive states were established (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This differentiation highlights how initial institutional setups can have enduring effects on long-run economic growth, illustrating the path-dependent nature of institutional frameworks. Additionally, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights the importance of considering the long-term evolution of institutions and focusing on short-term factors.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is a crucial aspect highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12). She argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective challenges the belief that efficiency alone drives institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Moreover, Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He argues that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This highlights how understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can have practical implications for policy-making and development strategies.

The enduring impact of initial institutional setups on long-run economic outcomes underscores the importance of considering both endogenous and exogenous factors in institutional development.

They argue that institutions establish incentives for investment and facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, which is crucial for fostering economic development. This institutional framework encourages long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. Extractive institutions, for example, were established where Europeans aimed to rapidly transfer resources back to their home countries. This colonial legacy significantly affected the current income per capita of these regions.

Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) work qualifies this mechanism by highlighting that institutional changes introduced during colonialism played a central role in shaping the long-run development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was influenced by population density and prosperity, which shaped European policies. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by other factors such as demographic characteristics and economic conditions at the time of colonization. Therefore, understanding long-run economic growth requires considering both institutional setups and the broader context in which they operate.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Austin, who highlights that the institutionalist approach has struggled with quantifying its claims and explaining why certain countries adopted growth-promoting institutions while others did not. North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth, as seen in the capital markets during the seventeenth century. This credibility was about rules and also about the institutions enforcing them. Austin's work complements this by showing how colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). These findings suggest that while institutional changes are pivotal, their impact is contingent on the broader political and economic context in which they occur. The complexity of these interactions means that a simplistic view of institutional change as a linear driver of growth overlooks the nuanced interplay between institutions, politics, and economics.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 is marked by factors such as merchant distribution, finance, and climate (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta argue that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe can be attributed to merchant distribution and finance. They suggest that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role today. This perspective challenges the notion that productivity gains were solely due to increased consumption of basic foodstuffs. Meanwhile, van Zanden highlights that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. The shift of economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea further complicates this narrative: after 1740 England became the most expensive country, indicating a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs; however, Amsterdam's wheat prices remained below those in England and France due to abundant supply and British export subsidies. This suggests that while merchant distribution and finance were important, institutional changes, such as investment in agriculture and the role of institutions in bringing about such investments, must also be considered (van Zanden 1999, p.9).

The literature on African women's economic contributions and institutional reforms in Africa highlights significant historical roles played by women in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that African women made important contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, contra views that marginalize their role. During the colonial period, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production. This segregation was reinforced by customary law, which was codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Women's contributions were significant; this underscores the need to recognize the multifaceted ways in which women engaged in economic activities. This pattern illustrates how institutional changes, such as land access policies, can shape economic development trajectories. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, encompassing both historical contributions by women and contemporary institutional reforms.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) jointly establish that colonial legacies in sub-Saharan Africa shaped educational development through distinct institutional pathways, which in turn influenced economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule fostered formal education more than other colonial powers by altering the perceived benefits of education through new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This mechanism is significant because it highlights how colonial policies could create incentives for investment in human capital, a critical factor in long-run economic growth. Simson (2019, p.2) builds on this by examining the post-independence period, where neopatrimonial tendencies often diverted resources away from public goods and services (Simson 2019, p.2). This pattern of resource allocation suggests that while colonial institutions may have laid some groundwork for education, the subsequent political and economic structures often undermined these foundations. Simson's analysis of post-colonial expenditure trends in East Africa reveals that military spending and debt service payments displaced investments in capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between colonial legacies and post-independence policies, where institutional frameworks set during colonial rule continued to influence economic trajectories but were also subject to significant modifications. The enduring impact of these institutional legacies on education and resource allocation highlights the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) find that African economies have been studied through historical economic data and trends and the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Their research focuses on reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, emphasizing the importance of understanding income distribution over time. This emphasis has practical implications for future growth prospects. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) identify that patterns of economic growth were tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These findings underscore the importance of historical data in understanding long-term economic development, particularly how specific conditions can affect future welfare. However, this focus on historical data rather than institutional frameworks limits our ability to fully grasp the mechanisms behind persistent poverty in African economies. This gap highlights the need for more comprehensive analyses that integrate both historical trends and institutional factors to provide a holistic understanding of economic growth in Africa.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) argue that collaborative research is essential to measure economic performance over long periods, which provides foundational data for understanding economic development. This approach has enabled scholars to present detailed overviews of long-term economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.16) underscores the significance of structural changes in economic development by highlighting that urbanization rates serve as key indicators of productivity and economic growth in pre-modern periods. These insights are vital for understanding how historical structural changes can influence long-term economic trajectories. Pamuk (2007, p.23) also points out that the institutional flexibility of regions like Holland facilitated rapid responses to new economic opportunities, illustrating how regional variations in institutional structures can shape economic outcomes. This suggests that while measurement of economic performance is crucial, it must be complemented by an understanding of institutional frameworks to fully grasp long-term economic growth.

The slave trade has left enduring legacies that shape contemporary economic outcomes in Africa by fostering a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) identify two channels through which the slave trade's impact on trust operates, both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law. Their analysis reveals that the internal channel, where individuals develop normative beliefs about trustworthiness shaped by historical experiences, is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores the profound psychological impact of the slave trade on African societies. Nunn further elaborates on this mechanism by highlighting that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, significantly longer than the period of official colonial rule. This extended duration allowed for the deep embedding of mistrust within societal norms and institutions (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn (2008, p.2) also notes that centralized governments with well-developed markets and trading networks were able to support trade with Europeans during this time, suggesting a conditional pattern where some regions' institutional development mitigated the adverse effects of the slave trade. This implies that while the slave trade fostered mistrust, it did not uniformly impede economic development across all African regions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World. The shared claim is that these conflicts drove the development of institutions like serfdom, guilds, and community structures. Ogilvie highlights how beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) note that extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to societies with different degrees of inequality, which affected the course of development through their impact on evolving institutions. This suggests a pattern where initial inequalities shaped subsequent institutional structures, reinforcing distributional conflicts over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that economic historians must carefully incorporate institutions into their research programs to avoid superficial analysis. This underscores the need for rigorous study of how distributional conflicts influence long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective highlights that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to efficiently utilise innovations. Without such institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited by rent-seeking behaviours (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be accompanied by appropriate institutional frameworks to ensure sustainable economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is crucial for understanding both historical and contemporary economic trajectories. Institutions are fundamental because they determine the incentives that drive investment and innovation (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, such as those seen in the creation of the Bank of England, exemplifies how institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This institutional framework is essential for sustaining economic growth over the long term. The historical evidence shows that where secure property rights and effective enforcement are established, economic development flourishes. For example, Acemoglu et al. document how European settlers in colonies like the United States set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Without such institutional progress, technological advancements alone cannot ensure sustainable growth; they must be accompanied by appropriate frameworks to mitigate rent-seeking behaviours and resolve conflicts induced by technological changes.

Institutional frameworks are about economic efficiency and also about resolving distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical record of colonialism provides a clear example: in colonies where European settlers were absent or competed less directly with indigenous populations, institutional prescriptions often changed in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The legacy of these institutions persists today. Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin further elaborates on this by highlighting how the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is found in the interaction between institutions and industrialization during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were critical in shaping current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15).
