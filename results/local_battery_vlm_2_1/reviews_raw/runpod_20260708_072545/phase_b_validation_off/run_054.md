The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it challenges economists to understand what drives prosperity over centuries. The stakes are high because the answer determines how societies can best organize themselves for sustained growth. One live disagreement revolves around whether institutional structures are the primary drivers or merely one factor among many, such as geography or population density. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment have been crucial in fostering economic development. Others posit that these institutions must be understood within a broader context of historical processes and political dynamics.

A second disagreement concerns the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, while others highlight the role of institutions as intermediaries that shape how other factors, such as technology or human capital, affect growth. This debate is crucial because it informs policy recommendations and our understanding of how to promote sustainable development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, which significantly influenced current income levels. These institutions ranged from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The presence or absence of these institutions was influenced by geography and also by historical and political factors. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the evolution of secure property rights in England, facilitated by institutional changes, was critical for economic growth. They argue that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This security is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by examining how different historical contexts have influenced institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that this was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century. This perspective underscores how historical processes can shape institutional outcomes and, consequently, economic growth.

The debate on institutions and long-run economic growth also involves understanding the broader context of historical evolution. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that economists see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe. This view underscores the importance of considering long-term dynamics of institutional change while focusing on snapshot comparisons.

Institutional quality is a critical factor in economic development, but it must be understood within the broader context of historical processes and political dynamics. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. He emphasizes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of economic growth.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth through an evolving process. Austin (2008, p.15) notes that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, contrary to the hypothesis that they would opt for lower production equilibria. This dynamic highlights how institutions can adapt over time in response to changing circumstances.

The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional quality in promoting economic development.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The establishment of extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates among Europeans was a key mechanism through which these policies impacted economic outcomes. These institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists, leading to persistent economic disparities.

In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as parts of Africa and Latin America, they established extractive states aimed at rapid resource extraction. This approach was different from settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on economic development in former colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s findings underscore that the initial institutional frameworks laid down by colonial powers have profound and enduring impacts on a country's economic trajectory, shaping everything from property rights to judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This influence underscores the critical role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. This perspective highlights the role of institutions in facilitating the diffusion of knowledge and innovation, which in turn drives long-run economic growth. The process by which technological progress interacts with institutional frameworks is complex and multifaceted. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that institutions play a critical role in ensuring that technological innovations are effectively utilized and integrated into the economy.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this by noting that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments for its efficient use. He argues that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, necessitate corresponding changes in societal structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that institutions must adapt to accommodate new technologies, fostering an environment where innovation can thrive. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving conflicts arising from technological changes, thereby ensuring that economic growth is not impeded by social unrest (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of political stability and effective governance in supporting long-run economic development.

The interplay between technology and institutions is evident in historical contexts where technological progress has led to significant economic growth. For example, the Industrial Revolution in Europe was marked by substantial advancements in technology, which were supported by institutional changes that facilitated the diffusion of knowledge and innovation. This period saw the establishment of scholarly societies and other formal institutions aimed at reducing access costs to new ideas (Mokyr 2005, p.49). These institutions played a pivotal role in fostering an environment conducive to technological progress and economic growth.

The relationship between technology, institutions, and long-run economic growth is further complicated by the need for coordination among various stakeholders. Mokyr (2005, p.49) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights that effective institutional frameworks are essential for coordinating efforts across different sectors, ensuring that technological advancements are harnessed efficiently. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr terms it, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, indicating the need for continuous institutional adaptation to sustain long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth through technological advancements is undeniable, but requires ongoing efforts to adapt and coordinate.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlight that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This divergence was driven by structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to significant shifts in economic development across regions. The service sector played a crucial role in this process, with merchant distribution and finance contributing to high productivity in traded goods sectors in northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This regional variation in productivity is evident in the differing economic trajectories of Tuscany and Holland post-Black Death, where institutional flexibility enabled Holland to rapidly respond to new opportunities while Tuscany struggled under strengthening monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23).

The wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 further underscores these structural changes. This divergence was due to geographical factors and also high levels of investment in agriculture and institutional changes that facilitated economic growth in northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The varying urbanisation rates across Europe during this period also indicate differing levels of productivity and economic development, with structural change being a key characteristic of early modern economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.16).

The great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth through regional variations in productivity and structural changes.

Broadberry and Gardner highlight that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This view challenges persistent narratives of African poverty. Broadberry and Gardner's argument is grounded in historical national accounting, which reveals a wealth of economic data for colonial-era Africa. This data shows that export production grew significantly in many regions during this period due to foreign trade. The timing and scale of growth varied across countries owing to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels supports Broadberry and Gardner's findings, demonstrating that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their work shows that the economic development trajectory in British Africa varied across regions, with some colonies experiencing significant growth during their colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The variation in growth patterns suggests that while certain factors may have temporarily affected African welfare development, these conditions were not necessarily structural impediments to long-term growth. This nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history underscores the importance of considering regional variations and specific historical contexts when assessing institutional frameworks' role in shaping economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a unique impact on educational development compared to other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial administration's emphasis on formal education fostered the development of local institutions, which altered African perceptions of the benefits associated with formal education. This shift was driven by new social and political frameworks that encouraged investment in human capital through education, which in turn influenced economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.15). In this context, the British colonial administration's policies played a significant role in shaping educational outcomes, as they introduced institutions that incentivised Africans to pursue formal education. Nunn qualifies this by highlighting that Africa's slave trades also played a role in shaping economic development, suggesting that colonial educational policies alone do not fully account for long-term economic outcomes.

One specific mechanism they jointly establish is the role of indigenous economic and political action in shaping institutional responses. Austin (2008, p.2), for example, shows how colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor. Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this by arguing that Africa's new economic history can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This indicates that indigenous economic actions can drive institutional changes that foster growth, even under colonial rule. Therefore, while indigenous economic and political actions can influence institutional development, the broader context of colonial governance remains a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth.

The development of the Bank of England (1694) and new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs, illustrates this point. This period saw a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, driven by fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War; these institutions were underpinned by credible commitment mechanisms that ensured enforcement. The development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This institutional evolution fostered a growing proportion of transactions in GNP, as specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that while institutional developments are crucial for economic growth, other factors such as bureaucratic structures may also play a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. The evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as those that emerged in seventeenth-century England, was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework provided the necessary credibility to ensure that investments were protected over time. As North notes, "The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting" (North 1989, p.9), underscoring how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and promote economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the different colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. In colonies where settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic development was encouraged (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which hindered long-term economic growth. These contrasting outcomes highlight how institutions can either facilitate or impede economic development based on their design and enforcement.

The role of institutions is interconnected with other factors. For instance, the bureaucratic structures inherited from historical periods, such as those in France under Louis XIV, also influence economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that while institutional developments are crucial for economic growth, they interact with broader political and administrative contexts to shape long-run economic performance.
