The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue at the heart of economic development debates. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustained economic progress. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions themselves drive economic growth, or if other factors such as geographic endowments, cultural beliefs, or colonial legacies are more determinative. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current economic outcomes by determining property rights and governance structures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Some scholars suggest that pre-existing cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions to reflect underlying power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The debate extends to the effectiveness of institutional reforms, with some advocating for a focus on 'good' institutions as crucial for providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8), while others question whether such reforms can truly address deep-seated structural issues. A shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth and development: they do so by influencing the security of property rights, and thus affecting transaction costs and market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The mechanisms through which institutions exert this influence remain a subject of ongoing scholarly inquiry.

North's work on institutions and economic growth in England argues that new financial instruments and political institutions during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and subsequent financial innovations are described as a "financial revolution" that enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework allowed England to overcome fiscal crises and establish a stable political environment conducive to economic development.

The evolution of these institutions involved establishing credible commitments that underpinned secure property rights. North and Weingast underscore that the political commitment to these rules was crucial for sustaining economic growth, as it ensured that the regime remained bound by the established institutions (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Institutional changes impact economic growth in complex ways. While North and Weingast's analysis shows a significant improvement in England's economic performance due to institutional reforms, other research suggests that similar improvements were observed in France despite different institutional frameworks. The development of secure property rights and credible commitments through political institutions remains pivotal in understanding long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. present compelling evidence that colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies, showing how extractive institutions were established to maximize European rents (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They argue that the mortality rates of Europeans influenced their settlement patterns and the types of institutions they implemented. Colonial powers actively chose these institutions to serve their interests. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these extractive institutions persisted over time, locking former colonies into patterns of underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional legacy helps explain the stark differences in economic performance observed today between countries with different colonial histories.

However, the persistence of these institutions is not solely due to their initial design but also to the political dynamics that sustained them. Acemoglu et al. note that politically powerful groups often resist institutional changes that could threaten their rents or power (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This resistance can prevent the adoption of more growth-oriented institutions, even when such changes might benefit the broader economy in the long run. Former colonies burdened with extractive institutions face significant barriers to economic development, as these institutions are designed to maximize short-term rents.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49)  argues that the increase in "useful" knowledge propels economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that technological progress and its efficient use are crucial for long-term growth; however, he also notes that this process necessitates institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from modern technology (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The interplay between technological innovation and the need for corresponding institutional changes is critical. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that such adjustments must be made without weakening the political fabric of society. This suggests a reciprocal relationship where institutions must adapt to technological advancements to sustain economic growth. Technological progress alone does not guarantee sustained growth; Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advances and prevent rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Thus, while useful knowledge drives economic growth, it requires supportive institutions to ensure sustainability.

Distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that the underlying cause of these institutions is rooted in the power dynamics between social classes. This perspective suggests that economic institutions are shaped by the interests of dominant groups who use their influence to structure rules and regulations in their favor. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the community guilds and merchant guilds were structured to maintain control over resources and limit competition from outsiders, thereby perpetuating existing power structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This dynamic is evident in the persistence of extreme wealth disparities, which were reinforced by institutional frameworks that ensured the elite maintained their privileged status over generations (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This nuanced understanding underscores that while distributional conflicts shape institutions, these institutions themselves become powerful tools for maintaining existing inequalities over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust which persists today. The historical trauma of slavery created deep-seated norms of mistrust among African populations, which continue to affect intergroup relations and economic interactions. The mechanisms through which this mistrust operates are multifaceted: it influences both external factors such as the rule of law and internal norms governing individual behavior. Austin highlights the importance of understanding these historical legacies in the context of institutional evolution, noting that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that while external factors like colonial governance played a role, internal dynamics within African societies were also crucial in shaping contemporary trust levels. Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize the importance of trust for various aspects of development, including economic growth, international trade, and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring impact of historical mistrust on contemporary trust levels underscores the need to consider long-term cultural and institutional factors in understanding Africa's economic trajectory.

Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence, challenging conventional views of Africa's economic stagnation (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This revisionist perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gupta's argument that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. The key mechanism driving this early economic growth in Anglophone African economies was the expansion of foreign trade and export markets, which stimulated local production. The timing and scale of this growth varied significantly across regions due to factors such as local resource endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The role of these institutions is crucial: they facilitated or hindered the integration of local economies into global trade networks, thereby influencing economic performance. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta highlight that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector was not solely due to production efficiency but also to merchant distribution and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that institutional support for commerce played a pivotal role in driving economic growth during the early modern period. This growth was unevenly distributed, benefiting skilled laborers more than unskilled ones. This pattern of uneven development underscores the complex interplay between institutions, trade, and economic outcomes, showing that while institutions can foster growth, they also shape its distribution.

Institutional reforms in Africa are pivotal for poverty reduction and have been shown to be effective across diverse contexts (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These interventions are often targeted at improving governance structures. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not inherently linked to ethnic fragmentation, indicating that institutional development can transcend societal divisions. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty is a product of specific structural conditions. They suggest that understanding the long-term economic development in Africa requires examining real wage levels and trends, which can inform future growth strategies. Hopkins also emphasizes that the focus on institutions has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain traction among development specialists, highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment. This suggests a broader implication: while institutional reforms are crucial for poverty reduction, their effectiveness is contingent upon creating an environment conducive to sustained investment and development. This underscores the need for a comprehensive historical analysis to inform effective institutional reforms aimed at long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) highlight that women played pivotal roles in African economic development, both before and during colonial rule. These contributions were often overshadowed by the codification of customary law under British colonial rule, which marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities.

The introduction of a dual legal system, comprising European courts based on common and civil law alongside native courts, further entrenched patriarchal structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Colonial policies often relegated women to less productive lands for subsistence farming, while men were more involved in cash crop production. This gendered division of labor was exacerbated by the codification of customary law, which formalized and reinforced existing power imbalances. This migration was a driving force behind the economic revolution that positioned the Gold Coast as a leading global producer of cocoa by 1911.

The demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit analyses made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational infrastructure laid the groundwork for future economic growth by equipping Africans with skills that could be leveraged in various sectors, including agriculture and trade. British rule impacted formal education across regions in varied ways, reflecting the complex interplay between colonial policies and local socio-economic conditions. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This change in educational landscape had profound implications for African societies, shaping future generations' economic opportunities and development trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented across various historical contexts. Institutions, as North (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argues, are the rules of the game that structure interactions between individuals and groups. These rules can either facilitate or hinder economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate this point by highlighting how European colonizers established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates. In areas with high mortality, extractive institutions were implemented, which focused on rapid resource transfer to the metropole rather than long-term economic development. Where Europeans settled permanently, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework set the stage for divergent economic trajectories among former colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on the impact of colonial institutions, emphasizing that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists. Similarly, Austin (2008, p.2) notes the importance of institutional evolution in shaping economic outcomes. The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as North (1989, p.6) argues, are critical for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. These findings underscore the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic conditions.

The historical evidence from former colonies reveals that institutions introduced during colonial rule have had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that variations in institutional quality across regions are closely linked to current income per capita levels, showing the persistence of these historical legacies. These findings are supported by North's work (North 1989, p.9), which highlights how financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth. The shared finding (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) is that institutional frameworks, whether colonial or indigenous, shape economic outcomes by influencing investment incentives and resource allocation.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between local socio-economic conditions and colonial policies. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This aligns with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that shared beliefs and institutional structures play a significant role in economic outcomes. The historical evidence from former colonies shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but also by the effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law and promoting progressive policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that institutional reforms, informed by new economic history, can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that institutions introduced during colonial rule played a central role in long-run development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
