The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains an active area of scholarly debate. The stakes are high for both theoretical and practical reasons: the underlying mechanisms that drive sustained economic development are not fully understood; moreover, many policymakers look to historical institutional change as a guide for current reform efforts. One live disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions shape long-run growth through their impact on investment incentives or other channels such as transaction costs. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they create and enforce property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and encouraging economic activity (North 1989, p.6). Others contend that institutions' role in promoting economic development is more nuanced: they may be shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritize rent extraction over long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Furthermore, the impact of institutions on long-term growth can vary across contexts and historical periods. This complexity underscores the need for a deeper understanding of how different types of institutions influence economic outcomes over time.

The development of financial institutions provides an illustrative example of how institutional change can facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, along with new financial instruments, significantly lowered the cost of transacting and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional shift was a response to fiscal crises and involved trading certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. Such political institutions, which specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms, are crucial for fostering economic growth by enabling market exchanges and encouraging investment. These developments highlight the critical role that institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on transaction costs and investment incentives.

Some scholars argue that the influence of institutions on economic growth is not solely determined by their efficiency but also by distributional conflicts. These conflicts can shape core economic institutions in ways that prioritize the interests of powerful groups over overall economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). For example, serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe were influenced by distributional struggles rather than purely efficient considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize long-run growth. Instead, it suggests that institutions may be shaped by political dynamics and power struggles, which can lead to outcomes that benefit certain groups at the expense of others. Understanding these distributional conflicts is essential for comprehending how institutions shape economic growth over the long term.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex issue that involves both geographic and institutional factors. Some scholars argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective emphasizes the importance of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. However, other scholars contend that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This view suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is more nuanced and depends on various factors, including indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by local economic and political dynamics. The complexity of these interactions underscores the need for a more detailed understanding of how colonial institutions influenced long-term economic development.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have played a significant role in shaping the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, according to scholars who emphasize the importance of institutional factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective argues that the interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of these colonies. However, other scholars suggest that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa by providing insights into how 'good' institutions matter (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This view highlights the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth. By examining the experiences of former European colonies and contemporary African countries, scholars can gain valuable insights into how institutional reforms can promote economic development and reduce poverty over the long term.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that institutions like the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering long-run economic growth. This development reflects a broader pattern where political institutions that enforce efficient property rights facilitate market exchanges. As such, the aggregate volume of transactions grows, and transaction costs fall relative to GDP as economies specialize and divide labor more efficiently (North 1989, p.6).

However, the implications of these institutional changes extend beyond mere economic efficiency. North and Weingast argue that the political commitment to enforcing property rights is crucial for sustained economic growth. This commitment underpins the development of markets and ensures that rules governing economic exchange are consistently enforced (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of England's financial revolution, as highlighted by the evidence from capital markets, underscores this point: England's institutions not only supported market exchanges but also ensured credible commitments to property rights enforcement. This political factor was essential for fostering long-term economic growth and development.

The significance of these institutional changes is further illuminated by comparative historical analysis. This perspective challenges simplistic views that attribute economic growth solely to financial or bureaucratic structures. Instead, it emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms.

The variation in European mortality rates led to different colonization strategies and consequently different institutional setups. In high-mortality regions, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, while in low-mortality areas they established institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment. These differing institutional frameworks, whether extractive or growth-oriented, persisted long after decolonization, shaping current economic outcomes. The specific mechanism by which these colonial institutions influenced economic growth is through their impact on investment and productivity. This dynamic underscores how colonial legacies continue to influence economic trajectories by shaping the incentives and constraints within which contemporary economies operate.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, suggesting instead that a more nuanced understanding is necessary. The thesis does not account for how colonial governments adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives and actions; Austin argues that colonial administrations seeking fiscal security encouraged indigenous commodity production when settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic reflects the role of political and economic forces in shaping institutional outcomes, which are often overlooked by more simplistic models. The historical context of African development is particularly illustrative: Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This enduring cultural impact underscores how historical events can shape long-run economic trajectories by influencing social norms and institutional frameworks. The legacy of colonial institutions is not merely about the initial conditions they set but also about their dynamic interaction with local economies and societies over time.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms necessary to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective is echoed by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who emphasize that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They suggest that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa could reveal how specific historical conditions have affected long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This focus on institutional frameworks and their impact on economic outcomes is crucial for identifying pathways to sustainable growth in Africa. This nuanced view suggests that while institutional quality is essential, other factors such as governance and policy reforms are also critical in shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Broadberry and Gardner contend that Sub-Saharan Africa's economic performance in the early twentieth century has been underappreciated (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta also note that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), this is a pattern that can be seen in other regions, including Africa. This perspective challenges the notion that African economies were uniformly stagnant during colonial times; instead, it suggests that economic growth was tied to foreign trade and export markets, which varied significantly across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, while these historical patterns of growth are evident, the role of institutional frameworks in shaping these outcomes remains less clear. Broadberry and Gupta note that high productivity in traded goods sectors could arise through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, suggesting that service sectors played a crucial role in economic growth during this period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The implication is that while institutions may not be the sole determinant of economic performance, they interact with other factors to influence long-run economic trajectories. Thus, understanding the specific mechanisms by which institutions shaped economic outcomes requires a nuanced examination of historical data and contextual factors, including but not limited to colonial legacies and trade dynamics.

van Zanden (1999, p.1); (Pamuk 2007, p.23) examine long-term economic changes in Europe, focusing on shifts in economic development and urbanization rates. These authors highlight that significant structural changes occurred across different regions of Europe during the early modern period. Van Zanden shows that the standard of living did not necessarily improve with economic development, as measured by urbanisation ratios (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This finding underscores a key pattern: economic growth in early modern Europe was not uniformly experienced across regions or translated into uniform improvements in living standards. This divergence suggests that local institutional contexts played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, even within the same overarching period of European development. However, Pamuk also notes that economic growth was not solely driven by institutional factors but was influenced by broader structural changes initiated by events like the Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Thus, while institutions may have facilitated or hindered economic development, they operated within a complex web of historical and environmental factors that ultimately determined regional trajectories.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that gender-based access to resources significantly impacts economic outcomes during African development. They argue that women's contributions to agricultural productivity were substantial but often overlooked, particularly in the context of cash crops. This dynamic was influenced by colonial policies that codified customary laws favoring men, which limited women's access to productive land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the introduction of formal education systems under British rule altered the perceived benefits of education for African communities.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are primary drivers in long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological innovation is the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that technological advances necessitate institutional adjustments for efficient use. For instance, modern technology requires changes in rural lifestyles and family structures to accommodate new production methods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Moreover, Mokyr (2005, p.49) recognizes the role of institutions in mitigating coordination failures which arise from technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54), suggesting that institutions play a secondary role in facilitating economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state is crucial for resolving conflicts induced by growth, emphasizing the importance of political stability in sustaining economic progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Institutions do not drive technological innovation but rather create an environment conducive to its adoption and sustainability.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. These authors contend that the distribution of power and resources among different groups in society significantly influences institutional development, often leading to outcomes that favor elites at the expense of broader societal efficiency. This perspective is illustrated by the historical trajectory of New World societies, where institutions evolved to entrench inequality. The differential factor endowments of these colonies, particularly the abundance of land relative to labor and the resulting extreme wealth disparities, fostered institutional frameworks that perpetuated elite dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). For instance, policies restricting competition and limiting access to resources were common, thereby sustaining the power of elites while hindering overall economic development. Ogilvie further underscores that distributional conflicts are not merely incidental to institutional formation but are foundational to understanding why certain institutions emerge and endure.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. show how European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, which had lasting impacts on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North highlights that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for long-term economic prosperity. Austin argues that colonial governments often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between institutions and economic development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is also significant. Ogilvie demonstrates that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution rather than abstract notions of efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. further illustrate how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that profoundly affected the development trajectories of former colonies. Hopkins emphasizes the importance of good institutions in providing incentives for investment and development, which is crucial for economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings on how colonial institutions influenced long-run development outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter significantly in shaping long-term economic growth, as shown by the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are critical for economic growth. This is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of how different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional outcomes (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

However, the precise role of distributional conflicts in shaping these institutions remains a point of contention. While Ogilvie emphasizes that distributional conflicts are foundational to understanding why certain institutions emerge and endure, Acemoglu et al. focus on how politically powerful groups shape institutions to maximize their rents (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparison of institutional development in Latin America versus North America, where differences in colonial institutions shaped by distributional conflicts and political power dynamics led to divergent economic outcomes.
