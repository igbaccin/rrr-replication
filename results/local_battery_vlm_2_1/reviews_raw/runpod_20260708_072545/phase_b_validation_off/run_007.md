The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and historical development. Institutions are widely recognised as crucial for fostering economic growth through the provision of secure property rights and efficient market exchanges (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Yet, there is a live disagreement about how exactly institutions influence this process: some scholars argue that institutions shape long-run growth through their impact on transaction costs and property rights, while others contend that distributional conflicts are more determinant. Furthermore, there exists debate over the extent to which colonial institutions have influenced contemporary economic outcomes in former colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These differences highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within specific historical and geographical contexts.

The question of whether institutions influence long-run growth through transaction costs and property rights is central to debates about economic development. North argues that the evolution of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms is critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). Secure property rights, in turn, facilitate market exchanges and support a growing economy by enabling long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin agrees that institutions are vital for economic growth but adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. Austin argues that the institutionalist approach needs to be enriched with quantitative testing and better explanations for why certain countries adopt growth-enhancing institutions while others do not (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both political and historical factors.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. A key mechanism through which this occurred is the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England, which facilitated long-term capital markets by reducing transaction costs. The financial revolution enabled more efficient market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was not merely an economic phenomenon but also a political one, as it involved a trade-off between certain rights and parliamentary revenues, driven by fiscal crises (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The political commitment to these institutions was critical for their success. However, the extent of this commitment varied across countries; North (1989, p.9) argues that England's unique political context allowed it to develop growth-enhancing institutions while France, despite similar economic performance initially, did not experience the same institutional changes.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies ( (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)). Acemoglu et al. illustrate that Europeans established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, prioritizing rapid resource transfer to the metropole over local investment and development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework was maintained by politically powerful groups who maximized their rents at the expense of long-run growth ( (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)). The persistence of these extractive institutions shaped the economic trajectory of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. note that "equilibrium institutions" may be designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists rather than foster long-term development, highlighting how colonial policies created enduring institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions constrained local growth by limiting investment and enforcing rules that benefited the colonizers over the indigenous population. Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that the interaction between these institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This suggests that while extractive institutions hindered growth, they did not entirely preclude it, especially where industrialization offered new avenues for economic advancement.

This period also saw the Black Death trigger significant long-term structural changes in Europe that led to notable economic divergences by 1450. Pamuk and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9); (Pamuk 2007, p.23) argue that this period saw the emergence of a wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions, driven largely by institutional flexibility. This flexibility allowed new towns to flourish in northwestern Europe through rural manufacturing under weak monopolies. Tuscany experienced stagnation due to strengthened existing monopolies. The economic divergence was further reinforced by shifts in urbanization rates. Pamuk (2007, p.16) notes that the key characteristic of economic growth during this era was structural change, exemplified by the shift from agrarian to industrial economies. This institutional flexibility and structural change were crucial for long-run economic growth within Europe.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) both examine how specific institutional structures in pre-industrial Europe shaped economic development by focusing on the role of distributional conflicts within core institutions. These authors argue that these institutions, such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were not primarily driven by efficiency but rather by the resolution of power struggles between different social groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates this point with the example of guilds, whose rules were often designed to protect the interests of craft masters at the expense of journeymen and apprentices. This focus on distributional conflicts highlights how economic institutions can be shaped by political dynamics. Austin's analysis builds on these observations, noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability shows that even within the constraints of pre-industrial societies, there was room for institutional change driven by local contexts. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how pre-industrial institutions functioned within their specific historical contexts.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, argue that historical poverty in Africa was not an immutable condition. This perspective challenges the notion of persistent poverty by highlighting that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic performance was intricately tied to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), illustrating how external economic factors drove growth within these regions. The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize the need for pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates to fully understand these economic dynamics. Their reconstruction of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies over their period of colonial rule provides a deeper understanding of long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue that Africa's slave trades have had direct adverse effects on long-run economic development over the past 500 years. Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) further shows that this impact is not merely a result of the slave trade's influence on the external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, but also through the internal norms of mistrust fostered by the slave trades. The legacy of these enduring cultural factors is evident in their impact on contemporary economic development, as they continue to hinder progress by fostering a climate of distrust. Nunn's work complements recent studies that highlight the role of trust in various aspects of social and economic life (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), underscoring how deeply the slave trades have influenced Africa's economic trajectory. As a result, the long-term effects of these historical events challenge views attributing Africa’s underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge propels economic development. Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating this process by reducing access costs to new knowledge and fostering an environment conducive to innovation. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights how institutions can mitigate coordination failures, ensuring that technological advancements are sustained over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that the interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is complex. Conflicts arising from growth-induced changes must be resolved without excessive costs to society for modern economic growth to be possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are essential in harnessing technological progress, their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt to and manage the socio-political challenges that accompany economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their ability to mitigate transaction costs and foster secure property rights. North argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs in a growing economy (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges by ensuring predictable and stable economic interactions, which in turn encourages investment and innovation. The historical development of institutions like the Bank of England illustrates how secure property rights can drive economic growth by lowering transaction costs and enhancing financial stability (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of political institutions that enforce property rights is crucial for sustainable economic development. North and Weingast show that credible commitments to institutional rules are essential for fostering economic growth, as they provide a stable framework for market activities (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions ensure that the regime is bound by the rules governing economic exchange, which in turn enhances confidence in long-term investments. The case of seventeenth-century England demonstrates how political stability and secure property rights can create a conducive environment for economic development.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth highlights the enduring effects of institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different types of institutions in colonies, which significantly influenced their current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Colonies with extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, experienced slower economic growth compared to those with inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and innovation. This historical evidence underscores how initial institutional choices can have long-lasting effects on economic development.

The reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the types of institutions introduced during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al also show that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutional framework established during colonial times shaped the economic trajectories of these regions, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive ones fostering it. This dynamic is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is found in the comparative analysis of colonial institutional legacies. The divergent paths of economic development in colonies with extractive versus inclusive institutions provide a clear measure of their impact (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This evidence shows that while institutions are essential for harnessing technological progress, their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt to and manage socio-political challenges accompanying economic growth.
