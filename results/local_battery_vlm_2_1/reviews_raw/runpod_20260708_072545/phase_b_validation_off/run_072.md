The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is whether and how institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing social interactions, can explain why some societies prosper while others languish. The debate hinges on two positions which diverge over the causal efficacy of institutions: one position holds that institutions are crucial drivers of economic growth, while another posits that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic endowments, play a more significant role in determining long-term economic outcomes. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of historical development paths.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping economic growth often centres on the question of whether institutions are exogenously determined by cultural factors or endogenously developed through historical processes. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Institutions matter because they provide a framework for secure property rights and enforceable contracts, which in turn foster economic activity. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive or inclusive institutions, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values are more fundamental in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The significance of institutions in long-run economic growth is further complicated by the role of historical context. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and low transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Hopkins, who highlights how institutional reforms can inform poverty reduction strategies, particularly among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin maintains that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the institutionalist approach needs to account for why institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This points to a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time, influenced by both exogenous and endogenous factors.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for establishing secure property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the credible commitment to enforce rules governing economic exchange, underpinned by political factors, is essential. This commitment facilitated the development of capital markets in England, which was crucial for economic growth. The evidence from capital markets shows this success (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The degree to which regimes were committed to enforce rules played a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth; it focuses instead on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden (1999, p.1) studies wage patterns across European cities and regions to reveal that the relationship between economic development and changes in living standards is not straightforward. This pattern is complicated by Pamuk's (2007) evidence of a regional economic shift towards the North Sea countries, where economic growth led to rising food prices relative to other areas. The structural shifts observed by Pamuk (2007, p.16) are particularly relevant because they highlight how urbanization and commercialization in regions like Holland were facilitated by institutional flexibility. This institutional context enabled new towns to flourish under weak monopolies, contrasting with the stronger monopolistic structures in Tuscany that hindered similar growth (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Pamuk's evidence shows that economic development led to a rise in relative prices of basic foodstuffs, reflecting broader structural changes in Europe during this period. The implications for long-run economic growth are significant, as they suggest that institutional frameworks capable of adapting to new opportunities were crucial for sustaining development over time.

Colonial rule in Africa shaped institutional change through both negotiation and imposition, highlighting African initiatives alongside exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) demonstrates that colonial institutions influenced economic development by altering incentives for investment. This influence was not uniform across regions; instead, it varied based on the specific institutional frameworks imposed by different colonial powers. British rule, in particular, fostered the development of formal education more effectively than other colonial regimes (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) elaborates that this educational impact was driven by African leaders' and families' cost-benefit analyses, which were influenced by new social and political institutions introduced during British rule. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this narrative by noting that the effectiveness of these institutions depended on broader governance structures, including the quality of public goods provision and the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This interplay between colonial imposition and local adaptation underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge are primary drivers of long-term economic development. This perspective is grounded in the idea that advancements in technology necessitate institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use and integration into existing societal structures. Mokyr highlights that technological improvements alone are insufficient for sustainable economic growth; they must be complemented by institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets elaborates on this point, noting that modern technology often conflicts with traditional modes of life and requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve these conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial for economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes that coordination failures can impede technological progress, necessitating policies to rectify these issues (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

This perspective also recognizes that institutional progress can be non-linear. This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding both the benefits and challenges associated with integrating technological advancements into existing economic frameworks.

The historical impacts of the slave trade on Africa highlight how the legacy of mistrust and underdevelopment can persist long after the initial event (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade instilled a deep-seated culture of mistrust within African societies, which continues to affect economic development today. This mistrust manifests both externally through reduced trust in institutions and internally through norms of mistrust among individuals. The internal channel, where mistrust is deeply ingrained within societal norms, is particularly significant, being at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates that these adverse effects extend into subsequent colonial and post-colonial eras beyond the period of slave trade. For instance, centralized governments like the Kongo kingdom were better equipped to facilitate trade due to their economic and social institutions, highlighting how institutional structures can mitigate or exacerbate the long-term impacts of historical events (Nunn 2008, p.19). The persistence of mistrust and underdevelopment in Africa underscores the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes.

Ogilvie and Austin assert that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions, alongside efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). They contend that these distributional conflicts often arise from struggles over resources and power among different social groups. One specific mechanism through which this occurs is the influence of powerful elites who shape institutional frameworks to maintain their dominance. For instance, Ogilvie highlights how serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply rooted in power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores that institutions are instruments of control as well as efficiency-enhancing tools. Ogilvie cautions against over-simplifying these dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), suggesting that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they must be examined within the broader context of institutional evolution. This nuanced view highlights how economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by a complex interplay of power struggles and efficiency considerations, with implications for understanding long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly influenced by the interplay between political and economic forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, not simply by geographic factors. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), illustrate how political power dynamics can shape economic outcomes. North (1989, p.6) reinforces this idea by highlighting that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. This shared finding shows that the creation of institutions that enforce secure property rights is essential for long-term economic development.

The evolution of these institutions is driven by distributional conflicts as well as efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that understanding how institutions are shaped by power struggles and rent-seeking behavior is crucial. Austin (2008, p.15), similarly, demonstrates that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a complex interplay between local forces and external influences. This nuanced view highlights how economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by a complex interplay of power struggles and efficiency considerations.

The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth is further underscored by the role they play in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) explains that the development of new financial instruments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Hopkins (2009, p.8), similarly, emphasizes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The historical evidence from the English financial revolution and the role of colonial administrations in shaping economic policies shows that institutions matter.

The remaining disagreement centers on whether efficiency or distributional conflicts primarily shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), however, show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This empirical comparison of institutional outcomes across different colonial settings settles the debate by demonstrating that both efficiency considerations and distributional conflicts play a role in shaping economic institutions.
