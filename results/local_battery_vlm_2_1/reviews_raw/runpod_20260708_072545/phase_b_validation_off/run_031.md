The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central topic in economic research, with scholars debating both the mechanisms and the extent to which institutions drive economic development. At stake is the fundamental question of what determines sustained prosperity over generations; is it primarily the quality of institutions that facilitate economic activity or are other factors more decisive? One live disagreement centres on the causality between institutions and growth: some argue that institutions are the primary drivers, while others contend that external factors such as geography or culture play a more significant role. Another point of contention revolves around the types of institutions that matter most: are extractive institutions that benefit a few at the expense of many just as effective in promoting long-term economic development as inclusive ones? The interplay between these factors shapes our understanding of economic growth and informs policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development.

The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth has been shaped by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2), who argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, with significant long-term effects on income per capita. These scholars highlight that institutions in high-mortality colonies were extractive; Europeans established states mainly to transfer resources to the metropole and did not focus on local economic development. This perspective is complemented by Austin (2008, p.15), who critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and argues that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, highlighting the adaptive nature of institutional responses to local conditions. Together, these studies underscore how initial colonization strategies and subsequent institutional adaptations have shaped long-term economic trajectories in former colonies.

Institutional frameworks are pivotal in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs and ensuring secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North (1989, p.6) argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for long-term economic development. This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's call to transform institutions from a fashionable concept into an indispensable tool for economic historians, emphasizing their role in shaping economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The creation of secure property rights and the reduction of transaction costs are essential components of institutional frameworks that foster growth.

The nature of these institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, plays a crucial role in determining long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that equilibrium institutions may be crafted to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, emphasizing the need to understand the motivations behind institutional design. This perspective is supported by Hopkins' work on African development, which underscores the significance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and fostering economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The quality and inclusiveness of institutions are critical factors that shape long-term economic trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by their interaction with other factors such as population density and prosperity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize how these interactions influence the policies pursued by European colonizers, shaping the institutional landscape of former colonies. The interplay between institutions and other factors such as population density and prosperity highlights the complex nature of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, a key factor in economic development.

The relationship between transaction costs and economic growth is complex. While falling transaction costs per exchange can indicate progress, the aggregate volume of transactions also grows. This expansion reflects increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the total number of exchanges. Thus, the growth in the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP is not merely an indicator of rising costs but rather a sign of economic complexity and efficiency.

North and Weingast further highlight the importance of credible commitment by regimes to property rights and market rules. This commitment underpins long-term economic growth, as it ensures stability and predictability in economic interactions (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and promoting economic development. This commitment also extends to the enforcement mechanisms that ensure these rules are upheld, creating a stable environment conducive to market activities.

Their core argument revolves around the divergence in colonization strategies employed by Europeans across different colonies. These extractive states differed from settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled and implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development, as extractive institutions hindered long-term growth by prioritizing short-term resource extraction over sustainable development. The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to influence current economic outcomes. This institutional design reflects the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which prioritized immediate gains over sustainable development. The legacy of these extractive institutions continues to shape economic disparities among former colonies today, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on contemporary economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks differently in societies established by European colonial powers, influencing long-term economic trajectories. They assert that the degree of inequality in wealth and political power from the outset led to varying institutions across these societies. This framework is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights how Africa's slave trades over nearly 500 years profoundly influenced its subsequent economic development. The influence of such historical factors on institutional frameworks was immediate and persisted over centuries, affecting long-term economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) note that the initial abundance of land and natural resources in New World societies did not uniformly lead to high living standards due to extreme inequality in wealth and political power. This inequality, in turn, influenced the evolution of institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating economic disparities. Nunn (2008, p.19) underscores this by noting how centralized governments and well-developed markets facilitated trade, suggesting that institutional frameworks capable of supporting such activities were crucial for economic development. Thus, the legacy of these historical contexts continues to shape contemporary economic disparities across former colonies, illustrating the enduring impact of unequal power structures on long-term growth trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share that institutional reforms in Africa have been directed at enhancing governance and economic conditions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This focus on institutions is driven by their crucial role in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the prominence of 'good' institutions has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) stress that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This suggests that while institutional reforms are important for economic development, they may not be the sole determinant of long-term growth trajectories in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty, indicating a need for further research into the historical determinants of economic development.

Technological advancements independently drive long-run economic growth by fueling the growth of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge, as a process of technological change, is independent from institutional adjustments. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by noting that technological progress has varied over centuries and regions, with each epochal innovation constituting a dominant source of sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These technological advancements necessitate corresponding institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, which Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges as crucial for sustainability. The economic growth driven by technological advancement is not limited to the industrial revolution but extends across different periods and regions (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Ogilvie challenges the conventional view of economic historians, contending that the core institutions of serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds cannot be understood as merely efficient responses to economic conditions. Instead, these institutions were rooted in power struggles over resource distribution among elites, commoners, and other societal groups. Austin extends this perspective by showing how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. Austin also notes that this pattern depended on specific historical contexts and power dynamics, suggesting significant variation in institutional outcomes across different colonial settings. This variability underscores the importance of understanding local distributional conflicts and their resolution in shaping economic institutions.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of power and resources within societies. Acemoglu et al. argue that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies establishing extractive institutions that hindered growth while others developed inclusive ones that fostered it (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin supports this view, noting that colonial governments often aligned their interests with indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, leading to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This alignment suggests that the interests of colonial powers significantly influenced institutional development and economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's assertion that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for long-term economic development. He argues that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Hopkins, who highlights the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Ogilvie questions whether efficiency-driven models fully account for institutional outcomes. She contends that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence shows that the interests of colonial governments and local power dynamics significantly shaped institutional outcomes. Austin's analysis illustrates that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, leading to varied institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This historical case demonstrates how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for long-term economic growth.
