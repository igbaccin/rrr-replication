The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question at the nexus of economics and history. At stake is whether institutional frameworks are pivotal drivers of economic development or merely epiphenomenal to deeper structural forces. The debate centers around two main positions. One camp posits that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and ensure rule of law, are foundational for sustained economic growth. These scholars argue that the quality of institutions directly influences investment patterns, technological innovation, and overall economic performance. The rival position contends that institutions are secondary to other factors such as geographical endowments, cultural traits, or exogenous shocks. The disagreement hinges on whether institutional frameworks are autonomous forces shaping economic trajectories or merely reflect deeper underlying conditions. This tension is evident in the varied explanations for why some regions thrive economically while others stagnate.

The debate over the role of institutions in long-run economic growth is exemplified by Acemoglu et al.'s work, which underscores the significance of institutional quality in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They argue that the type of institutions established during colonization, whether extractive or inclusive, has lasting effects on economic development. This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on how institutions reduce transaction costs and enhance property rights security, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Both scholars highlight that efficient institutions are crucial for long-term economic prosperity.

Austin challenges the notion of a straightforward causal relationship between institutions and economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). He argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of historical factors, suggesting that institutional changes are not always the primary drivers of economic development. This view is complemented by Ogilvie's critique, which questions the efficiency-driven explanations for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Instead, she posits that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs play a more significant role in shaping these institutions.

The significance of institutional quality in determining long-run economic growth is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. They attribute this phenomenon to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism, which had lasting impacts on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores how initial institutional frameworks can set a trajectory that persists over generations, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is also highlighted by Hopkins's work, which emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. He argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This view aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on how extractive institutions hinder long-term growth by maximizing rents to politically powerful groups and fostering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The debate over whether institutions are primary drivers of economic growth or secondary to other factors remains unresolved. Scholars agree that institutional quality plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast argue that institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for economic growth by securing property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs. North notes this decline reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The emergence of these institutions facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of transactions in GNP due to increased specialization. This institutional framework ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange, which North and Weingast argue is a critical political factor for sustained growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, the broader implications of these institutional changes are qualified by evidence from capital markets, suggesting that while institutions were crucial, they were not the sole determinant of economic performance. The comparative evidence from capital markets underscores the complexity of economic growth, where institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run outcomes.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is a fundamental driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that technological advancements constitute the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective contrasts with views that prioritize institutions as the primary cause of economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by asserting that modern technology necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. He explains that technological progress requires resolving conflicts arising from modernization without excessive costs or long-term political weakening, underscoring the role of the sovereign state in peacefully managing these transitions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further qualifies this by suggesting that while institutional progress complements technological advancements, it is not always irreversible. Without such institutional support, economic improvements might be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This indicates that although technological progress is crucial for long-run growth, its benefits can be limited without complementary institutional developments.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The institutional frameworks established by colonial powers were shaped by their expectations of mortality and settlement patterns. This is because European settlers sought to create stable environments conducive to long-term habitation and prosperity.

The mechanism linking mortality rates to institutional outcomes involves the incentives of colonial powers. In areas with low mortality, Europeans were more likely to invest in institutions that promoted sustainable growth, as they planned to reside there permanently. This difference in institutional design had profound implications for economic outcomes. Colonies with extractive institutions faced significant barriers to economic growth due to the lack of incentives for investment and innovation.

Acemoglu et al.'s findings underscore the importance of initial conditions and institutional design in shaping long-run economic growth. This stream complements the broader understanding that technological progress, while crucial, requires supportive institutional frameworks to drive sustainable economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2) argue that Africa's slave trades have had a profound adverse effect on economic development, suggesting an alternative explanation to institutional factors. These authors demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn 2008, p.2). The internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channels, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This finding highlights how the legacy of slavery continues to influence economic development by shaping social attitudes that hinder cooperation and economic activity (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.19). These findings align with broader research on the importance of trust for economic outcomes, suggesting that the enduring mistrust resulting from the slave trades could be a significant barrier to Africa's economic growth. This persistent mistrust may also reinforce other institutional weaknesses by making collective action and policy implementation more challenging.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share an interest in the historical determinants of African economic development and poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They suggest that African economic underdevelopment is not solely attributable to persistent structural impediments. Hopkins highlights that African societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. They reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of historical data in understanding current economic conditions and suggests that a deeper grasp of long-term economic development can inform future policy decisions.

However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that the colonial era introduced a dual legal system that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, which codified the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This codification had significant implications for economic development, as it marginalized women's contributions to food farming. Colonial policies thus shaped economic development by influencing gender dynamics and resource allocation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This historical context highlights how institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic outcomes by shaping social structures and resource distribution, even if the structural impediments are not persistent over time.

Austin explains that the institutionalist approach, while emphasizing secure private property rights, struggles with quantitative testing and explaining why favorable growth institutions were adopted in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with local contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) similarly advocates for transforming institutions from mere fashion accessories into indispensable tools, suggesting that economic historians should incorporate institutions deeply into their research programs. This aligns with Austin's argument that colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic actions by adjusting institutional prescriptions, illustrating how local dynamics can shape institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions against over-reliance on shared beliefs as the sole driver of institutions, emphasizing instead the role of distributional conflicts. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic development, their impact is mediated by complex interactions between local contexts and broader historical forces.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across multiple studies. Institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For example, the Bank of England's creation and subsequent financial innovations illustrate how secure property rights facilitated economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. Where European settlers were present, institutions enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment; elsewhere, extractive states transferred resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The impact of institutions varies across contexts. Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that local dynamics can significantly shape institutional outcomes, even within broader historical forces. The creation of extractive institutions was often a response to fiscal security and material interests, rather than a predetermined path (Austin 2008, p.2).

Distributional conflicts also play a crucial role in shaping institutions. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were driven by distributional struggles rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding power dynamics within societies when examining institutional development.

The new economic history of Africa further illustrates how institutions can influence long-run economic outcomes. Hopkins shows that 'good' institutions provide essential incentives for investment and are crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, Englebert's findings indicate that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality; instead, the effectiveness of institutions depends on broader socio-political factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to varied colonization strategies and associated institutions, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Yet the remaining disagreement centers on whether shared beliefs or distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation for the persistence of pre-industrial institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) than do efficiency considerations. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the adaptability of colonial administrations to indigenous economic actions, which shows that local dynamics can significantly shape institutional outcomes even within broader historical forces (Austin 2008, p.15).
