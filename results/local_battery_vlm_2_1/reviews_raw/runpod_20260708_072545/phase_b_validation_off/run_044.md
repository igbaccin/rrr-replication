Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, yet there is significant debate over how and to what extent they influence this process. At the heart of this disagreement lies the question of whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and climate, or if they emerge endogenously through historical processes driven by political and economic forces. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, determines a society's long-term economic trajectory. Others contend that the impact of institutions on growth is mediated by other variables, such as cultural beliefs and the specific historical contexts in which they operate.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2002, p.33) present a strong case for the endogenous development of institutions through colonial policies, noting that extractive institutions persist because they maximize rents to European colonists and promote long-term growth. This perspective underscores the importance of political power dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) adds nuance by highlighting how indigenous economic and political actions can influence colonial administrations, suggesting that institutions are not merely imposed but also negotiated.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by North's work, which emphasizes the critical importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie, who advocates for a more comprehensive integration of institutions into economic analysis, suggesting that they are indispensable tools for understanding historical economic development. North's focus on property rights and transaction costs aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on the political dimensions of institutional change, highlighting how secure property rights can foster economic growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs. The creation of the Bank of England (1694) was a pivotal moment, marking what has been described as the English financial revolution. This institution, along with other new financial tools, dramatically lowered the cost of transacting and fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The political model outlined in North's work underscores how the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of fiscal crises, highlighting the interdependence of political and financial institutions in shaping economic outcomes. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) caution that the commitment to these rules by the regime is critical; a sovereign bound by such rules fosters an environment conducive to economic growth. This underscores the importance of credible commitment in institutional development, suggesting that without a stable political framework, even robust financial institutions may not suffice for sustained economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional frameworks established during the colonial period were not uniform but varied based on factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This pattern is particularly evident in colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where European settlers established extractive states focused on resource extraction rather than local investment.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) further elaborate that these institutions were not merely a byproduct of geographic factors but were deliberately designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional legacy persisted even after independence, continuing to impede economic development in many former colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) point out that the impact of these institutions is not uniform and can be qualified by other factors, such as population density and prosperity at the time of colonization. This highlights the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, where initial conditions play a crucial role in shaping outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality have restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). They posit that these societies' institutional frameworks were shaped by initial conditions that favored elites. These conditions included extreme wealth, human capital, and political power disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In this context, institutions evolved to reinforce existing inequalities, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. This dynamic is evident in regions like Latin America where colonial institutions often entrenched elite dominance by restricting access to education, land ownership, and political representation. Nunn highlights that Africa's slave trades had a profound impact on the continent’s economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). These trades disrupted social structures and weakened local economies, creating conditions that perpetuated underdevelopment long after the abolition of slavery. This institutional legacy has influenced contemporary economic outcomes in both regions. Africa's experience with slave trades was not uniform across all areas; some regions were better able to adapt and maintain trade networks due to factors such as centralized governance and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This variability underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, where local conditions play a crucial role in shaping outcomes.

The literature on colonial impacts on African economic development explores how colonial rule shaped institutional change through both negotiation and imposition, highlighting the role of African initiatives alongside exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that colonial powers often imposed institutions that were not aligned with local contexts, leading to poor economic outcomes. This process was not unidirectional; African societies actively engaged in negotiating the terms of colonial rule, which influenced the institutional landscape. Frankema's work (Frankema 2012, p.15) shows that demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. These calculations were shaped by the new social and political institutions implemented during colonial rule, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. Hopkins notes that despite these adaptations, many African governments still struggle with providing quality public goods due to poor institutions, which are linked to Africa's dismal economic record.

The economic performance in post-colonial Africa reflects the intricate balance between industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. Simson (2019, p.2) highlights that the patronage structures in post-independence African economies often led to government resources being used for buying political loyalty rather than delivering broad-based public goods. This "neopatrimonial" logic, where resources are treated as private property of political patrons, resulted in policy-making focused on consumption rather than investment in infrastructure and services. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that the availability of economic data for Africa's colonial era and post-independence period has been underutilized until recently, which is crucial for understanding the region’s economic growth patterns. This data can reveal how factors such as foreign trade and export markets influenced economic growth in African countries. Simson (2019, p.11) notes that military spending escalated significantly during the independence period, driven by regional instability and domestic dissent. This shift in expenditure priorities had profound implications for capital investments, which are critical for long-term economic development. These trends were particularly pronounced in Kenya and Tanzania, where high debt burdens displaced both capital and recurrent spending. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The downstream implication is that these economic trends influenced by political and institutional factors have had lasting effects on Africa’s development trajectory.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.2) both argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's analysis focuses on how these conflicts drove the evolution of core economic institutions such as serfdom, community structures, and guilds. She contends that these institutions emerged from power struggles between different social groups. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding institutional development through the lens of conflict rather than purely economic logic. This adaptive behaviour is crucial as it shows how institutional prescriptions could shift based on the incentives created by local conditions. Austin further illustrates this through Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This example demonstrates that colonial institutions were not monolithic but responsive to the economic and political contexts in which they operated. This responsiveness suggests that institutional changes can be influenced by local actions and incentives, thereby shaping long-run economic outcomes in complex ways.

In some colonies, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others they set up extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole. These institutional differences had significant impacts on current income per capita. Ogilvie argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights how institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which may not always align with long-run economic growth. The historical evidence from colonial contexts illustrates that the design of institutions is influenced by local conditions and political dynamics, underscoring the complexity of institutional change.

North emphasizes the importance of institutions in providing secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported long-term capital markets.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the experience of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes were influenced by population density and prosperity, which shaped the policies pursued by Europeans. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. Hopkins highlights that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional reforms in eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.
