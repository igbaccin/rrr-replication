The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions can explain persistent disparities in wealth across nations, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture. One live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Another debate centers on how colonial institutions influenced the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. Institutions matter because they determine the incentives for investment and economic development, but their origins and impacts remain hotly debated. The central tension is between those who argue that institutions are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) and those who propose that institutions are shaped by a broader set of factors including efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs. These institutional developments fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for instance, was a pivotal moment in this process. This institution, along with other financial innovations, dramatically decreased the cost of transacting, reflecting an increased security of property rights over time. This security is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, laying the groundwork for future economic development (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast note that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction. As specialization and division of labor increased, so did the aggregate volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as evidenced by the remarkable success in capital markets. The political factors underpinning this growth included the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and how these rules were enforced and changed (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced the long-run economic growth of former colonies. These institutions were shaped by the specific policies and strategies Europeans adopted based on mortality rates and other local conditions, leading to diverse institutional outcomes. In high-mortality regions, Europeans often implemented extractive institutions designed for rapid resource extraction rather than fostering sustainable development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that these policies also interacted with local population density and prosperity, further complicating the institutional landscape. The persistence of these institutions post-independence had profound effects on economic development, as extractive frameworks hindered investment and productivity gains. This interaction between colonial policies and local conditions underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic performance.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that some African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early 20th century, challenging previous assumptions about Africa's economic performance. This finding shared with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9). These scholars' work suggests that these economies were more integrated into global trade networks than previously thought. The growth of export production in these regions was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This integration into global markets had significant implications for economic development, as it facilitated the transfer of technology and capital. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that the benefits of this integration were not evenly distributed, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled ones. The uneven distribution of gains from trade highlights the complex interplay between global economic forces and local institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden and other scholars who have studied economic performance in Europe (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 1999, p.1) find that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not directly addressed but rather focuses on other factors such as wage data, economic development patterns, and collaborative research methods. This framework includes factors like training costs, entry barriers, interest rates, and the number of years skilled workers earn higher wages. In this context, guilds played a crucial role as institutions by guaranteeing fair execution of apprentice contracts in western Europe, thereby facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). These institutions have also been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades. This dual nature of guilds highlights a nuanced role that institutions can play in economic development: while they may promote skill acquisition, they can also create barriers to market efficiency.

The collaborative research methods highlighted by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) further illustrate the complexities involved in measuring long-term economic growth (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). They emphasize that estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error, which increase as one goes back in time. This highlights the methodological challenges in studying long-run economic growth. These challenges include subjective estimates of underlying data and the need for collaborative efforts to refine measurements. This finding suggests that while institutions may influence economic performance, other factors such as shifts in regional economic centers and relative price levels also play significant roles.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their role was pivotal in the transition from subsistence farming to cash crop production, which drove economic development in regions like the Gold Coast. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances and gender inequities. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated access to land for cash crops. This dynamic was exacerbated by colonial legal systems that introduced a dual structure of European courts based on common and civil law, and native courts that enforced customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. However, this process was not uniform across all regions; the specific factor endowments and historical contexts shaped how these inequalities manifested (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The role of women in economic development is further complicated by the interaction between gender dynamics and institutional frameworks, which influenced access to resources and opportunities for growth. This interplay underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development, where institutions not only shape but are also shaped by underlying social structures.

Institutional reforms are crucial for eliminating poverty in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial legacies have shaped the institutional landscape of African countries. Colonial institutions influenced economic development by altering the perceived benefits of formal education, which was tied to new social and political structures (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational framework fostered investment incentives essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Simson 2019, p.2). The impact of colonial rule on African governance extended beyond education to include military expenditure and debt service payments. Simson (2019, p.2) shows that post-independence expenditure trends were heavily influenced by these factors, leading to significant shifts in spending priorities within East Africa (Simson 2019, p.11; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Neopatrimonial tendencies in African governance have complicated the delivery of broad-based public goods and services. This highlights the importance of understanding how colonial legacies continue to shape contemporary economic policies and development trajectories in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), along with Nunn (2008, p.2); (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust. This enduring mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it erodes social capital and hinders cooperation. One specific mechanism through which this mistrust operates is by reducing intergroup trust, which is crucial for economic exchange and collective action. The slave trade's impact on trust is not merely an external effect but also deeply internalized within individuals' norms of behavior. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the deep-seated nature of this issue (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalization of mistrust creates a pervasive environment where cooperation is difficult to achieve, thereby stifling economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) further underscores that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, significantly outlasting the period of colonial rule and leaving a lasting imprint on African societies (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context emphasizes the enduring legacy of the slave trade, which continues to shape contemporary economic trajectories. The mistrust cultivated during this era persists today, influencing modern economic interactions and development outcomes.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently leveraging technological progress in fostering long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that technological advancements necessitate complementary institutional changes to integrate innovations into society effectively. This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where societies that successfully adapted their institutions to accommodate new technologies experienced sustained economic growth. However, Mokyr also notes that without concurrent institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutions in ensuring that technological innovations are sustainably integrated into the economy. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a pivotal role in mitigating such conflicts without weakening the societal fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions is essential for long-run economic growth, as it ensures that technological progress translates into sustainable development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), in arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, highlight the need to understand historical institutional change as a response to endogenous political and social dynamics. Both authors suggest that the adoption of growth-promoting institutions is not merely an outcome of exogenous shocks but rather a result of complex interactions within societies. This mechanism underscores the importance of endogenous factors in shaping institutions over time. She argues that institutions should serve as indispensable instruments rather than mere fashion accessories in the toolkit of every economic historian. This nuanced view suggests that while endogenous factors drive institutional change, the complexity of these changes requires a deep understanding of historical context and the interplay between various social, political, and economic forces.

Pamuk (2007, p.16) shows that long-term structural changes due to the Black Death led to a significant wage gap between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450. The institutional flexibility of regions such as Holland enabled them to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, while Tuscany's rigid monopolies hindered its growth (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This divergence highlights how institutions can influence regional economic trajectories. Kuznets (1966) emphasized that structural change, such as the shift from rural to urban economies, is a key characteristic of economic growth in the early modern and modern eras, supporting Pamuk's findings on the importance of institutional flexibility (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This divergence was not uniform across Europe. While northern Italy experienced significant economic stagnation, regions like Holland thrived due to their adaptive institutions. This institutional divergence highlights how the Black Death's impact was shaped by preexisting institutional frameworks.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in their ability to facilitate efficient market exchanges and provide secure property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth. This perspective is complemented by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The flexibility of these institutions allows for rapid adaptation to new economic conditions, as seen in the development of towns and rural manufacturing in regions like Holland. This institutional adaptability fosters economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth highlights the enduring effects of historical institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional outcomes in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, regions like the United States and Australia developed institutions enforcing the rule of law, while others, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, were characterized by extractive states. This divergence underscores how initial institutional choices can have lasting effects on economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies further illustrates the significance of institutions in shaping long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). For example, regions with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists experienced slower economic development compared to those with more inclusive institutions. This historical evidence supports the idea that institutions play a crucial role in determining long-run economic outcomes.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions provides additional insight into how institutions influence growth. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are key to understanding pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by North's emphasis on the importance of secure property rights in facilitating market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Ultimately, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate efficient market exchanges and provide secure property rights. Both Acemoglu et al. and North show that institutions matter for economic development: they are shaped by politically powerful groups and can either foster or hinder growth depending on their design (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The disagreement lies in whether these institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, highlighting how different historical periods and paths complicate the relationship between institutions and growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The specific historical evidence from colonial institutional frameworks settles this disagreement by demonstrating that initial institutional choices have lasting effects on economic development.
