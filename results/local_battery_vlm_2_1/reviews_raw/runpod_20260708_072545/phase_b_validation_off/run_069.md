The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake are competing explanations for why some economies flourish while others stagnate. One key disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms that drive institutional evolution and their subsequent impact on economic performance. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and rule of law, arguing that these institutions foster investment and growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Other scholars contend that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs play a more significant role in shaping core economic institutions. These perspectives highlight the complex interplay between political factors, institutional design, and long-term economic outcomes. The debate underscores the need to understand how different institutional arrangements influence economic growth over extended periods.

The importance of institutions in fostering economic development is well-documented. North (1989, p.9) argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) argument that institutions established during the colonial period significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies. They note that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional outcomes, such as extractive states in high-mortality regions and more inclusive institutions in low-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions promote economic growth by encouraging investment through secure property rights and rule of law. Extractive institutions hinder growth by diverting resources from productive activities. This framework highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic performance.

The role of political factors in shaping institutions is another critical aspect of this debate. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that credible commitments to secure property rights are essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They argue that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering a stable framework for economic activity, which in turn supported long-term economic development. This perspective underscores the importance of political stability and institutional design in creating an environment conducive to investment and growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further supports this view by noting that effective institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Effective institutions ensure that economic actors can trust in the stability of property rights and contractual arrangements, thereby encouraging long-term planning and investment.

Institutional evolution in Africa provides a contrasting perspective on the role of institutions in economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests that a more nuanced understanding of institutional change is necessary to fully grasp its impact on long-term economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also challenges the efficiency-driven explanation for pre-industrial European institutions, arguing instead that distributional conflicts play a significant role in shaping these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective highlights the importance of considering the social and political context in which institutions emerge and evolve. The debate over institutional causality in Africa underscores the need to examine how historical and contextual factors shape institutional outcomes and their impact on economic growth.

Institutions play an essential role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency of markets and facilitating transactions (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. The English financial revolution exemplifies this dynamic; the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development catalyzed a long-term capital market, which is critical for economic growth itself.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions often serve to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-term growth in the extractive states established by European colonizers. This institutional legacy has had profound implications for contemporary economic development, with extractive institutions hindering investment and economic progress. This historical divergence underscores how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic performance. The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of understanding how historical and contextual factors shape institutional outcomes and their impact on long-run growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast show that the English institutional changes in the 17th century were pivotal in securing property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth. These changes included the establishment of a credible commitment by the state to enforce property rights and contracts, which was essential for the development of capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework fostered an environment where economic actors could invest with confidence, knowing that their rights would be protected. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how differences in factor endowments across New World societies led to varying levels of inequality, which in turn shaped the evolution of institutions. These unequal distributions of wealth and power influenced government policies, often favoring elites and restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional bias towards elites had long-term effects on economic development, perpetuating inequality and hindering growth. The interplay between institutional frameworks and socioeconomic factors thus plays a critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were efficient responses to economic conditions and deeply influenced by power dynamics and societal norms. This perspective aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon’s findings that historical events like the slave trade can have enduring effects on contemporary social attitudes, such as mistrust (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of these institutions persists in shaping economic outcomes long after their initial formation. However, Frankema points out that the impact of colonial rule varied significantly across different regions, suggesting that the specific historical context and institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers also played a crucial role in shaping educational development (Frankema 2012, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The enduring effects of these historical institutions highlight how early distributional conflicts can have lasting implications for economic growth.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are complementary factors in long-run economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr, along with Kuznets, argues that technological advancements require corresponding institutional changes to be effectively harnessed for sustained growth. For example, the introduction of new technologies often necessitates adjustments in social structures and ideologies to accommodate these innovations. This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in historical contexts where economic growth was facilitated by the resolution of conflicts arising from technological change (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The expansion of useful knowledge, as Mokyr emphasizes, is a primary driver of this process. The sustainability of economic improvement depends on complementary institutional progress that addresses coordination failures and prevents rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights how institutional adjustments are crucial for leveraging technological advancements to achieve long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress is further underscored by the need for policies that rectify coordination failures. As Mokyr points out, without such institutional support, the benefits of technological improvements can be eroded over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This dynamic suggests that while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, they must be accompanied by institutional frameworks that ensure their effective and sustained use. The historical record shows that societies which successfully navigated these challenges experienced more robust and enduring economic development.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta emphasize that high productivity in the traded goods sector of northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This focus on merchant networks highlights how structural changes, such as urbanization and commercialization, influenced early modern economic growth. Pamuk further elaborates on this divergence by noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The flexibility of institutions in regions like Holland allowed for rapid adaptation to new economic opportunities, whereas regions with stronger monopolies, such as Tuscany, experienced stagnation. This institutional flexibility is crucial because it enabled regional economies to respond dynamically to demographic and economic shocks, fostering growth in areas where institutions were more adaptable. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) cautions that the relationship between economic development and changes in living standards was not straightforward; there is no clear correlation between urbanization ratios and improvements in the standard of living during this period. This suggests that while structural changes and institutional flexibility played significant roles in early modern economic growth, their impact on living standards was complex and varied across regions.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) along with Nunn (2008, p.2) examine historical economic performance in Africa, highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The adverse effects of slave trades on subsequent economic development are well-documented; for example, the Kongo kingdom's centralized government and developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). These conditions underscore the importance of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes: the presence of a strong central authority and robust trading networks enabled regions like the Kongo kingdom to engage effectively in foreign trade. This institutional framework supported economic activity and fostered growth by linking local economies to broader markets. Nunn (2008, p.19) cautions that these conditions were not universal; many other African societies lacked such institutions, limiting their ability to participate in sustained trade. The variability in institutional structures across Africa highlights the complex interplay between political organization and economic development, suggesting that regions with more adaptable and centralized institutions were better positioned to leverage external markets for growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) along with Peters (2004, p.19) highlight significant economic contributions by African women and deepening social divisions over land. These migrations were pivotal for both food security and cash crop production, showcasing how women navigated institutional constraints to contribute to economic growth.

The codification of customary law under colonial rule exacerbated gender inequalities. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) document how patriarchal alliances reinforced women's subordinate status through legal frameworks that privileged men in land access. This process was further complicated by the dual legal system introduced during colonial rule, where European courts operated alongside native courts, often to the detriment of women’s rights. This dynamic illustrates how institutional changes, while aimed at modernization, can inadvertently create new barriers to inclusive growth.

The interplay between gender dynamics and institutional frameworks reveals a complex landscape where women's economic contributions are both facilitated and constrained by the same systems. Peters (2004, p.5) points out that agricultural officers often viewed African landholding practices as communal and inhibiting individual investment, which further marginalized women who were already relegated to less productive lands. This institutional bias limited women’s economic opportunities and shaped broader patterns of land use and productivity across Africa.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2009, p.19; 2014, p.3) emphasize that accurate measurement of economic performance is crucial for understanding long-term growth trends. They argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping these measurements through their influence on skill development and data collection. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how formal institutions, such as guilds, facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills by ensuring fair execution of apprentice contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This institutional framework supported economic growth and created conditions for collaborative research to improve measurement accuracy. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) note that Angus Maddison's work on national accounts, while subject to margins of error, sparked a wave of scholarly engagement that refined these measurements over time (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This collaborative effort has led to more detailed and accurate long-term economic growth data, which is essential for understanding historical economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) acknowledges that guilds' attempts to form local cartels could counterbalance their beneficial effects on skill transfer by restricting entry into trades. This tension between fostering skills and maintaining market efficiency underscores the complex role of institutions in economic growth.

Colonial administrations in Africa, through their policies and interests, significantly influenced economic development trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin shows that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for labor. This policy shift was driven by the colonial administration's need for fiscal security and a desire to align with local economic incentives. Hopkins adds that this approach helped African economies develop by fostering investment in productive activities, which were essential for long-term growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that the impact of these policies varied across different regions and historical periods. This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes, highlighting that while some colonial policies facilitated economic development, others may have hindered it depending on local conditions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to create secure property rights and enforce contracts, which are fundamental for fostering investment (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.2). Institutions matter because they shape the incentives that drive economic behavior. North argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, with extractive institutions hindering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The variability in institutional outcomes across different regions and historical periods highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local conditions. Hopkins shows that African economies developed through investment in productive activities fostered by colonial policies aligned with local economic incentives (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that these policies had varied impacts depending on regional and temporal contexts, suggesting that while some colonial institutions facilitated development, others may have hindered it (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15). This variability underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with local conditions to influence economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. extend this argument by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding illustrates how institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic development depending on their design and implementation. Ogilvie emphasizes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This points to the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics underlying institutional design.

The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement is found in the contrasting experiences of European colonies with high versus low mortality rates. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, had lasting negative effects on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This evidence highlights the critical role of institutional design and implementation in shaping long-term economic outcomes.
