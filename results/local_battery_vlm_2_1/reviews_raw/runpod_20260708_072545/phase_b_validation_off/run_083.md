The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is whether the quality and nature of political and economic institutions are decisive factors in determining a nation's prosperity over the long term. One live disagreement centres around the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth, with some arguing that institutions matter primarily because they facilitate secure property rights and efficient markets. Others contend that institutions' impact on growth is mediated through their effects on distributional conflicts and political stability. A third perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in fostering credible commitments from governments to investors.

One stream of scholarship focuses on how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, which in turn significantly affected economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other instances, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin critiques this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate highlights the complexity of how institutions shape economic growth, with some scholars emphasizing the direct impact of colonial institutions on current economic outcomes, while others point to the need for a more nuanced understanding of historical paths.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are critical for economic growth; they shape the incentives and constraints that guide individual behavior and investment decisions. North elaborates on how the development of new financial instruments, such as those introduced during the English financial revolution, reduced transaction costs by enhancing property rights security (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) expand on this idea by showing that extractive institutions can also be imposed on colonies, which has lasting negative effects on economic development. They show that the type of institution established in a colony was influenced by European mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mortality rate determined whether Europeans would settle and establish property rights-enforcing institutions or create extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate on this mechanism, highlighting that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not designed to maximize long-run growth, but rather to benefit politically powerful groups or colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development is complex and multifaceted, with both direct and indirect effects.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share that colonial institutions, particularly those introduced by British rule, played a significant role in shaping the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. The British colonial administration's emphasis on governance and institutions created an environment where formal education was seen as a means to access better economic prospects and social mobility. The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth was not uniform across all African regions. The effectiveness of colonial education systems varied widely due to differences in local governance and the extent to which indigenous populations were integrated into the colonial administrative structures. This variability highlights that while British rule generally promoted formal education, its success in driving long-term economic development depended heavily on the specific institutional arrangements and governance practices implemented at the local level(Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Austin agrees with this institutionalist perspective but points to the challenges of testing it quantitatively. While North and Weingast focus on the political underpinnings of economic growth, Austin extends this view by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local economic conditions (Austin 2008, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This adaptability could foster a higher production equilibrium, challenging the idea that institutions are static and uniform across regions. Austin also cautions that the effectiveness of these institutional changes varied widely due to differences in local governance and integration of indigenous populations into administrative structures (Austin 2008, p.15). This variability highlights that while historical institutional changes influenced economic development, their impact on long-run growth depended heavily on specific conditions and adaptations.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800, a finding shared by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1). This divergence is not merely an artifact of modern industrialization but reflects deeper historical processes. Broadberry and Gupta posit that the service sector played a crucial role in driving early modern economic growth in Europe, particularly through merchant distribution and finance. They argue that high productivity in the traded goods sector was as much a result of efficient services as it was of agricultural production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This focus on the service sector highlights how institutional changes facilitated economic development by enhancing trade and financial activities.

Van Zanden's analysis supports this view, noting that the shift in economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea was marked by significant changes in relative price levels of basic foodstuffs. The rise in grain prices in England after 1740 indicates a broader trend where economic growth led to increased costs for essential goods (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This pattern suggests that institutional factors, such as trade policies and financial innovations, were instrumental in shaping the economic landscape of early modern Europe. Van Zanden also cautions against overestimating the impact of these changes on living standards, noting that there is no clear relationship between economic development and improvements in the standard of living during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impacts, where economic growth did not uniformly translate into better living conditions for all. The nuanced relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is further complicated by regional variations and the uneven distribution of benefits within societies.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) argue that technological progress is the fundamental driver of economic growth. The accumulation of useful knowledge propels societies forward by fostering innovations that transform production sectors and living standards. This process requires institutional adjustments to efficiently integrate new technologies into society. These adjustments facilitate the resolution of conflicts arising from the adoption of modern technology, which can disrupt traditional modes of life and social structures. This integration demands political stability and a cohesive national state to manage growth-induced conflicts peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress often necessitates ideological shifts as well, making the interplay between technology and institutions dynamic and complex. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, economic improvements may not be sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores a critical condition: while technological advancements spur growth, the sustainability of such growth hinges on concurrent institutional developments.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions. They contend that the structure of pre-industrial Europe's institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, can be better understood through the lens of power dynamics between different social groups. These institutions often served to entrench existing inequalities by favoring elite interests, thus perpetuating a system where wealth and political power were concentrated in few hands (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective underscores how institutional arrangements are deeply rooted in the socio-political struggles of the time. This focus on distributional conflicts does not negate the importance of broader cultural beliefs in shaping institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) highlights that while shared beliefs form part of a society's institutional structure, they do not fully explain the efficiency or inefficiency of these institutions; indeed, the game-theoretic view described by Ogilvie is repudiated, which suggests that distributional conflicts are more central to understanding institutional outcomes. This nuanced approach reveals how economic institutions are shaped by both power dynamics and cultural contexts, with distributional conflicts playing a crucial role in determining their form and function.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in their ability to influence and be influenced by political power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation was driven by the differing interests of colonizing powers, with some establishing rule-of-law-based systems and others implementing extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These divergent institutional frameworks have persisted over time, significantly impacting current economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by collapsing different historical periods into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2).

The impact of institutions on long-run growth is further underscored by their role in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North's work highlights how secure property rights, enforced through effective political institutions, are crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution exemplifies this dynamic, illustrating how institutional changes can lead to dramatic declines in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).

Moreover, distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates that shared beliefs form part of a society's institutional structure but do not fully explain the efficiency or inefficiency of these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The game-theoretic view described by Ogilvie is repudiated, suggesting that distributional conflicts are more central to understanding institutional outcomes. This nuanced approach reveals how economic institutions are shaped by both power dynamics and cultural contexts, with distributional conflicts playing a crucial role in determining their form and function (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The persistence of extractive institutions can be understood through the lens of equilibrium institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that these institutions are shaped by the interests of those in power, who may prioritize short-term gains over sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is reinforced by Hopkins's argument that understanding Africa's economic history requires recognizing how poor institutional quality has contributed to its dismal record. Good institutions provide incentives for investment and are essential for development; thus, addressing poverty among the bottom billion in Africa involves reforming these institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The divergence in European colonization strategies, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions, underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on current economic conditions. Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence provided by the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century plays a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, showing how institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding relative incomes among these regions.
