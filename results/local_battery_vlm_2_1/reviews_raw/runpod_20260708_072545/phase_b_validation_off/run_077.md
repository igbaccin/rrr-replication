The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has garnered significant scholarly attention. At stake is whether the institutional framework can be credited for sustained economic development or if other factors such as geography and culture are more determinative. One key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions drive long-term economic outcomes, as opposed to exogenous factors like climate or natural resources. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current economic conditions in former colonies. This perspective posits that extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonizers at the expense of local populations, have persisted and continue to impede growth. Other scholars contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, suggesting that institutional changes are often responses to underlying economic dynamics and not independent drivers of growth. Additionally, there are debates about whether the efficiency or distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions. This tension highlights the complexity of understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The disagreement extends to whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or other factors such as population density and prosperity. Institutions matter in economic growth and development, and their role in shaping long-term outcomes remains a contentious issue.

North and Weingast argue that institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The development of financial instruments during this period was pivotal. Financial innovations such as the creation of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and have been described as the English financial revolution. This reduction in transaction costs reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. These institutions were instrumental in creating a secure environment for market exchanges, thereby facilitating specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast highlight that political factors underpinning economic growth are crucial for fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these financial institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. A critical factor in this process is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to enforcing and upholding these rules. North and Weingast note that the institutional changes were not revolutionary on their own; they worked in conjunction with other factors such as the elaboration of bureaucratic structures inherited from previous regimes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Acemoglu et al. posit that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their argument hinges on the premise that European colonizers established extractive institutions where mortality rates were high, as these regions often lacked incentives for long-term investment. This institutional framework was designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than fostering economic growth in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions persisted and hindered economic development by discouraging private property rights and rule of law. In their 2001 paper, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutional legacies still affect current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). For instance, they found that countries with extractive colonial institutions have lower levels of investment and economic growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions. This institutional framework has enduring effects on the economic trajectory of these nations. However, the persistence of these institutions is not merely a result of their initial design but also due to the reluctance or inability of local political elites to enact meaningful institutional change (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue. These scholars highlight that the institutional landscape of early modern societies was influenced by power dynamics and the interests of ruling elites as well as efficiency considerations. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts operated is via the control over labor markets, where powerful groups often imposed institutions like serfdom to maintain their dominance (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where the persistence of serfdom can be traced back to the ability of elites to enforce institutional frameworks that benefited them at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Furthermore, this pattern extended beyond labor markets; it also influenced the structure of guilds and communities, which were shaped by the interests of those in power rather than by considerations of overall productivity (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This institutional framework evolved over time, with elites adapting institutions to maintain their control. Sokoloff and Engerman show that in New World societies with high inequality, the persistence of these unequal institutions impeded economic development by restricting competition and innovation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional rigidity highlights a broader pattern: while distributional conflicts initially shaped institutions to maintain elite power, they also created long-term barriers to economic growth by locking in inefficient structures.

Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that Africa's slave trade fostered an enduring culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This persistent mistrust has been shown to significantly impact trust levels in Africa today. Nunn argues that this mistrust has had a profound effect on economic development in Africa, countering views that attribute underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trade's legacy of mistrust is evident in the underdeveloped institutions and social structures that hinder economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that innovations in technology are crucial for sustained economic growth over long periods. Technological progress requires institutional adjustments to efficiently utilize new technologies, highlighting how the interplay between technological changes and societal structures is essential for growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The emergence of epochal innovations, which spread widely across regions, exemplifies this dynamic: these breakthroughs necessitate not only technical advancements but also complementary institutional developments. This process underscores that while technology drives growth, institutions must adapt to integrate these innovations effectively. Mokyr acknowledges that the sustainability of economic improvements relies on institutional progress complementing technological advancements; otherwise, rent-seeking behaviors could erode these gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The role of institutions in facilitating economic growth becomes evident when considering how societal structures must evolve alongside technological changes to ensure long-term prosperity.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and new economic history of Africa highlight that colonial institutions have had lasting effects on African economies (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by neglecting historical nuances, while Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the new economic history can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty. Colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on local incentives and economic actions, contrary to the notion of uniform colonial policy (Austin 2008, p.15). For example, some colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labor, showing how institutions could evolve in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that institutional changes can be driven by both exogenous and endogenous factors, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. Hopkins qualifies this by emphasizing that while 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, their effectiveness depends on broader governance frameworks (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The implication is that institutional reforms in Africa must consider both historical legacies and contemporary governance structures to foster long-term economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that African poverty is a product of specific economic conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels across British Africa, revealing that economic performance varied significantly across regions and over time. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) underscore the importance of foreign trade and export markets in driving these variations, noting that factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played crucial roles. These findings suggest that African economies responded dynamically to changing economic conditions, including shifts in global demand for exports. This underscores the need for more detailed historical analysis to fully grasp how African economies have evolved over time and what factors have driven their performance.

European development between 1500 and 1800 offers crucial insights into the structural changes that have influenced long-term economic growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) share a focus on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts in Europe during this period (Pamuk 2007, p.23; van Zanden 1999, p.1). The absence of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living is evident through these measures. This lack of correlation suggests that other factors, such as institutional flexibility, may have played a more significant role in driving economic growth than previously thought. Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights how Holland's institutional flexibility allowed it to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities, while Tuscany’s stagnation can be attributed to the strengthening of existing monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This pattern underscores that institutional arrangements can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing a region's ability to adapt to changing conditions. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) further elaborates on this by illustrating how shifts in economic development focus from Holland to England were accompanied by changes in the relative price levels of basic foodstuffs, indicating broader structural transformations (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Pamuk qualifies that these structural changes varied significantly based on regional institutional contexts (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These insights into Europe's economic development between 1500 and 1800 suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with both adaptive flexibility and rigid monopolies influencing outcomes in distinct ways.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident through their impact on property rights and transaction costs. North demonstrates that the establishment of secure property rights is crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.9). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that extractive institutions, which fail to protect property rights, can hinder long-term growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 17th century England illustrates how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth. By contrast, colonial administrations that prioritized extractive policies often failed to create institutions that encouraged investment and long-term development. This highlights the importance of institutions that support secure property rights and low transaction costs for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence from Europe shows that institutional changes can have profound effects on economic outcomes. North argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that the reversal of fortune among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The creation of new financial instruments in England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting the increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. This demonstrates that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-term economic development by influencing property rights and transaction costs.

The precise remaining disagreement on how institutions shape long-run economic growth is whether these institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the idea that these institutions were designed to maximize economic efficiency. North’s argument that efficient property rights are crucial for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth shows that institutional design can have significant long-term effects on economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from Europe supports the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions but also highlights the importance of secure property rights and low transaction costs for sustained economic growth. This is evident in the case of England, where institutional changes led to a significant decline in transaction costs and fostered economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North 1989, p.9).
