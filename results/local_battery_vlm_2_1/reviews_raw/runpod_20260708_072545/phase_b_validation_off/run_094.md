The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development; if so, how they operate to affect growth; what kinds of institutions matter most; and why some countries develop effective institutions while others do not. This debate pits institutionalists against other schools of thought that emphasize geographic factors or cultural determinants as the key drivers of long-run economic growth. A prominent position is that institutions are the principal cause of economic development, with credible commitment underpinning growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This view posits that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms foster economic activity by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Another perspective suggests that distributional conflicts and power dynamics are more influential than efficiency concerns in shaping core economic institutions. Another view is that institutions matter less because they are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or culture. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are endogenous to political processes or exogenous to geographic and cultural factors, and which kinds of institutions, those designed for growth or those maximizing rents, are most critical for long-run economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This institutional development was a result of both economic needs and political factors. This financial revolution was pivotal for long-term capital markets and economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate on the political underpinnings of this institutional change. They highlight that secure property rights were about more than just rules governing economic exchange; they were also about institutions enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This enforcement was crucial for reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The political commitment to these rules was a critical factor in this process.

The role of financial institutions in reducing transaction costs is evident in historical examples such as the Bank of England, but it is also important to consider how broader political institutions enforce property rights.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in different colonies. Inclusive institutions, such as those set up in the United States, Australia and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive states, like those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks was driven by differences in European mortality rates and settlement patterns. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita today.

Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the design of these institutions was influenced by both geographic factors and the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The equilibrium institutions established in former colonies were often extractive, maximizing rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth. This institutional legacy has had profound implications for economic development, as these extractive institutions continue to hinder investment and economic activity in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-run development trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings suggest that while colonial institutions have had a lasting impact on economic outcomes, their effects are mediated by historical contexts and opportunities for growth.

Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies African economic history by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The complexity of institutional evolution in Africa is evident in colonial administrations that adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, contrary to the notion of rigidly extractive institutions. For instance, Austin notes that in areas where European settlers were not present, colonial governments often encouraged commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). They argue that the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates leaves significant gaps in understanding the long-term economic development in Africa. By reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, they reveal that the colonial period was marked by varied institutional responses to local conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg also note that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is necessary to assess the possibilities of future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This implies that while historical institutional paths were complex and adaptive, their long-term effects on economic development require further investigation.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts long-run economic growth. They demonstrate that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust was primarily through individuals' internal norms of mistrust. This cultural legacy of mistrust, rooted in centuries of exploitation during the slave trades, has profound implications for contemporary Africa. The persistence of this mistrust can hinder economic development by reducing social capital and cooperation, which are crucial for effective governance and market functioning (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) underscores how trust is integral to various aspects of economic activity, from trade to firm management practices. Nunn also notes that the impact of slave trades on Africa's development involved more than just mistrust; it included institutional and economic disruptions (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while cultural factors like mistrust are significant, they operate in conjunction with other historical legacies to shape Africa's long-run economic trajectory.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) posit that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective highlights the role of innovation as a primary engine for sustained economic development. Technological progress generates new production methods, products, and services that can significantly enhance productivity and stimulate economic growth. Institutions serve as facilitators; they must adjust to accommodate these technological changes efficiently. Kuznets (1973, p.6) elaborates on this by noting the necessity of resolving conflicts arising from technological advancements without weakening societal cohesion. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully managing such growth-induced conflicts. This implies that while institutions are important, their primary function is to support and sustain the benefits of technological progress rather than being the drivers of economic growth themselves. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further underscores this by recognizing the potential for coordination failures without institutional progress to complement technological advancements. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that economic reasonableness, the rational allocation of resources, is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress. This suggests that while institutions are essential for managing and sustaining economic growth driven by technology, they do not replace the fundamental role of technological innovation in driving long-term economic development. The relationship between institutions and technological advancements is thus complementary but distinct.

Distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by power struggles among different groups than by concerns for economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns, and she argues that institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds emerged from these conflicts. The institutional frameworks were deeply rooted in the power dynamics of their respective societies.

The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to a specific mechanism: elite capture. Elites in both pre-industrial Europe and New World societies utilized their power to shape institutions that maintained their advantage, ensuring that any institutional adjustments did not threaten their dominant positions. This pattern is evident in the policies implemented by elites in New World societies, which restricted competition and reinforced inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) adds a nuanced qualification to this mechanism: cultural beliefs that legitimized these power structures also influenced the persistence of institutions. These beliefs created a shared narrative that made institutional changes difficult to implement. The implication is that distributional conflicts shaped initial institutional frameworks and sustained them through a combination of elite capture and cultural justification, making long-term economic growth contingent on overcoming these entrenched systems.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. Institutions that provide secure property rights and low transaction costs are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). For example, the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 17th century England reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth by increasing the security of property rights over time. This historical evidence underscores that institutions matter because they shape the economic environment in which individuals operate (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for reducing transaction costs per exchange.

Cultural beliefs that legitimize power structures also influence the persistence of institutions. These shared beliefs create a narrative that makes institutional changes difficult to implement, thereby sustaining entrenched systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped initial institutional frameworks and maintained them through elite capture and cultural justification, making long-term economic growth contingent on overcoming these entrenched systems. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast's argument that the degree to which a regime or sovereign is committed to enforcing rules underpins economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets in seventeenth-century England shows that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic development. This shared finding supports the idea that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is agreed to be foundational: institutions create the framework within which economic activity occurs by defining and enforcing property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Disagreement remains over whether efficiency concerns or distributional conflicts primarily shape these institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than long-run growth, highlighting the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence from former colonies shows that the reversal in relative incomes was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the specific historical context of colonialism influenced the design of institutions, which in turn affected long-run economic growth.
