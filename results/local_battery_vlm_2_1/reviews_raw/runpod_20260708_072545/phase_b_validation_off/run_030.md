The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Scholars have long debated whether institutions are the primary drivers of sustained economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. The live disagreement centres on the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, determine economic outcomes over the long term. Some researchers argue that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, thereby promoting growth. Others contend that institutional changes alone cannot fully explain economic development, suggesting that factors such as population density, prosperity, or cultural traits also play essential roles. The debate is complicated by the historical context: whether institutions introduced during colonialism or other periods of significant change have enduring impacts on contemporary economies. This review examines how scholars have conceptualized and measured the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth, highlighting key findings and theoretical frameworks that illuminate this complex relationship. A central theme emerging from these studies is the enduring influence of historical institutional legacies on modern economic performance.

The long-term economic trajectory of former European colonies was significantly influenced by the institutions established during colonial rule, which were shaped by European policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans implemented distinct colonization strategies based on mortality rates and settlement patterns, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, characterized by property rights and the rule of law, fostered investment and economic growth in colonies like the United States and Australia. Extractive institutions in regions such as the Congo or Gold Coast were designed to quickly transfer resources back to Europe, hindering long-run development. These colonial legacies persisted even after independence, shaping contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to varying institutional frameworks, which had enduring effects on current income per capita. This persistence of extractive institutions highlights the profound impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-term economic performance. However, while colonial institutions played a pivotal role, it is crucial to note that these institutions were not static; they evolved over time in response to local and global dynamics. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights. This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by providing a stable foundation for market exchanges. North (1989, p.9) highlights that this transformation was driven by political institutions specifying efficient property rights and enhancing enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). This nuanced view suggests that the specific nature of institutional changes, rather than their mere presence or absence, is critical in determining long-term economic outcomes (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England exemplifies how political stability and secure property rights can drive economic growth, setting a precedent for future institutional developments.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to advantage elites and restrict competition, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective is rooted in the observation that New World societies, despite starting with abundant land and natural resources, developed extreme wealth disparities from their inception. These inequalities were not merely economic but also extended to human capital and political power, creating a stratified society where institutions evolved to protect elite interests (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Broadberry and Gupta further elaborate on this institutional bias towards elites by highlighting how high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance. These sectors played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to their role in contemporary economies (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The implications of these findings are significant for understanding long-run economic growth: elites' control over institutions can perpetuate inequality by limiting access to resources and opportunities. Broadberry and Gupta qualify this by noting that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers experiencing a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that while institutional biases may persist, their effects can vary across different segments of society.

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr argues that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets supports this by noting that epochal innovations have been dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world. The institutional adjustments required for efficient use of innovations can be complex, involving changes in social norms and political structures. Mokyr notes that without these adjustments, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets acknowledges that conflicts arising from technological progress must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to occur, suggesting a nuanced relationship between technology and institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutional structures in facilitating technological advancements, as they provide the framework necessary for innovation to drive long-term economic growth. Institutions play a crucial role in mitigating conflicts that arise from technological progress and ensuring that society remains cohesive during periods of rapid change.

This is particularly evident in Africa, where historical factors like the legacy of the slave trade have significantly shaped economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that European colonial policies and their economic legacies cannot be simply compressed into a single narrative, but must be understood through the lens of institutional evolution and policy experience. This perspective aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon's findings on how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The legacy of these historical factors continues to influence African economic development by shaping societal norms and institutions, such as trust and property rights. The persistence of this mistrust has broader implications for economic development, as it affects interpersonal interactions and the efficacy of formal institutions. Austin (2008, p.15) further illustrates that where European settlers were absent, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which influenced institutional prescriptions and economic outcomes. This dynamic underscores how historical legacies can interact with contemporary policies to shape long-term economic trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this by showing that trust, a critical component of social capital, is essential for various aspects of economic development, including trade and political institutions. This shared finding underscores the enduring influence of historical factors on African economic development through their impact on institutional frameworks and societal norms.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in the literature on economic development. Institutions matter because they determine how resources are allocated and managed over time (North 1989, p.9). In this regard, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, establishing extractive institutions where mortality rates were high and inclusive institutions in areas with low mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach explains how historical legacies can influence contemporary economic outcomes through the establishment of enduring institutional frameworks. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that secure property rights are a critical factor in fostering economic growth and development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), showing that credible commitment underpins long-term capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The English financial revolution, for example, illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).

The influence of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of African development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that trust, a critical component of social capital, was severely undermined by the slave trade, leading to persistent mistrust within Africa today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores how historical factors can shape contemporary economic outcomes through their impact on institutional frameworks. Similarly, Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the complexity of institutional evolution in African countries, noting that colonial administrations often altered their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15), which influenced fiscal security and production equilibria.

The dynamic interplay between historical legacies and contemporary policies underscores the enduring influence of institutions on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective complements Austin's (2008) argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), showing that historical legacies can interact with contemporary policies to shape long-term economic trajectories. North (1989, p.9) further illustrates how institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The precise historical evidence that settles these disagreements is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies shows that it was related to industrialization, and provides evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in long-run development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
