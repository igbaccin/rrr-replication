The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with competing explanations for how and why certain institutional frameworks foster development while others hinder it. At stake is the ability to understand historical patterns of growth and stagnation, as well as to inform contemporary policy aimed at promoting sustained economic progress. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they create secure property rights and enforce rules governing economic exchange, thereby fostering investment and growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others contend that the impact of institutions is more nuanced and can be better understood when viewed through the lens of distributional conflicts. This debate reveals a fundamental tension between views that prioritize efficient property rights as the key to economic development, and those that emphasize the role of power dynamics and shared beliefs in shaping institutional outcomes. The central question remains: what specific mechanisms by which institutions influence long-run growth?

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and others argue that the creation of secure property rights is pivotal in reducing transaction costs. This reduction facilitates market exchanges and fosters investment, thereby driving long-term economic development. The development of financial instruments like those created during the English financial revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and enhance economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality; these institutions significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation highlights how the establishment of extractive institutions can hinder economic development by failing to enforce property rights and encourage investment. Acemoglu et al (2002) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not just geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions can lock countries into a cycle of underdevelopment, as these institutions are designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote long-run growth.

Historical institutional changes introduced by colonialism shaped economic outcomes in different contexts (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, while Austin points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This simplification obscures the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic responses. One specific mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes is the adaptation of institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic action. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often modified their policies based on the incentives created by local economic activities (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to expectations based solely on extractive motives. Austin's analysis adds nuance to this pattern by showing that where European settlers were absent from competing for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial governments' interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied significantly depending on the presence and influence of settler populations. The variation in institutional adaptations underscores the importance of local context in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as different settings led to distinct responses from colonial administrators and, consequently, diverse economic trajectories.

The literature on colonial impacts on African economic development explores historical and structural factors influencing poverty and growth in Africa but does not explicitly focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that new economic history can inform institutional reforms, highlighting that "good" institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) argument that understanding long-term economic development in Africa requires a deeper grasp of historical conditions and their impact on current welfare development. Both perspectives suggest that structural impediments are not the sole determinants of African poverty, implying that institutional reforms could play a pivotal role in economic growth. Hopkins also observes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly influence the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This qualification underscores that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness is shaped by complex social dynamics and historical legacies. These insights suggest that institutional reforms in Africa must be tailored to address specific historical and social contexts to foster long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts were the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The core economic institutions of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe are better explained by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie posits that these institutions were shaped by power struggles among different social groups seeking to maximize their share of resources (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that institutional arrangements in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutions. However, Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing the role of beliefs in shaping institutions, noting that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure but not the game-theoretic view where 'beliefs are institutions' (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This nuanced perspective underscores that while distributional conflicts are pivotal, their impact is mediated by complex social dynamics.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Frankema (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15), reveal that gender dynamics and access to resources significantly influenced economic outcomes in African development. One key mechanism through which this influence operated was land allocation under colonial rule. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men controlled the more valuable cash crop lands, a pattern reinforced by customary law codified during colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of resources had profound economic implications, as it limited women's participation in the lucrative cash crop economy and restricted their economic agency. The broader impact of this resource allocation was economic and social: it entrenched patriarchal structures that persisted long after the colonial period. However, Broadberry and Gardner caution against overgeneralizing these patterns, noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors like local endowments and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while gender dynamics were crucial, they interacted with other contextual factors to shape economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-run economic growth through reduced trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust has both internal and external dimensions; Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that while external factors like the rule of law play a role, the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as significant in shaping intergroup trust. These findings resonate with broader literature on the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). However, the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond mere mistrust: Nunn highlights that Africa's simultaneous experience of four slave trades over nearly 500 years had a profound and lasting adverse effect on economic development. Moreover, the centralized governance structures like those in the Kongo kingdom, which facilitated trade with Europeans, underscore how institutional frameworks could mitigate some of the negative effects of the slave trade. Thus, understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires acknowledging the deep-seated cultural and social legacies left by historical events such as the slave trade.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective highlights how the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels innovations that propel economies forward. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are crucial as they enable societies to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes, thereby facilitating sustained economic growth. Economic progress is about technological advancements and societal capacity to adapt and integrate these innovations into existing frameworks. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores that while technological progress is essential, it must be supported by robust institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic sustainability. Kuznets (1973, p.6) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the importance of political stability in sustaining economic development. This suggests that technological and institutional developments are intertwined, with each reinforcing the other to drive long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutions in colonies, with some promoting investment and rule of law while others were extractive. This variation in institutional frameworks directly impacted current income per capita, highlighting the enduring effects of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation, compressing different historical periods and paths. He shows that European settlers' presence or absence influenced colonial governments' policies, which in turn shaped local institutions and economic development trajectories.

The role of political stability is crucial in sustaining long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that different measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and rule of law, significantly affect economic outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of political stability in creating an environment conducive to economic development.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had profound effects on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's (2007, p.3) call for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution, emphasizing the need to incorporate institutions into economic analysis comprehensively.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into how institutional reforms can eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not designed to maximize long-run growth. Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights have blocked the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement in Latin America, which has significant implications for Africa's development trajectory. The historical evidence shows that European settlers' presence or absence influenced colonial governments' policies (Austin 2008, p.15), which in turn shaped local institutions and economic development trajectories.
