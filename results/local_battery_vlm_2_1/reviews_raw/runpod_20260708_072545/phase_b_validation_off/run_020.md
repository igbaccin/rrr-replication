The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory, as it addresses how societies create and maintain the conditions for sustained prosperity. Institutions are the rules that structure political, social, and economic interactions; they can be formal laws or informal norms. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions matter at all, and if so, which ones do: those rooted in efficiency or those shaped by distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that efficient property rights and secure transactions are crucial for growth, while others contend that the distribution of power and resources within societies fundamentally shapes institutional outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.2) highlight how colonial institutions determined long-term economic trajectories, suggesting that the nature of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, was critical in shaping current income per capita levels. This perspective is echoed by North (1989, p.6), who posits that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges. The argument that institutions matter in economic growth is further supported by Ogilvie, who emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programmes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Yet, Ogilvie also suggests that shared beliefs can shape institutional structures, introducing a layer of complexity to the relationship between institutions and economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) provides a nuanced view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. He posits that different historical periods and paths complicate the direct link between colonial institutions and long-run growth, suggesting that indigenous economic and political action also influenced institutional outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not merely about establishing efficient property rights but also involves understanding the complex interplay between power dynamics, historical contexts, and institutional evolution.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) underscore that seventeenth-century England's institutional evolution was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights over time. This development was crucial as it facilitated long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North and Weingast argue that these changes were not merely economic but deeply political, involving a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises. This political dimension highlights the importance of credible commitments by the regime to enforce property rights and maintain market stability.

North (1989, p.6) extends this framework by noting that as economies grow, transaction costs initially rise due to increased specialization and division of labor. However, effective institutions specifying efficient property rights can mitigate these costs, leading to a growing proportion of transactions in GNP. This dynamic illustrates how institutional evolution is not just about reducing existing transaction costs but also about adapting to new economic complexities. The implication is that the effectiveness of institutions in fostering growth depends on their ability to evolve with changing economic conditions.

This perspective suggests that while institutions may establish property rights, their enforcement and adaptability are critical for sustained economic growth. The institutional framework in seventeenth-century England not only facilitated market exchanges but also ensured that these exchanges were secure and reliable over time. This was a fundamental shift from previous periods where such security was lacking, thereby fostering long-term economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights that European colonization strategies varied widely. In some colonies, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment; in others, they created extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound economic implications. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, led to persistent economic underdevelopment. This institutional legacy continues to influence current income per capita. Acemoglu et al.'s findings are robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Acemoglu et al. also note that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, highlighting that the legacies of colonial rule are not merely historical artifacts but ongoing determinants of economic performance.

In this context, understanding how power dynamics and historical legacies shaped these conflicts is essential for a holistic view of institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that distributional conflicts provide a more compelling explanation than efficiency alone for the persistence of institutions such as serfdom, communities, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is grounded in the idea that these institutions often served to entrench the interests of elites, even when they were inefficient from an economic standpoint. These distributional conflicts often manifest in the form of elite control over key economic resources and decision-making processes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illustrate this dynamic by showing how societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This pattern is evident in New World societies where extreme wealth disparities led to institutional frameworks that maintained the status quo rather than promoting economic efficiency.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was instrumental in fostering the development of formal education systems, which were influenced by local political leaders' and families' cost-benefit analyses (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education in African societies. These changes had a profound impact on economic development trajectories. This dynamic is evident in the divergent paths of Anglophone African economies, which by the early twentieth century had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further qualify the historical economic development in British Africa by reconstructing real wage levels and trends, which reveal a more nuanced picture of long-term African welfare development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and impacts contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The mechanism involves both external and internal channels, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one, an insight that complements recent studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). External factors include the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. However, Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that Africa's slave trades had adverse effects on economic development beyond those attributable to colonialism or more recent events, the historical context is crucial for understanding how mistrust was cultivated over centuries (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). In this period, the service sector in north-western Europe played a pivotal role in driving economic growth. This sector's contribution encompassed merchant distribution and finance as well as production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The productivity gains in traded goods sectors were significant, with skilled laborers reaping larger surpluses over basic food needs than unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This uneven distribution of economic benefits highlights the complex interplay between institutional changes and economic development. However, van Zanden argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.9), suggesting that the fruits of early modern development may have been more dispersed than often assumed.

This perspective challenges optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, indicating that institutional factors alone do not determine long-run economic outcomes. The shift in economic development from southern Europe to countries bordering the North Sea further underscores the role of geographical and institutional factors in shaping economic trajectories (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This dynamic illustrates how regional differences in economic development can be influenced by a combination of institutional changes, investment patterns, and geographical advantages.

Institutional changes such as those in agriculture often led to high productivity levels (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), but these gains did not always translate into uniform improvements in living standards.

Therefore, examining how these adjustments were made and their implications for technological progress is vital for understanding long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the interplay between technological changes and societal adaptations is essential for sustained economic progress. Technological innovations, such as those in agriculture or industrialization, require complementary adjustments in institutions and ideologies to be fully integrated into society's productive capacities. For example, Kuznets (1973, p.6) notes that modern technology demands shifts away from rural life patterns and extended family structures, necessitating institutional changes to accommodate these new realities. Mokyr (2005, p.54) expands on this by highlighting the role of institutions in reducing access costs to useful knowledge, thereby facilitating economic growth. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress must complement it to ensure sustainable economic improvement. This interdependence suggests that while technological advancements are pivotal, their impact is contingent upon supportive institutions and ideologies that facilitate their adoption and diffusion.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Austin (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that the new economic history of Africa can provide valuable insights for institutional reforms aimed at alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective challenges oversimplified causal narratives, such as the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, by highlighting the diverse historical paths and institutional evolutions across different regions. For example, colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous economic activity and responded to local incentives often implemented more effective institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between local agency and colonial policies suggests that African institutions were shaped by endogenous factors as well as imposed.

The role of ethno-linguistic fragmentation in shaping African institutions is another critical aspect. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that while some argue ethnic diversity hinders the provision of public goods, it is possible for ethnically heterogeneous societies to develop progressive institutions and uphold the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view underscores the importance of contextual factors in institutional development, moving beyond simplistic correlations between ethnicity and economic performance. The implications are significant: effective institutional reforms must consider the unique historical and social contexts of African nations.

Institutional development is a crucial determinant of long-run economic growth. Institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing investment decisions and productivity levels (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The nature of institutions in former colonies has been shaped by historical factors including the strategies adopted by European colonizers. These strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional frameworks that either encouraged or hindered growth. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment, while in others they created extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to affect current economic conditions.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the divergent paths taken by former colonies. Extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over local development, have impeded economic progress in many regions. Inclusive institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts have fostered growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical context of colonial rule is critical; where European settlers were present, they often competed for local labor, influencing the institutional landscape. In areas without significant settler presence, indigenous economic activities and political actions drove changes in colonial policies (Austin 2008, p.15).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the historical development of financial systems. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing economic efficiency and growth. This financial revolution underscores how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.9). Distributional conflicts have sometimes led to inefficient institutional arrangements. For example, serfdom and guild systems in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by power dynamics rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of institutions is further illustrated by the historical experiences of different regions. The reversal of fortune thesis highlights how initial conditions and colonial policies influenced long-term economic trajectories. Regions with high mortality rates among European colonizers tended to develop extractive institutions, which persist today and affect income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Regions where Europeans settled and established inclusive institutions have seen more robust economic growth. The historical evidence shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether these institutional effects can be fully explained by the initial conditions set during colonization or if other factors, such as post-colonial policies and internal dynamics, play a significant role. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that indigenous influences were crucial. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the initial institutional frameworks established during colonization have enduring effects on long-run development. The empirical comparison of regions with different colonial experiences settles this disagreement by demonstrating that the nature of institutions introduced during colonization has a lasting impact on economic outcomes.
