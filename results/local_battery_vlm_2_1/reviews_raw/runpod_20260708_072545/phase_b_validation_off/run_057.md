The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, as it engages with foundational questions about the drivers of prosperity and stagnation across societies. The stakes are high: if institutions are pivotal, then understanding their genesis and evolution is key to promoting development. Some scholars argue that resource-extracting institutions have hindered long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). However, there is a live disagreement about the mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes: some contend that institutions matter fundamentally because they define and enforce property rights, while others suggest that distributional conflicts are more critical in shaping core economic institutions. Therefore, understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth requires navigating these competing explanations and their implications for development policy.

North argues that the creation of political institutions specifying efficient property rights is crucial for fostering market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). He posits that secure property rights are essential for a functioning capital market, which in turn drives economic growth. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). According to this view, institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are conducive to economic development. This narrative suggests that European settlers established extractive states in colonies with high mortality rates, whereas in regions like the United States and Australia they set up institutions that fostered growth.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the institutionalist approach, while emphasizing secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, faces challenges in explaining why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not others. Austin’s argument underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This view aligns with Ogilvie's critique that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie contends that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that institutional outcomes are shaped not only by efficiency considerations but also by broader social and political dynamics.

Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights the role of colonial policies in shaping institutional outcomes and their lasting impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al.'s thesis is related to the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies, suggesting that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This view aligns with Hopkins' argument that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by defining property rights, reducing transaction costs, and fostering market exchanges. Distributional conflicts and broader social dynamics also play a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes. Understanding these complex interactions is crucial for promoting development and addressing poverty.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial institutions in 17th-century England significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and other financial instruments dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, which reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, a critical factor for both capital markets and overall economic growth. North (1989, p.9) notes that this development was a response to fiscal crises, such as those arising from the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This institutional evolution facilitated market exchanges by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, demonstrating how political factors underpin economic growth and market development. The degree to which a regime is committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange is crucial for sustainable growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast also note that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought when compared to France's economic performance before the French Revolution. This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent.

These institutions were not merely reflections of geographic endowments but were actively shaped by European settlers' mortality rates and economic interests. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis demonstrates that the impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes is not uniform; instead, it varies depending on the specific institutional arrangements established during the colonial period. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis also highlights that the long-run effects of these institutions are mediated by subsequent political and economic developments. For example, post-colonial policy choices can either reinforce or mitigate the initial institutional legacies. This nuanced understanding underscores the complex interplay between historical institutions and contemporary economic performance in former colonies.

The literature on African economic development and colonial legacies explores how institutional changes influenced public goods provision but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment. This perspective implies that the colonial institutions' legacy might be more fluid than static. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates complicates assessing whether poverty was persistent or transient (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This gap in data underscores the need for further research to fully understand the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic development.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the impact of the slave trade was about both the loss of human capital and the erosion of trust within African societies. This mistrust, they posit, is a persistent legacy that continues to affect economic outcomes today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). A key mechanism through which this mistrust manifests is the internalization of norms of mistrust among individuals, which is at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law and trustworthiness of others. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings complement broader research on the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). The historical context of Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, underscores the deep-rooted nature of these effects. This period is significantly longer than the era of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to about 1960. A notable example is the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This kingdom's ability to support trade with Europeans highlights the potential for economic growth in regions with strong institutional frameworks, even during periods of intense slave trading. This suggests that the adverse effects of the slave trades on African economic development varied based on pre-existing institutional strengths (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, as the growth of useful knowledge underpins sustained advancement (Mokyr 2005 (Mokyr 2005, p.49), Kuznets 1973 (Kuznets 1973, p.1)). This perspective emphasizes that technological advancements constitute the dominant source of growth. Mokyr notes that the accumulation of knowledge facilitates economic improvements by enhancing productivity and innovation, whereas Kuznets illustrates how epochal innovations have historically driven long-term growth across various regions. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustainable economic development. Kuznets highlights that modern technology necessitates significant institutional changes to accommodate new production sectors and resolve conflicts arising from these transformations. These adjustments are essential for maintaining political stability and ensuring the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further elaborates on this by stating that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements risk being undermined by rent-seeking behaviors, thereby limiting their long-term benefits (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This dynamic suggests that while technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, it requires supportive institutions to fully realize its potential. Kuznets also points out that the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully resolving conflicts induced by technological changes, ensuring that modern economic growth remains sustainable over time (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

They propose that the adoption of certain institutions is more aptly explained through the lens of power dynamics and political incentives than by efficiency or technological factors alone. This view suggests that institutions are shaped not merely by exogenous factors like geography or culture, but also by endogenous processes driven by the interests and conflicts among different social groups. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional policies in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that institutions evolve dynamically based on the incentives created by indigenous activities, which can lead to higher production equilibria. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that shared beliefs also play a crucial role in shaping institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), suggesting that while distributional conflicts are important, they do not operate in isolation from cultural and ideological factors. This nuanced understanding implies that the study of institutions must account for both material interests and ideational forces to fully grasp their impact on economic growth.

Institutions play an indispensable role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating incentives and constraints that influence economic outcomes. They are the result of political processes and power struggles that determine how resources are allocated and who benefits from them (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Shared beliefs also contribute to institutional structures, but they do not operate independently of material interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism. The type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the long-run development trajectories of their colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. highlight that extractive institutions were established in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, which persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had lasting effects on economic growth.

The persistence of these institutional legacies is further supported by evidence from other regions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in shaping long-run development outcomes.

While distributional conflicts are crucial in shaping institutions, they do not operate in isolation from cultural and ideological factors. Austin argues that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that local conditions and responses also influenced institutional outcomes, highlighting the complexity of institution-building processes.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term capital markets and economic development. He shows that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change facilitated market exchanges and supported economic growth.

Institutional reforms can also inform contemporary policy-making. Hopkins argues that understanding the historical experience of Africa can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people by providing insights into effective institution-building (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a shared finding across many studies, which show that institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. While Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive institutions in shaping long-run outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), Hopkins highlights the importance of understanding historical experiences to inform contemporary institutional reforms (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement type or case that has already been used, showing that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in shaping long-run development outcomes.
