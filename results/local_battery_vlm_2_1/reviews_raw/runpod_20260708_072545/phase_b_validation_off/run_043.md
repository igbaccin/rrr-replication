The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some societies thrive economically while others stagnate, and how historical and political factors influence this trajectory. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions play a more significant role. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutional frameworks, particularly those governing property rights and political stability, can foster sustained economic development. Another key point of contention is whether these institutions are endogenous to economic conditions or exogenous factors that shape economic outcomes independently. The interplay between credible commitment and long-term growth highlights the nuanced relationship between political institutions and economic performance (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This sets the stage for exploring how different institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic development over extended periods.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes of former colonies. European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and the ease of settlement. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly exploit resources and transfer them back to the metropole, such as those seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Conversely, where Europeans could settle more easily, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as observed in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) provide further evidence that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to these institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. The implications are profound: countries burdened with extractive institutions face an uphill battle in achieving sustained economic development.

North and Weingast argue that institutions such as the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of this bank led to a significant decline in transaction costs by increasing security for property rights over time. This financial revolution was crucial because it facilitated a long-term capital market, which is essential for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) describes that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a direct result of fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, highlighting how institutional changes can be driven by urgent needs and lead to broader economic benefits (North 1989, p.9). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is reflected in the growth of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. This results in falling costs per transaction while the size of the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets, where secure property rights fostered economic growth. This implies that while specific institutional developments were crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth, other factors such as bureaucratic efficiency also play a role.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) find that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more positive impact on formal education compared to other colonial powers. This dynamic is rooted in the institutional frameworks established by British colonizers, which facilitated missionary activities and altered the perceived benefits of formal education. The cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families shifted due to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These changes fostered a more conducive environment for educational development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the institutional frameworks implemented during British rule contributed to broader economic improvements (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner also note that these patterns were tied tightly to foreign trade and export markets. This dependency suggests that while formal education was a positive outcome of British colonial institutions, it was not sufficient for sustained growth without robust market integration (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). It focuses instead on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts. For example, van Zanden (1999, p.1) shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.1), while Pamuk (2007, p.23) argues that institutional flexibility enabled late medieval Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The shift in economic focus to countries bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England, highlights how regional economic shifts influenced relative price levels of basic foodstuffs. This pattern suggests that economic development during this period was characterized by distinct regional trajectories across Europe. However, van Zanden (1999, p.1) also notes that differences between English and Continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760 (van Zanden 1999, p.9), indicating that while regional shifts were significant, they did not immediately translate into stark price divergences. This nuance underscores the complexity of economic development in Europe during this era, where institutional factors played a subtle yet crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are critical drivers of economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) both argue that the efficient use of innovations underpins sustained economic development. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is pivotal. This perspective aligns with Mokyr's emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. However, Mokyr also underscores that coordination failures can impede this process and suggests policies to rectify them (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to be possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is essential, the institutional environment must facilitate its integration into the economy without excessive social or political costs. This dynamic relationship suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that this growth is sustained over the long term.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This legacy of mistrust has significant implications for contemporary economic development. The slave trade not only depleted African populations but also fostered a deep-seated lack of trust among individuals and groups, which continues to hinder economic progress. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that this mistrust is deeply ingrained within African societies, affecting both intergroup relations and individual norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin further underscores the enduring impact of historical legacies on contemporary institutional frameworks. The institutional prescriptions imposed by colonial administrations often reflected their own interests rather than those of the indigenous populations. These prescriptive changes, driven by fiscal security and economic incentives, shaped the institutional landscape in ways that continue to influence modern African economies (Austin 2008, p.15). However, Austin notes that these institutional legacies are not uniform across Africa; colonial governance varied significantly depending on local conditions and the presence or absence of European settlers. This variability suggests that while historical mistrust is a pervasive factor, its economic implications differ based on regional contexts and specific institutional adaptations.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic interactions and enforce property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutions across colonies, with some fostering investment and rule of law while others were extractive. This institutional variation significantly influenced current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Institutions matter because they shape the incentives and constraints that guide individual and collective behavior over time (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering economic growth, as shown by North's analysis of the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9).

The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence modern economies. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local conditions and economic incentives. This adaptability resulted in varied institutional outcomes across Africa, reflecting the complex interplay between historical contexts and institutional evolution. The development of trust within societies is another critical factor shaped by institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and impacts economic development. This internal norm of mistrust, combined with external factors like the rule of law, underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in economic growth.

The historical evidence from capital markets provides a clear indication of how institutional changes can drive economic performance (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate that the success of seventeenth-century England's political institutions was remarkable. These institutions not only governed economic exchange but also ensured that rules were enforced and could be changed in ways that fostered secure property rights. This shows how credible commitment underpins growth by providing a stable framework for long-term investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The variability in institutional legacies across Africa, as noted by Austin (2008, p.2), highlights the need to consider regional contexts when examining the role of institutions in economic development. Austin points out that while historical mistrust is pervasive, its economic implications differ based on specific institutional adaptations and local conditions.
