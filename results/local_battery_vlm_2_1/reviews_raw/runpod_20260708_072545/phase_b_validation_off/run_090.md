The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that lies at the heart of debates surrounding economic development and prosperity. Institutions are seen as pivotal in facilitating economic transactions and fostering growth by providing the necessary incentives for investment and innovation. The live disagreement in this area centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they are merely one of several factors that influence long-run economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic development. Others contend that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent upon other factors such as geography, culture, and historical legacies. This debate underscores the complexity of understanding the mechanisms through which institutions shape economic growth over extended periods.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that colonial legacy is crucial to understanding the divergence in income levels among former European colonies. They contend that the institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-run development trajectories, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive ones fostering it. This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter because they determine the security of property rights and the efficiency of market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England exemplifies how secure property rights can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating investment and innovation.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) warn that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the causal relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic outcomes. He argues that this thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, failing to account for the complex interplay of factors that influence economic development. This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables over time.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another critical aspect of this debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the persistence of institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize economic growth, suggesting instead that they often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2) further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not necessarily conducive to long-run growth. They assert that powerful political groups shape these institutions to maximize their rents. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics underlying institutional design and how they influence long-term economic outcomes. The interaction between institutions and other factors such as population density and prosperity is crucial in determining the long-run development trajectories of former colonies (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England (1694) played a pivotal role. This institution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and was described as the English financial revolution. The Bank of England’s success was remarkable; it was underpinned by political factors that committed the regime to rules governing economic exchange and enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North argues that this commitment was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This political model highlights how institutional changes can lead to long-term economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that these colonial institutions were pivotal in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. Economic incentives of colonial powers drove this pattern of institution-building. The resulting institutional frameworks, whether extractive or inclusive, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. This historical pattern underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes in former colonies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) illustrate how this institutional change was driven by the negotiation and imposition of policies during colonial rule in Africa. This influence was not uniform across the continent. For instance, British colonial rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, altering African political leaders' calculations about the benefits of formal education (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education in Africa depended on various cost-benefit analyses made by both macro and micro-level actors. This educational impact had downstream implications for economic development, as it altered local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify the extent of this influence by showing that many Anglophone African economies already had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while colonial policies did shape institutional frameworks, their impact on economic growth was mediated by pre-existing economic conditions.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlight that technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth. The interplay between these factors can vary significantly over time and across regions, with some technological breakthroughs constituting "epochal innovations" that drive sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Without such coordination, economic improvements may be limited and ultimately unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Efficient use of innovations requires technological advances as well as resolving conflicts from these changes, which can strain societal cohesion. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts and maintaining the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that institutional progress is not always irreversible or monotonic. This suggests that while technological progress and institutional adjustments are essential for economic growth, their impact can be mediated by various factors, including the resilience of societal institutions in the face of technological change. The efficient use of innovations requires a nuanced understanding of both technological and institutional dynamics.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Austin, argue that Africa's slave trade legacy has left a lasting impact on economic development through the persistence of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This mistrust is rooted in the historical exploitation and betrayal experienced during the transatlantic slave trade, which has fostered deep-seated suspicions among African societies. These suspicions have eroded social capital, hindering collective action and cooperation essential for economic growth. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates involves both internal norms of distrust and external environmental factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings show that the internal norm of mistrust is at least twice as significant as external factors. This internalised mistrust creates a self-reinforcing cycle where individuals are less likely to engage in cooperative economic activities, further stunting development. Austin qualifies this by noting that colonial policies sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, suggesting that the impact of mistrust on economic outcomes can be mitigated by specific institutional interventions (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutions in shaping economic development trajectories.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie contends that the persistence of serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds can be better understood through the lens of distributional conflicts. Power struggles among different social groups deeply influenced these institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman also highlight how inequality in New World societies led to institutional arrangements that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic is evident in policies that protected elite interests at the expense of broader economic development.

The institutional outcomes of these distributional conflicts had significant implications for long-run growth. The persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds, which were shaped by power dynamics rather than efficiency, hindered economic progress. For instance, guilds often restricted entry and innovation, limiting the potential for technological advancement and economic diversification. This aligns with Ogilvie's observation that "shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society" (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The role of institutions in shaping long-run growth involves a complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary power dynamics.

Sokoloff and Engerman further elaborate on how these institutional arrangements were influenced by factor endowments and inequality. The extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to the development of institutions that favored elites. This dynamic is evident in policies that protected elite interests at the expense of broader economic development. Ogilvie notes that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they do not operate in isolation from other factors such as cultural beliefs and historical legacies. The role of institutions in shaping long-run growth must be seen as a complex interplay between these various factors.

This highlights the importance of understanding how power dynamics influence institutional development and economic outcomes over the long run.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The development of secure property rights is pivotal for economic growth, as it reduces transaction costs and encourages investment. North explains that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). Austin further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15).

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes. In some colonies, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they set up extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional development had lasting effects on economic growth, as seen in the differing trajectories of former European colonies. The persistence of these institutions over time shows that initial colonial policies continue to influence long-run economic outcomes.

The interplay between power dynamics and institutional development is crucial for understanding how institutions shape economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie emphasizes the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. She argues that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. also highlight this point, noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if they are shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maintain their control (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Acemoglu et al.'s work on the reversal of fortunes among former European colonies shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were key factors in determining long-run economic growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the development trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins adds to this by emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment and are essential for development.

Austin qualifies the notion that institutions alone determine economic growth. He shows that shared beliefs and historical legacies also play a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes and long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work underscores the complexity of institutional development, where cultural beliefs and historical contexts interact with power dynamics to influence economic outcomes.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of former European colonies. This analysis shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups have persistently lower levels of income per capita (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The measurement type used in these analyses involves comparing current economic indicators with historical institutional practices, settling the debate by showing that extractive institutions have a lasting negative impact on economic development.
