The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic theory and history. At stake are competing explanations for why some nations thrive economically while others stagnate over centuries. The key disagreement centers on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions. Some scholars argue that institutions directly influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, enforcing property rights, and facilitating market exchanges. Others contend that institutions are merely a proxy for deeper cultural or historical forces, or that they are secondary to geographic determinants like climate or disease environments. A third position emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes, suggesting that institutions may be more about power dynamics than efficiency.

The debate over whether institutions matter hinges on their ability to create credible commitments and enforce property rights. North argues that institutions reduce transaction costs by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms. This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges and encourages economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita in the long run (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions extends beyond economic efficiency to include power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Ogilvie challenges the notion that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were efficient, arguing instead that they were shaped by distributional conflicts among different social groups. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions emerge (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin further complicates the picture by suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating that institutional evolution is a complex process influenced by multiple factors over time. This perspective emphasizes the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other determinants of economic growth .

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs. This reduction was instrumental in fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. These institutions facilitated market exchanges and supported the development of a long-term capital market that was critical for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) highlights that this English financial revolution reflected an increased security of property rights over time, which is essential for both capital markets and overall economic growth. The political model outlined in Section 2 of North's paper illustrates how the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the Hundred Years' War laid the groundwork for these institutional developments (North 1989, p.9). This fiscal crisis underscored the need for credible commitments from the state, which in turn supported the evolution of secure property rights. Institutional changes alone cannot drive economic growth; they must be part of a broader framework that includes effective enforcement and adaptability. The political factors underpinning economic growth are thus as important as the rules themselves (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the variation in European mortality rates led to divergent colonization strategies and institutions. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. The impact of colonial institutions is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonial rule, with a central role played by the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that these findings are robust across various measures of institutions, suggesting the quality of institutions matters for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This underscores the enduring influence of colonial-era institutional legacies on contemporary economic performance.

The significant roles played by both colonial rule and indigenous initiatives in shaping African economic outcomes, as emphasized by Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18), challenge the simplistic view of institutional legacies. This literature highlights how colonial governments often encouraged commodity production even where European settlers were absent, aligning with indigenous interests to foster economic growth. Colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives created by indigenous action. For example, Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view of colonial rule shows that institutions are not merely imposed but also negotiated and shaped by local dynamics.

For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to encourage their subjects' material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins supports this perspective by arguing that Africa's dismal economic record is linked to poor institutions and that understanding these institutional dynamics can inform policy reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This implies that effective institutional reform requires a deep understanding of both colonial legacies and indigenous initiatives.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African economic performance has been more dynamic than often portrayed, with periods of growth and divergence in real wages across different regions. This perspective challenges the notion of a persistent historical impediment to African development by highlighting the availability of extensive economic data for the colonial era and beyond. Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrate that these economies exhibited varying patterns of growth and decline tied to factors such as foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner further elaborate on this dynamic by showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, suggesting institutional factors played a crucial role in determining economic outcomes during this period. This implies that while periods of growth were possible, the sustainability of these gains was contingent on the effectiveness of local institutions in responding to changing economic conditions.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) demonstrate that economic growth during the early modern period was already divergent between Europe and Asia. This divergence is evident in the distinct patterns of economic development and living standards across different regions, highlighting the complex interplay of factors influencing long-run economic growth before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta note that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the service sector played a pivotal role in early modern economic growth, with implications for the distribution of development gains across different segments of society. Van Zanden also points out that the focus of economic development shifted to countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England, leading to significant changes in relative price levels of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This regional shift underscores how geographic and economic factors intertwined to shape early modern growth patterns. This implies that while certain regions experienced substantial economic growth, the benefits did not uniformly translate into better living conditions for all.

Distributional conflicts are key to understanding economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies, challenging efficiency-focused interpretations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the structure of these institutions reflects how elites shaped them to maintain their power. This is evident in the persistence of serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in Europe, which Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues were maintained despite their inefficiency because they served the interests of the ruling classes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This inequality was exacerbated by initial factor endowments, which led to extreme disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these inequalities had profound implications for economic development, as they shaped institutions that perpetuated unequal access to resources and opportunities. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the shared beliefs of a society can be part of its institutional structure, influencing how institutions evolve over time. This evolution is driven by efficiency considerations but is deeply intertwined with distributional conflicts. This insight underscores the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that underpin economic institutions, as these dynamics can significantly influence long-term economic growth patterns.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that Africa's economic underdevelopment can be attributed to the enduring impact of the slave trade, which fostered a culture of mistrust. This mistrust, they claim, persists and significantly influences contemporary trust levels, thereby offering an alternative explanation for Africa's economic struggles compared to institutional theories. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that Africa’s slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, highlighting their profound and lasting effects on the continent’s development trajectory (Nunn 2008, p.2). This long duration stands out from the approximately 75-year period of official colonial rule, suggesting that the legacy of slavery is deeply embedded in Africa's economic history. Moreover, Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that sustained trade was facilitated by centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom (Nunn 2008, p.19). This observation underscores how pre-colonial institutions were critical for supporting external trade, which was crucial during the era of slave trades. The mistrust engendered by these trades has had enduring effects on trust levels, even affecting economic development today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) provide evidence that internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that addressing the cultural legacy of mistrust is paramount for fostering economic growth in Africa, as it highlights a significant barrier to development that persists independent of institutional frameworks.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr's emphasis on useful knowledge aligns with Kuznets' view that epochal innovations are pivotal in sustained growth. These innovations necessitate institutional adjustments to be efficiently integrated into economies, as they often conflict with existing societal structures and ideologies. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for economic growth. Kuznets highlights the importance of resolving conflicts arising from technological changes without excessive costs or long-term weakening of political structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr's work reinforces this idea by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are fundamental to growth, they must be accompanied by appropriate institutional changes to ensure long-term economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for sustainable development and investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied significantly based on mortality rates, leading to different institutional setups in colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, had profound impacts on current income per capita. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, both critical for long-term economic growth.

The evolution of institutions is a product of efficiency but also of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights are crucial in shaping institutional structures. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how power dynamics influence the development of institutions that either promote or hinder economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) adds that colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, emphasizing the adaptive nature of institutions in response to local conditions.

Furthermore, the role of institutions is underscored by their ability to facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs over time. North (1989, p.6) explains how efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms lead to falling costs per transaction, thereby fostering economic growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) observation that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth.

The significance of institutional adaptation is further emphasized by the need for complementary progress in technological advancements and political structures. Hopkins (2009, p.8) points out that 'good' institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. This echoes North's (1989, p.9) argument about the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic growth.

A shared finding across these sources is that the quality and type of institutions established in a region significantly influence its long-run economic trajectory. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show how different measures of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all correlate with economic outcomes. This is supported by Ogilvie's (2007, p.3) call to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, highlighting their indispensable role in economic history.

The remaining disagreement centers around whether institutions are shaped primarily by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is countered by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.6) emphasis on population density and prosperity as key factors influencing European policies in colonies, which directly shaped institutional outcomes. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from the measurement of long-term income per capita across former colonies, showing how extractive institutions introduced by colonialism led to lower economic growth compared to inclusive ones (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
