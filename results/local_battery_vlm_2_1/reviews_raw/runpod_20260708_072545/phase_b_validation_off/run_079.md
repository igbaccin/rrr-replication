The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians alike. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, with implications for policy and development strategies. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. One camp argues that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, is crucial for sustained economic development. This perspective posits that institutions shape incentives for investment and innovation, thereby fostering long-term growth. Another camp suggests that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent on other factors such as population density and prosperity, which influence the policies pursued by colonizers. The debate also extends to whether distributional conflicts or efficiency considerations are more critical in shaping core economic institutions. This tension underscores the complexity of understanding how institutions interact with other variables to drive economic growth over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2) propose that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional frameworks in colonies. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they created extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on current income per capita. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood through patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth. This perspective highlights the political dynamics underlying institutional design, where powerful groups shape institutions to maintain their control and extract resources. North (1989, p.6) argues that the creation of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms should lead to falling transaction costs per unit of output and a growing proportion of GNP from market exchanges.

(Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8) argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The prominence attached to the role of institutions enables the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the evolution of new financial tools led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This development was pivotal because it increased the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further highlight that the success of this institutional setup was evident in capital markets, showing a strong commitment to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement. Their analysis suggests that while institutions are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structures may not significantly hinder growth. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple variables in understanding long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The divergence in institutional frameworks was largely driven by mortality rates among Europeans in these regions. Colonies with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subjected to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. Areas with low European mortality, like the United States and Australia, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions persisted over time, influencing current income per capita by hindering long-term economic growth. This approach resulted in equilibrium institutions that prioritized short-term gains over long-run growth, as they were designed to benefit the colonizers at the expense of local populations.

Acemoglu et al. also note that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who often resisted changes that might threaten their control or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), suggesting a complex interplay between political power and institutional design. This nuanced view underscores the enduring impact of colonial legacies on economic development, as these institutions continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) argue that the legacy of British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was not uniformly negative for educational development. Frankema shows that demand for missionary education in the region was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by local political leaders, which were influenced by the new social and political institutions implemented under British rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that, while colonial legacies may have constrained economic growth overall, they also created specific incentives for educational development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) add a nuanced view to this picture: export production patterns in African countries were tightly linked with local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This implies that the impact of colonial rule on economic growth was mediated by these factors, highlighting a complex interplay between institutional legacies and other contextual variables. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this view by noting that British rule was more conducive to educational development than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). Therefore, the specific nature of colonial institutions, particularly those introduced under British rule, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories in sub-Saharan Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) assert that specific institutional reforms in Africa are targeted at reducing poverty but do not directly address long-run economic growth. The shared finding is that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes, particularly through their impact on investment incentives.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This indicates a need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth in Africa, as well as the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This legacy significantly impacts contemporary trust levels, which are crucial for economic development. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade operates through both external and internal channels. Externally, it affects the rule of law and the perceived trustworthiness of others; internally, it shapes individuals' norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This dual mechanism highlights how historical legacies can have enduring effects on social capital, a critical factor in economic growth. Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.15) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations sometimes responded to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that while the slave trade's legacy is profound, it is not the sole determinant of contemporary trust levels.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe by influencing how resources and opportunities were distributed among different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie contends that institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds emerged not from efficiency considerations but from struggles over the distribution of power and resources. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily designed to maximize economic performance or efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument by showing how different factor endowments in New World societies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped institutional development. They argue that extreme wealth disparities and political imbalances in these colonies resulted from initial conditions and were the product of institutional design for economic growth. This perspective underscores the role of distributional conflicts in creating enduring institutions that perpetuated existing inequalities.

Ogilvie's analysis highlights how beliefs about legitimacy and rights, as well as cultural values, played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These shared beliefs acted as part of the institutional structure, influencing political legitimacy and individual rights. This dynamic is evident in New World societies where extreme wealth disparities were maintained through institutional frameworks that protected elite interests.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and power within societies. The extractive institutions established by European colonizers in regions with high mortality rates illustrate how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequality over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists and promote long-run growth, highlight the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions is further supported by Ogilvie's argument that shared beliefs can reinforce distributional conflicts and justify existing power structures. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal in relative incomes among these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of beliefs in shaping institutions is crucial for understanding economic growth. North underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, a dimension essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective emphasizes that institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience are key factors in understanding long-term economic growth. The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin's argument that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action further supports this view (Austin 2008, p.15).

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth because they shape the distribution of resources and power within societies. This point is grounded by the fact that extractive institutions established by European colonizers in regions with high mortality rates illustrate how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequality over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement concerns whether these extractive institutions are a result of efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie shows that core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This precise historical evidence settles the disagreement, as it demonstrates how shared beliefs can reinforce distributional conflicts and justify existing power structures, thereby perpetuating economic inequalities over time.
