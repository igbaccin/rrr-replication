The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of development economics and economic history. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules of the game that structure human interaction, are seen by some scholars as fundamental drivers of economic performance. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play a more significant role. Some argue that institutions matter because they create incentives for investment and innovation, which in turn drive economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Others contend that while institutions are important, they are not the sole determinant of long-run economic growth; instead, they interact with other factors to produce economic outcomes. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development in both developed and developing countries. Understanding whether and how institutions shape economic growth can guide policymakers in designing effective strategies for promoting sustained economic prosperity.

Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonialism significantly influenced long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Austin (2008, p.2) both highlight how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes in former European colonies. In places like the United States and Australia, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which fostered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) adds that these institutional differences were shaped by various factors, including the presence or absence of European settlers and the specific economic interests of colonial administrations. These findings underscore the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic performance.

The mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth are complex and multifaceted (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. For example, the persistence of serfdom in pre-industrial Europe can be attributed to the power dynamics between different social groups, which prioritized maintaining existing power structures over promoting economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that efficient institutions are always adopted for their economic benefits, suggesting instead that institutional change is often driven by political and social considerations. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further emphasizes the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This interplay between institutional quality and economic outcomes highlights the need for policies that promote effective governance and rule-based systems.

Institutions matter not just in their direct effects on economic growth but also through their indirect influence on other critical factors. North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions play a crucial role in reducing transaction costs, which are essential for facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic development (North 1989, p.9). For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. This perspective underscores how institutions can create a stable and predictable environment that encourages long-term investment and economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this point by showing how different measures of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive and property rights, are strongly correlated with economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This shared finding across multiple studies reinforces the idea that robust institutions are essential for sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue that involves complex interactions between historical legacies, political dynamics, and economic factors. Understanding these interactions is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at promoting economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the creation of institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This decline reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade-off where certain rights were relinquished to Parliament in exchange for revenues, illustrating how political dynamics shaped institutional evolution (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework underpinned the growth of capital markets, which became evident through the increased specialization and division of labor that multiplied aggregate exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) caution that the regime's commitment to these rules is critical; the degree of credible commitment by the sovereign significantly impacts how these institutions are enforced and changed. This nuance highlights that while institutional evolution was crucial, the sustainability of economic growth also depended on the political will to uphold these institutions.

Acemoglu et al. illustrate how European colonists established extractive institutions in areas with high mortality rates, which persisted and affected current income per capita. This institutional framework was a deliberate policy choice by European colonizers. This mechanism highlights how the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to impact economic outcomes in former colonies. The persistence of extractive institutions underscores the need for institutional reforms that promote inclusive growth, which focuses on more than merely extracting resources.

The new economic history of Africa highlights that institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. This perspective is supported by Frankema's argument that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, highlighting that demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political arrangements. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century; this evidence suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was more nuanced than previously thought (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner also note that growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including political institutions. This variation underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on African economies during the colonial era, highlighting the need for further research into the specific mechanisms through which institutions shaped economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The interplay between endogenous African initiatives and exogenous colonial influences underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional change in Africa, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these dynamics can inform policies aimed at promoting inclusive growth.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn contends that the impact of these slave trades was profound and long-lasting. Over nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, Africa simultaneously experienced four distinct slave trades, trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, Atlantic, and internal African slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extensive period of exploitation left deep economic scars that persisted well into the colonial era. The adverse effects of these slave trades included a significant depletion of human capital, disrupted social structures, and weakened political institutions. These factors collectively undermined Africa's ability to develop economically in ways similar to other regions (Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn’s analysis is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Austin qualifies this perspective. He notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the impact of slave trades on economic development was not uniform across Africa and that local conditions played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. Governors like Clifford opted for higher production equilibria, indicating that colonial policies could be responsive to local incentives and actions. The interplay between the legacy of slave trades and the varying responses of colonial administrations highlights the complex nature of Africa's economic development trajectory.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr elaborates on this by showing how the proliferation of useful knowledge, particularly through scientific advancements, underpins sustained economic improvement. This intellectual capital accumulation was complemented by institutional developments that facilitated its diffusion and application. Kuznets (1973, p.1) describes a pattern where technological progress required corresponding adjustments in societal institutions to harness innovations effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The implication is that technology drives growth, but this process depends on the ability of societies to manage the conflicts arising from modernization. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is more irreversible than institutional changes, the latter are essential for sustaining economic improvement over the long term (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that although technology is the mainspring of growth, institutions play a crucial role in mediating its effects and ensuring sustained development.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions emerged primarily as a result of power struggles between social groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate how inequalities in New World societies led to the development of institutions that favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. A specific mechanism by which distributional conflicts influenced institutional outcomes was through the control of political power. Elite groups used their political influence to design institutions that protected their interests at the expense of broader economic efficiency (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in Europe's guild systems, where craft and merchant guilds often acted as barriers to entry for new competitors, thereby maintaining the status quo and limiting innovation. This highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that, while beneficial for certain groups in the short term, may ultimately hinder long-term economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating stable frameworks that facilitate investment and innovation. North shows that effective property rights and their enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6), while Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that inclusive institutions, such as those established in settler colonies like the United States, foster economic development by encouraging investment and protecting private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings are supported by North's argument that secure property rights are essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6), highlighting how institutions can drive sustained economic performance.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is not always straightforward. For instance, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often lead to institutional arrangements that benefit certain groups in the short term but hinder long-term development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s observation that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can stifle economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin further complicates this picture by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, as different historical paths and periods must be considered to understand how institutions evolve and impact economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted. While inclusive institutions can drive sustained development by encouraging investment and innovation, distributional conflicts and extractive institutions often lead to arrangements that hinder long-term economic performance. The historical evidence from settler colonies versus non-settler colonies shows that inclusive institutions foster growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
