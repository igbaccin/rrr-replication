The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is the relative weight to assign to institutions as causal factors versus other determinants such as geography, culture, or technology. One live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primary drivers of economic development or secondary enablers of underlying structural forces. In particular, one camp argues that institutions directly influence growth by providing stable frameworks for market exchanges and investment, while another posits that institutions evolve in response to deeper, often exogenous factors like culture or geographic endowments. The tension between these views underscores the need to understand how institutions shape economic outcomes over the long term. This is because the causal mechanisms at play determine whether institutional reforms can effectively drive growth, or if such efforts are likely to be futile without addressing underlying structural issues.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth and development. They assert that the establishment of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms significantly reduces transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and fostering long-term capital markets. This institutional framework is critical for economic growth as it encourages investment and ensures the stability needed for sustained development. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this through the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs by securing property rights (North 1989, p.9). The consequent financial revolution underscores how institutional changes can drive economic growth by enhancing market efficiency and encouraging investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this argument by noting that institutions shaped by colonial powers often maximized rents for colonists and promoted long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights a crucial implication: the type of institution established can significantly influence whether economic growth is sustained or stymied. Therefore, understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions are formed and enforced is essential for designing effective policies that promote economic development over time.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a critical role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This argument is shared by Austin (2008, p.2) who emphasizes the importance of institutional evolution in shaping long-term economic outcomes, noting how patterns of investment and policy experience over centuries are crucial to understanding contemporary economic performance (Austin 2008, p.2). The mechanism through which this occurs involves credible commitments from the regime or sovereign to enforce rules governing economic exchange. This credibility is essential for fostering a stable environment where private property rights can flourish, thereby encouraging long-term investments and economic growth.

The process of institutional evolution varies across different historical contexts. For example, Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behaviour suggests that while secure property rights are crucial, the specific paths through which institutions evolve can vary significantly depending on local conditions and the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth involves establishing secure property rights and the dynamic interplay between institutional frameworks and local contexts. This interplay shapes how effectively institutions can promote sustained economic development over time.

Long-term structural changes in Europe from 1500 to 1800 were significant but did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Van Zanden's analysis of wage data across European cities reveals a complex relationship between economic development and living standards, suggesting that urbanization alone was insufficient for widespread prosperity (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This perspective is corroborated by Pamuk, who highlights the institutional flexibility in regions like Holland as a critical factor enabling rapid adaptation to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Regions with rigid monopolies, such as Tuscany, struggled to keep pace. Structural changes required economic shifts and adaptable institutions that could respond to emerging conditions. Therefore, the relationship between long-term structural changes and economic development in Europe was mediated by institutional frameworks that varied significantly across regions. The interplay between these structural shifts and local institutional contexts shaped the trajectories of economic development during this period.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. This variation in colonial policies created different incentive structures for investment in human capital and economic development. Hopkins highlights how the new economic history of Africa brought attention to the role of institutions by showing that 'good' institutions provide essential incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating colonial impacts on economic development were not uniformly negative. This suggests that while colonial rule imposed certain institutional frameworks, local conditions and policies could mitigate or exacerbate their effects on long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) explore how specific historical factors, such as the role of women in African society, influenced economic development. They show that pre-colonial and colonial African economies were significantly shaped by the contributions of women, who were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming but played crucial roles in agricultural production. This dynamic was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule, which codified the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The impact of these gendered institutions on economic development is further illuminated when considering how the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the enduring consequences of historical institutional frameworks (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The internal norms of mistrust fostered by the slave trade are at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This finding underscores the deep-seated nature of these norms and their profound influence on contemporary African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Mokyr argues that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets elaborates on this idea, stating that modern technology necessitates institutional adjustments to facilitate its efficient use and integration into society. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional changes is dynamic, with technological progress often outpacing societal readiness to adapt. Mokyr further emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress to support technological innovations, the sustainability of economic improvements would be significantly limited. Kuznets points out that resolving conflicts arising from these technological shifts is crucial for sustained growth. If not managed effectively, these conflicts can weaken the political fabric of society and impede long-term economic development. This highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in managing the social and political challenges posed by rapid technological change.

Distributional conflicts are central to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and their impact on long-run economic growth. Ogilvie asserts that these conflicts offer a more compelling explanation than institutional efficiency for the emergence of serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in this period. This perspective is shared by Sokoloff and Engerman who highlight how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The mechanism involves the concentration of power and resources among a privileged few, which restricts competition and maintains inequality. This dynamic creates a feedback loop where economic institutions reinforce existing power structures, thereby hindering broader economic development. This pattern varies across all societies. Sokoloff and Engerman note that while factor endowments initially set the stage for these inequalities, the persistence of unequal institutions over time significantly affects long-term development trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This implies that addressing distributional conflicts is crucial for achieving sustainable economic growth, as it directly impacts the evolution of institutions that either support or hinder development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding development trajectories across societies. Institutions create and enforce rules that shape incentives for economic actors, thereby influencing investment decisions, productivity, and overall economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical development of institutions such as the Bank of England exemplifies how secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs and fostering a stable environment for long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic development.

The historical legacy of colonial institutions further illustrates how institutional structures can either support or hinder economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates, with extractive states in high-mortality colonies and more inclusive institutions in low-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on current income levels, showing that institutional legacies can persist over centuries and significantly influence long-term economic outcomes. Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with local contexts (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This interaction is crucial for explaining why some countries adopted growth-promoting institutions while others did not.

The persistence of unequal institutions over time significantly affects long-term development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North's argument that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). Institutions that enforce secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs and fostering stable environments for investment and market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The persistence of unequal institutions over time significantly affects long-term development trajectories, as seen in the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate.
