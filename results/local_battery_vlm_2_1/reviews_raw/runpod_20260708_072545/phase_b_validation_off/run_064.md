The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of development economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is understanding how societies transition from poverty to prosperity over extended periods, with implications for policy design and intervention strategies. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play a more significant role. This debate is crucial because it informs how economists and policymakers approach development challenges, particularly in regions that have historically lagged behind.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the direct causal impact of institutions on economic growth through mechanisms such as secure property rights and effective governance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional frameworks in colonies, with some adopting extractive states while others established rule-of-law systems. These institutional differences significantly influenced current income per capita levels. This perspective is supported by North's work, which highlights how the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The argument that secure property rights are critical to long-term capital markets and economic growth underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic development.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. These institutional changes were driven by fiscal crises that necessitated trade-offs between certain rights and parliamentary revenues. North and Weingast further emphasize the importance of credible commitments from the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange. This commitment is crucial for the development of markets and sustained economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The effectiveness of these institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their capacity to evolve with shifting economic landscapes. The evolution of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms is essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic development over time (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutions were diverse and varied according to European mortality rates and colonization strategies. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these colonial institutions means that former colonies with extractive institutions have struggled to achieve sustained economic growth, while those with more inclusive institutions have fared better. The implication is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to affect economic outcomes in former colonies, even as these countries seek to modernize and develop their economies.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) identifies the role of institutions as pivotal in shaping economic outcomes. He notes the importance of "good" institutions, which provide incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This includes examining how specific historical or geographical conditions have impacted African welfare development, which is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also observes that while institutions are important, the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not exclusively determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The implication is that while historical and geographical factors are important, they do not dictate long-term economic outcomes in Africa. Therefore, institutional reforms must consider both endogenous and exogenous influences to foster sustained economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Austin 2008, p.2) examine specific institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. One mechanism through which institutions shaped European economies was via colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic action. For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged commodity production to align with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This pattern indicates that institutional changes in pre-industrial Europe stemmed from intricate interactions between colonial powers and local populations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) adds that distributional conflicts often influenced the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The implication is that institutional changes were not always driven by a straightforward pursuit of economic growth but could be shaped by power dynamics and conflicting interests.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27). The slave trade in Africa had profound adverse effects on the continent's long-term economic development. This phenomenon resulted from colonialism as well as the extended period of slavery that lasted nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that this extensive slave trade period had lasting impacts on African economies, shaping them in ways that are still evident today. The mechanism through which the slave trade affected economic development was multifaceted; one key aspect was the erosion of trust within communities. The transatlantic slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight how this lack of trust, which has been documented as crucial for economic development by other scholars, significantly hindered Africa's progress. This internal erosion of trust, more than external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, was a primary channel through which the slave trade impacted African economies. Nunn (2008, p.19) adds that the absence of sustained local trade before European contact further compounded these issues. The historical context shows that even when centralized governments and well-developed markets were present, as in the Kongo kingdom, they were not sufficient to overcome the economic setbacks caused by the slave trade.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations, suggesting that while technology drives growth, institutions facilitate its absorption. Mokyr (2005, p.49) points out that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, indicating that institutions play a crucial role in mitigating rent-seeking behaviours that could erode economic gains. This interaction between technological progress and institutional flexibility underscores the complex dynamics at play in long-run economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to mitigate rent-seeking behaviors that could erode economic gains and facilitate societal adjustments to technological advancements (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks, which significantly impacted current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The English financial revolution was a pivotal moment where institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, both critical to long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs over time (North 1989, p.6).

The interaction between technological progress and institutional flexibility is also evident in the historical context of colonial administrations. Austin highlights how colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, demonstrating that institutions adapted to local conditions and economic actions (Austin 2008, p.2). This adaptability is crucial because institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily prioritize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies, which shaped the institutional landscape in former colonies and influenced their industrialization paths (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The new economic history of Africa provides further insights into how institutions can inform reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins argues that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, a key driver of development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with North's view on the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns how to explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for institutions like serfdom, communities, and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is in colonial administration policies which show how indigenous economic actions influenced institutional prescriptions, demonstrating the adaptability of institutions to local conditions. This shows that distributional conflicts are not the only factor shaping core economic institutions.
