The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central debate among economists and historians alike. At stake is an understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods and whether certain institutional frameworks are more conducive to sustained prosperity than others. A key disagreement centers on how much institutions, as opposed to other factors like geography or culture, influence long-term economic outcomes. One position holds that specific types of institutions, those that enforce property rights and encourage investment, are pivotal for growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Yet another perspective posits that distributional conflicts are a more significant driver of institutional development in pre-industrial societies, suggesting that the focus on efficiency may overlook crucial power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another view argues that institutions are not the sole determinant of economic performance and that other factors such as fiscal crises and political legitimacy play equally important roles in shaping growth trajectories. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the complex nature of institutional influence on long-run economic growth, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how different institutional frameworks interact with broader historical contexts to foster or hinder economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) share the view that institutions are foundational to long-term economic growth. North argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms lower transaction costs, fostering market exchanges and economic development. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that European colonists established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates; in high-mortality areas they set up extractive institutions that hindered long-term growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). One specific mechanism through which institutions shape economic growth is the creation of financial instruments and markets. North illustrates this with the English financial revolution, where the establishment of the Bank of England reduced transaction costs and promoted economic growth (North 1989, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that even efficient institutions may not always promote long-term growth if they are extractive in nature or designed to maximize short-term rents rather than foster sustained development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights the importance of understanding the specific design and objectives of institutions in assessing their impact on economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) illustrate that the political commitment to enforce rules underpinned the development of markets, highlighting how the Glorious Revolution's constitutional settlements shaped expectations about future policy stability. This institutional change was pivotal in encouraging investment and economic growth by providing a credible commitment to property rights. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that this process involved more than just establishing new institutions; it also required adapting them over time in response to economic incentives, as seen in the colonial context where changes in institutional prescriptions were driven by indigenous economic action. The role of institutions in promoting growth should be examined alongside other factors such as fiscal security and political stability, which can affect the adoption and effectiveness of these institutions (Austin 2008, p.15).

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) share that skill premiums are predictive of long-term economic performance in Africa. They argue that the skill premium is determined by factors such as training costs and institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This mechanism is evident in the role of guilds in Western Europe, which facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills but also acted as local cartels, restricting entry and maintaining high wages. This dynamic is crucial for understanding economic performance in regions like Africa, where historical data show varying levels of per capita income influenced by factors such as export markets and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) observe that the timing and scale of growth in export production differed significantly across countries. This variation underscores the importance of local endowments and transport costs, which can modulate the impact of skill premiums on economic performance. The interplay between skill premiums and institutional frameworks thus emerges as a critical factor in shaping long-term economic outcomes in Africa.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) along with Hopkins (2009, p.18) examine various factors influencing African economic development without directly addressing how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that women played a significant role in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. Women were often relegated to less fertile lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production, a dynamic reinforced by colonial rule’s codification of customary law (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This gendered division of labor had profound implications for economic development, as it shaped patterns of land use and agricultural productivity. Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Historical accounts often overlooked the role of women in economic activities, despite their significant contributions to agriculture and trade. This oversight suggests a broader pattern where certain groups' economic roles are underrecognized, potentially leading to incomplete institutional reforms that fail to fully harness the productive capacities of all segments of society.

The invention of custom under colonial rule reinforced existing gender inequalities, limiting women's access to resources and economic opportunities. This historical context underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks can either perpetuate or mitigate such inequalities. The contributions of women in pre-colonial Africa highlight the potential for inclusive development strategies that recognize and build on the diverse roles played by different segments of society.

The legacy of mistrust instilled by the slave trade has had profound and enduring effects on African development, as evidenced by Nunn and Wantchekon's finding that it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional legacy is deeply rooted in the historical trauma of slavery, which has shaped contemporary social dynamics. The mistrust fostered by the slave trade manifests as a pervasive lack of faith in others' intentions and actions, hindering collective action and economic cooperation. Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks can either perpetuate or mitigate such inequalities. The persistence of mistrust is a structural barrier to economic growth and social cohesion, as it erodes the foundations of trust necessary for effective governance and market functioning. Frankema (Nunn 2008, p.19) demonstrates that sustained trade did not occur until centralized governments with well-developed markets emerged, highlighting how institutional frameworks can facilitate or impede development trajectories. The legacy of mistrust instilled by the slave trade persists in contemporary African societies, shaping economic and social interactions in profound ways.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the main driver behind economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They contend that these conflicts shaped key institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communal structures, which were not primarily determined by efficiency considerations but rather by power dynamics among different social groups. Ogilvie specifically highlights how shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutional changes that might enhance property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The implication is that institutional frameworks can become entrenched in ways that favor existing power structures rather than promoting economic efficiency. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they interact with broader cultural and ideological factors to determine institutional outcomes. The interplay between these forces underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay of distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs. Institutions are the rule-based structures that guide human interaction (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), and their effectiveness in promoting economic growth depends on how they allocate power and resources among different groups within society. Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights significantly influence institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These beliefs can either support or hinder economic growth, depending on whether they foster efficient property rights and contract enforcement mechanisms. The historical evidence from Europe shows that cultural factors played a crucial role in shaping institutions, with some societies developing more productive institutions than others (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The creation of secure property rights is a critical factor in long-run economic growth. North argues for the primacy of institutions over other factors such as population and savings rates, showing how institutional changes can dramatically reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This is evident in the development of financial instruments like those created by the Bank of England, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs. The establishment of these institutions reflects increased security of property rights over time, which is essential for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of colonialism on institutional development provides further insight into how institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional frameworks in colonies with high mortality rates versus those where settlers established extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions, with some developing more productive institutions than others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The reversal of fortunes among former European colonies highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. show that the relative incomes of these colonies reversed due to the introduction of extractive institutions by colonial powers, not just geographic factors alone (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continues to influence economic development in these regions today.

Institutional reforms are crucial for addressing poverty and promoting economic growth in Africa. Hopkins argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional changes that eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people on the continent (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The effectiveness of these reforms depends on creating institutions that provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Austin's work further supports this by showing how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions cannot be overstated. These conflicts often favor existing power structures rather than promoting economic efficiency, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic growth.

Institutions matter fundamentally to long-term economic growth because they shape how power and resources are allocated within societies, as North shows (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) . Hopkins notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not always correlate with poor institutional outcomes in Africa. This disagreement settles when comparing relative rates of investment in the former colonies versus their African counterparts, showing a direct link between institutions and economic growth.
