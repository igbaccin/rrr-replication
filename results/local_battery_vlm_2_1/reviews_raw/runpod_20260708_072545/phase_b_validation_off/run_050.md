The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science. At stake intellectually is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. A live disagreement exists between those who emphasize the primacy of institutional frameworks in driving economic growth and those who attribute it to other factors, such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks. The former group posits that well-designed institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and ensure contractual enforcement, are crucial for fostering economic development. Some scholars posit that cultural norms and historical events, like the slave trade, significantly influence economic outcomes (Author Year p.N). This debate highlights the complexity of economic growth and the multifaceted nature of its determinants.

The centrality of institutions to long-run economic growth is underscored by North's seminal work, which argues that institutions matter significantly in economic development (North 1989, p.6). North posits that institutions define property rights and enforce them effectively. This enforcement reduces transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and thereby fostering economic growth. In a related vein, North and Weingast highlight the role of political institutions in securing property rights through credible commitments (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Their analysis of seventeenth-century England demonstrates how institutional changes, particularly those governing public choice, were pivotal in establishing secure property rights and promoting economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for the long-term sustainability of economic institutions.

The causal relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that the institutionalist approach, while valuable, faces challenges in quantitative testing and explaining why certain countries adopted growth-promoting institutions while others did not. This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes.

The debate extends to the specific types of institutions that matter most. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, characterized by rapid resource extraction and weak property rights enforcement, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of initial institutional choices on long-term economic development.

Distributional conflicts, along with efficiency considerations, shape institutional outcomes. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped more by distributional struggles than by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view challenges the notion that institutions always evolve towards maximizing economic efficiency, suggesting instead that they often reflect the balance of power among different social groups. The role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic outcomes is also significant. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural perspective complements institutional analyses by highlighting how historical events can shape long-term economic behaviors and attitudes.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal for long-run economic growth. They contend that the creation of effective institutions, such as the Bank of England in 1694, led to a significant decline in transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution illustrates how political and economic institutions interact to facilitate market exchanges and support economic growth. In colonies with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole rather than investing in long-term growth. This pattern suggests that the type of institutions established by colonizers had profound effects on the long-run economic trajectories of these regions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize this point, arguing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism rather than geographic factors. This highlights a crucial implication: the initial design and purpose of institutions can have enduring effects on economic outcomes, even if those institutions are extractive or inefficient in the long run.

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are crucial for fostering long-run economic growth. They show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was key to this development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The authors demonstrate that the English Parliament's ability to constrain the monarch and establish a credible commitment to property rights facilitated economic growth by creating an environment where investors could expect returns on their investments. This credible commitment was underpinned by institutional changes that bound the sovereign to enforce rules governing economic exchange, thus fostering market development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast's analysis highlights that the political factors underpinning economic growth are not merely about the rules of exchange but also about how these rules are enforced and changed. The success of this institutional framework is evident in the robust performance of capital markets during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Ogilvie critiques the notion that institutions are always efficient, arguing instead for a distributional conflict approach to understanding economic institutions. She contends that institutions often reflect the interests of powerful groups rather than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the institutional framework established in seventeenth-century England may have been shaped by the power dynamics of the time, with property rights evolving to protect the interests of the elite. This view implies a more nuanced understanding of property rights and economic growth, where institutions are not just about efficiency but also about the distribution of power and resources.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective posits that technological progress and innovations play a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge serves as the primary driver of economic growth. This implies that technological advancements create new opportunities for productivity gains, which in turn stimulate economic expansion. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires corresponding adjustments in institutions and ideologies to facilitate efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests a dynamic interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation, where the latter supports rather than drives growth.

Mokyr's focus on the growth of useful knowledge introduces an important caveat. He acknowledges that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This means that even though technology is a primary driver, institutions play a supportive role in reducing access costs and coordinating efforts. The implication here is that although technological advancements are essential for economic growth, they need to be accompanied by institutional progress to fully realize their potential benefits. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions may not be the primary drivers of long-term economic development, they still play a critical role in facilitating the effective use of technological innovations and ensuring sustained economic improvement.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that Africa's slave trades have been pivotal in shaping the continent’s economic development. The adverse effects of these trades are not merely historical artifacts but have enduring consequences on contemporary African societies. The internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. The significance of this mistrust is underscored by its pervasive impact on various aspects of economic development, including international trade and political institutions. Nunn's analysis highlights the temporal extent of these trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, compared to the approximately 75-year period of official colonial rule. This extended duration suggests that the slave trade's impact on African development is more profound and lasting than other historical factors (Nunn 2008, p.2). The persistence of this mistrust, even in the absence of direct external influences, underscores its deep-rooted nature and the need for targeted interventions to address it.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that this impact is characterized by historical economic indicators and trends. Their work highlights the importance of understanding long-term economic development in Africa, including income distribution patterns during the colonial era. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels to assess the persistence of poverty across British African colonies from 1880 to 1965. This analysis is crucial for comprehending the structural effects of colonial rule on long-term welfare development in Africa, as it provides a more nuanced view of economic performance during this period.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) provide additional insights into the economic dynamics of African countries by examining the relationship between export production and foreign trade. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These variations underscore the complexity of Africa's economic development during colonial rule and the need for a more detailed understanding of these historical trends. This is particularly important when evaluating the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The interplay between export production and political institutions highlights the multifaceted nature of African economic performance under colonial rule, suggesting that economic growth was influenced by a combination of local factors and external influences. This availability of data underscores the potential for further research into how institutions shaped long-run economic growth in Africa during this period.

This approach can be extended to other regions and time periods, providing a comprehensive understanding of how institutions influenced economic performance in Africa over the long term. The detailed examination of historical economic indicators and trends is essential for identifying the specific mechanisms through which institutions shaped economic growth during the colonial era.

This is exemplified by the persistence of inequality in New World societies, which is deeply rooted in institutional frameworks that favoured elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)(Austin 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2) share the view that these institutions restricted competition, thereby entrenching economic disparities. This dynamic was evident in colonial policies that often prioritized elite interests over broader economic development (Austin 2008, p.15), a pattern that persisted through various government interventions. For instance, colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production to secure fiscal stability and align with the interests of their subjects (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies often reinforced existing inequalities by favouring those already in positions of power. This institutional bias towards elites not only limited economic competition but also shaped long-term development trajectories, leading to persistent disparities in wealth and political influence across New World societies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This enforcement mechanism is crucial for fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established institutions that varied widely in their effectiveness, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional frameworks directly influenced long-term economic outcomes. For instance, colonies with extractive institutions experienced persistent disparities in wealth and political influence (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin (2008, p.2) complements this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis often oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that the institutional evolution and policy experience over centuries are key to understanding long-term economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illuminated by their role in shaping distributional conflicts, which provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie emphasizes that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society and that these beliefs can influence economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth by enforcing rules governing economic exchange and ensuring that regimes are bound by these rules. This commitment is essential for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic development.

The historical evidence shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported long-term capital markets. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England provide a stark contrast to other European countries, where similar economic performance did not lead to revolutionary institutional changes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets and the enforcement of property rights, which were critical for economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement centers on whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due solely to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or if geographic factors also play a significant role (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by the evidence from capital markets, which shows that institutional changes were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The historical data on colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous commodity production to secure fiscal stability further supports this view (Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, as shown by the reversal in relative incomes among these regions. The institutional frameworks established during colonial times directly influenced economic outcomes and shaped long-term development trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
