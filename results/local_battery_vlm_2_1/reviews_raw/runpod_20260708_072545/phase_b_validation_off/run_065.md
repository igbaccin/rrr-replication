The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that lies at the heart of understanding development trajectories across nations. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography and culture, play more decisive roles. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars argue that certain types of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are essential for sustained economic development. Others contend that institutional frameworks alone cannot fully account for economic growth, suggesting that broader cultural and historical contexts also play crucial roles. The extent to which institutions can be engineered or reformed to spur economic growth remains a contentious issue, with implications for policy-making and economic theory.

A key stream of scholarship emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on long-run economic growth by focusing on the enforcement of property rights and the rule of law. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes in colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North also highlights the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights through institutions like the Bank of England exemplifies how institutional frameworks can lower transaction costs and support long-term capital markets. These findings underscore that effective institutions can create an environment conducive to investment and economic development by ensuring that property rights are protected and enforced. Furthermore, North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their analysis shows how credible commitment mechanisms underpin long-run economic performance.

The role of institutions is further elucidated by research on colonial legacies and their enduring effects on economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution over time is necessary to grasp how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon contribute to this discussion by showing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic conditions highlights the persistent influence of past institutions on current economic performance.

Another stream of scholarship explores how distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for pre-industrial Europe's serfdom, community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds than efficiency does (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that institutional frameworks are often shaped by power dynamics and distributional struggles as well as economic considerations. Ogilvie's emphasis on the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutions complements North’s view that institutions reflect broader social norms and values.

The interaction between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex, involving multiple factors that shape institutional outcomes and their effects on economic development. The evidence from historical and contemporary studies underscores the importance of secure property rights and rule of law in fostering economic growth. It also highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how broader cultural and historical contexts influence institutional frameworks and their impact on long-run economic performance. This interplay between institutions, culture, and history is crucial for comprehending the diverse trajectories of economic development across nations.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, emphasizing their role in shaping long-term economic development beyond neoclassical theories' focus on population and savings.

In colonies with high mortality rates, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, whereas in other regions, institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment were set up (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these colonial institutions significantly impacted current income per capita, underscoring the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the design and persistence of extractive institutions can hinder economic development by prioritizing short-term gains over sustainable growth. The historical legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the critical role of institutional frameworks in determining long-run economic performance.

Building on this idea, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The specific mechanism through which institutions fostered secure property rights involved the commitment of the regime to enforce these rules. Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that the effectiveness of property rights institutions can be influenced by local dynamics.

This interplay between institutional design and local context underscores the complexity of fostering secure property rights. The evolution of these institutions was a process shaped by various factors including colonial administration's needs for fiscal security and the incentives created by indigenous economic activities. As such, the development of effective property rights institutions requires both robust political commitments and adaptability to local conditions.

The accumulation of useful knowledge propels economic development by enabling new production methods and fostering innovation. Mokyr's work highlights how access costs to knowledge can be reduced by formal institutions, thereby fostering a more efficient diffusion of useful knowledge. This suggests that the sustainability of economic improvement depends on coordinating technological and institutional progress.

Kuznets notes that modern technology may conflict with traditional social structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The successful integration of new technologies into society requires resolving these conflicts without weakening the political fabric, underscoring the importance of a strong national state in managing growth-induced changes. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that economic reasonableness is essential for sustainable technological progress and that coordination failures can hinder economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks illustrates how economic growth is driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge as well as the ability to integrate and sustain these innovations within a society's institutions.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade's impact on African economic development is deeply rooted in the erosion of intergroup trust. They demonstrate that this mistrust is an internalised norm, making it at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn reinforces this by showing how the prolonged period of slave trades shaped Africa's economic development. This highlights that the adverse effects on trust are deeply ingrained, influencing subsequent generations' economic trajectories. The persistence of mistrust, therefore, continues to hinder African economies today.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts drive key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They both focus on how inequality shapes institutional development. Ogilvie’s analysis of serfdom, the community, the craft guild and the merchant guild reveals that these institutions were shaped by conflicts over power and resources as well as efficiency considerations alone. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman show how New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implication is that where distributional conflicts are intense, economic institutions may fail to promote growth and actively hinder it. Sokoloff and Engerman's work demonstrates that these institutions can be enduring, with their impact on development persisting over centuries (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This persistence highlights the deep-seated nature of institutional structures shaped by distributional conflicts and their long-term influence on economic trajectories.

North (1989, p.6) argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling costs per transaction, which in turn supports economic growth. This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who highlight the critical role of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution exemplifies how institutions can reduce transaction costs and enhance long-term capital markets, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9).
