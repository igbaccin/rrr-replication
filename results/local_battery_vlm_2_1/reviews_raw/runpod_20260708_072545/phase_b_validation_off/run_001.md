The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it directly engages with the fundamental drivers of prosperity and development over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes versus other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements. One position emphasizes that institutions are paramount in determining long-term growth trajectories; another argues that institutions alone cannot explain economic performance without considering external influences like colonial legacies and extractive practices.

A key debate involves whether institutions designed for resource extraction hinder or foster economic development in the long run: some scholars argue that these institutions, while detrimental initially, can eventually adapt to promote growth. Another perspective posits that the impact of institutions is contingent on their alignment with broader historical contexts, such as colonial policies and local socio-economic conditions. Moreover, there are views that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle where cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts play significant roles in shaping economic outcomes.

The debate over the role of institutions in long-run economic growth is not just academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies worldwide. Understanding how institutions influence economic trajectories can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable growth, particularly in regions historically disadvantaged by extractive practices or colonial legacies.

North and Weingast assert that England's 17th-century institutional innovations significantly reduced transaction costs by securing property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and new financial instruments marked a pivotal moment, as they facilitated a long-term capital market essential for economic growth. This period saw a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights, a crucial dimension for both capital markets and economic expansion.

North elaborates that this financial revolution was driven by the need to manage fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and ensure their enforcement is pivotal. This institutional framework lowered costs per transaction and expanded the transactions sector, which became a growing proportion of GNP as specialization and division of labor increased. North further notes how the evolution of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges was integral to this process (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of these institutions on economic growth is nuanced. North and Weingast acknowledge that while England's institutional changes were significant, France's economic performance before the French Revolution suggests that institutions alone did not determine economic outcomes. This implies that while secure property rights are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structures also play a role in shaping long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping the institutional frameworks that persisted after independence. This influence was driven by the different colonization strategies adopted by European powers. In high-mortality regions, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole; these institutions were not conducive to investment or economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Conversely, in areas with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, Europeans settled permanently and developed institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutional differences have had lasting effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that current income per capita in former colonies is significantly affected by these historical institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, the persistence of extractive institutions is not solely due to European policies; local political dynamics also play a crucial role (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The establishment of extractive states often reflects the interests of politically powerful groups who may resist institutional changes that could threaten their rents or political power. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between historical institutions and contemporary economic performance, underscoring how colonial policies have shaped long-run growth trajectories in former colonies.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had distinct impacts on educational development compared to other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that the impact of institutions on development can be traced back to differences in factor endowments. These differences led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, which persisted over time and affected the course of development through their impact on evolving institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The presence of British rule thus shaped local educational trajectories by fostering demand for education among African populations who saw new benefits from formal schooling in a rapidly changing institutional environment.

The slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development and are argued to have shaped long-run economic outcomes through their impact on trust (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom because it had a centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that the economic development of African societies was influenced by their institutional frameworks, which were strengthened or weakened by the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). The persistence of mistrust resulting from the slave trade has profound implications for contemporary African economies; it is argued to be a significant factor in reducing trust in both external environments and internal norms. This cultural legacy of mistrust can thus impede economic development by undermining social cohesion and hindering collaborative efforts essential for growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The impact of the slave trades on African economic development extends beyond immediate historical effects, continuing to shape institutional frameworks and societal attitudes that influence long-run growth trajectories.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) posits that the accumulation and dissemination of technological know-how are essential for sustained economic development. Institutional adjustments are necessary to integrate new technologies efficiently into society, which Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes can lead to substantial changes in social structures and ideologies. The integration process can be challenging because it requires resolving conflicts between traditional ways of life and modern technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This dynamic is evident in the historical context where the advent of industrialization necessitated significant institutional reforms to accommodate new technologies, highlighting that economic growth is contingent on both technological progress and the ability of institutions to adapt. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that this process can be uneven and subject to setbacks if institutional progress does not keep pace with technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, the sustainability of economic improvement depends on the complementary development of institutions that can manage and integrate new technologies effectively.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. The latter tends to compress different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, which can oversimplify causation. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is by altering the incentives faced by colonial administrators. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that institutional prescriptions were responsive to local economic and political actions. These findings highlight a nuanced view of institutional change where local dynamics and power struggles play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the importance of considering historical context and local conditions when examining the relationship between institutions and long-term economic growth.

The institutional framework established by European colonizers had profound implications for economic growth in their colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the type of institutions Europeans introduced, whether inclusive or extractive, significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. This perspective is reinforced by North and Weingast, who assert that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of stable property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms were critical in fostering economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth involves complex power dynamics as well as efficiency. Ogilvie highlights how the persistence of certain institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, can be better understood through distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) . These findings are supported by Austin, who argues that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, leading to institutional changes that facilitated growth in certain contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared insight underscores the importance of considering local dynamics when examining the relationship between institutions and long-term economic growth.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, provides a nuanced view. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that these institutions significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective is complemented by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). The Bank of England's creation and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. The underlying point of agreement among scholars is that institutions play a crucial role in economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for certain core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Acemoglu et al emphasize the importance of extractive versus inclusive institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the persistence of certain institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were shaped by local dynamics and power struggles, which played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes across different regions (Austin 2008, p.15) .
