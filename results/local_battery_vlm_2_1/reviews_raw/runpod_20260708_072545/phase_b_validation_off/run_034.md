The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and political science, with significant implications for understanding the trajectory of nations' wealth and development. At stake is whether the quality of institutions can be considered an independent causal variable that explains differences in economic performance over time. One live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by economic needs or if they are a result of historical accidents and political power dynamics. The former perspective emphasizes that efficient institutions emerge to facilitate growth, while the latter argues that institutions often reflect distributional conflicts and power struggles, with efficiency being a secondary consideration.

A related debate focuses on how institutions influence long-run economic outcomes: do they operate through specific mechanisms such as secure property rights and rule of law, or are their effects more diffuse? This question is crucial for policy implications: if the former, then targeted institutional reforms could yield substantial economic benefits. If the latter, policies would need to be broader and more comprehensive in addressing a variety of factors that influence institutions. Another key point of contention lies in whether the role of institutions can be generalized across different historical contexts or whether they are contingent on specific cultural, political, and social conditions. This debate has significant implications for applying institutional theories to diverse economic settings.

Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize the importance of credible commitment underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), highlighting how political institutions that enforce rules can foster secure property rights. However, these claims stand in a complex relationship with the work of Austin (2008, p.2), who notes that the "new institutionalist" economic historiography has struggled to explain why some countries adopt growth-enhancing institutions while others do not (Austin 2008, p.2). This tension suggests that the causal mechanisms linking institutions and growth may be more multifaceted than simple efficiency arguments imply.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth by establishing property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North (1989, p.9) argues that the development of institutions like the Bank of England significantly lowered transaction costs, enhancing market exchanges. This institutional evolution is crucial because it reflects increased security in property rights, fostering economic growth by enabling long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this idea by showing that different colonization strategies led to distinct institutions: settler colonies developed rule-of-law-enforcing institutions, while extractive states were established in high-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This difference highlights how institutional frameworks can either promote or hinder economic development. The persistence of extractive institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al., indicates that political power dynamics and rent-seeking behaviors can maintain suboptimal institutions even when they do not maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies a complex interplay between initial institutional setups and subsequent economic trajectories.

Building on this, North and Weingast (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) argue that secure property rights are crucial for economic growth because they foster an environment where individuals can invest in the future without fear of expropriation. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England exemplifies this dynamic, as these institutions created a stable framework for economic activity. This institutional stability was pivotal in transforming England's economic trajectory.

While the role of secure property rights is paramount, it is also important to recognise that institutions are not merely static frameworks but evolve over time, shaped by societal beliefs and political dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) contends that institutions should be viewed as dynamic entities in economic history. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions adapt to changing circumstances. This evolution involves distributional conflicts that shape the core economic institutions of a society.

This interplay is further illustrated by Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who examine how colonial institutions shaped economic growth trajectories in Africa and the New World through distinct pathways of institutional evolution. When European settlers were absent, colonial administrations frequently encouraged indigenous commodity production as a means to secure fiscal stability. The expansion of such production benefited from incentives created by local economic activities; Governor-General Clifford's policy illustrates this dynamic, where he opted for higher production equilibrium in response to these incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This approach highlights how colonial institutions could adapt to local conditions and incentivize economic growth. The persistence of inequality in New World societies reveals that institutional changes did not uniformly drive growth; they often advantaged elites at the expense of broader economic development. The stark differences in factor endowments across these societies led to varying degrees of inequality from the outset, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved and influenced long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern suggests that while colonial institutions could spur growth through commodity production, they also entrenched inequalities that constrained broader economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack's focus on women and colonial rule in Africa demonstrates that while the economic impact of colonial institutions was significant, it often came at the expense of broader development. This is shared by Frankema (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule frequently marginalized women, relegating them to less productive lands and reinforcing patriarchal structures that hindered overall economic growth. The dual legal system introduced by colonial powers often privileged men in access to land and resources, particularly for cash crops. This dynamic was evident in the Gold Coast, where men dominated cocoa production, a key driver of the region's economic revolution (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Meanwhile, British rule fostered formal education more than other colonial regimes. The perceived benefits of formal education shifted as new social and political institutions were implemented under British rule. This shift altered the cost-benefit calculations for African families and leaders, influencing their demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema argues that this educational development often reinforced existing inequalities by favoring those already privileged within colonial societies. Thus, while formal education expanded under British rule, its impact on broader economic development was limited by the entrenched social hierarchies and gender disparities perpetuated by colonial institutions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.11) show that economic reforms in Africa have been shaped by institutional changes. In particular, the shift towards 'good' institutions has been crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This focus on governance and institutions enabled new economic histories of Africa to gain traction among development specialists, who recognized the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Instead, it highlights specific mechanisms such as military expenditure and capital investments that influence economic policy. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.18) shows that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. Simson notes that East African countries' post-independence expenditure trends were influenced by industrial policy and debt service payments. While these factors are crucial for understanding short-term economic policies, they do not fully capture the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth. This suggests that while institutional reforms can address immediate economic challenges, their role in shaping sustained economic development remains complex and multifaceted.

This complexity is evident in the varying impacts of institutional changes across different African countries. For example, Simson (2019, p.2) highlights how military spending and debt service payments influenced economic policies post-independence. This indicates that institutional reforms can have varied effects depending on the specific context and historical circumstances.

The slave trade's legacy of mistrust significantly impedes long-run economic growth in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, deeply ingrained over centuries, continues to influence contemporary trust levels, which are crucial for economic development. The prolonged duration of these slave trades suggests that their impact is a deep-seated influence that shapes long-term economic outcomes. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights how centralized governance and well-developed trading networks were essential for facilitating foreign trade in regions like the Kongo kingdom. This indicates that while institutional factors are crucial, they must be understood within the broader context of historical legacies such as the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to determine the structure and effectiveness of property rights, which in turn influence investment decisions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, thereby significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North's argument that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as evidenced by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). These institutions reduced transaction costs, which facilitated market exchanges and economic growth. Austin further emphasizes the importance of institutional evolution in long-term economic outcomes, noting that European settlers' presence influenced colonial governance structures. This highlights how historical legacies shape contemporary economic trajectories through institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.2).

Institutional differences across regions have profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than long-run growth were prevalent in many colonies, leading to persistent economic disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie's assertion supports this perspective; distributional conflicts often drive the creation of core economic institutions. For example, serfdom and guild systems in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by power dynamics and shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These historical institutional frameworks continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The legacy of colonialism plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories across former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among these regions is directly linked to institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, which prioritized extractive policies over development-oriented strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy is further underscored by Hopkins' observation that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments; rather, it is the presence of poor institutions that hinders economic progress (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The new economic history of Africa shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. This shared finding indicates that effective institutional frameworks are essential for fostering long-term economic growth.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the interaction between institutions and industrialization during the nineteenth century (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, demonstrating how institutional changes introduced by colonialism directly influenced economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
