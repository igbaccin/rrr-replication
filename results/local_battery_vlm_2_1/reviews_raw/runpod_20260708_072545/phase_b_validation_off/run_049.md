The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. Institutions are the rules that structure human interaction and can influence growth by affecting incentives for investment and innovation. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods has enduring effects on economic development. Others posit that institutional changes alone cannot account for long-term economic growth and that distributional conflicts within societies also shape key economic institutions. This debate highlights a tension between views that emphasize the efficiency-driven evolution of institutions and those that focus on power dynamics and political economy.

A first stream focuses on the lasting impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive institutions that protected property rights to extractive ones designed for resource exploitation (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). These initial conditions had profound and enduring effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. He suggests that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe played a central role in long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The debate underscores the complexity of institutional influences on economic development.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North argues that the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 was a pivotal moment in this process. This institution introduced new financial instruments and led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which reflects an increased security of property rights over time. The Bank of England's creation was a response to fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, illustrating how political institutions can evolve to support economic growth. North and Weingast underscore this point by highlighting that the success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets, as they provided a stable environment for economic activities. However, North also notes that while falling transaction costs reduce individual exchange costs, the aggregate volume of exchanges increases due to specialization and division of labor, leading to a growing proportion of transactions in GNP (North 1989, p.6). This implies that although institutional changes lowered transaction costs per exchange, the overall economic activity became more complex, requiring robust institutions to manage increasing market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions depended on European mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality, they implemented extractive institutions designed to exploit resources rapidly rather than fostering local development. This institutional framework persisted over centuries, shaping the economic trajectories of these colonies. These institutions did not encourage investment or enforce property rights, leading to stagnant economic growth. Regions with high European settlement, like the United States and Australia, developed inclusive institutions that protected property rights and promoted investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between these institutional frameworks and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This nuance underscores the complex relationship between colonial policies, institutional design, and economic outcomes.

Institutional adjustments and technological progress are crucial for economic growth as they facilitate the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology necessitates significant changes in societal structures to ensure compatibility with traditional rural life. These shifts are essential because technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional adjustments, which can reduce growth-induced conflicts and promote economic expansion. This coordination ensures that economic improvements are sustainable over the long term.

Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies historical causation (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) share this view that historical causation is complex and multifaceted. This complexity is evident in how colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes, with settler presence influencing labour competition and commodity production. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning their interests with those of local populations (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the adaptive nature of colonial institutions, which responded to local incentives and actions, rather than following a uniform path. In some cases, colonial administrations modified their institutional frameworks in response to economic and political developments within the colonies. The nuanced interplay between colonial policies and indigenous responses underscores that historical causation is shaped by contextual factors and adaptive processes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This enduring legacy of mistrust persists today, impacting various dimensions of economic development including international trade and political institutions.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) highlight the divergent impacts of British colonial rule on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa. This institutional framework shaped by British colonial rule influenced educational outcomes as well as broader economic implications. For instance, the timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This institutional framework influenced the development of human capital in Africa by shaping educational outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) underscore that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves unanswered questions about long-term economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts between social groups are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions. These scholars contend that the structure of economic institutions is not merely an efficiency-driven outcome but rather a result of power dynamics and conflicts among different societal factions. The mechanism through which this occurs involves the institutionalization of policies that favor elite interests, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, serfdom and craft guilds were maintained because they served the interests of powerful elites who benefited from the status quo (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutional frameworks that advantaged elites through various government policies restricted competition and perpetuated inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, while not always efficient in maximizing economic growth, were sustained because they aligned with the interests of those who held political power. However, Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing efficiency as a driving force behind institutional development, noting that shared beliefs can also play a significant role (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they operate within a broader context of cultural and ideological factors that influence institutional outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the political dynamics that shape them. Institutions are not merely static structures but evolve through processes influenced by power distributions and shared beliefs. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, creating different institutional frameworks in colonies based on mortality rates and extractive versus inclusive institutions. These frameworks persisted over time, significantly impacting current economic outcomes. North (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) concurs with this view of the historical persistence of institutions, noting that shared beliefs also play a crucial role in shaping these structures. In pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the importance of understanding how these institutions were influenced by both efficiency considerations and the interests of politically powerful groups. This dynamic interplay between political power and institutional development is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who argue that credible commitments underpin economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England where secure property rights fostered market development.

The historical evidence from colonial institutions highlights the enduring effects of early institutional frameworks on long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than purely geographic factors. This perspective is reinforced by North's emphasis on secure property rights and transaction costs as critical components of economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic expansion. Austin (2008, p.15) adds nuance to this discussion by showing that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more dynamic relationship between colonizers and the colonized than previously acknowledged.

While there is broad agreement on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, disagreement persists over the relative importance of efficiency versus distributional conflicts in driving institutional change. North (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure of a society. Ogilvie (2007, p.3), however, cautions against overemphasizing efficiency and highlights the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. The historical evidence from colonial institutions settles this disagreement by showing that both factors are at play: institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups and by shared beliefs about property rights and economic growth.
