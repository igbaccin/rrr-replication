The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding whether and how political and economic structures can foster sustained prosperity over extended periods. The debate centers on the extent to which institutions themselves are causal drivers versus being mere byproducts of other factors, such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that specific types of institutions, those that enforce property rights and ensure secure contracts, are crucial for long-term growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others contend that the impact of institutions is more nuanced, influenced by historical contexts and interactions with other economic factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This interplay highlights a critical tension: whether institutions are primary drivers of economic growth or if their effects are mediated through complex historical and contextual variables.

North and Weingast elaborate on this by highlighting how political factors underpin economic growth and market development through institutions governing rules enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These institutions include the Bank of England, which facilitated capital markets and enabled economic transactions at lower costs.

This institutional shift had profound implications for economic activity. As North explains, the emergence of effective property rights and their enforcement mechanisms led to a significant decrease in transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This reduction was essential for fostering a dynamic economy characterized by increased specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplied the volume of exchanges.

The impact of these institutions on economic growth varies across different historical contexts. North and Weingast contend that while the creation of the Bank of England and related financial instruments had a transformative effect on British economic performance, similar institutional changes did not necessarily yield comparable results elsewhere. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting long-term economic growth depends significantly on their interaction with other historical and contextual factors.

This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate. While the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England clearly reduced transaction costs, their impact was mediated by broader political and historical contexts.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, and that these institutions were shaped by struggles between different social groups over resources and power (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The craft guilds and merchant guilds, for example, were not merely efficient responses to market failures; they were the product of conflicts among artisans, merchants, and other economic actors. These institutions restricted competition and regulated markets in ways that benefited certain groups at the expense of others. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this view to New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They show how these inequalities shaped institutional development in ways that perpetuated economic disparities. The differential paths of development observed in the New World can be traced back to institutions that emerged from these unequal power dynamics.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions against overgeneralizing this perspective (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She advocates for a nuanced approach that recognizes the complex interplay between efficiency and distributional conflicts. While institutional change is often driven by power struggles, it can also be influenced by broader economic factors and historical contexts. This complexity means that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires considering multiple interacting forces.

The implication is that focusing solely on efficiency or distributional conflicts may oversimplify the dynamics of institutional change. Institutions are multifaceted constructs shaped by a range of factors, including but not limited to power struggles and resource allocation. This nuanced view underscores the importance of contextualizing institutions within their broader historical and economic settings.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The mechanism through which these institutions shaped economic growth is rooted in the differential settlement patterns of Europeans across colonies. Colonies with high European mortality rates, such as those in Africa and parts of Asia, were characterized by extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction; these institutions did not promote long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reason for this divergence lies in the differing colonization strategies employed by Europeans: where mortality was low (e.g. North America), settlers established institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis underscores the enduring impact of these colonial institutions on economic outcomes by showing how they persistently influenced income levels long after decolonization.

The implications of this institutional legacy are profound, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This reversal can be attributed to the interaction between colonial institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights that these institutions were designed to extract rents for European colonizers, reinforcing a pattern of economic underdevelopment in many former colonies. This mechanism is further qualified by the observation that equilibrium institutions in colonial contexts were often shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized immediate gains over sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The literature on colonial rule in Africa highlights how institutional changes were influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) describes the new economic history of Africa as an area that can inform institutional reforms; he argues that a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for upward mobility and economic advancement (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic is further qualified by the observation that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth varied based on local initiatives and the specific policies implemented by different colonial powers. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that some colonial institutions may have fostered economic development despite the broader extractive nature of colonial rule (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This pattern was not universal; the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological progress is essential for long-run economic growth. Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the diffusion of innovations and managing societal conflicts arising from technological change (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that modern technology requires significant institutional adjustments. He observes that societies must adapt their political and ideological frameworks to accommodate technological advancements, which can lead to growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These conflicts can be resolved peacefully through the intervention of strong national states, thereby ensuring sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state's role in resolving such conflicts is pivotal for maintaining long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that African societies' mistrustful culture persists as a legacy of the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This cultural trait has far-reaching implications for economic development. The persistent mistrust impedes the formation of social capital, which is crucial for fostering cooperative behaviour and collective action essential for growth. Austin notes that colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic actions shaped institutional prescriptions in Africa, highlighting how historical events can influence contemporary economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). Moreover, the legacy of the slave trade has created a deep-seated mistrust among African societies, which in turn affects their willingness to engage in cooperative ventures and trust external institutions. This cultural trait is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hinders the formation of social capital necessary for growth. This variation in institutional responses suggests that the impact of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted. The legacy of mistrust in African societies, rooted in the slave trade, continues to shape their economic trajectories by limiting trust in external institutions and hindering social capital formation.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating the conditions that foster investment and development. The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is critical for economic advancement. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth. He shows that the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby facilitating long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who argue that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates. In colonies with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were set up to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, whereas in places like the United States and Australia, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This variation in institutional responses highlights how historical events can have lasting impacts on economic outcomes.

The legacy of colonial policies continues to influence contemporary economic trajectories. Austin underscores that colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on local conditions, such as the presence or absence of European settlers and the need for fiscal security (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to some hypotheses about colonial economic policies. Hopkins further emphasizes that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people on the continent (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding highlights the enduring significance of historical institutions in shaping current economic realities.

The role of mistrust and social capital in African societies is a critical factor that has been shaped by historical events, particularly the slave trade. The legacy of mistrust continues to limit trust in external institutions and hinder the formation of social capital necessary for growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This mistrust is rooted in the extractive nature of colonial institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists; these institutions did not promote long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistent impact of these historical institutions underscores the complexity and multifaceted nature of their influence on contemporary economic outcomes. The specific measurement of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive or property rights indices, shows that these factors are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The central point of agreement is that historical institutional legacies play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. Austin's work highlights the adaptability of colonial policies to local conditions, showing that institutions were not uniform across Africa (Austin 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions established by European colonizers have had persistent negative effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence of colonial policies and their impact on contemporary economic outcomes settles the disagreement, showing that both adaptability and extractive nature are crucial factors in understanding long-run economic growth.
