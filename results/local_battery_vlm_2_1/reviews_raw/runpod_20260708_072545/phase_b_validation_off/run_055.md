The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. The stakes are high as this question speaks to the very foundations of development policy and economic theory. At one extreme, some scholars argue that institutions matter greatly for economic growth because they structure incentives, reduce transaction costs, and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). Some scholars argue that distributional conflicts drive core economic institutions more than efficiency considerations (Author Year, p.N). These debates are not merely academic: they directly inform policy decisions aimed at fostering growth in developing economies.

The debate over the role of institutions is particularly pronounced when considering the long-term impacts of colonialism on economic development. The extractive nature of certain institutional frameworks established by European colonizers is a key factor in understanding the current state of many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in mortality rates led to varied colonization strategies and, consequently, different institutional outcomes: some colonies received institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective highlights how initial conditions influenced by colonial policies have lasting effects on economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s work demonstrates that differing European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. This implies that colonialism's legacy involves enduring institutional frameworks influencing economic outcomes as well as immediate exploitation.

North and Weingast argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic development by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They highlight the impact of specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England. This financial revolution significantly lowered the cost of transacting, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth. North and Weingast highlight that these developments concern political factors underpinning them, such as the regime's commitment to enforcing rules, in addition to economic rules. This underscores how credible commitments from political institutions can drive long-term economic growth by ensuring secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast recognize that while institutional changes are crucial, their impact on economic growth is not always immediate or straightforward (Author Year, p.N). For instance, they note that the bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV in France did not necessarily hinder economic growth as much as traditionally thought.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that colonial rule in Africa influenced institutional change through the negotiation and imposition of policies. The institutions imposed by colonial powers varied significantly depending on colonizing power interests, strategies, and local conditions. This variation was driven by differing cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional change did not uniformly translate into economic development (Author Year, p.N). Broadberry and Gardner show that despite these variations in colonial policies, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This indicates that while colonial institutions influenced African economies, other factors such as local endowments and transport costs also played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Akyeampong and Fofack and Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight that African women played significant roles in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Women were integral to agricultural production, particularly in food farming. The introduction of cash crops under colonial rule led to their marginalization from prime agricultural lands (Author Year, p.N). This shift was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary law, which codified women's subordinate status (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The de-privileging of women in land access was a critical structural impediment that limited their economic contributions. The lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates complicates this assessment (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The literature on economic policies and land conflicts in Africa illustrates how political-economic processes and social dynamics intertwine to shape state expenditure patterns (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Simson's work shows that post-independence African states often prioritized consumption over public goods, with government resources allocated to buy loyalty rather than deliver broad-based services. The complex interplay of these factors highlights how institutional frameworks can divert resources away from productive investments, ultimately hindering long-run economic growth.

van Zanden (2009, p.19); (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) and his collaborators investigate economic performance in Europe without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run growth. The literature focuses on other factors such as skill premiums and wage data; their work suggests that institutions can play a dual role in shaping human capital investments, which in turn influence economic development. van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how guilds in Western Europe emerged to facilitate the intergenerational transfer of skills, acting as formal institutions that guaranteed fair execution of apprentice contracts. These same institutions could also restrict entry into trades and attempt to keep wages above market-clearing levels, thereby forming local cartels. This dynamic underscores how institutional frameworks can both foster and hinder economic growth by influencing the supply and demand dynamics of skilled labor. The implications of this are seen in how different regions adapted to economic changes; for example, van Zanden (1999, p.9) notes the shift in economic development towards North Sea bordering countries like Holland and England. These dynamics are complex; van Zanden (1999, p.1) argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. This suggests that while institutions may influence economic performance, their impact on long-run growth can be nuanced and context-dependent.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. They propose that these institutions were shaped by power struggles between different social groups rather than efficiency considerations alone. Ogilvie elaborates on this point, noting that shared beliefs are part of a society's institutional structure (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the idea that institutions evolve solely to promote economic efficiency; instead, it suggests that they often reflect the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain or enhance their status and wealth. For instance, serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be seen as mechanisms that concentrated power and resources in the hands of elites, thereby perpetuating inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this dynamic by examining New World societies, where initial conditions led to extreme wealth disparities. These inequalities were reinforced through institutions that favored elite groups, restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the majority of the population (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights how distributional conflicts can lock in patterns of inequality, shaping long-term economic trajectories. This mechanism is particularly evident in societies where elites had the power to influence institutional design, ensuring that their interests were protected and amplified through legal and political structures. Ogilvie cautions against over-simplifying this relationship; while institutions are indeed shaped by distributional conflicts, they also evolve in response to a complex interplay of factors including cultural beliefs and historical contingencies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade engendered a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day. This deep-seated mistrust significantly impacts contemporary levels of trust and has profound implications for economic development. The mechanism through which the slave trade instilled mistrust involved both external environmental factors and internal norms. External factors include the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, while internal norms refer to deeply ingrained attitudes towards cooperation and reciprocity. Nunn and Wantchekon found that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade has shaped African societies in ways that go beyond immediate institutional frameworks, embedding a pervasive sense of mistrust that continues to affect social and economic interactions. Austin's findings complement this by showing that indigenous commodity production was often encouraged by colonial governments when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that while external factors like colonial policies played a role, the internal cultural shifts driven by the slave trade were equally, if not more, impactful in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, highlighting the accumulation of useful knowledge as the key factor. Mokyr's focus on the growth of useful knowledge as a mainspring contrasts with Kuznets' emphasis on epochal innovations, but both authors agree that institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently utilizing these innovations. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptability is evident in historical examples where societies have had to resolve conflicts arising from modernization. Mokyr notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets' work further underscores the importance of a strong political fabric in peacefully resolving these conflicts, emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in maintaining social cohesion during periods of rapid change (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements drive economic growth, their impact is contingent on the ability of institutions to adapt and manage the resulting social and political challenges. The sustainability of economic improvement thus relies on complementary institutional developments ensuring long-term stability in addition to technological progress.

Pamuk shows that the Black Death initiated structural changes in Europe by 1450, leading to a wage gap between north-western Europe and other regions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This divergence was driven by demographic shifts and economic responses to the plague as well as institutional factors. Pamuk highlights that the Black Death led to labour shortages, which in turn drove up wages in northwest Europe. This wage increase fostered a more dynamic economy with higher productivity, particularly in sectors like manufacturing and trade (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta expand on this by noting that high productivity in traded goods sectors was sustained through merchant distribution and finance, indicating that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during this period. This interplay of demographic shifts and institutional adaptability highlights the complex nature of economic divergence in Europe.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. The role of institutions is evident in the establishment of property rights and their enforcement (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of institutions on long-term growth can be seen through the lens of colonization strategies. European settlers adopted different approaches to colonization, setting up institutions that either enforced the rule of law or established extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of colonies. For instance, regions like Holland, which had strong political authorities and weak monopolies, were better equipped to respond to new economic opportunities (Austin 2008, p.2).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies highlights the significance of institutions in long-run growth. The interaction between colonial institutions and industrialization played a pivotal role in shaping the development paths of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks.

Despite shared beliefs about the importance of institutions, disagreements persist regarding the specific mechanisms through which they operate. Some argue that distributional conflicts are more critical than efficiency in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement lies in the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic development. Hopkins shows that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This finding aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The empirical comparison of institutional quality across different regions and historical periods reveals that efficient property rights enforcement and secure transaction costs are critical drivers of economic development.
