The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The stakes are high because the causal mechanisms at play can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development. Some scholars argue that institutions are critical for economic growth by providing secure property rights and enforcing contracts, while others contend that distributional conflicts within societies shape core economic institutions. A third perspective posits that European colonization strategies were pivotal in establishing extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. This debate hinges on the fundamental question of whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts and historical contingencies.

The relationship between secure property rights and long-run economic growth is a central theme in this literature. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Specifically, he points to the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments as crucial factors that facilitated economic growth by increasing security in property rights (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who emphasize the role of political institutions in enforcing rules and providing secure property rights. They note that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), highlighting how political factors are essential for fostering economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in securing property rights and promoting economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. provide a nuanced view by arguing that the type of colonization strategy adopted by Europeans significantly influenced the institutional framework established in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They suggest that where settlers went to establish rule-of-law institutions, they set up systems that encouraged investment, while in areas with high mortality rates, extractive states were established. This highlights how historical contingencies and colonization strategies can shape long-term economic outcomes. The results of their study are robust across different measures of institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The English financial revolution of the 17th century exemplifies how institutional changes can drive economic development. These developments facilitated long-term capital markets and spurred economic growth by fostering investment and innovation. The establishment of rule-of-law institutions in areas with low mortality rates encouraged investment, while extractive states in high-mortality areas hindered economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrating how institutional frameworks established under different historical contingencies shape long-term economic trajectories (Author Year, p.N). This underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic development, as they can either foster or impede growth depending on their nature and the context in which they are implemented.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of institutions that enforced rules and committed regimes to long-term growth strategies were pivotal. Secure property rights enabled individuals to invest with confidence, knowing their assets would be protected. This institutional framework underpinned economic development by creating a stable environment conducive to investment and innovation. For instance, the Glorious Revolution in England established a constitutional monarchy that limited the power of the sovereign, thereby protecting private property rights from arbitrary seizure. Austin (2008, p.2) further supports this perspective by highlighting how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. The adoption of growth-fostering institutions varied across regions; local contexts and historical contingencies significantly influenced these outcomes. For example, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for labor, illustrating how institutional responses varied based on local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This underscores the importance of contextual factors in determining the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth.

The shared finding is that institutions play an important role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and skill development (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the significance of guilds in western Europe as formal institutions that ensured the fair execution of apprentice contracts, thereby fostering intergenerational skill transfer. This mechanism was crucial for economic growth because it addressed institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades and reduced the costs associated with producing skilled workers (van Zanden 2009, p.3). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further emphasizes that technological progress requires complementary institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from modernization, such as adapting to new family structures and urban lifestyles. This underscores the importance of institutions in managing societal changes induced by economic growth, ensuring that technological advancements are efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their ability to support both technological innovation and societal adaptation.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (van Zanden 1999, p.1), along with van Zanden (1999, p.1), demonstrate that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. This suggests that service sectors played a crucial role in early modern economic growth, much like in the modern period. The implications of this are far-reaching: if living standards increased through falling relative prices of manufactured goods rather than increased consumption of basic foodstuffs, then the benefits of early modern development were likely distributed unevenly. This aligns with van Zanden's (1999) finding that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Van Zanden shows that the shift in economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England, led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. This pattern underscores how institutional changes, such as those facilitating investment in agriculture and leading to high agricultural labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15), are crucial for understanding early modern economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta's work thus highlights that institutions can shape economic growth by influencing the distribution of development benefits and the relative prices of goods, thereby affecting living standards.

A reevaluation of historical economic performance in Africa reveals higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding suggests that economic performance in Africa was not uniformly stagnant during the colonial era, as often portrayed. The export market played a significant role in shaping economic growth patterns within African colonies. Broadberry and Gardner highlight how factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while external trade was crucial, internal institutional frameworks also played a pivotal role in determining economic outcomes. By reconstructing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, they provide a nuanced view of economic conditions that challenges simplistic narratives of persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the need for more detailed historical data to accurately assess Africa's economic past and its implications for future growth.

Colonial rule's influence on African institutions underscores the deep and lasting impact of external governance on societal growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) share the finding (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in determining economic outcomes, particularly through their impact on land distribution and legal structures. Colonial rule often marginalized women by restricting access to productive lands and reinforcing patriarchal norms through codified customary law. This institutional framework limited women's economic contributions and hindered overall economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor was further entrenched by colonial legal systems that codified customary laws favoring male land ownership. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this pattern by arguing that British rule, compared to other colonial powers, was more conducive to developing formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa. This educational infrastructure laid the groundwork for future economic development by enhancing human capital and fostering a skilled workforce (Frankema 2012, p.15). Colonial institutions' broader impact on African economic development is complex; these systems often introduced dual legal frameworks that complicated governance and economic policy implementation. Understanding this complexity is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms aimed at promoting long-term economic growth in Africa.

The historical impacts of the slave trade on African economic development highlight how the legacy of mistrust persists and affects contemporary trust levels, which in turn influences economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade fostered a deep-seated culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust influences present-day social dynamics and economic interactions by reducing intergroup trust (Author Year, p.N). The slave trade's adverse effects on Africa's economic institutions are multifaceted; they include the direct impact on human capital as well as indirect consequences on social cohesion and governance. This mistrust can impede collective action and undermine the effectiveness of formal institutions, thereby hindering economic growth. For instance, while some areas experienced prolonged economic stagnation due to the slave trades, others demonstrated resilience by developing centralized governance structures that facilitated trade and economic activity. This variation underscores the need for nuanced approaches in understanding how historical legacies shape contemporary economic development in Africa.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and beyond (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate how these conflicts influenced the formation of institutions that favored certain groups over others. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, such as serfdom, guilds, and communal structures, were shaped by power struggles between different social classes (Author Year, p.N). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions emerged solely to enhance productivity or economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) provide a complementary view, showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to protect elite interests. These institutions restricted competition and maintained social hierarchies, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie suggests that the influence of distributional conflicts extends beyond Europe, impacting global economic development. This broader implication highlights the enduring significance of institutional design in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The historical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident across various contexts and periods. Institutions designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment have fostered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This is seen in societies where European settlers established systems that protected property rights and promoted market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating growth (North 1989, p.6).

Institutions prioritizing elite interests often stifle competition and maintain social hierarchies. These extractive states, characterized by their focus on transferring resources to a dominant group rather than fostering broad-based economic development, illustrate how institutional design can perpetuate inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Such institutions maximize rents for the politically powerful and highlight the critical role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The enduring significance of institutional design is further underscored by its impact on global economic development. Colonial institutions, shaped by the interests of European powers, have had lasting effects on the economies of former colonies. These institutions often reflected the strategic goals of colonial administrators rather than the needs of local populations, thereby shaping long-run economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of these institutional legacies shows how historical processes continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in their impact on industrialization and fiscal policy. The creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England, for example, led to a significant decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This development illustrates how institutional changes can enhance economic efficiency and promote growth by securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. Additionally, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political dynamics, demonstrating the adaptive nature of institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Ultimately, the consensus across these studies is that institutions matter for long-term growth because they shape incentives and constrain behavior. This debate is settled by the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic outcomes: historical evidence shows that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have had a lasting negative effect on the economies of former colonies.
