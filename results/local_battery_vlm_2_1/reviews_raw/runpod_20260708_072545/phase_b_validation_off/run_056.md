The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding whether and how the rules governing economic exchanges influence sustained economic development. One position holds that institutions are central to economic growth because they create incentives for investment and innovation by securing property rights and enforcing contracts. This view emphasizes the importance of credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) and transaction costs reduction in long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). Some scholars posit that distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions, while efficiency considerations are also important (Author Year p.N). The latter view suggests that institutions are often the result of power struggles among different groups within society.

Institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs by facilitating market exchanges and securing property rights (North 1989, p.6). For instance, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who note that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in some colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These scholars argue that the presence or absence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can have long-lasting effects on economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) highlights the significance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. He shows how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, these institutions enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In others, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that while institutional frameworks can significantly influence economic development, the specific nature of these institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, plays a pivotal role in determining their impact on growth. The presence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is essential for fostering an environment conducive to long-term economic development.

Building on this foundation, North and Weingast, along with Ogilvie (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), demonstrate that the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This institutional change is rooted in a broader transformation of political structures, which enabled more effective enforcement of property rights and contracts. The development of credible commitment mechanisms underpins this process, as these mechanisms allowed for the creation of stable expectations regarding future economic interactions. This political stability, in turn, facilitated the emergence of robust financial markets, which played a pivotal role in supporting economic development.

The extent to which these institutions can be generalized across different historical contexts is debated. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for understanding economic history, emphasizing their role in shaping long-term outcomes. This perspective underscores the importance of considering how institutions evolve over time and the specific conditions under which they operate.

This historical context is crucial because technological advancements and their interplay with economic growth are pivotal in shaping long-term development trajectories (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is central to sustained economic improvement. This perspective highlights how technological progress, as a primary driver of economic growth, requires complementary institutional adjustments to ensure its sustainability. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this by noting that technological changes introduce new sectors and alter production patterns, necessitating institutional and ideological adaptations for efficient integration into the economy. These adaptations are crucial because they facilitate the resolution of conflicts arising from technological advancements without weakening the political fabric of society. Mokyr's work underscores how formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, reduce access costs to knowledge by fostering coordination among researchers and practitioners (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This institutional support is essential for sustaining economic growth driven by technological progress. The role of institutions in managing technological changes and fostering economic development thus becomes evident.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) extend this analysis by challenging the narrative of persistent African poverty through a historical lens. They show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, thus providing a more nuanced view of Africa's economic development during this period. This finding is crucial because it highlights how the economic conditions in these regions varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in export production and its correlation with foreign trade underscore the importance of external market dynamics in shaping economic growth within African colonies. This pattern of growth and decline, tied closely to export markets, suggests that the colonial experience was shaped by a complex interplay of local conditions and global economic forces.

Historical national accounting data for Sub-Saharan Africa has been relatively underutilized in comparative historical work on economic growth despite its central importance. The availability of large amounts of economic data for both the colonial era and post-independence periods offers a promising avenue for deeper analysis, particularly when integrated with studies focusing on real wage levels. This integration could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how economic conditions evolved over time, offering insights into the long-term effects of historical events on contemporary African economies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Understanding these trends is essential for assessing future growth possibilities and identifying the enduring impacts of specific historical or geographical conditions on African welfare development.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further emphasize that these trends are directly linked to the adverse effects of historical events such as the slave trades. This claim is supported by Nunn's work showing that the impact of the slave trade on African economies was significant and enduring, lasting for nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900 (Nunn 2008, p.2). The adverse effects are both economic and cultural, as evidenced by a culture of mistrust that developed within Africa due to these trades. This mistrust persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal norm of mistrust is at least twice as large as external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, which highlights the deep-rooted nature of these cultural impacts. Nunn and Wantchekon note that trust plays a crucial role in various aspects of development including economic growth, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This underscores the broader implications of the slave trades' adverse effects on African development, suggesting that addressing this historical legacy is essential for future economic progress.

These findings suggest that institutional changes introduced by colonialism favored elites and restricted competition, contributing to persistent inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) alongside Austin (2008, p.2) argue that these institutional changes were not merely about securing property rights but involved complex interactions with local economies. The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was contingent upon the pre-existing social structures and power dynamics in colonized societies. These institutions often reinforced existing inequalities by favoring elite interests, which in turn restricted broader economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in how colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production only when it aligned with their fiscal goals, rather than promoting widespread economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of inequality suggests that the institutional framework established by colonial powers was not universally beneficial for long-term economic development. Instead, these institutions often perpetuated existing disparities, highlighting the need to consider the broader social and political context in which institutions operate.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic interactions and allocate resources. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates; extractive states were set up in high-mortality colonies, while more inclusive institutions were introduced where Europeans settled permanently. These institutional differences had lasting impacts on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9).

Colonial administrations often tailored their policies to maximize fiscal benefits rather than promoting widespread economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production when it aligned with their fiscal goals, illustrating how institutions can be shaped by the interests of the ruling elite. This perspective is reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who argue that credible commitment underpins growth; regimes bound by rules governing economic exchange foster secure property rights, which are critical for long-term development.

The persistence of inequality in former colonies highlights how institutions can perpetuate existing disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This dynamic is evident in colonial administrations that encouraged production only when it benefited their fiscal goals (Austin 2008, p.15). The broader social and political context in which institutions operate is crucial; institutional frameworks established by colonial powers often perpetuated inequalities, showing that the benefits of these institutions were not universally distributed.

The empirical evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show the remarkable success of England's institutional changes in fostering economic growth, contrasting with France's performance before the French Revolution. This underscores how credible commitment to property rights can drive long-term development, settling disagreements about the role of institutions by demonstrating that secure property rights are a critical factor in economic growth (North 1989, p.9).
