Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing investment incentives and resource allocation. The causal mechanism is of interest to scholars because it underpins the efficacy of institutional reforms and development policies. At stake intellectually are two positions: one that institutions are paramount, even when other factors appear to be at work; another that institutions are merely part of a broader picture in which they are shaped by exogenous forces such as geography or culture. The disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors, and how to weigh their role against these other forces. This debate is crucial because it determines whether institutional reforms can be effective standalone interventions for promoting economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2) both highlight the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth, but they differ on the specifics of how this occurs and what factors influence their formation. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates in colonies, leading to extractive states in high-mortality areas and more inclusive ones elsewhere (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the importance of historical context and initial conditions in determining institutional outcomes. Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced view of how institutions evolve over time (Austin 2008, p.2).

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of institutions in economic growth. North argues that institutions matter because they define property rights and reduce transaction costs, which are crucial for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.6). This perspective emphasizes the role of political institutions in creating efficient property rights and facilitating market exchanges, which can drive economic growth by increasing specialization and division of labor.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the transformation of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in securing property rights. This institutional evolution led to a significant reduction in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth by fostering long-term capital markets. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments exemplifies how political institutions shaped market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). These changes reflect increased security in the time dimension of property rights, a critical factor for both sustained capital markets and overall economic development (North 1989, p.6). The success of this institutional framework is evident in the robust performance of English capital markets, which underscores the pivotal role of credible commitments by the regime to enforce these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast emphasize institutional changes as important, yet they recognize that economic growth results from a complex interplay of factors.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate this by showing how different European colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks in colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s findings are bolstered by their extensive use of alternative measures of institutional quality, which consistently show similar results (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This pattern is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The implications of these institutional frameworks are profound; they shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, influencing their ability to industrialize and develop over the long term (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions were extractive but they were not necessarily static. Over time, changes in political power dynamics and economic conditions could lead to institutional reforms, although such changes might be slow and incremental. The legacy of colonial institutions thus continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that British colonial rule fostered higher per capita incomes in Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century due to its institutional framework. This framework was more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers, as Frankema's work highlights. The demand for missionary education was influenced by new social and political institutions implemented under British rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, creating a dynamic where African families and political leaders saw value in investing in education. This shift involved more than access; it was about the perceived utility of education in navigating the new colonial context.

The institutional framework under British rule thus had significant implications for economic development. Broadberry and Gardner's analysis (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) underscores that the growth patterns in these economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, which were themselves influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The interplay between these factors indicates that institutional changes introduced by British rule were part of a broader economic ecosystem.

The enduring impact of these colonial institutions on contemporary economic development is evident in the legacy they left behind. The higher per capita incomes achieved by the early twentieth century were a direct result of the institutional framework that supported education and trade, setting a foundation for long-term economic growth. This legacy underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over extended periods.

This complexity is further compounded by the diverse trajectories of African economies over time. Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) highlight the need to challenge oversimplified narratives about African economic history, emphasizing that institutional frameworks have evolved through a series of dynamic interactions rather than following a linear path. These authors argue that colonial institutions were not monolithic and did not uniformly affect all regions; instead, they adapted in response to local conditions and indigenous actions. Austin (2008, p.2) shows how colonial governments often adjusted their policies based on the incentives created by local economic activities (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial administrations encouraged commodity production that aligned with local interests, illustrating a more nuanced interaction between colonial powers and African economies.

This dynamic interplay is further qualified by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue against the notion of persistent poverty in Africa due to historical structural impediments. Instead, they suggest that economic conditions were influenced by specific historical and geographical factors (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the idea that African poverty is an inevitable result of long-term structural issues, instead proposing a more fluid understanding where institutions evolve in response to changing circumstances. By reconstructing real wage levels across British Africa during colonial rule, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) provide empirical support for this view (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its impact on economic development in Africa over time.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie's work highlights how economic institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were often shaped by the interests of powerful groups rather than efficiency considerations. These institutions reflected the balance of power among different societal factions, with elites frequently using their influence to maintain systems that favored their interests. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be understood as a means for landowners to secure a stable labor force and maintain control over agricultural production. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis, which shows how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies, such as those limiting land ownership and access to education, helped maintain the status quo by preventing lower classes from challenging elite dominance. This institutional framework had significant implications for economic growth. While it provided stability for elites, it also hindered broader economic development by stifling innovation and limiting the participation of a larger segment of the population in economic activities. As a result, these societies often experienced slower rates of economic growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is rivaled by technological and institutional adjustments which facilitate efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets argues that sustained economic growth requires technological progress along with complementary institutional and ideological changes. He emphasizes that the successful integration of new technologies into society depends on resolving conflicts between traditional ways of life and modern economic practices (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr's work complements this perspective by highlighting that without these institutional adjustments, technological advancements can be unsustainable and may even be eroded over time. Mokyr also notes that institutional progress must align with technological advancements to ensure sustained economic improvement; otherwise, rent-seeking behaviors can undermine these benefits. This underscores a crucial mechanism: the alignment of institutions with technological changes determines whether growth is sustainable or ephemeral. Therefore, the interaction between institutions and technology is pivotal for long-term economic development.

The interaction between institutions and technology is pivotal for long-term economic development. This dynamic relationship underscores that institutions are not static; they must adapt to technological changes to ensure sustained growth. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and other financial innovations led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). In turn, this institutional adaptation facilitated economic growth by supporting long-term capital markets. Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of institutions in shaping technological adoption by noting that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and colonization strategies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), which influenced subsequent economic outcomes. The alignment of these institutions with new technologies determined whether they fostered growth or stagnation, showing that institutional effectiveness depends on their ability to integrate and adapt to technological advancements.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions further highlights the importance of institutional adaptation. Ogilvie argues that core pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores how institutions evolve to balance power dynamics within society, which can either support or hinder economic growth. Austin challenges the simplification of causation in historical analyses, emphasizing that different paths and periods must be considered when examining institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). For example, colonial administrators adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, showing how institutions can evolve based on local conditions and interactions.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the persistence of extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights how institutional design can perpetuate inequality, as seen in former colonies where extractive institutions were established to benefit European colonists. Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa demonstrates the crucial role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development, thereby underscoring their importance in long-term growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The persistence of these extractive institutions shows how institutional legacies can shape current economic outcomes.

The specific interaction between colonial policies and technological changes provides historical evidence that settles the disagreement. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established very different colonization strategies, with associated institutions that either enforced the rule of law or set up extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison demonstrates how institutional design influenced economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the importance of aligning institutions with technological changes for long-term growth. The measurement of institutional quality through indicators like property rights and rule of law shows that these factors are crucial for sustained economic development.
