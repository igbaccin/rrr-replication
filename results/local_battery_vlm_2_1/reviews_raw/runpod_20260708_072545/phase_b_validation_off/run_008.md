The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with competing views on how and to what extent institutions matter for development trajectories. Institutions can be defined as the rules that structure political, social, and economic interactions; they influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, constraining behavior, and facilitating coordination. The live disagreement focuses on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to growth processes, and if they are causal factors in development. Some scholars argue that institutions are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth by providing a stable framework for investment and exchange. Others contend that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic conditions, play more significant roles. This discussion is crucial because it informs policy recommendations and our understanding of how societies develop over time. Institutions shape incentives and constrain behavior, making them a fundamental factor in the trajectory of economies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in their influence on property rights and transaction costs. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflected an increased security of property rights, which are critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. Similarly, North and Weingast highlight the importance of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, leading to falling costs per transaction as economies grow (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This perspective underscores how secure property rights foster investment and facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering these secure property rights, demonstrating the direct impact of institutional frameworks on economic development.

The causal role of institutions is further supported by studies that link colonial institutions to current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies with high mortality, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional legacies persist and significantly affect current income per capita, highlighting how historical institutions shape long-term economic trajectories. This perspective aligns with North's view that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing their role in structuring incentives and constraining behavior (North 1989, p.6). The enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economies underscores the importance of understanding institutional frameworks to grasp long-run economic growth.

The debate over whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous is central to understanding their causal role. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating a more complex relationship between institutions and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that broader socio-economic factors may influence institutional changes and growth. Similarly, Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, challenging the efficiency-driven view of institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These arguments highlight the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its interplay with other socio-economic factors, suggesting that institutions may be endogenous to growth processes. The complexity of this relationship underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends to their impact on development policies and poverty reduction. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in their influence on policy recommendations and our understanding of how societies develop over time.

Colonies like the United States saw the establishment of institutions that enforced rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, characterized by secure property rights and effective enforcement, fostered economic growth by reducing transaction costs and promoting market exchanges.

This institutional design significantly affected the long-run development of former colonies, demonstrating that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies underscores the profound impact of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) found consistent results across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These findings highlight that strong institutions, which enforce secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges, are essential for sustained economic growth and development over the long term.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They identify credible commitment as a key mechanism through which political institutions can facilitate long-run economic development. By establishing rules that constrain rulers' discretionary power, these institutions foster an environment where investors can confidently engage in market activities without fear of expropriation. This credibility is essential for the functioning of capital markets and the broader economy.

This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors, such as cultural beliefs or technological advancements. This call underscores the significance of understanding how institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over extended periods. The role of credible commitment presents complexities. Therefore, the interplay between institutional design and political will shapes the trajectory of economic growth. This underscores the importance of establishing strong institutions and ensuring they are backed by a credible commitment from those in power, which remains a critical factor in sustaining long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to favor elites through various government policies. This dynamic restricts competition and contributes to the persistence of inequality by shaping institutional frameworks that protect elite interests at the expense of broader economic development. The initial factor endowments, such as land abundance relative to labor, created environments where wealth, human capital, and political power were highly concentrated from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This concentration of resources led to the establishment of institutions that reinforced these inequalities by limiting access to education, political participation, and economic opportunities for the majority. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this perspective by highlighting the enduring effects of historical factors such as the slave trade on contemporary societal issues like trust and economic development. This interplay between institutional design and historical legacies underscores how deeply entrenched inequalities can perpetuate through institutional mechanisms, even long after the initial conditions have changed.

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49) both argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels long-term economic development. This process involves generating new ideas as well as their effective application and diffusion across societies. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological advancements necessitate significant institutional adjustments to be fully realized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). For instance, the shift from rural lifestyles to urban industrialization requires changes in social structures and ideologies; these changes can be conflictual if they challenge entrenched norms. This is consistent with Mokyr's assertion that coordination failures can impede economic progress without complementary institutional advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of useful knowledge, as posited by Mokyr, underscores the importance of formal institutions in reducing access costs to new ideas and technologies; these institutions facilitate the dissemination and adoption of innovations. Kuznets cautions that these conflicts must be resolved peacefully for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is crucial, its benefits are contingent on the ability of societies to adapt institutionally without incurring excessive social costs.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that the effects of colonial rule on African economic development were more nuanced than uniformly detrimental. The role of British colonial rule in fostering formal education, as Frankema posits, challenges simplistic views of colonial legacies. This institutional legacy is pivotal because it fostered a demand for missionary education among African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework altered the perceived benefits of formal education by creating new social and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) support this view with evidence that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that British rule facilitated economic growth through its institutional impact on education, which in turn fostered economic development. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), illustrating a downstream implication of these institutions: they perpetuated gender-based inequalities that constrained broader economic growth.

The nuanced effects of British colonial rule on African development are further complicated by the role of formal education. This suggests that the institutional legacies of British rule were not merely imposed but also actively engaged with by local populations. This integration was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, which varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors including political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings suggest that while British colonial rule fostered formal education and economic growth, its impact on African development must be understood in relation to broader social and economic dynamics.

The literature on colonialism and institutional reforms in Africa highlights how African initiatives played a significant role in shaping institutional change, challenging oversimplified views of historical causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks the complexities of different historical paths and periods, suggesting that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic is evident in cases where European settlers were absent, leading colonial administrations to encourage commodity production and adjust institutional prescriptions based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). Englebert's findings, as cited by Hopkins, suggest that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the provision of public goods or the development of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that while colonial reforms were influential, the effectiveness of institutional changes depended on local adaptations and the capacity of African societies to develop governance structures that could withstand ethnic diversity.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) share that there was a long-term shift towards higher relative prices of basic foodstuffs in north-western Europe, which had implications for economic growth and living standards. This phenomenon is tied to the role of merchant distribution and finance in fostering high productivity in the traded goods sector (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The rise of skilled labourers' wages in this region contributed to a larger surplus over basic food needs, which suggests that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) builds on this by showing that the economic development of Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not uniformly translate into improvements in living standards. This implies that while institutional changes such as those fostered by guilds could promote skill transfer, they also risked forming local cartels that restricted entry into trades (van Zanden 1999, p.1; van Zanden 2009, p.3). The rise of the North Sea countries as economic leaders after 1740 further complicates this picture. This shift highlights how regional economic development can lead to varying price levels for basic foodstuffs, influencing living standards and economic growth in complex ways (van Zanden 1999, p.9).

Institutions are critical to economic growth; they provide incentives for investment and shape long-term development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8). For Acemoglu et al., institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment foster growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly argues that institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The institutional framework established in a society influences its economic trajectory by determining how resources are allocated, transactions occur, and investments are made. This is evident in the creation of the Bank of England, which lowered transaction costs and enhanced long-term capital markets, thereby promoting growth (North 1989, p.9).

The historical evolution of institutions also plays an important role in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers adopted different strategies, leading to varied institutional frameworks in their colonies; these differences significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, but acknowledges that economic growth is linked to patterns of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions should not be overlooked. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective suggests that the design and enforcement of institutions are influenced by power dynamics within society, which can either promote or hinder growth. Austin further supports this view by noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This shows that institutions can have a lasting effect on economic development, shaping the trajectory of countries for centuries.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions fundamentally shape long-term economic growth through their influence on investment and resource allocation. There remains disagreement over whether distributional conflicts or efficiency concerns primarily drive institutional design (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from former European colonies shows that the reversal in relative incomes was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and opportunities for development during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
