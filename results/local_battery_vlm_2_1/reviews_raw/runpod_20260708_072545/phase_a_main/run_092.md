The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians alike. At stake is whether the trajectory of nations' economies can be explained by the institutional frameworks that govern their societies. The crux of this debate lies in understanding how institutions influence economic outcomes over extended periods, particularly when compared to other potential drivers such as geography or culture. One camp posits that institutions are pivotal in determining long-term economic performance, shaping incentives and constraints that guide individual behavior and societal development. Another stream focuses on the role of geographic factors, arguing that natural endowments and climatic conditions significantly influence economic outcomes by affecting agricultural productivity and health conditions. The debate also extends to cultural explanations, which emphasize shared beliefs and values as determinants of institutional structures and economic performance. A closer look at how institutions are formed and evolve shows that they can be shaped by historical events and political processes, which influence long-term economic growth.

For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had profound effects on current income per capita. They highlight how different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks: some colonies adopted systems that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others implemented extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer to the metropole. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic development, suggesting that the nature of these early institutions can lock in patterns of growth or stagnation. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) emphasizes the importance of institutional change in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs and enhanced property rights security. This institutional evolution reflects a broader historical process where political institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms, thereby fostering economic growth through increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6).

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North 1989 identifies that the English financial revolution of the late seventeenth century was marked by a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This institutional evolution is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth, as it facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs. These mechanisms foster economic growth by increasing specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This perspective underscores the importance of political commitments to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement, which are essential for sustained economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. Their work shows that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persist and affect long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) document the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, providing evidence that institutional changes during colonialism played a central role in these disparities. The specific mechanism they identify is that European colonists established institutions designed to maximize their rents rather than promote long-run growth. This extractive approach was particularly pronounced in areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, leading to the establishment of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over investment and development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that this institutional design was influenced by the interaction between population density, prosperity, and colonial policies. They show that the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century was closely tied to these institutions, highlighting how colonial-era decisions shaped long-term economic trajectories. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies has had lasting effects on their economic development, illustrating how historical institutional changes can have profound and enduring impacts on growth. This underscores the importance of understanding the legacy of colonial institutions in shaping contemporary economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Frankema 2012, p.15), together with Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial education policies, and the gender dynamics they engendered, played a crucial role in shaping African economic development. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often marginalized women's access to land and resources, reinforcing patriarchal structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional change had profound implications for agricultural productivity and economic growth, as women were frequently relegated to less productive lands. British rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, altering the economic landscape by fostering a literate workforce. This educational shift had downstream effects on economic outcomes, as it created new opportunities for skilled labor and entrepreneurship. The legacy of these educational policies continues to influence contemporary economic trajectories in Africa, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial institutions on long-term development.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This impact is rooted in the profound disruption of social structures and economic systems that ensued from the forced removal of millions of people. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that the slave trades lasted for nearly 500 years, a period significantly longer than the 75-year span of official colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). The extensive duration of the slave trade suggests its pervasive influence on African societies. This disruption undermined the development of centralized governance and robust economic institutions, which are crucial for sustained growth (Nunn 2008, p.2). This mechanism is important because trust is central to various aspects of economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trades' legacy of mistrust continues to shape African societies, influencing everything from individual behavior to broader economic policies and social interactions.

The growth of useful knowledge drives economic growth and requires both technological advancements and institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both emphasize that the interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations is crucial for sustained economic growth. These innovations necessitate significant adjustments in institutions to resolve conflicts arising from their implementation. Mokyr's (2005, p.49) work suggests that without these institutional progressions, the sustainability of technological improvements would be limited and could eventually erode due to rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further notes that societies must peacefully resolve growth-induced conflicts through sovereign states with strong political fabrics. This peaceful resolution is necessary for modern economic growth, as it ensures that technological advancements are integrated into the economy without excessive costs or long-term weakening of societal structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while institutional adjustments are necessary, they must be continuously adapted to maintain economic growth over time.

Distributional conflicts are better predictors of core economic institutions than efficiency concerns, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue. These scholars contend that the institutional structures in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by power dynamics rather than purely economic considerations. For example, serfdom and guilds in Europe emerged from efficiency needs as well as from the elite's desire to maintain control over resources and labor (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). In New World societies, this dynamic is evident in how institutions evolved to favor elites through policies that restricted competition and preserved inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This focus on distributional conflicts highlights a critical mechanism: the role of elite interests in shaping institutions. However, Ogilvie points out that while shared beliefs are part of institutional structures, they do not solely determine them as game-theoretic views might suggest (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This nuanced view underscores the complexity of how distributional conflicts interact with cultural factors to shape economic institutions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al (2001) and North (1989, p.9). Institutions are the rules of the game that structure interactions among individuals (North 1989, p.6). They are critical to fostering secure property rights and economic growth. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9), a development that North (1989, p.9) argues was crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself.

The historical evolution of institutions governing public choice further underscores their significance. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the political factors underpinning economic growth include rules governing economic exchange and how these are enforced (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is supported by Ogilvie's assertion that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure, though they do not solely determine them as game-theoretic views might suggest (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Distributional conflicts also play a role in shaping institutions. Acemoglu et al (2002) argue that extractive institutions can persist because politically powerful groups may obtain fewer rents with private property or fear losing political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with Ogilvie's view on the institutional structure of pre-industrial Europe, where core economic institutions like serfdom and guild systems were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The influence of elite interests in shaping institutions is evident. Acemoglu et al (2001) show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence settles the remaining disagreement by showing how specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism reversed relative incomes among former European colonies.
