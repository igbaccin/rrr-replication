The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes driven by political and social dynamics. Some scholars argue that the institutional framework, including property rights and governance structures, directly influences economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and innovation. Others posit that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper cultural or geographical determinants, which ultimately drive long-run growth.

Institutions matter in shaping economic development because they define the rules governing economic exchange and how these rules are enforced (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by North's argument that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. The Bank of England's establishment and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutions can reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and fostering a long-term capital market (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is crucial for understanding why some economies grow while others stagnate.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al. who argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies. In some cases, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they established extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design has had lasting effects on current income per capita, demonstrating the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions. The persistence of these institutions underscores their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth (Author Year p.N). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always efficiency-enhancing and highlights the political dynamics underlying their creation and persistence.

Across these studies, a shared finding is that the institutional framework established during colonial rule has had profound and lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The enduring impact of these institutions suggests that they play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

This development fostered long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement. The emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing increasingly effective enforcement was critical to this process (North 1989, p.6). These institutions facilitated market exchanges, leading to a growing proportion of the economy dedicated to transactions as specialization and division of labor increased. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which underscores their role in fostering economic growth and market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the political commitment to enforce rules governing property rights was a critical factor in this process. They acknowledge that while these institutions were revolutionary for England, France's similar economic performance suggests that institutional changes alone may not fully account for all variations in growth trajectories. This perspective highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors influencing long-run economic development.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that Europeans established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns; in areas with high mortality like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies leading to persistent underdevelopment. This perspective is nuanced by the recognition that institutional changes do not fully account for variations in growth; other factors, such as industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, also played crucial roles (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their interaction with broader economic and historical contexts determines long-run economic outcomes.

Historical institutions shaped by inequality have influenced economic outcomes in Africa (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies has persisted over time. While these historical factors are critical, they are not the sole determinants of African development. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational advantage did not uniformly translate into economic growth due to the complex interplay between historical institutions and broader socio-economic factors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that historical patterns of economic growth in Africa are more nuanced than previously thought. They demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging earlier characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding is supported by recent work in African economic history, which has shown the availability of extensive economic data for both the colonial era and the post-independence period (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further highlight that high productivity in certain sectors can arise through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in contemporary economies. These findings underscore the importance of considering diverse factors beyond institutional frameworks when examining long-run economic performance and divergence between Europe and Africa. The complexity of these historical patterns indicates that a broader understanding of socio-economic dynamics is necessary to fully grasp the determinants of economic growth in these regions.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), emphasizing the importance of recognizing diverse historical trajectories in economic development. Ogilvie supports this perspective by arguing that institutions are indispensable tools for economic historians to understand long-run economic performance. A key mechanism through which institutions shape outcomes is via distributional conflicts, which provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These include serfdom, the community, and craft guilds, which were shaped by underlying power dynamics rather than merely economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin's work on colonial Africa illustrates this dynamic: colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions, leading to outcomes that defied simple efficiency maximization. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to Nunn's hypothesis, showing how local incentives and political considerations shaped institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view of institutions challenges the idea of straightforward causal relationships between institutions and economic growth, suggesting instead that these relationships are mediated by complex historical processes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the finding that the growth of useful knowledge is fundamental to economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits technological progress as a primary driver, suggesting that innovations in technology and intellectual advancements are more critical than institutional frameworks. Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth; Kuznets argues that modern technology requires institutional adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are crucial because they enable societies to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes without excessive costs or long-term weakening of political structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Kuznets highlights the role of the sovereign state in peacefully resolving such conflicts, underscoring the importance of a stable political fabric.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that institutional progress complements technological advancements to sustain economic improvement. He notes that without this complementary progression, rent-seeking behavior could erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutions play a supportive role in maintaining and enhancing the benefits of technological progress. The interaction between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is dynamic; it requires both the creation of new knowledge and the development of mechanisms to integrate this knowledge into societal structures. This interplay between technology and institutions highlights that economic growth depends on the synergistic relationship between technological advancements and institutional frameworks. The sustainability of economic improvement hinges on both technological progress and institutional adaptation, making it clear that while technology drives growth, institutions are essential for sustaining it.
