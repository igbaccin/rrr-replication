The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. Institutions are the rules that guide human interaction, and their quality can significantly impact economic outcomes. The debate centers around how institutions influence economic growth over extended periods. One position argues that certain types of institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and secure contracts, are crucial for fostering long-term economic development. Another view posits that the efficiency of these institutions is less important than their ability to resolve distributional conflicts within society. The core disagreement revolves around whether efficient institutions or those that manage social tensions better determine long-run economic growth.

The debate on how institutions influence economic growth has profound implications for understanding historical economic trajectories and for designing effective policies in developing countries today. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth. This perspective suggests that the type of institutions established during colonization had lasting effects on economic development. Meanwhile, North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth and development because they influence transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. These findings underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over time.

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that these financial innovations were pivotal in reducing the cost of transacting, which reflects increased security of property rights over time. This security is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This includes the rules governing how economic exchanges are enforced and how they may be changed. A key factor in this process is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also note that while institutions like the Bank of England played a significant role in fostering economic growth, bureaucratic structures inherited from previous regimes did not necessarily hinder progress. This suggests that financial institutions reduce transaction costs through mechanisms that vary with the broader political and historical context.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the varying colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to different institutional outcomes in their colonies; one extreme saw the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, while another saw extractive states focused on resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions is linked to the political power dynamics at play, where powerful groups may resist institutional changes that could threaten their dominance or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions can entrench inequality and hinder economic progress. However, Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that population density and prosperity influenced European policies, suggesting that the interaction between these factors and institutional design played a central role in shaping long-run development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The growth of useful knowledge is central to economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlight that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic growth. Institutions play a pivotal role in facilitating this process by reducing access costs to new knowledge and ensuring efficient use of innovations. For example, Mokyr (2005, p.49) discusses how formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, were established to lower barriers to accessing and sharing useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) acknowledges that these institutional adjustments are not always smooth; conflicts can arise between traditional societal structures and the demands of modern technology. These conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs to maintain political stability and enable long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Such resolution requires a strong, cohesive state capable of managing technological changes and mediating social tensions. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is essential for harnessing knowledge-driven economic growth.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had profound economic consequences, particularly by fostering a culture of mistrust. This persistent mistrust has hindered African development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) showing that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on intergroup trust. The pervasive nature of this mistrust can be traced to a specific mechanism: the internalization of norms of mistrust within individuals, which are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalization suggests that African societies have developed deeply ingrained attitudes toward distrust, which persistently affect economic interactions and development. Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that this mistrust is not merely a psychological phenomenon; it has tangible economic implications by affecting the capacity of local societies to engage in sustained trade, as seen in the early European encounters with African kingdoms like Kongo which had centralized governance and well-developed markets. It is important to note that the impact of the slave trades on Africa's development was not uniform across regions or time periods. The specific historical contexts and institutional responses varied significantly, suggesting that while the slave trade had a detrimental effect, its implications were moderated by local conditions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. These scholars contend that the institutional structures in these regions were shaped more by power dynamics and elite interests than by concerns for economic efficiency. In pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie emphasizes how serfdom, communal governance, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution among different social groups. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily designed to enhance economic productivity. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that in New World societies, institutional frameworks often favored elites through policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality. This dynamic was particularly pronounced in regions with high initial levels of inequality, where elite groups used their political power to shape institutions that perpetuated their dominance.

The persistence of these institutions had significant implications for long-term economic development. Ogilvie suggests that the institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe were products of deep-seated conflicts over resources and power. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who note that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to institutions that reinforced these disparities. For instance, policies that restricted competition and limited access to economic opportunities for non-elites were common. The downstream implication of this institutional framework was a perpetuation of inequality, which hindered broader economic growth and development. Ogilvie cautions against overgeneralizing the impact of these institutions, noting that while distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping core economic institutions, other factors such as cultural beliefs and exogenous shocks also influenced institutional outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that British colonial rule in Africa fostered the development of formal education systems more effectively than other colonial powers. This educational investment was driven by demand for missionary education, which was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). These calculations were shaped by new social and political institutions implemented during British rule. This economic performance was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, highlighting the role of political institutions in facilitating growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that British rule had positive effects on education and economic development but did not universally lead to sustained prosperity. The historical record shows periods of both growth and stagnation, indicating that the long-term impact of colonial institutions was complex and varied across regions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This variability suggests that while formal education and export markets were significant drivers of economic performance, other factors such as local endowments and transport costs also played crucial roles. The legacy of British rule in Africa is thus one of mixed outcomes, where institutional frameworks laid the groundwork for economic development but did not guarantee uniform progress across all areas.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) jointly challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that African economic development has been shaped by complex historical paths and institutional evolution. (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). A key mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced long-term economic growth in Africa was the strategic response of colonial administrations to local incentives. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning their institutional prescriptions with local economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive approach contrasts with simplistic models that assume uniform institutional imposition. The effectiveness of these adaptations was not guaranteed, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that while 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, their development is contingent on broader governance frameworks and historical contexts (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This qualification underscores that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth was mediated by complex interactions between local endowments, political dynamics, and institutional adaptations. This nuanced view suggests that while colonial institutions laid foundational elements for economic development, their long-term effects were shaped by a myriad of factors that varied across different regions in Africa.
