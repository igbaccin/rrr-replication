The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure political, economic, and social interactions, determine a nation's economic trajectory over centuries. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. Proponents of institutional primacy argue that stable, inclusive political and economic institutions foster secure property rights, encourage investment, and promote innovation, thereby driving sustained economic growth. Some scholars highlight alternative factors such as climate, disease environments, or cultural beliefs as key determinants of a society's economic performance. The debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies, as understanding the root causes of economic growth can inform effective interventions to alleviate poverty and promote prosperity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North (1989, p.9) contends that the creation of financial instruments and institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs, which reflected increased security in property rights over time. This dynamic is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) expand on this by showing that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups. These differences in institutional frameworks significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth, highlighting how institutional design can shape economic development paths (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how political power influences institutional choices and their subsequent impact on economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that secure property rights are fostered by the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England. They identify credible commitment as a key mechanism for economic growth. North and Weingast show that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 established a constitutional structure that limited the monarch's power, ensuring that political elites could not expropriate wealth without facing severe consequences. This institutional change created an environment where property rights were secure, encouraging investment and long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need to integrate institutions into long-term research programs in economic history. She argues for a more nuanced approach to understanding how institutions shape economic development, highlighting that shared beliefs can be part of the institutional structure of society. This perspective complements North and Weingast's focus on political factors underpinning economic growth by adding a cultural dimension. Ogilvie suggests that beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights are crucial for understanding why some societies develop institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, while others do not. This integration of shared beliefs into the institutional framework provides a more comprehensive view of how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes.

The evolution of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England illustrates how institutional changes can foster economic growth by ensuring credible commitment from political elites.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) share the view that technological advancements are pivotal for economic growth. Mokyr posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the key driver, while Kuznets emphasizes that economic growth requires institutional adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations. Technological progress often necessitates changes in societal structures and ideologies. For example, industrialization demands a shift from rural living patterns toward urban centers (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This transition is not seamless; it requires resolving conflicts between traditional ways of life and new technological realities. Kuznets highlights that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully managing these growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Mokyr further elaborates on the need for institutional progress to complement technological advancements, noting that without such progress, economic improvements may be unsustainable. He underscores the importance of reducing access costs through formal institutions and suggests policies to rectify coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is supported by Kuznets' observation that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family patterns, necessitating institutional changes for sustained growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that colonial institutions shaped by inequality can lead to varying economic outcomes through the differential impact on property rights. These institutions, which often favored elites, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. For instance, in Latin America, the unequal distribution of land and resources led to the establishment of institutions that protected elite interests, thereby hindering broader economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The same mechanism is also evident in Africa's slave trades, which had adverse effects on economic development by disrupting social and political structures. Nunn highlights that these slave trades created conditions where centralized governance and well-developed markets were essential for sustaining trade and economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). Frankema (2012, p.15) suggests that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education compared to other colonial powers, which further impacted institutional frameworks. This underscores how different colonial experiences shaped institutional trajectories, with lasting effects on economic development. Sustained trade and economic growth frequently demanded centralized governance and well-developed markets, conditions not uniformly present across African societies.

Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that pre-1950 African economies were more robust than previously thought, challenging persistent poverty narratives (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They find that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage trends in British African colonies between 1880 and 1965, which shows that poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon but rather fluctuated with economic conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These findings suggest that African economies were more responsive to global trade and market forces than previously acknowledged. The role of political institutions in influencing these economic outcomes is intricate. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner note that while export production was a significant driver of growth, it was also tightly linked to foreign trade and dependent on political stability (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This dependence highlights how institutional frameworks could either facilitate or hinder economic development, depending on their alignment with global market demands and local conditions.

The literature on colonialism and institutional reforms in Africa highlights the complexities of historical paths and the role of African initiatives in shaping institutional change (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that colonial institutions were not uniformly imposed but adapted to local conditions. This adaptation was driven by indigenous economic and political actions, which influenced colonial administrations to adjust their institutional prescriptions. For instance, where European settlers were absent, the expansion of commodity production aligned with the interests of colonial governments, leading to shifts in institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15). These adaptations demonstrate that African initiatives played a significant role in shaping institutional change, even within the constraints of colonial rule. Hopkins adds that while institutions matter for development due to their role in providing incentives for investment, specific outcomes hinge on local contexts and historical trajectories. This highlights the importance of understanding the unique pathways through which institutions influence economic growth in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The literature suggests that African initiatives within colonial frameworks could drive institutional reforms, but these reforms' effectiveness varied based on local conditions and historical context.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to provide incentives for investment and secure property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8). North argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic development. The creation of the Bank of England, for instance, lowered transaction costs and enhanced security over time, thereby facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is essential as it encourages investment by providing a stable environment where returns can be reliably realized.

Institutions do not function independently; their effectiveness relies on local contexts and historical trajectories. Hopkins notes that while good institutions are crucial for development, their outcomes vary based on specific conditions (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is evident in the varied experiences of colonial Africa, where institutional reforms driven by African initiatives within colonial frameworks showed mixed results due to differing local conditions and historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The presence of European settlers, for example, influenced the type of institutions established. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The historical evidence shows that institutions matter for long-term economic growth due to their role in securing property rights and providing incentives for investment, a shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9). There is disagreement on whether these institutions are primarily driven by efficiency or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective contrasts with North's emphasis on efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms as the key to long-term economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the role of European settlers in shaping institutions: In colonies where Europeans were present, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
