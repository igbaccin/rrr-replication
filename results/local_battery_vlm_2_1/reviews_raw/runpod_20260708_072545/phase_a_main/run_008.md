The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages scholars across disciplines. At stake intellectually is the understanding of how societal structures and rules influence economic trajectories over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic development. Some scholars argue that institutional changes often align with pre-existing conditions like population density and prosperity, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes. The debate also extends to the effectiveness of colonial institutions in determining the economic fate of former colonies. For instance, some argue that extractive institutions imposed during colonial rule have had lasting negative effects on economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutional frameworks continue to impact contemporary economic conditions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth involves both their immediate effects and their enduring influence through various mechanisms, including the enforcement of property rights and the facilitation of market exchanges. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlights that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional change was pivotal in fostering economic growth by creating a stable environment for long-term capital markets. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may design them to maximize rents and promote long-run growth. This dynamic is evident in the context of colonialism, where extractive institutions were often established to benefit European colonists at the expense of local economic development. The enduring effects of these historical institutional frameworks underscore their critical role in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the influence of cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts. The legacy of colonial institutions, which were often extractive in nature, continues to influence contemporary economic conditions in former colonies. This historical context underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks, shaped by both cultural beliefs and political dynamics, continue to impact long-term economic growth.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further enriched by the insights from African studies. This perspective aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can inform policies aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and cultural contexts that shape institutional frameworks and their impact on long-term economic growth.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the emergence of new financial instruments dramatically lowered transaction costs.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) underscore the broader implications of these institutional changes. They argue that the success of these reforms was evident in capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic growth. The establishment of institutions governing public choice was crucial for enforcing rules and ensuring that the regime remained committed to them (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This commitment to credible enforcement mechanisms is key because it creates a stable environment conducive to long-term investment and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of these institutions was complex. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War played a significant role in shaping the political landscape that eventually led to these institutional reforms, demonstrating how historical contexts can influence the trajectory of economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. establish that European settlers established institutions that varied widely based on mortality rates and settlement patterns, leading to different institutional outcomes across colonies; for instance, extractive institutions were set up where mortality was high because Europeans sought rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern of institution-building is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that the interaction between population density, prosperity, and European policies shaped the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The institutional legacies of colonialism have persistent effects on economic outcomes. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varies with the specific historical and political conditions under which these institutions were established.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin contends that this thesis overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution and its interaction with local economic and political contexts, which were often shaped by indigenous responses to colonial policies. For instance, in areas where European settlers did not compete for African labor, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon further illustrate this point by highlighting how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting economic development through both internal norms and external environmental factors such as rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This enduring mistrust has significant implications for contemporary economic issues, as it affects trust in institutions and governance, which are crucial for long-term economic growth. Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how historical legacies influence current economic conditions, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varies with specific historical and political contexts.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) highlight how colonial legacies shape African economic development by influencing institutional structures. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa shows how "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions under colonial rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shift underscores how colonial policies could have long-lasting impacts on economic outcomes by shaping educational attainment and workforce skills. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that women's marginalization under colonial rule, particularly through land allocation and customary law, further constrained economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered impact of institutions suggests that the broader institutional framework was deeply intertwined with social structures. This nuanced understanding of colonial impacts on African development underscores how the institutional legacies of colonialism vary based on specific historical and political contexts. The influence of these institutions on long-run economic growth is evident, but their effects are mediated by complex social dynamics that require further investigation to fully understand their implications for contemporary African economies.

Van Zanden's work on skill premiums in African economic history and Broadberry and Gardner's findings on per capita income levels in Anglophone Africa share a focus on historical perspectives that shape long-run economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). These analyses highlight the significance of historical wage trends as indicators of economic performance, emphasizing that institutions play a role in shaping these trends. Skill premiums, for instance, are influenced by institutional factors such as training costs and barriers to entry into skilled trades. In this context, guilds in western Europe exemplify how formal institutions can facilitate the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions may also impede economic growth by forming local cartels that restrict entry and maintain artificially high wages. This dynamic illustrates a broader pattern: while institutions can support long-run economic growth through skill development, they can also hinder it through restrictive practices.

This finding underscores how historical data can challenge prevailing narratives about African economic performance. Frankema and van Waijenburg caution against overgeneralizing from these patterns; they argue for a nuanced understanding of real wage trends across different colonial contexts in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Their work highlights the need to explore specific determinants of economic development, such as political institutions and local endowments. This suggests that while historical income levels provide valuable insights into long-run economic growth, their interpretation must account for diverse institutional and geographical factors.

This diversity in historical wage trends and income levels across Africa indicates that the effects of institutions on long-term economic performance are complex and context-dependent.

Mokyr's argument aligns with Kuznets' observation that epochal innovations are dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, as Kuznets notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully. The sovereign state plays a pivotal role in this process, ensuring that conflicts arising from economic growth do not weaken the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Mokyr qualifies this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are the primary driver, they must be accompanied by effective institutions to ensure long-term economic growth. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr terms it, is essential for sustaining the benefits of technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the core economic institutions of serfdom, community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were driven by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. One mechanism through which these conflicts operated was the allocation of land and resources: elites in societies with greater inequality could secure government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This led to the persistence of inequality over time. Ogilvie qualifies this perspective by noting the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutional structures. She critiques game-theoretic views which equate 'beliefs' with 'institutions', and highlights how these shared beliefs can block economic progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they must be understood within a broader context of cultural and ideological factors that influence institutional development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either facilitate or hinder development. Institutions matter because they define the rules of the game and shape incentives (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Shared beliefs influence institutional structures, which can block economic progress if they are not aligned with productive activities (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not prioritize long-run growth. Instead, these institutions were extractive and served the interests of the politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is evident in the case of colonies where Europeans established extractive states rather than investing in institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the impact of transaction costs. The development of new financial instruments and institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This underscores how institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can drive economic development by reducing costs per transaction and increasing the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was related to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political actions, shaping the path of economic growth over time (Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement between shared beliefs as a blocker or facilitator of progress is seen in the case of craft guilds. Ogilvie shows that the decline of these institutions was not due to efficiency but because of changes in beliefs about the afterlife, which influenced economic behavior (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This historical evidence underscores how shared beliefs can either facilitate or hinder institutional development, depending on their alignment with productive activities.
