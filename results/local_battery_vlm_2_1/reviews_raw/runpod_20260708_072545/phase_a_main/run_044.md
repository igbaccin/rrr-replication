The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is whether institutions can be the primary driver for sustained economic development. The live disagreement centres around two main positions. One position argues that institutions are key to fostering secure property rights, facilitating market exchanges, and promoting economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, highlighting how regimes committed to rules governing economic exchange can drive long-term growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The other position contends that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and may not always encourage economic development. These institutions might be designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic outcomes is more complex and contingent on historical and political contexts (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This tension sets the stage for understanding how institutions influence economic growth over extended periods.

Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism underlying this effect is rooted in the differential colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. This approach aligns with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these extractive institutions were actively shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maximize their rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound implications for economic development, as they have hindered the emergence of productive and inclusive economic systems. Acemoglu et al. also highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, this institutional legacy is not deterministic; variations in population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, suggesting that local conditions played a role in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of these regions. This interplay between institutional design and local contexts underscores the complex nature of economic development pathways in post-colonial societies.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more positive impact on formal education compared to other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders and families, influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions. This institutional framework altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to both economic opportunities and cultural prestige (Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial administration's emphasis on infrastructure development and governance facilitated these educational initiatives. The impact of British rule varied across different regions within sub-Saharan Africa. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that patterns of growth in export production were tied to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These variations indicate that while British rule generally promoted formal education, the effectiveness of these policies was contingent on local conditions. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa is complex, with both positive and nuanced effects on economic development.

Economic divergence during the early modern period reveals how regional shifts and sector-specific productivity gains can drive economic growth trajectories. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that economic growth during this era was unevenly distributed, with certain regions experiencing significant increases in living standards while others lagged behind. This sectoral shift highlights how regional economic activities could lead to divergence in development patterns.

This picture is complicated by the lack of a clear relationship between economic development and improvements in living standards. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) argues that changes in the relative price levels of basic foodstuffs, such as grain, varied significantly across regions, with England experiencing higher prices after 1740 due to shifts in supply and demand dynamics. This suggests that while economic development was occurring, its benefits were not uniformly distributed across different sectors or regions.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are pivotal in driving economic growth, as both Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the efficient use of innovations is crucial for sustained development. Economic growth requires technological advancements to be effectively integrated into societal structures; this process necessitates adaptive changes in institutions and ideologies. Kuznets (1973, p.6) highlights that modern economic growth hinges on resolving conflicts arising from these integrations, underscoring the role of the state in managing such transitions peacefully. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates that institutional progress complements technological advancements, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by inefficiencies or rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technology and institutions is critical for long-term economic growth, as it ensures that innovations are not only developed but also effectively utilized within a society's framework. The role of the state in managing these transitions underscores how institutional stability can facilitate technological progress, thereby driving economic development forward.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This mistrust is rooted in historical experiences of exploitation and betrayal. It has persisted over time and continues to affect economic outcomes by undermining social cohesion and inhibiting collective action. The persistence of this cultural legacy can be seen as a rival explanation for the long-run economic growth patterns observed in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production where European settlers were absent, suggesting that institutional changes could occur in response to local incentives and actions. This implies that even within a framework of mistrust, certain institutional adjustments might still facilitate economic growth. The pervasive nature of mistrust complicates this process, as it can erode the trust necessary for effective collective action and institutional functioning (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The challenge lies in understanding how to mitigate the effects of historical mistrust while fostering institutions that support long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Institutions that enforce secure property rights are pivotal for fostering economic development. For example, the creation of the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs reflects an increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

Institutions evolve in response to political and economic pressures. For instance, colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on local incentives and actions. This adaptability shows that even within a framework of mistrust, certain institutional adjustments can facilitate economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of historical mistrust complicates this process, as it erodes the trust necessary for effective collective action and institutional functioning (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The historical evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of how institutions underpin economic growth.
