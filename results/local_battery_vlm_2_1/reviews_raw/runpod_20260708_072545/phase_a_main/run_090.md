The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake intellectually is the understanding of whether institutions are fundamental drivers of economic development or merely incidental to other factors such as geography or culture. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions, specifically those that enforce property rights and rule of law, directly influence economic outcomes over the long term. The opposing views range from those who argue that certain institutional frameworks are necessary for sustained growth to those who contend that institutions are secondary to other factors in determining a region's economic trajectory.

The debate often revolves around whether institutions themselves are endogenous or exogenous to economic development. Some scholars posit that institutions arise organically as societies evolve, while others suggest that they are deliberately created to foster economic growth. This tension highlights the complexity of understanding institutional roles and their interactions with other variables such as technology, human capital, and political stability.

The debate also involves the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate. For instance, some scholars emphasize the role of credible commitment in institutional frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Credible commitment underpins growth by providing a stable environment where economic actors can make long-term investments with confidence. This perspective is shared by multiple scholars who argue that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North and Weingast 1989 argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast 1989 report, "Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This article focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, not simply the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. The English financial revolution was critical for economic growth because it enabled long-term capital markets by enhancing the time dimension of property rights. North 1989 argues that "the emergence of political institutions that specify 'efficient' property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges." (North 1989, p.6) This institutional change was not merely about rules but also about how these rules were enforced, thereby creating a stable environment for investment. The evolution of these institutions helped England maintain a competitive edge over France despite similar economic performance.

Distributional conflicts were central to shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) agree that these conflicts drove the development of serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. They argue that these institutions were influenced by distributional concerns, where powerful elites used their influence to maintain control over resources and limit competition. The power dynamics within societies led to the creation of institutions that favored certain groups at the expense of others, creating lasting impacts on economic growth. These institutions influenced immediate economic outcomes and had long-term effects on societal structures. The persistence of inequality, as noted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), was maintained through government policies that restricted competition and favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4), illustrating how distributional conflicts embedded in institutions can perpetuate economic disparities over generations.

Colonial rule in Africa influenced institutional change through negotiation and imposition dynamics, shaping long-term economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights how the new economic history of Africa has illuminated the role of institutions in development. He notes that while ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine institutional quality, the legacy of colonial rule has significantly impacted African governance structures. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) elaborate on this by showing how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects intergroup trust. This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social cohesion necessary for effective institutional functioning. The persistence of colonial-era institutions and the cultural legacies they engender have shaped African economies in enduring ways, influencing everything from governance to social norms. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that British rule was more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers, illustrating how different colonial strategies affected educational outcomes. This variation in institutional development highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and long-term economic growth, underscoring the importance of understanding these historical dynamics when assessing contemporary African economic performance.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women were integral to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The role of women in agriculture and trade was significant. Broadberry and Gardner also highlight the economic contributions of African economies by the early twentieth century. They show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence at this time (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This evidence challenges previous views that marginalized both women's roles in Africa's economic history, and the overall performance of African economies under colonial rule. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing the diverse contributions to economic development across different periods and regions in Africa. The significant role of women in economic activities is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack’s discussion on patriarchal alliances and customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). They point out that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system, which often codified the subordinate status of women. This institutional framework had lasting effects on gender dynamics and economic participation. Broadberry and Gardner also observe that these patterns were shaped by various factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These elements underscore how institutional structures shaped economic outcomes in colonial Africa. The interplay between women's contributions and the broader economic landscape of colonial Africa illustrates a nuanced picture. Women’s roles were integral to both agricultural production and trade, despite facing significant barriers under patriarchal legal systems introduced during colonial rule. This dynamic highlights the need for more comprehensive studies that recognize diverse economic activities and their contributors in African history.

This perspective underscores the importance of useful knowledge. The spread of innovations demands technological progress as well as institutional and ideological adjustments to promote efficient use. These adjustments are necessary for resolving conflicts that arise from economic growth, ensuring sustained development without weakening societal cohesion.

The role of institutions in this context is multifaceted. Kuznets argues that modern technology necessitates significant changes in societal structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This includes adapting to new production sectors and managing the pace of technological change. Mokyr similarly emphasizes the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements, noting that without such progress, economic improvements may be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

These insights suggest a dynamic interplay between technology and institutions. Mokyr describes the growth of useful knowledge as more than just innovation; it also involves creating an environment where these innovations can flourish. This involves reducing access costs to knowledge and fostering coordination among various economic actors. Kuznets' work supports this idea by highlighting the crucial role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The state's authority, based on loyalty and a sense of community, is essential for managing the social and political challenges that accompany technological progress.

This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the complexity of economic growth. While technological advancements are necessary for long-run economic development, they alone are insufficient without complementary institutional changes. These changes ensure that technological progress translates into sustainable economic growth, highlighting the importance of both factors in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Integrating new technologies requires both innovation and a supportive institutional framework to address associated challenges and conflicts.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The colonial institutions established by Europeans varied significantly based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic outcomes, shaping long-term income disparities between former colonies.

The simplicity of this narrative faces challenges from the complexity of historical paths and variability of colonial experiences (Austin 2008, p.2). Colonial administrations often adapted their strategies in response to local conditions and indigenous actions. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, altering institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability indicates that the impact of colonial institutions was shaped by a complex interplay of local factors and colonial policies. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is better understood as a result of this dynamic interaction between colonial institutions and local conditions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their capacity to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of secure property rights, such as those established by the Bank of England, reduced transaction costs and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, which specifies efficient property rights and provides effective enforcement, has a direct impact on the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from Europe shows how political institutions that ensure secure property rights have been instrumental in promoting long-term capital markets. These findings align with Austin's observation that colonial governments sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political dynamics, highlighting the adaptability of institutions to different contexts (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity.
