The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, and scholars have long debated the mechanisms through which this influence occurs. The stakes are high because if institutions can be shown to drive growth, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could potentially transform economies. One significant disagreement lies between those who emphasize the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonists established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, with some promoting growth and others hindering it (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North (1989, p.9), meanwhile, posits that secure property rights are crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.9), suggesting a more nuanced view where the efficiency of institutions matters. The extent to which these institutional frameworks can be altered through policy remains a contentious issue. For instance, if extractive institutions are deeply rooted in historical contexts and hard to change, then policy interventions may have limited effectiveness. If inclusive institutions can be fostered through deliberate reforms, there is hope for significant economic transformation. The debate hinges on whether the path-dependent nature of institutional development allows for meaningful intervention or whether historical trajectories lock economies into predetermined outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) describe how specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs. The Bank's establishment in 1694 led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting by increasing security around property rights and facilitating long-term capital markets. This financial revolution reflects a broader institutional change that underpins economic growth by enhancing the efficiency of market exchanges. This institutional framework was the product of fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, demonstrating that political institutions specifying efficient property rights are integral to fostering economic development.

North's work (North 1989, p.6) on transaction costs highlights how the emergence of these political institutions leads to falling per-transaction costs as economies grow and specialize. The size of the transactions sector thus becomes a growing proportion of GNP, emphasizing the role of efficient property rights enforcement in facilitating market exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further underscore this point by focusing on the critical political factor of regime commitment to rules governing economic exchange. Credible commitments from the sovereign underpin the stability required for economic growth, as evidenced by the success of England's institutional changes during this period.

This is particularly evident when comparing England to France: despite similar economic performance before the French Revolution, the institutional differences between the two countries suggest that specific institutional changes were key drivers of growth. The bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not hinder economic growth as much as previously thought, indicating that the nature and enforcement of institutions are more critical than their complexity.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) assert that distributional conflicts shape institutions alongside efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were driven by struggles over wealth and power distribution among elites and commoners. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This perspective highlights how institutional structures can be manipulated to maintain the status quo, promoting neither efficiency nor growth. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that guilds were tools used by craft masters to control labor markets and suppress competition from journeymen and apprentices. This institutional design, in turn, hindered economic growth by stifling innovation and entrepreneurship among the broader population. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions against oversimplifying these dynamics: distributional conflicts are crucial drivers of institutional change. Beliefs and norms also play a significant role in shaping institutions, as seen in the enduring influence of Catholic beliefs on guilds' rules in medieval Europe.

Austin (2008, p.2) and other scholars (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) re-evaluate historical economic narratives by focusing on how different paths influenced African economic outcomes. A key mechanism they identify is the role of colonial administrators in shaping institutions. Colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating incentives for local economic action that could influence institutional prescriptions. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between colonial policies and local responses as a driver of institutional evolution and long-term economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by emphasizing the need to consider the broader context of governance reforms in Africa, which have expanded to incorporate institutions and organizations crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. This suggests that while colonial policies played a significant role, the effectiveness of institutional changes also depends on contemporary governance frameworks and their ability to foster economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficient use of innovations, driving long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological progress requires significant institutional changes to facilitate the integration of new technologies into society. These changes encompass legal frameworks as well as ideological shifts to accommodate the demands of advancing technology. For example, Kuznets notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This incompatibility necessitates adjustments in societal norms and values alongside institutional reforms.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) builds on this perspective by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as a primary driver of economic growth. He underscores that technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional advancements to ensure sustainable economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr's analysis highlights coordination failures and the need for policies to address them, suggesting that institutions play a pivotal role in facilitating the efficient use of new technologies.

The interplay between technology and institutions is further complicated by the potential for rent-seeking behaviors. This dynamic underscores the necessity of robust institutions to mitigate such risks and ensure that technological advancements translate into long-term economic growth.

Institutional adjustments are thus essential for harnessing the full potential of technological innovations. This variability underscores the need for ongoing institutional reforms to adapt to new technological developments and maintain economic growth.

This nuanced relationship between technology and institutions highlights the complexity of long-run economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) find that Africa's slave trades have left a lasting imprint on the continent's economic development. The central mechanism for this effect is the erosion of trust. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that African societies with higher levels of slave exports experienced lower levels of trust among their populations (Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust, in turn, has profound implications for economic growth. Trust is a critical factor in facilitating economic transactions and fostering cooperation, which are essential for development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade's impact on trust operates through both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law, and internal norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal channel, where individuals develop a deep-seated mistrust due to historical experiences, is particularly significant. This persistent mistrust hampers economic development by reducing cooperation and increasing transaction costs. Nunn (2008, p.2) points out that the impact of the slave trade on trust and subsequent economic development varies across regions (Nunn 2008, p.19). Some African societies, such as those with centralized governments and well-developed markets, were better equipped to mitigate the adverse effects of the slave trade. This variation underscores the importance of pre-colonial institutional structures in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident across different historical contexts and theoretical frameworks. Institutions matter fundamentally for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.2). These institutional changes were crucial in fostering economic growth by reducing barriers to investment and exchange. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the importance of understanding how institutions evolve over time, emphasizing that economic historians see patterns of institutional evolution as key to long-term growth.

The extractive nature of colonial institutions significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The distinct colonization strategies employed by Europeans, ranging from settlement with rule-of-law enforcement to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer, highlight the enduring effects of early institutional frameworks. This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-term development of former colonies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this by emphasizing that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions.

Institutional frameworks influence how societies respond to external shocks like colonialism (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the development of property rights and contract enforcement. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due more to institutional changes introduced by colonialism than to geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding shows that good institutions, crucial for providing incentives for investment, are essential for development. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change.

The variation in African societies' responses to colonialism underscores the importance of pre-colonial institutional structures. This variation shows that some societies were better equipped to handle extractive institutions due to their existing governance frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Hopkins (2009, p.18) concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect public goods, reinforcing the idea that societal cohesion and effective institutions are key drivers of economic growth. This shared finding shows that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still develop progressive institutions.
