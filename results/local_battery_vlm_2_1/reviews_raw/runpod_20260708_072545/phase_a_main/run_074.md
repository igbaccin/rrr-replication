The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians, with debates centering on the mechanisms through which institutions influence development trajectories. Institutions are widely recognized as crucial for economic performance; however, there is a live disagreement over whether these institutions function by fostering efficiency or distributing resources, and whether they are shaped by exogenous factors like geography or endogenous processes such as political conflict. The stakes here involve understanding how to design policies that promote sustainable growth. One position posits that institutions facilitate long-term growth through secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, while another argues that distributional conflicts and political power dynamics play a more significant role in shaping these institutions. The interplay among geography, politics, and institutions remains central to the debate on economic development.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is often explored through the lens of colonial history. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies depending on mortality rates in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, with significant implications for current income per capita. This perspective underscores how initial conditions can shape institutional trajectories and economic outcomes over centuries. Austin challenges the simplistic view of colonialism's impact by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks the complexity of historical paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He argues that the institutional evolution in industrial economies cannot be reduced to a single narrative, emphasizing instead the multifaceted nature of economic development. This suggests that long-run growth is influenced by a combination of factors, including political and economic actions within colonies themselves.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that financial innovations and political institutions in 17th-century England were pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time, a critical factor for both capital markets and overall economic growth. This institutional development involved trading certain rights to Parliament for revenues during fiscal crises (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast argue that this institutional change fostered secure property rights by establishing credible commitments from the regime, which were essential for market development. This is evident in the evolution of English capital markets, where political factors underpinned economic growth by ensuring that rules governing exchanges were both enforced and adaptable (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast acknowledge that while institutional changes were crucial, France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England's.

Distributional conflicts often determine the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) presents that these distributional conflicts shaped key institutions such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective challenges the conventional view that efficiency concerns alone dictated institutional structures. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) illustrate how inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating disparities. The implications of this distributional conflict mechanism are far-reaching: it suggests that even when institutions appear efficient, they may be maintaining existing power structures rather than optimising economic outcomes for the broader population. This dynamic can hinder long-term growth by entrenching inequality and limiting innovation (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in their former colonies. These institutions ranged from inclusive to extractive, depending on the Europeans' mortality rates and settlement patterns. Inclusive institutions encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, while extractive ones were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive institutions maximised rents for European colonists in colonies with high mortality rates among Europeans, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast. A direct implication of these extractive policies is that they hindered local economic development by restricting investment and innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This dynamic is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where those with inclusive institutions experienced more robust industrialization and economic growth during the 19th century. The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes in many former colonies.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, suggesting that institutional dysfunction can occur independently of ethnic diversity. This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) analysis, which underscores the importance of understanding long-term economic development trends to assess future growth potential in Africa. They reconstruct real wage levels across British African colonies to understand income distribution and structural effects on welfare development. This historical approach highlights that institutional factors are crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 GDP estimates for Africa limits our ability to fully grasp these long-term effects. This gap in historical data implies that while institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes, more comprehensive data is needed to fully understand their impact on African economic development over time.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological changes require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This suggests a reciprocal relationship where institutions facilitate the adoption and diffusion of technologies, which in turn drive long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that without these complementary improvements in institutions, the sustainability of technological progress would be limited. Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, the institutional environment must evolve to support and sustain this growth over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This culture of mistrust has enduring effects on contemporary social and economic interactions. It operates through both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin also notes that colonial institutions evolved in response to local economic and political actions. This evolution could have mitigated some of the institutional damage caused by the slave trade, highlighting how local agency can temper the long-term effects of historical traumas such as the slave trade (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuance does not negate the broader impact of mistrust on economic development, as documented in numerous studies that underscore trust's critical role in various economic and social domains.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is undeniable. Institutions determine how societies allocate resources and enforce rules, thereby influencing economic outcomes over extended periods. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation directly impacted current income per capita by shaping the institutional frameworks that either encouraged investment or facilitated extraction. North argues that efficient property rights and effective enforcement are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). These institutions create an environment conducive to long-term capital markets and economic development.

The historical context of colonialism highlights how institutions can be shaped by external forces and local agency. Austin shows that colonial institutions evolved in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which sometimes mitigated the negative effects of extractive practices (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction underscores the importance of local agency in shaping institutional outcomes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The legacy of colonial institutions persists in contemporary economic development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how institutions introduced by colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, emphasizing the long-term impact of institutional changes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins argues that understanding this history can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The enduring effect of mistrust on economic development is evident. Austin highlights how local agency can temper the long-term effects of historical traumas, such as the slave trade (Austin 2008, p.2). However, Acemoglu et al. indicate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistent mistrust generated by such institutional frameworks can significantly hinder economic development.

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the interaction between colonial policies and local responses. Austin shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous actions, highlighting how local agency can influence institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, which interacted with opportunities for industrialization (North 1989, p.9). This interaction shows that while mistrust and extractive institutions can persist, local agency and historical context play crucial roles in shaping long-term economic outcomes.
