The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake are the fundamental determinants of a country's prosperity over centuries, and whether these drivers can be influenced by policy. The live disagreement hinges on two main positions: one emphasizes the primacy of institutions in fostering economic development, while the other views geographic factors or cultural legacies as more decisive. Proponents of institutional primacy argue that secure property rights and effective governance are crucial for sustained growth, whereas those focusing on geography or culture contend that these factors inherently constrain a nation's economic potential. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding historical development paths and designing policies to promote economic growth. A key question is whether institutions can be consciously shaped to drive long-term prosperity or if they are merely epiphenomena of deeper, less malleable forces.

For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with some promoting investment and others being extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North also emphasizes the role of institutions, asserting that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Both perspectives underscore how institutions can either facilitate or hinder long-term development. However, Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique suggests a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth.

The interplay between institutions and economic outcomes is further illuminated by Acemoglu et al., who posit that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective aligns with North's argument about the importance of political institutions in specifying property rights and enforcing them effectively (North 1989, p.6), highlighting how these mechanisms can drive economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon provide additional insight into the cultural dimensions, showing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and affects trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores how historical institutions and cultural legacies can shape contemporary economic outcomes.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is another critical aspect. North and Weingast argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth include not just the rules governing exchange, but also how these rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further complicates this picture by emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution, suggesting that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between institutions and local contexts underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.

North's work on institutions and economic growth in Europe highlights that the establishment of key institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of such financial institutions played a pivotal role in securing property rights, which is crucial for long-term economic growth. North argues that the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security in property rights over time. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth by enabling efficient allocation of resources. The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, which indicates a strong commitment to secure property rights. This suggests that while institutions like the Bank of England were instrumental in reducing transaction costs and fostering growth in England, the impact of similar institutional changes varied across different European contexts. The dynamic interplay between political stability and economic development reveals that secure property rights, enforced through effective institutions, are essential for long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, prioritizing resource extraction over investment and development. These institutions persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions well beyond the colonial period. The impact of these institutions was profound: they constrained economic growth by limiting investment opportunities and enforcing policies that benefitted European colonizers rather than local populations. The institutional framework established during colonialism created a self-reinforcing cycle where extractive policies hindered economic development, leading to persistent poverty. Acemoglu et al. also show that these patterns are not merely due to geographical factors but are directly linked to the institutions imposed by colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, the long-term effects of these institutions were not uniform across all colonies; some regions managed to overcome these institutional barriers over time through significant reforms and policy changes. This variation highlights the complex interplay between historical institutions and contemporary economic outcomes, suggesting that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-run growth, their impact could be mitigated by subsequent institutional reforms.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that previous characterizations oversimplify causation in Africa. This is because they compress different historical periods and paths, failing to account for the varied economic contexts and institutional responses within African colonies. One specific mechanism through which institutions shaped economic outcomes is the response of colonial administrations to indigenous economic action. This adaptive behaviour suggests that colonial institutions responded dynamically to local conditions and pressures. This variation is also shaped by the broader economic context of each colony. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), indicating that economic performance was not uniformly dismal as often portrayed. This highlights a nuanced picture where local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions all played pivotal roles in shaping economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings underscore the importance of considering the diverse historical and economic contexts within Africa to fully understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This effect is driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African families. The implications of this educational expansion are significant: it suggests that the colonial period had varied effects on different regions of Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) explore this by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies, finding that economic development during colonial rule was not uniformly negative. This historical analysis shows that poverty in Africa results from specific historical or geographical conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The British colonial education system thus played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic growth trajectories of former colonies, as it fostered human capital development which was crucial for future economic performance. This highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. The growth of scientific and technical knowledge enabled by these societies reduced access costs to new ideas, fostering an environment where innovation could thrive. This mechanism is further supported by Mokyr's (2005, p.49) observation that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements alone would not have been sufficient for sustainable economic improvement. Kuznets (1973, p.1) reinforces this point, noting that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby ensuring that technological progress can be efficiently utilized. This highlights a nuanced view where technology and knowledge are pivotal for sustained economic development along with institutions.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) posit that the slave trade fostered mistrust within Africa, which has persisted to this day. The slave trade's impact is substantial; it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect on trust levels within Africa. This mistrust is not solely due to the external environment but is deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn (2008, p.2) underscores that this mistrust has profound economic implications, as it hinders both intra-African and international trade by limiting the effectiveness of social institutions. The lack of trust impedes investment and cooperation, crucial elements for sustained economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This persistent culture of mistrust within Africa is a significant factor in the continent's underdevelopment, rivaling the role of institutional frameworks in long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Acemoglu et al.'s work highlights how European colonial strategies established different institutional frameworks across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions ranged from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in places like the United States and Australia, to extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer, as observed in regions such as the Congo and the Gold Coast. This variation in institutional design had profound implications for economic development, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth by maximizing rents for European colonists rather than fostering sustainable economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The legacy of these colonial institutions persists today, influencing current income per capita levels.

North's seminal work underscores the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and other financial innovations during the English financial revolution exemplify how institutional changes can lower transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth. North argues that secure property rights are essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic development. This perspective is shared by Austin, who acknowledges the importance of institutions in shaping economic paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's analysis complements North's by emphasizing the need to understand institutional evolution over different historical periods, rather than compressing diverse experiences into a single narrative.

North and Weingast further elaborate on how political institutions govern public choice and enforce rules, which are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Their analysis of seventeenth-century England shows that credible commitments to property rights and stable governance fostered economic development. This historical evidence aligns with the broader argument that institutional frameworks underpin long-term economic success. Nunn and Wantchekon's research adds another layer by showing how cultural factors, such as mistrust arising from historical events like the slave trade, also shape economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These authors highlight that both external environmental factors and internal norms contribute to the persistence of mistrust, which in turn affects trust levels critical for economic development.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of institutional versus cultural factors. North's argument emphasizes institutions as the key driver of economic growth through secure property rights (North 1989, p.9) whereas Nunn and Wantchekon show that cultural factors such as mistrust significantly affect economic outcomes by hindering trust, which is crucial for development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The historical evidence of colonial institutional design and its long-term effects settles this disagreement. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis demonstrates that the extractive institutions imposed during colonial times have lasting impacts on current economic conditions, highlighting the enduring influence of institutional frameworks over cultural factors in shaping economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
