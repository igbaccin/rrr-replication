The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is an understanding of why some regions prosper while others stagnate over centuries. There are two competing explanations for this phenomenon. One stream argues that long-term economic outcomes are primarily determined by the quality of institutions, which shape incentives, enforce property rights, and facilitate market exchanges. Another perspective suggests that cultural beliefs and norms drive economic performance by influencing how individuals interact and make decisions, while institutional frameworks are also influential (Author Year p.N). This disagreement revolves around whether institutions or culture is the primary driver of long-term economic growth.

The first stream focuses on the role of institutions in fostering economic development. Institutions are defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules such as laws and informal constraints like norms and conventions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who contends that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The argument is that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and provide a stable framework for economic activity.

North argues that institutions are pivotal in economic growth and development, as they reduce transaction costs and provide a stable framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This argument is grounded in the idea that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms facilitate long-term capital markets. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments exemplifies this dynamic. These institutional developments reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast elaborate on this by focusing on how political institutions specify "efficient" property rights and enforce them effectively (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This enforcement capability is critical for fostering economic growth, as it ensures that the rules governing economic exchange are adhered to. The influence of these institutional changes on economic performance can be complex. North and Weingast acknowledge that while these developments were significant, they did not universally revolutionize economic outcomes across different contexts (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This qualification underscores the importance of considering specific historical and political factors when evaluating the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Building on this historical context, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks; settler colonies like the United States and Australia established institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment, whereas extractive states in regions like the Congo and Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This variation in colonial institutions had lasting effects on economic development. The divergence in long-run outcomes is attributed to whether these institutions fostered or hindered investment and growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further argue that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, shaping the institutional landscape of colonies. This nuance underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, highlighting how the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to shape economic trajectories today.

This interplay between colonial institutions and long-term economic performance suggests that understanding the historical context is crucial for evaluating contemporary economic disparities among former colonies.

Distributional conflicts are key drivers behind the design of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that these conflicts shaped core institutions such as serfdom and guilds, not efficiency considerations. The distributive effects of institutions can be seen through the lens of power dynamics: powerful groups often design institutions to maintain their status, even if these institutions are economically inefficient. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that the persistence of serfdom in some regions was due to landowners' ability to impose it on peasants rather than any efficiency gains (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This is consistent with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies: where elites held disproportionate power, institutions were designed to protect their interests. They argue that the extreme inequality in these societies led to institutions that restricted competition and maintained elite dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

The distributional approach qualifies efficiency-based explanations for institutional design by highlighting the role of political power. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that guilds' rules often served to protect members' interests against competitors, even if this reduced overall economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This insight is crucial because it suggests that institutions designed under conditions of distributional conflict may persist long after those conditions change. These institutions can therefore have lasting effects on economic performance and development paths in a way that cannot be explained by efficiency considerations alone.

The slave trade in Africa engendered a pervasive culture of mistrust that has had enduring effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the transatlantic slave trade caused widespread mistrust, which persists today. The internalisation of this mistrust is more significant than external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This phenomenon is particularly relevant to Africa's economic trajectory because it suggests a lasting impact on societal norms and behaviours, which may not be easily reversed or mitigated by institutional reforms alone (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates that this mistrust is compounded by the historical context of slave trades, as well as the socio-political structures they engendered. For instance, the Kongo kingdom's centralized government and developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans, highlighting how pre-colonial institutions could shape economic outcomes even before colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.19). The enduring mistrust from the slave trade may have overshadowed these institutional advantages, creating a complex interplay between historical trauma and institutional design. This indicates that while institutions matter, their effectiveness can be significantly influenced by deeper societal attitudes shaped by historical events.

This historical context underscores the importance of technological innovations in transforming societies by altering modes of life and requiring institutional adjustments to facilitate efficient use of new technologies. These changes require broader societal shifts, including ideological adaptations and new political structures. The sovereign state emerges as a crucial entity in managing growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological progress does not lead to social disintegration (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr acknowledges the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements, suggesting that without such institutional support, economic improvements may be unsustainable. This implies a nuanced relationship between technology and institutions, where technological progress can drive growth but requires supportive institutions to maintain long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to create and enforce property rights, which are crucial for fostering investment and technological progress (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North argues that the development of efficient property rights and their enforcement through political institutions are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional framework supports economic growth and ensures that the benefits of growth are sustained over time.

The historical evidence from colonial experiences further illustrates the significance of institutions in economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). For example, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, which in turn affected long-term economic performance. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates differed from the more inclusive institutions set up in settler colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of initial institutional choices on economic trajectories.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This reversal can be attributed to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, where supportive institutions facilitated long-term development. North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England further supports this view, showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

While there is a shared finding that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), there remains a disagreement on the precise mechanisms through which this influence operates. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts drive core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Author Year p.N). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely designed to maximize long-run growth and suggests that they may also serve to maintain the interests of politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Empirical comparisons across different historical contexts, such as those provided in Nunn and Wantchekon's study, settle this disagreement. Their findings show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic development, highlighting how initial institutional choices can have long-lasting effects on societal trust and economic performance.
