The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development and historical political economy. The stakes are high because the answer shapes our understanding of both the past and the future of global prosperity. The debate centers on whether institutions that enforce property rights and rule of law are necessary for sustained economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological progress play a more significant role. One position argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development; another maintains that these institutions are merely one factor among many, and that their impact is contingent on various historical and contextual elements.

North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight the importance of secure property rights as a foundation for long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights is essential for reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and encourages investment. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This suggests that the long-term development of these economies was fundamentally shaped by the institutions established during colonial rule.

However, Austin (2008, p.15) points out that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that European settlers' presence or absence influenced indigenous commodity production and institutional prescriptions in colonies, indicating a more complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. This complexity is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments.

Austin's critique underscores the need to consider multiple factors beyond institutional frameworks when examining long-run economic growth, aligning with North and Weingast's (1989) (North and Weingast 1989, p.29) emphasis on credible commitment as a critical political factor. North and Weingast argue that the success of seventeenth-century England in fostering secure property rights was underpinned by credible commitments to rules governing economic exchange. This suggests that institutions must not only be present but also effectively enforced and committed to by the regime or sovereign.

The interplay between institutional frameworks, historical contexts, and economic outcomes is nuanced and multifaceted.

This institutional change reflects an increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The establishment of these institutions was a response to fiscal crises and involved trading certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. This suggests that the credibility of commitments by the regime or sovereign is a critical political factor in ensuring that property rights are secure and that economic institutions facilitate market exchanges. This implies that the evolution of political institutions not only reduces transaction costs but also supports increasing specialization and division of labor, which are essential for economic growth. The interplay between secure property rights, credible commitments, and institutional development highlights the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, particularly in historical contexts like seventeenth-century England.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mortality rates of Europeans in different colonies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole. However, in areas with lower mortality, like the United States and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional development had profound implications for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these "equilibrium institutions" were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions into the modern era has been a critical factor in shaping current income disparities among former colonies.

The role of population density and prosperity further complicates this picture. This interaction highlights how geographic factors, though important, are mediated through institutional channels to influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al.'s work underscores that the long-run development of former colonies was significantly shaped by the interplay between institutions and the historical context in which they were established.

In contrast, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Pamuk 2007, p.23) show that institutions in societies with greater inequality in the New World were shaped by elite interests, which restricted competition and maintained their advantage. The factor endowments of these colonies, particularly the abundance of land relative to labor, led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. These initial inequalities influenced institutional development, as elites implemented policies that preserved their power and limited economic opportunities for others (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in the long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which altered wage structures and urbanization patterns across Europe. For example, Holland's institutional flexibility allowed it to rapidly adapt to new economic opportunities, while Tuscany's rigid monopolies hindered its growth (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Pamuk 2007, p.16). The persistence of inequality in the New World can be attributed to the enduring impact of these initial conditions on institutional development.

Similarly, Hopkins and others (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) show that African colonial institutions were shaped by both endogenous initiatives of Africans and exogenous factors imposed by colonial powers. This dynamic is evident in the development of formal education systems, where demand was driven by local cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This perspective highlights how institutional changes influenced by both local initiatives and colonial policies shaped the trajectory of economic development. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). However, this growth was intricately tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This interplay between endogenous and exogenous factors in shaping African colonial institutions underscores the complex nature of economic development in the region.

This complexity is further highlighted by Austin (2008, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), who argue that diverse historical paths and institutional evolutions in Africa challenge simplistic causal narratives about economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) emphasizes the responsiveness of colonial institutions to indigenous economic and political actions. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to some hypotheses, indicating African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further underscore this point, noting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP estimates has led to an incomplete understanding of long-term economic development in Africa. This approach helps assess future growth possibilities and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected African welfare development.

The slave trade had significant adverse effects on Africa's economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on intergroup trust in Africa. The adverse effects are not solely attributable to external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others; instead, they are deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust that were cultivated during centuries of enslavement. Nunn underscores this point by highlighting that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, a period significantly longer than the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule. The prolonged nature of these slave trades left enduring imprints on African societies, shaping economic development in profound ways. This historical context underscores how the legacy of slavery continues to influence Africa's socioeconomic landscape today.

This technological perspective is rooted in the idea that advancements are key to fostering economic development, with institutions playing a complementary role by facilitating the efficient use of these innovations. Mokyr's work emphasizes the importance of useful knowledge, suggesting that it is the growth of this knowledge that propels economic growth. Kuznets elaborates on how technological progress requires institutional adjustments for its effective implementation. These adjustments are crucial because they enable societies to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes without undermining their political stability. For instance, Kuznets highlights that modern technology often clashes with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, necessitating significant social and ideological shifts (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting technological advancements, but it also implies that the primary driver of economic growth is not institutions themselves, but rather the knowledge and technologies they help to disseminate. However, Mokyr points out that while technological progress can be relatively straightforward, institutional progress is often more complex and less irreversible (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This nuance suggests that although technology is a key driver, its benefits are contingent on effective institutional support.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological advancements and secure property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies receiving extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer while others established institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law. This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced economic outcomes, highlighting how effective institutional support is crucial for technological progress to drive growth. North (1989, p.9) similarly emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms in promoting economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments reflects a critical shift towards enhanced security and reduced transaction costs, demonstrating that institutions can significantly lower barriers to economic activity.

The complexity of institutional change further illustrates its impact on long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial policies introduced by European powers shaped the long-term development trajectories of their colonies, with extractive institutions often designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than foster sustainable growth. This perspective aligns with North's view that institutional changes are driven by politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term gains over long-term economic benefits (North 1989, p.6). The nuanced interplay between political power and institutional design reveals how institutions can either facilitate or hinder technological progress, depending on the incentives they create.

The historical evidence from colonial experiences shows that institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in determining economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. This aligns with North and Weingast's argument that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth, illustrating how institutional changes can drive long-term economic development. However, the precise impact of institutions on growth remains a subject of debate, particularly when considering the role of technology versus institutional support.

The shared finding is that institutions matter for technological progress and long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The remaining disagreement concerns whether technological progress or institutional support is the primary driver. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that different measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, yield similar results in explaining economic outcomes. This indicates that while technology is a key driver, its benefits are contingent on effective institutional support. The historical evidence from colonial experiences shows that extractive institutions often designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than foster sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that the precise impact of institutions on growth varies depending on the specific historical context and the nature of the institutions in place.
