The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question at the heart of development economics and economic history. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. At the core of this debate lies a disagreement over whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for long-term economic prosperity. Others propose that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs shape institutional structures in ways that prioritize rent-seeking over efficiency. A third perspective emphasizes the historical context of colonialism and its impact on institutional development, suggesting that initial conditions set by colonizers have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory.

This disagreement is particularly evident when comparing the experiences of former European colonies with different colonization strategies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that in some colonies Europeans established extractive institutions to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, while in others they set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional design has had lasting effects on current income per capita, highlighting the importance of initial conditions set by colonial powers. Similarly, North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), as seen in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England.

This debate underscores the need to understand how different institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over the long term.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change facilitated economic growth by enabling long-term capital markets and enhancing market exchanges through falling transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast's argument is consistent with the political-economic framework that emphasizes the role of institutions in mitigating uncertainty and fostering credible commitment. This institutional evolution was complex; it involved surmounting significant fiscal crises and political challenges such as those presented during the Hundred Years' War. This underscores that while institutions are critical, their development is contingent on historical and political contexts that shape their effectiveness in promoting economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In particular, European colonization strategies varied widely; where mortality rates were high, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had profound effects on current economic outcomes.

The mechanism by which these institutions shaped long-run growth involves their effect on investment and productivity. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional design led to underinvestment in productive activities, stunting economic growth over the long term. These findings are consistent with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who show that differences in institutions explain a significant portion of the variation in current income per capita across countries. The impact of these institutions varied; population density and prosperity at the time of colonization also influenced the policies pursued by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, while lower mortality rates allowed for more productive institutions. This interaction between institutional design and local conditions further shaped economic trajectories in former colonies.

The long-run effects of colonial institutions are evident in the industrialization patterns of former colonies. The opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in their development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Countries with extractive institutions faced barriers to industrialization, while those with more productive institutions were better positioned to take advantage of industrial opportunities. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic growth pathways today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts drove core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. The authors show that these institutions were shaped by elite interests, which sought to maintain their power and wealth through various government policies that restricted competition. This is evident in the evolution of institutions such as serfdom and guilds in Europe, where elite control over labor and markets was a central feature. Similarly, in New World societies, institutions were designed to favor elites, often at the expense of broader economic growth. For example, in Latin America, the persistence of extractive institutions that privileged elites has been linked to the region's long-term economic stagnation (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). In fact, the legacy of these institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as seen in the enduring disparities between regions with different colonial histories and institutional legacies. This suggests that distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping institutions that persist over time and have lasting effects on economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15), among others (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15) re-evaluate historical economic performance in Africa by examining the specific colonial policies that shaped indigenous commodity production. These scholars show that colonial governments often encouraged local commodity production when settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This dynamic created incentives for indigenous economic and political action, which in turn influenced institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations sought fiscal security and responded to the needs of their subjects by changing policies based on these incentives. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this view by noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while colonial policies played a role, other factors also influenced economic outcomes.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that Africa's slave trades have had a profound impact on the continent’s economic development, challenging views that attribute underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. The slave trade created a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies, which continues to affect intergroup dynamics today. The authors find that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting its enduring influence (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is an external consequence of the slave trade and a deeply ingrained internal norm within individuals, which has significant implications for economic development. Nunn supports this view by noting that the period of African slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, far longer than the era of colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extended duration suggests that the slave trade had a more profound and lasting impact on Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn (2008, p.2) also highlights that sustained trade in certain regions was facilitated by well-developed markets and trading networks supported by centralized governments like the Kongo kingdom (Nunn 2008, p.19). This underscores that while the slave trade had adverse effects, pre-colonial institutional structures could mitigate some of its negative impacts.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores that while institutions may facilitate growth, it is the advancement in technology that propels economies forward. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on these adjustments, noting that modern technology demands shifts in societal structures and ideologies. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional changes can lead to significant economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) cautions that without complementary institutional progress, economic improvements may be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

This suggests that while institutions are crucial for harnessing the benefits of technological progress, their role is secondary to the primary driver of growth: advancing technology.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical contexts. Institutions are critical for harnessing technological progress; their quality significantly influences the sustainability of economic improvements (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that extractive institutions established by European colonizers in high-mortality regions persistently affected current income per capita, highlighting how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder growth over extended periods (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is echoed by North (1989, p.9), who argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are pivotal for economic development. The Bank of England's establishment and subsequent financial innovations illustrate how institutions can reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Moreover, North (1989, p.9) shows that the evolution of political institutions specifying efficient property rights is essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This institutional framework ensures that transaction costs per unit decline while the aggregate volume of exchanges increases, reflecting a growing economy (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that regimes adhere to rules governing economic exchange. Their analysis of seventeenth-century England shows how political institutions enforcing property rights fostered secure markets, which in turn drove economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This finding aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) argument that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth. The historical evidence from capital markets indicates that institutional changes were crucial in determining economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Austin adds a nuanced perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. He contends that different historical periods and paths must be considered to fully understand how institutions influence long-run growth. For instance, colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between institutions and local contexts underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic development.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions fundamentally shape long-term economic growth by influencing property rights enforcement and transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise mechanisms through which institutions affect growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts explain key pre-industrial European institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the view that institutions are primarily designed to maximize economic growth. The historical evidence from colonial administrations adapting to indigenous actions provides a clear case where institutional changes were driven by local economic dynamics. This empirical comparison shows how specific institutional adaptations can lead to different economic outcomes, settling the debate on the primary drivers of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15).
