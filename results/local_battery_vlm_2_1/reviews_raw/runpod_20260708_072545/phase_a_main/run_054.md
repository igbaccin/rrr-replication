The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding development trajectories across countries and historical periods. This question is pivotal because it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive economic prosperity over extended periods; it also highlights how institutions can either facilitate or hinder sustained economic progress. The debate centers around two core positions: one emphasizes institutional frameworks as primary drivers of growth, while another focuses on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping these institutions and their impact on long-run development.

Some scholars argue that European colonies with high mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutions persisted over time, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring influence of colonial-era institutional legacies on contemporary economic outcomes. Simultaneously, another line of thought posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; instead, it suggests a more nuanced view where institutions evolve in response to various factors over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This evolution stems from colonial policies and indigenous economic and political actions. Consequently, the institutional framework of a society can be shaped by both exogenous and endogenous forces, influencing long-term economic growth trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth through the reduction of transaction costs. The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly lowered these costs, fostering economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This institutional framework was crucial for creating a stable environment conducive to economic activity. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. These financial innovations were integral to the English financial revolution, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This security is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself.

The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England also involved a political dimension, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues during fiscal crises. This process highlights how political stability and credible commitments can underpin long-run economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also propose a nuanced view where these institutional changes have an impact that is complex. They argue that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth in France, implying that institutions per se were not revolutionary enough to cause significant divergence in economic performance between England and France. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other factors influencing long-term economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The European settlers' mortality rates determined the type of institutional structures they established in their colonies. In high-mortality regions, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on economic development. This pattern is evident in the contrast between settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, and regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were the norm. The persistence of these institutional frameworks highlights how colonial policies continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) extend this argument by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, emphasizing the enduring effects of colonial-era institutional legacies on long-term economic trajectories.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) question the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by examining historical economic data and colonial impacts. A shared finding with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) is that much of the continent was above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, highlighting a significant divergence from prevailing narratives about African economic history. This perspective underscores the importance of re-evaluating the impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic trajectories in Africa. The mechanism by which this occurs is through the influence of foreign trade and export markets on local economies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels to assess the extent of poverty, finding that specific historical or geographical conditions have had a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development rather than structural impediments as widely believed. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that patterns of growth in export production varied based on factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This suggests that the economic impact of colonial rule was shaped by a complex interplay of factors across regions. Consequently, understanding these dynamics is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth in Africa.

Further complicating this view, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue that the transatlantic slave trade directly harmed African economic development by fostering enduring mistrust among Africans. The slave trades' destructive impact on social capital is a key mechanism through which they impaired long-run growth. This mistrust, rooted in the historical exploitation and violence of the slave trades, persists to this day, affecting both intra-group and inter-group relations. Nunn finds that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, dwarfing the period of official colonial rule by a factor of seven. The slave trade's legacy is not merely historical; it continues to influence contemporary African economies through its effects on social capital and institutions. Nunn also notes that the centralized government and developed markets of the Kongo kingdom facilitated early trade with Europeans, suggesting that pre-colonial political and economic structures played a crucial role in Africa's engagement with global trade networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This highlights how the legacy of the slave trades interacts with other historical factors to shape African economic development.

This perspective emphasizes the growth of useful knowledge as a pivotal factor in economic development, suggesting that institutional adjustments are necessary to accommodate and sustain technological advancements. These innovations require corresponding changes in institutions and ideologies to resolve conflicts arising from modernization, ensuring the political stability needed for economic progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to new knowledge, thereby fostering technological advancement. This suggests that while institutions are crucial for managing the social and political implications of technological change, their primary function is to support and sustain the growth of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). However, Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress must complement technological advancements to ensure sustainable economic improvement; otherwise, rent-seeking behavior could erode these gains. This implies that while institutions are important, they are not the fundamental cause of long-term economic growth but rather serve as enablers of technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Distributional conflicts are central to understanding the evolution of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. These distributional conflicts are rooted in the political economy of pre-industrial societies, where various groups vied for control over resources and decision-making processes. For instance, colonial administrators often adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions, opting for higher production equilibria when it suited their interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutions manage power and resource distribution in addition to being efficiency-enhancing mechanisms.

This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional development, where political struggles and power dynamics play crucial roles alongside economic considerations. By recognizing this, historians can better appreciate how institutions shape long-run economic growth through their influence on resource allocation and societal structures.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing resource allocation and societal structures. The creation of secure property rights is a key mechanism through which institutions foster economic development. North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Moreover, the evolution of institutions governing public choice significantly impacts economic outcomes. North and Weingast demonstrate that political factors underpinning economic growth include rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules and determining how they may be changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Credible commitment to property rights is essential for sustained economic development, as seen in the historical evolution of England's financial institutions (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had profound effects on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In some cases, they set up extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions significantly affected current income per capita by shaping resource allocation and societal structures in former colonies.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is also crucial. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional development, where political struggles and power dynamics play crucial roles alongside economic considerations.

A shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth. North shows that transaction costs are reduced by secure property rights, which facilitate market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise measurement of these effects remains a subject of debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique highlights the need for nuanced analysis in understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement involves examining colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, thereby changing their institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15).
