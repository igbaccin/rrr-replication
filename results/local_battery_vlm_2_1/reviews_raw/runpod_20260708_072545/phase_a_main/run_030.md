The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue at the heart of economic history and development economics. At stake intellectually is understanding whether institutions are the primary drivers of sustained economic prosperity or if they merely complement other factors. The live disagreement centres around two main positions: those who argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and efficient markets; and those who contend that institutional explanations oversimplify complex historical processes and overlook other critical factors such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks. Austin and Ogilvie both critique the prevailing focus on institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, suggesting that a broader view is necessary to capture the full range of influences at play. Austin argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions than efficiency considerations alone. The debate over the role of institutions in long-run economic growth is far from settled, with scholars continuing to grapple with how best to incorporate institutional analysis into broader frameworks of economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. They establish this by showing how different institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder investment and development. For instance, North's analysis of the English financial revolution illustrates how new financial instruments and secure property rights lowered transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in promoting economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage long-term growth if they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than fostering development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, suggesting that while strong institutions can drive growth, their effectiveness depends on how they align with broader economic goals.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight the political factors underpinning this growth, focusing on credible commitments that ensure rules are enforced. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes the need to integrate institutional analysis into long-term research programs, emphasizing that institutions shape not only economic exchange but also how rules are enforced and changed. This underscores the importance of credible commitment mechanisms in ensuring sustained economic growth. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by noting that distributional conflicts often drive institutional changes more than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that while institutions can foster growth, their evolution is also influenced by political and social dynamics that may prioritize the interests of powerful groups over broader economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological innovation is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of this process. Kuznets elaborates on how the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These technological advances drive economic growth and necessitate institutional adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles, large family patterns, and veneration of undisturbed nature, necessitating changes in the social fabric to accommodate these innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores how technological progress requires complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without such institutional adjustments, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited and might eventually be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that these growth-induced conflicts must be resolved peacefully to maintain the political fabric of society. This suggests that while technological innovation is a key driver of economic growth, its benefits are contingent on effective institutional adjustments and conflict resolution mechanisms.

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) show that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not directly link to changes in living standards. This period saw significant structural shifts, such as the rise of urbanization rates and regional economic disparities (van Zanden 1999, p.1). These shifts were driven by institutional flexibility, which enabled regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new opportunities. In Holland's case, weak monopolies allowed for the development of rural manufacturing and the growth of new towns (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This institutional environment fostered economic dynamism despite limited changes in living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This flexibility was not universal; regions like Tuscany, with strong monopolies, experienced stagnation. These findings underscore the importance of institutional settings in facilitating or hindering economic development, even when overall living standards do not immediately reflect these changes.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15), shaping the region's institutional landscape and subsequent economic trajectories. Frankema highlights that demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations, altered by new social and political institutions implemented under British rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was driven by African leaders' macro-level decisions and families' micro-level choices, which in turn were influenced by the institutional changes brought about by colonial governance. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This economic development was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, which were themselves shaped by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also caution that these patterns of growth varied significantly between and within countries. The implication is that while British rule facilitated certain economic developments through education and trade, the uneven implementation of these institutional changes led to diverse economic outcomes across sub-Saharan Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) jointly argue that colonial rule in Africa shaped economic outcomes through the formation of institutions, yet these institutional changes did not directly address the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Colonial administrations often responded to local economic conditions by adjusting their policies and institutions. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments encouraged commodity production that aligned with indigenous interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive institutional response suggests that colonial rule was responsive to local contexts, fostering varied economic outcomes across Africa.

The impact of these institutional changes on long-run growth remains complex. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This mechanism underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth, even if the colonial context introduced specific distortions and inefficiencies. The adaptive nature of colonial institutions highlights that while they did not uniformly promote long-run growth, they could facilitate economic activities that were beneficial under certain conditions. Nevertheless, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence contemporary African economies, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on present-day development trajectories.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) examine specific historical factors in African economic history such as gender roles which, while adjacent to the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, highlight how African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that patriarchal alliances and customary law codified the subordinate status of women under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This mechanism underscores the enduring influence of historical gender dynamics on contemporary economic structures. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it affects both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law. The importance of trust in economic development is well-documented. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings complement these studies by providing insights into the origins of this mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of property rights and the rule of law. The establishment of secure property rights is central to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This security lowers transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and encouraging investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The historical context of colonialism highlights how different institutional frameworks were imposed on colonies, significantly impacting their long-term development. European settlers established varied institutions based on mortality rates and the presence of disease in colonized regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions ranged from inclusive to extractive, with lasting effects on current income per capita (Austin 2008, p.15).

The reversal of fortunes experienced by former colonies underscores the enduring impact of institutional legacies. The interaction between colonial institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century was pivotal in shaping long-run development trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This interaction shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had profound effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of trust within societies is also critical. The legacy of the slave trade in Africa has created a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting both internal norms and external factors like the rule of law (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This mistrust impedes economic development by undermining social cohesion and inhibiting investment.

Institutional reforms are essential for eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional changes can inform policy and drive development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). By understanding the historical impact of institutions, policymakers can implement reforms that foster growth and reduce poverty.

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter significantly for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The precise remaining disagreement lies in whether institutions are shaped primarily by exogenous factors like geography or endogenous forces such as political power struggles. Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2). This is settled by Acemoglu et al.'s detailed empirical comparison of former colonies, which demonstrates that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the key drivers of economic divergence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
