The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue within economic scholarship. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and sustain prosperity over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they shape the incentives for investment and economic development (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Some scholars argue that institutions and economic growth are linked through intricate historical processes and colonial legacies (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The debate over the role of institutions in long-run economic growth involves a deep dive into how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, impact economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that European settlers established varying institutional frameworks in their colonies, which significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The presence of extractive institutions in some colonies, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Institutions influencing economic growth are dynamic entities shaped by political power and distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were driven more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions solely evolve to maximize economic outcomes, suggesting instead that they are deeply rooted in social and political dynamics.

This insight underscores the complexity of institutional design and its implications for long-run economic growth. The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-term development of former colonies, illustrating how historical institutional choices can have lasting effects on economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The argument that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth is further supported by North's assertion that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with the idea that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for fostering investment and economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth extends beyond historical contexts to contemporary policy debates. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into institutional reforms that could help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective highlights the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in economic development, suggesting that effective policy interventions can be designed based on historical lessons.

Institutional design is pivotal to long-run economic growth. Institutions shape incentives for investment and economic activity, and their historical evolution reflects complex interactions between political power, distributional conflicts, and economic opportunities. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing policies that promote sustainable economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth. Specifically, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the advent of novel financial instruments dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, which reflects increased security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was a response to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated trade-offs between certain rights and parliamentary revenues. The political institutions that emerged from these crises specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, leading to falling transaction costs per exchange. As specialization and division of labor increased, so did the aggregate volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic highlights how secure property rights facilitated by institutional changes underpin long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) suggest that these institutions' impact was intertwined with broader political contexts. The success of England’s financial revolution depended on the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights and maintaining a stable political environment. This commitment was critical for fostering secure property rights, which in turn supported economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering both institutional design and political stability when examining the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) argue that the quality of institutions is crucial for economic development in Africa. Hopkins explains that "good" institutions are pivotal because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective aligns with Frankema's finding that British colonial rule was more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers, suggesting a link between institutional quality and educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa during colonial rule. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that women were often marginalized from land access due to customary laws codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization highlights how institutional changes can have differential impacts on various segments of the population, potentially limiting the overall economic benefits. The complex interplay between institutions and economic development in Africa underscores the need for nuanced policies that consider these historical legacies while promoting inclusive growth.

Van Zanden and Broadberry and Gardner share that the literature on African economic performance has overlooked the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). They argue that the focus on other factors such as capital accumulation and technological change obscures the critical role played by institutional frameworks. Institutions can determine the skill premium, a key predictor of long-term economic performance, by influencing the costs and barriers to entry into skilled trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). For instance, guilds in western Europe facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills but also acted as local cartels, potentially limiting overall productivity gains. This dual role highlights how institutions can both support and hinder economic development by shaping the supply and demand dynamics within skilled labor markets. Frankema and van Waijenburg suggest that a better understanding of these institutional determinants could provide insights into future growth potential in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

This early divergence suggests that pre-industrial institutional differences were already setting the stage for future economic paths. This divergence can be attributed to significant differences in productivity within traded goods sectors, with northwest Europe experiencing higher productivity due to merchant distribution and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This mechanism highlights how economic institutions facilitated trade and financial services, driving growth in specific regions. Pamuk 2007 adds that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16), further emphasizing the role of institutional factors in shaping economic trajectories. Van Zanden 1999 shows no clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This suggests that while institutions may drive economic growth, they do not necessarily translate into uniform improvements in living standards across different regions.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) share the finding that African post-independence economic trends were shaped by regional socio-economic dynamics rather than overarching institutional factors. The literature on this subject often focuses on the role of patrimonial governance structures in shaping public expenditure patterns and resource allocation. Under these systems, political elites treat state resources as personal assets, distributing them to secure loyalty rather than investing broadly in public goods (Simson 2019, p.2). This dynamic is evident in East Africa's post-independence spending trends, where military expenditures and debt service payments became significant components of national budgets, often at the expense of capital investments and recurrent spending. For example, Kenya saw subsidies and interest payments peak at 30% of total expenditure during the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). This shift in expenditure patterns was not unique to Kenya; Tanzania also faced a substantial debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure. Such regional variations in public spending reflect the complex interplay between political dynamics and economic policies, underscoring the importance of understanding local contexts when analyzing post-independence economic trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time. They argue that the institutional impacts of slavery and inequality in New World societies resulted from policies that advantaged elites at the expense of broader economic development. The differentials in factor endowments, such as land abundance relative to labor, led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These inequalities were reinforced by institutions that restricted competition, thereby maintaining elite dominance and hindering overall growth. While colonial rule lasted only about 75 years, the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, deeply embedding institutional legacies that persist to this day. Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective posits that advancements in technology and knowledge are more fundamental than institutional factors. One mechanism through which this occurs is the reduction of access costs to new technologies, facilitated by formal institutions such as scholarly societies (Mokyr 2005, p.49). These societies played a pivotal role in disseminating and coordinating technological progress, thereby enabling sustained economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets highlights that technological progress is crucial but also requires institutional and ideological adjustments to address modernization conflicts. Such adjustments are essential for the efficient use of innovations and for maintaining social cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6) . These findings suggest that while technological advancements drive economic growth, their impact is contingent upon complementary institutional progress.

Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation in economic history (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique underscores that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths. One mechanism by which this simplification occurs is through ignoring how distributional conflicts shaped core institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates this by showing that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, rather than following a uniform path (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) advises against overemphasizing the role of institutions alone. She suggests that while institutions are crucial, they must be understood in conjunction with broader socio-economic factors and cultural beliefs. This nuanced view highlights that institutions do not operate in isolation but are deeply embedded within complex social structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Thus, understanding long-run economic growth requires a multifaceted approach that considers both institutional frameworks and the underlying distributional conflicts that shape them.

The implications of this critique extend beyond historical interpretations to contemporary policy-making. Recognizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions suggests that policies aimed at fostering economic growth must also address underlying social inequities. This perspective challenges simplistic views of institutional reform, emphasizing the need for comprehensive strategies that consider both institutional frameworks and broader socio-economic dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. This dynamic is evident in the English financial revolution of 1694, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs due to increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional developments are crucial for fostering long-term economic growth, as they provide a stable framework that encourages investment and economic activity (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Institutions also play a pivotal role in mediating distributional conflicts, which can significantly impact economic outcomes. For instance, the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates highlights how initial institutional frameworks can shape long-term economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, the evolution of colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous commodity production shows how institutional responses to local economic and political actions can influence growth (Austin 2008, p.15).

The importance of institutions extends beyond their immediate economic functions; they also reflect broader socio-economic dynamics. The creation of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting market exchanges, thereby stimulating economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by the observation that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The historical evidence from various contexts demonstrates that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth. For example, equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often extractive, prioritizing the interests of European colonists over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the need for comprehensive institutional reforms that address underlying social inequities and promote inclusive growth.
