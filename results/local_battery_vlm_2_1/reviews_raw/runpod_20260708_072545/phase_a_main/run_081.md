The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and development studies, as it addresses whether and how the rules that govern society can foster sustained prosperity. At stake intellectually are the mechanisms through which institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over extended periods; these mechanisms may be more or less relevant depending on the historical context and the specific institutions under consideration. The live disagreement centers around the relative importance of different institutional factors, such as property rights enforcement versus distributional conflicts, in determining long-term economic performance. Some scholars emphasize the role of secure property rights and rule of law, while others point to the significance of cultural beliefs and internal norms of mistrust.

Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates in colonies, which significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach underscores how initial institutional setups can have long-lasting effects on economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2), suggesting that a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary to fully grasp economic growth dynamics. Both perspectives converge on the idea that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, but they differ in their emphasis on the specific mechanisms and historical contexts involved.

North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development by reducing transaction costs and specifying efficient property rights (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s focus on the importance of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie, on the other hand, suggests that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This view complements North's emphasis on political factors but shifts the focus towards internal norms and cultural beliefs as drivers of institutional change. The interaction between these perspectives reveals how both external enforcement mechanisms and internal cultural dynamics can shape long-run economic growth.

The role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth is another key aspect highlighted by scholars. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective emphasizes the importance of political factors underpinning economic growth, such as the degree to which a regime is committed to enforcing rules. Nunn and Wantchekon provide additional evidence that trust plays a significant role in economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), suggesting that cultural beliefs and internal norms can also influence long-run economic outcomes. This stream underscores how different institutional factors, including political commitment and cultural beliefs, interact to shape economic growth over time.

Austin critiques the oversimplification of causal mechanisms in institutional theories and argues that historical paths and periods are compressed (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique highlights that different historical periods and paths are often merged into a single narrative, obscuring the complexity of institutional evolution. For example, the persistence of serfdom in pre-industrial Europe can be understood through power struggles between elites and peasants. This perspective underscores that institutional changes are often driven by the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain or enhance their control over resources, thereby shaping economic outcomes in ways that may not align with efficiency goals. Furthermore, Austin illustrates how colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and pressures, challenging the notion of a uniform path to institutional development. The interplay between historical context and distributional conflicts thus offers a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that historical economic performance varies significantly across regions, challenging conventional narratives about economic development. They show the importance of national accounting in understanding Africa's economic history, revealing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the role of foreign trade and export markets in driving economic growth, as Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate through case studies of various countries. Export production was a key factor in economic growth, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The timing and scale of this growth varied widely, illustrating the complex interplay between these factors.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this analysis to the pre-1800 era, arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway by then. They posit that high productivity in traded goods sectors in northwestern Europe was driven by production, merchant distribution, and finance. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in early modern economic growth, much like it does today. The uneven distribution of economic benefits is evident in the differing surpluses over basic food needs between skilled and unskilled laborers. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further note that institutional change typically led to investment in agriculture, resulting in high agricultural labour productivity in developing parts of northwestern Europe. This highlights how institutional factors can drive economic growth through increased productivity, a mechanism that aligns with the broader theme of institutions shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the mainspring of economic growth and development. This perspective contrasts with institutional theories of long-run economic growth by focusing on the role of innovation and knowledge dissemination rather than institutions. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this idea, noting that modern technology necessitates significant adjustments in both institutions and ideologies to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that while technological progress is pivotal, it must be accompanied by institutional changes to sustain economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights the importance of resolving conflicts arising from technological advancements without excessive costs or long-term damage to societal cohesion. He underscores that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully managing these conflicts, ensuring that economic growth can proceed smoothly (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is essential, institutional stability and adaptability are also necessary for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges this interplay by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements could be eroded over time due to rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of both technological innovation and institutional adaptation in driving long-run economic growth.
