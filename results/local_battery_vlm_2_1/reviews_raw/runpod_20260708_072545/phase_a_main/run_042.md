The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. The debate centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. One position argues that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic growth. This perspective posits that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures can lead to increased investment, innovation, and overall economic prosperity. Another position contends that institutional changes alone cannot fully account for long-run economic growth; instead, it emphasizes the interplay between institutions and other factors such as population density, prosperity, and cultural beliefs. This view suggests that while institutions matter, they are not the sole determinant of economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The stakes in this debate are high, as understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth in developing countries.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional outcomes in their colonies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, illustrating how institutional design can be influenced by the interests of politically powerful groups. This perspective is consistent with North's argument that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term capital markets and supporting economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) share that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The differential impact of European colonization on institutions can be traced through two primary mechanisms: the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions and the persistence of these institutions over time. These mechanisms are shaped by the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in different colonies, which influenced their settlement patterns and institutional designs. In colonies where mortality was high, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, European colonizers set up extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional design had profound implications for economic development, as extractive institutions hindered long-term growth by focusing on resource extraction and fostering local economic activity. The persistence of these institutions over time further exacerbated the economic disparities between colonies with different institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document that this reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, highlighting how the interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrial development played a central role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This underscores the importance of understanding both the initial institutional design and how these institutions evolved over time and interacted with broader economic trends.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that specific institutional developments can significantly alter economic growth trajectories by affecting transaction costs and property rights security. North (1989, p.9) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 triggered a financial revolution, drastically reducing transaction costs. The fiscal crises during the Hundred Years War prompted this institutional change, demonstrating how political pressures can drive economic reforms.

They argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), which is evident in the success of England's capital markets during this period. This institutional framework ensured that the regime was bound by the rules, creating a stable environment for economic activities. North (1989, p.9) notes that while falling transaction costs are beneficial, the growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP indicates increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This implies that although institutional changes can spur growth, they also necessitate adaptations to new economic structures.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that economic institutions in pre-industrial societies were shaped primarily by distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations. Ogilvie highlights the role of beliefs as part of a society's institutional structure, noting how shared beliefs can influence economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who point out that the variation in development paths across societies can be attributed to differences in institutions, which are themselves rooted in distributional conflicts. These conflicts often led to policies that advantaged elites at the expense of broader societal growth. For example, in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were designed to restrict competition and maintain elite power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, driven by distributional concerns, had long-term implications for economic development. Ogilvie cautions against overemphasizing the role of beliefs as institutions; she repudiates a game-theoretic view that equates beliefs with institutions. Instead, she advocates for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within economic systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This nuance is crucial for appreciating the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design in shaping long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) underscores this point by noting that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress must complement it to ensure sustainable economic improvement. This interplay between technology and institutions is pivotal, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that modern technology necessitates significant adjustments in both institutional frameworks and societal ideologies. These adjustments are essential for the efficient use of innovations and the resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs or long-term political weakening (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr's analysis of formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, further illustrates how reducing access costs to knowledge can drive economic growth; he highlights that institutional progress is vital for sustaining technological advancements. Kuznets cautions that these adjustments must be made peacefully and efficiently within the political fabric of society to avoid hindering long-term economic development. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the growth of useful knowledge and ensuring that technological progress translates into sustained economic growth.

The literature on economic contributions and colonial impacts in Africa highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, highlighting the importance of governance in providing incentives for investment. This aligns with Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014) assertion that African women played a significant role in economic growth, particularly through their contributions to food farming and cash crops such as cocoa, which were pivotal during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often marginalized women's access to land, reinforcing patriarchal structures that could hinder long-term economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This suggests that colonial institutions played a role in shaping educational outcomes, which are critical for long-term economic growth. The impact of British rule on education underscores how colonial institutions could influence public goods provision, but the broader implications for long-run economic growth remain nuanced.

Van Zanden argues that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic performance through their influence on skill premiums (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The skill premium, as van Zanden explains, reflects the costs and benefits of investing in human capital. This investment is influenced by factors such as training costs, institutional barriers to entry, interest rates, and the expected duration of higher wages. Broadberry and Gardner's work on African economies further underscores this point by highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that institutional factors, including those governing trade and market access, were significant in driving economic performance. Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that the relationship between institutions and long-term welfare is complex; they argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This nuanced view implies that while institutions can foster economic growth by influencing skill premiums, their effects are contingent on broader economic and social conditions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27)  argue that historical factors such as the slave trade have significantly shaped African economic development. They focus on how these legacies have created unique challenges for Africa that are distinct from institutional frameworks emphasized in broader literature. This mistrust is deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms. This internalized mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it affects trust in institutions and interpersonal relationships, both crucial for fostering economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that while historical factors like the slave trade have had lasting effects, they are part of a complex web of influences that also include colonial policies and economic incentives. The legacy of these historical factors means that African countries face unique challenges in developing robust institutions that can support long-term economic growth.
