The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic development over extended periods: are institutions the primary drivers, or do other factors such as geography, culture, or population dynamics play more significant roles? This debate pits those who argue that institutions are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth against those who contend that distributional conflicts or exogenous cultural beliefs better explain core economic institutions. Additionally, some scholars propose that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, emphasizing the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies. Understanding this debate is crucial for grasping how societies develop economically over time.

The role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth has been a central theme in historical studies of economic development. North argues that the creation of financial institutions such as the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflects increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who highlight the political factors underpinning economic growth, specifically, how secure property rights are enforced through credible commitment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, as outlined by North, aligns with the broader argument that efficient enforcement of property rights is crucial for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This emphasis on institutional foundations underscores how secure property rights can foster a stable environment conducive to investment and economic development over time.

The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional development facilitated a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). North also highlights that efficient property rights and their enforcement reduce transaction costs, fostering economic growth through increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The evolution of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights is crucial for understanding the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. This institutional framework not only governs economic exchange but also enforces rules and manages changes to these rules. North and Weingast assert that a critical factor in this process is the regime's commitment to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of England’s institutions in fostering secure property rights underscores their pivotal role in long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The type of institutions established by European colonizers varied widely based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. These extractive states focused on resource extraction rather than fostering local economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework persisted over time, influencing current income per capita in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The establishment of extractive institutions was not merely a function of geographic factors but a direct result of colonial policies designed to maximize European rents. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups within the colonies, who often resisted changes that could undermine their control or reduce their immediate benefits (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Acemoglu et al. argue that these extractive institutions did not encourage economic development but instead maximized short-term gains for colonizers, leading to long-term stagnation in many former colonies. The persistence of these institutions is a critical factor in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This institutional legacy continues to affect economic outcomes today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on long-run economic growth.

Colonial rule played a pivotal role in shaping institutions in Africa through the negotiation and imposition of policies that influenced long-term economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins highlights that colonial policies often reflected the interests of colonizers rather than local needs. For instance, the development of formal education systems was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were altered by new social and political institutions imposed during British rule (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). The benefits of formal education were tied to these new institutions, illustrating how colonial policies could reshape local priorities. Broadberry and Gardner note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial rule did not uniformly stifle economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that while colonial policies were often extractive, they also created conditions under which local economies could grow. However, Broadberry and Gardner acknowledge that this growth was tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, which were influenced by a range of factors including political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings indicate that the legacy of colonial rule in Africa is complex, with both positive and negative impacts on economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) posit that distributional conflicts were instrumental in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds emerged from struggles over wealth and power rather than efficiency considerations. These institutions reflected the interests of dominant groups who sought to maintain their status through restrictive practices. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this perspective by demonstrating how similar distributional conflicts in New World societies led to the establishment of institutions that reinforced inequality. They note that "the great majority were characterized virtually from the outset by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power" (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern suggests that economic institutions are not merely tools for enhancing efficiency but also mechanisms for entrenching social hierarchies. However, Ogilvie cautions against over-reliance on any single explanatory framework; she urges scholars to consider the multifaceted nature of institutions and their historical contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced approach is essential for understanding how distributional conflicts shaped economic growth in both Europe and its colonies.

The slave trade had a direct negative impact on Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists to this day and significantly impairs economic growth, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trade is not merely historical but continues to shape contemporary African economies. The internalization of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon identify as at least twice as impactful as external factors like rule of law, is a critical mechanism through which the slave trade's effects endure (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This persistent mistrust complicates economic cooperation and inhibits the development of robust institutions. The slave trades' adverse effects on economic development are not limited to social trust but also encompass broader institutional frameworks. Nunn notes that Africa's pre-colonial political structures, such as those in the Kongo kingdom, were capable of supporting trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This implies that while some African societies had the potential for economic growth prior to colonialism, the slave trades disrupted these trajectories by depleting human capital and undermining social cohesion. While Nunn and Wantchekon's work highlights the enduring impact of mistrust on economic development, it also underscores the necessity of considering both internal and external factors in understanding Africa's economic history.

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge underpins sustained economic improvement. This technological progress, driven by epochal innovations, requires complementary institutional adjustments for efficient use and diffusion within societies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These institutions facilitate the peaceful resolution of conflicts arising from economic growth, thus ensuring the sustainability of technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology necessitates significant societal changes, including shifts in rural lifestyles and family structures, to accommodate innovations effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state plays a crucial role in managing these transitions without undermining social cohesion or political stability.

Institutions must complement technological advancements to prevent rent-seeking behaviors from eroding economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver, its benefits are maximized through supportive institutional frameworks. As societies adapt to new technologies, the role of institutions in managing growth-induced conflicts becomes evident. The sustainability of long-run economic growth relies on this harmonious interaction between technological innovation and institutional development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to manage technological advancements and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The evolution of financial systems, such as the creation of the Bank of England, exemplifies how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms facilitate market exchanges and lower transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This is evident in North's discussion of the English financial revolution, which highlights the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The development of these institutions reflects a broader trend where political stability and secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth. This finding is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the establishment of extractive states in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent income disparities, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence from colonial experiences shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not promote long-run growth. This is consistent with the argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to benefit politically powerful groups rather than fostering economic development.

This historical dynamic underscores the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
