What role do institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth? This question is central to understanding why some nations thrive while others stagnate and has sparked intense debate among economists over the past few decades. The crux of this disagreement lies between those who argue that institutions are paramount in driving economic development, and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Institutions can be broadly defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal constraints like laws and regulations, as well as informal norms and conventions. The debate hinges on whether these institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they merely complement other factors. This question is pivotal because it determines how policymakers should approach development strategies: should they focus on institutional reform, or prioritize other areas? Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions matter because they shape economic outcomes by influencing incentives and constraints (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North posits that the creation of secure property rights is crucial for long-term growth and that political institutions play a significant role in this process (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

A key area of contention involves the nature of these institutional frameworks. Some scholars emphasize the importance of "inclusive" institutions, which foster economic growth by promoting competition, secure property rights, and rule-based governance. Others argue that extractive institutions, which concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, can also persist over long periods and significantly impact current income per capita (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This debate underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and their role in economic development. For example, Acemoglu et al. discuss how European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive, depending on various factors including mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The legacy of these colonial institutions continues to influence the economic trajectories of these regions today.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This institutional evolution enabled economic growth through the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework fostered a growing proportion of transactions in relation to GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The successful evolution of these institutions reflects the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange, which is crucial for sustained economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing the rules governing economic activity. This commitment to secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms underpins long-run economic growth by creating a stable environment conducive to investment and innovation.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. This pattern contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand where Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, as evidenced by current income per capita differences among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The institutional framework established during colonialism played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. This institutional legacy continues to influence development outcomes, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. The interaction between colonial institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century was pivotal in determining the long-run development trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is further qualified by the specific policies pursued by European powers, which were influenced by population density and prosperity levels in the colonies. This interaction underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonialism have enduring effects on development pathways. The persistence of these institutional legacies implies that understanding the historical roots of current economic disparities requires a deep examination of colonial policies and their long-term consequences.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share the finding that these institutions are better understood as outcomes of power struggles rather than efficient responses to economic conditions. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutional design is the control over resources and labor, with elites often imposing institutions that maintained their privileged status. This dynamic highlights how institutional designs can perpetuate inequality by embedding the interests of dominant groups into the very fabric of economic systems. However, this perspective is qualified by the specific policies pursued by European powers in their colonies, which were influenced by population density and prosperity levels in the colonies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The interaction between distributional conflicts and colonial policies underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonialism have enduring effects on development pathways.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that understanding long-run economic growth in Africa requires a nuanced approach that considers different historical periods and development paths. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This is because colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15)(Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin illustrates this mechanism through the actions of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium despite Nunn's hypothesis. This underscores that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions and not uniformly extractive, as often portrayed. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), however, caution against overemphasizing structural impediments in African economic history. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This qualification suggests that while institutions mattered, their impact was mediated by specific historical and geographical conditions, which varied across different periods and regions. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is complex and contingent on local contexts and historical trajectories.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within African societies. This phenomenon persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust in Africa. The authors estimate that at least half of this effect is due to internal norms of mistrust, rather than external factors such as the rule of law or the perceived trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mechanism is particularly potent because it creates a self-reinforcing cycle where low levels of trust hinder economic development, which in turn further erodes social cohesion and trust. The negative impact of the slave trade on economic growth is thus not merely a matter of lost labor or capital but also of damaged social capital (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn demonstrates that the duration and intensity of Africa's slave trades far exceeded those of colonial rule, spanning nearly 500 years compared to about 75 years of official colonial administration. The prolonged nature of the slave trade allowed it to deeply embed itself in African societies, shaping their institutions and social structures in ways that persist long after the end of the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical legacy continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes through its enduring effects on trust and social capital.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1), (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. The accumulation of knowledge facilitates innovation, which in turn drives productivity gains and economic growth. This process is not merely about the creation of new technologies but also involves the diffusion and adoption of these innovations within societies. Kuznets highlights that "modern technology" requires significant adjustments in institutions and ideologies to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, ensuring that economic growth can proceed without excessive social costs. Mokyr emphasizes that while technological progress is relatively irreversible, the institutional progress necessary to sustain this growth can be more tenuous (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that although technological advancements are essential for long-term economic development, the sustainability of these gains depends on complementary institutional changes that may not always materialize. Therefore, while technological progress lays the groundwork for economic growth, the stability and continuity of this growth hinges on the ability of societies to adapt their institutions accordingly.

The continuity of long-run economic growth depends on the evolution of institutions that facilitate and sustain technological progress. Acemoglu et al. show how European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of extractive institutions in some regions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, highlights the critical role of institutional design in shaping economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This finding is reinforced by North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

The persistence of institutions over time underscores their importance in maintaining economic stability. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical processes by compressing different periods into a single narrative, which overlooks the nuanced evolution of institutions (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie's contention that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, often drive the formation of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These insights reveal that institutional continuity is not merely about maintaining efficient structures but also about addressing underlying social and political dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the interaction between colonial policies and local economic conditions. Hopkins emphasizes that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This approach aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s thesis on the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which highlights the central role of institutions in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence and empirical comparisons presented by these authors demonstrate that institutional changes introduced during colonialism significantly influenced economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that differences in European mortality rates led to varied colonization strategies, which in turn shaped the institutions of colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). This historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that the persistence of extractive institutions is a direct result of colonial policies designed to maximize rents for Europeans.
