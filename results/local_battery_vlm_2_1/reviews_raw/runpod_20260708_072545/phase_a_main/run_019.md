The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central debate in economics and economic history, with scholars arguing over the mechanisms through which institutions influence development trajectories. The dispute revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations, distributional conflicts, or exogenous cultural factors. This disagreement is critical because it determines how policymakers should approach institutional reform to foster growth: understanding the causal role of institutions can inform strategies for economic development. For instance, if institutions are driven by efficiency concerns, then reforms should focus on creating more efficient property rights and market exchanges. If distributional conflicts or cultural beliefs underpin institutions, addressing these underlying factors may be necessary for sustainable growth. The disagreement is also crucial because it affects how we interpret historical economic development: the extent to which institutional changes can explain long-term economic outcomes versus other factors such as geography or culture.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction was a critical factor in fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. These changes were part of a broader institutional evolution that included political factors underpinning economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the performance of capital markets. This suggests that the regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing property rights and market exchanges was crucial for sustained economic development. North (1989, p.9) qualifies this by noting that while transaction costs per transaction were falling, the aggregate volume of exchanges was increasing due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This implies that as economies grow more complex, the role of institutions in managing these complexities becomes even more critical.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutional frameworks in societies with high inequality have historically favored elites. In Latin America, this dynamic led to policies that restricted competition and perpetuated disparities by limiting the development of institutions that could foster broad-based economic growth. The legacy of slavery in Africa similarly shaped institutions in a way that hindered economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). High levels of inequality in these societies meant that elites had both the incentive and the power to shape institutions to their advantage, often at the expense of broader societal progress.

The specific mechanism through which this occurred was the control over political power by elites. This control allowed them to enact policies that maintained their dominance while stifling competition from other social groups. In Latin America, for example, the concentration of wealth and political power among a small elite led to institutions that protected their interests and did not promote inclusive economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). A similar dynamic played out in Africa, where the slave trade depleted human capital and created a culture of mistrust that persisted long after the abolition of slavery. This mistrust further eroded the social capital necessary for robust economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

The downstream implication of these institutional frameworks is clear: they perpetuate inequality by limiting opportunities for broader segments of society to participate in economic activities. This pattern is evident in both Latin America and Africa, where historical inequalities have left lasting imprints on economic structures. Nunn qualifies this by noting that the impact of slavery on African development is attributable to both mistrust and the direct depletion of human capital through the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This qualification underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional impacts on long-run economic growth, suggesting that while mistrust is a significant factor, it is part of a broader set of issues that include direct economic losses and the erosion of social cohesion.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that the type of colonial institution established was heavily dependent on the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in these regions, which led to vastly different institutional outcomes. In areas where European settlers faced high mortality risks, such as tropical Africa and parts of Asia, extractive institutions were implemented. These institutions were designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the colonizing power and did not foster local economic development. In regions with lower mortality rates, such as North America and Australasia, settler colonies were established with institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as extractive institutions hindered local development while more inclusive ones facilitated it. The downstream implication of this mechanism is that the legacy of colonial institutions persists, shaping contemporary economic outcomes and development trajectories. It is important to note that while these institutional legacies are significant, they do not operate in isolation. Other factors, such as post-colonial policies and global economic conditions, also play crucial roles in determining a country's long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) highlight how British colonial rule in Africa shaped educational development through the introduction of new social and political institutions, altering African perceptions of formal education's benefits. Missionary schools played a pivotal role in this process. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) and van Waijenburg (2012) point out how the timing and scale of growth in export production varied between countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This process varied across Africa. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) indicate that despite these variations, many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that while colonial educational policies had profound effects on economic development, their impact varied significantly based on local conditions and institutional frameworks.

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements are pivotal for sustained economic development. According to Mokyr, the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels economic growth and necessitates institutional adjustments to facilitate its efficient use. This perspective is supported by Kuznets's observation that technological progress requires complementary institutional changes to resolve conflicts arising from modernization (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Specifically, Kuznets highlights that the introduction of modern technology demands significant shifts in societal structures, such as reducing large family patterns and altering rural modes of life. Moreover, technological advancements often lead to coordination failures, which require institutional solutions to ensure sustained economic development. Mokyr also acknowledges that technological progress is more monotonic than institutional progress but still subject to erosion by rent-seeking activities. This suggests that even though technology drives economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on effective institutions that minimize rent-seeking behavior and ensure efficient resource allocation (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, remains the primary driver of long-run economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role in facilitating this process.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a more nuanced explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They point out that the adoption of growth-enhancing institutions results from both efficiency considerations and political economy dynamics and power struggles within societies. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, highlighting that institutional changes were often influenced by indigenous interests rather than exogenous factors (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that understanding the historical context of distributional conflicts is crucial for comprehending the evolution of institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this perspective by emphasizing the need to integrate institutions into long-term research programs and avoid treating them as mere fashion accessories in economic history (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that while distributional conflicts are significant, a comprehensive understanding of institutional change requires a nuanced approach that considers both power dynamics and long-term structural factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), while Acemoglu et al show that secure property rights are critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political institutions governing public choice, such as those in seventeenth-century England, were crucial for fostering secure property rights. North and Weingast demonstrate the success of these institutions through evidence from capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), showing that credible commitment underpins growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical context of distributional conflicts is also significant. Acemoglu et al. illustrate how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, European settlers adopted contrasting approaches in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates. Austin qualifies this by emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation. He shows how colonial governments often responded to indigenous economic and political action, leading to changes in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the need for a nuanced approach that considers both power dynamics and long-term structural factors.

The debate over whether institutions are shaped by efficiency or distributional conflicts remains central. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth is evident in the historical evidence from capital markets and colonization strategies. The creation of financial instruments and secure property rights in England fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates illustrate how institutional design can perpetuate inequality and hinder development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Comparative analysis of these historical cases shows that the interaction between institutions and long-term development is critical.
