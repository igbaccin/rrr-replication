The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that lies at the heart of understanding why some societies prosper while others stagnate. Institutions are the rules and structures that govern human interaction, including those governing property rights, law enforcement, political systems, and social norms. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns or by distributional conflicts. One position argues that efficient institutions emerge from rational choices aimed at maximizing economic growth; another posits that these institutions are often the result of power struggles among different groups in society and do not necessarily prioritize efficiency.

The stakes are high: identifying the underlying mechanisms that drive institutional development can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. A clear understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is essential for designing effective policies that promote prosperity. The question remains whether institutions are shaped primarily by a rational pursuit of efficiency or by the complex interplay of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutional evolution governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional shift facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by creating a more secure environment for investment and exchange. The political institutions that emerged during this period were instrumental in specifying efficient property rights and ensuring their effective enforcement, which in turn reduced transaction costs and fostered market exchanges.

The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, where England's performance stood out (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast observe that institutional changes were crucial; they interacted with broader economic and political factors to drive growth (Author Year, p.N). This perspective challenges simplistic views that attribute economic development solely to institutional reforms by highlighting the interconnected nature of political and economic systems.

North (1989, p.9) further elaborates on this dynamic by illustrating how the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights led to a growing proportion of transactions in relation to GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied aggregate exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This suggests that the institutional framework reduced transaction costs and expanded the overall volume of economic activity. North's argument is nuanced by the recognition that these institutions were critical for growth; they operated within a broader context of evolving political and economic conditions.

The interplay between secure property rights and institutional enforcement underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic growth. The English experience illustrates how institutional changes can create a stable environment conducive to investment and market development, but also how these changes must be understood in relation to broader historical and political contexts. This dynamic highlights the enduring importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories over the long term.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The distribution of wealth and power influenced the development of institutions like serfdom and guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions were shaped by the interests of those in power who used them to maintain control over resources. These findings suggest that institutional development was often driven by the desire to protect existing power structures; efficiency considerations alone did not drive it. The implication is that understanding long-run economic growth requires considering how power dynamics influence institutional design, even when such designs may not be optimal for overall economic performance. This dynamic underscores the complexity of institutional change over time, as distributional conflicts continually reshape institutions in ways that reflect underlying power structures.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) explains that the new economic history of Africa focuses on how different institutional frameworks affected economic outcomes, emphasizing that institutional quality is crucial for providing incentives for investment. This focus aligns with Akyeampong and Fofack's (2014, p.15) analysis of gendered land access under colonial rule, which shows that customary law codified by colonial powers significantly altered economic participation (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The marginalization of women from productive lands illustrates how institutional changes can reshape economic activities. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this pattern by noting that the impact of colonial rule varied across different regions and policies (Frankema 2012, p.15). This variability suggests that the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced long-run economic growth are complex and context-dependent. Understanding these nuances is essential for grasping how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder sustained economic development in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa have had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development. This impact is mediated through the erosion of trust within African societies. The slave trade created a pervasive culture of mistrust, which persists today and significantly impedes economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.19)  further illustrates this pattern by highlighting how the absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets hindered sustained trade in many African regions. This erosion of trust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the very social capital necessary for productive economic activities. Nunn also notes that the duration of slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period much longer than the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This temporal dimension underscores the deep and enduring impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic trajectory. The legacy of mistrust, compounded by the lack of institutional frameworks that support trade, continues to shape African economies today.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly emphasise that technological advancements are fundamental to long-term economic development. This perspective posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge leads to technological progress, which in turn drives economic growth. The mechanism through which this occurs is the reduction of access costs to new ideas, enabling widespread adoption and application of innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by noting that technological advancements are crucial; they must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability. This is because technological progress alone can lead to coordination failures and rent-seeking behavior, which may erode the benefits of economic improvements over time (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The implication is that while knowledge and technology are primary drivers, institutions play a vital role in managing the social and political conflicts arising from these advancements, thereby sustaining long-term growth. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is essential for achieving enduring economic development.

Acemoglu et al. argue that these extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This institutional framework persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita levels in former colonies, demonstrating the long-term impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin qualifies this by noting that institutions are shaped by political power dynamics; thus, even when extractive institutions were present, local actions could influence their evolution. For instance, colonial administrations might alter institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political initiatives (Austin 2008, p.15) ). The relationship between institutions and growth is further complicated by the role of shared beliefs and cultural factors. North argues that secure property rights are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions; efficiency considerations are not always the primary factor. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize long-term growth, suggesting instead that they may reflect power dynamics and shared beliefs within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by North's argument that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise impact of institutions on growth remains a subject of debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Hopkins shows that Africa's dismal economic record is linked to the presence of poor institutions, which can be addressed through informed institutional reforms (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
