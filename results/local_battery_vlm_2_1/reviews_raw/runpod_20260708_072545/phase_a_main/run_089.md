The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that has sparked debate among economists and historians alike. At stake intellectually is the question of whether institutions are fundamental drivers of economic development or merely secondary factors influenced by other determinants such as geography, culture, or technological progress. The live disagreement centers on two main positions: one argues that institutions, particularly those governing property rights and political stability, are crucial for sustained economic growth; the other posits that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper underlying factors, such as geographical endowments or cultural beliefs, which ultimately determine economic outcomes.

The debate is not merely academic. Understanding the causal mechanisms behind long-run economic growth has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies. If institutions are indeed the primary drivers, then efforts to foster economic growth should focus on institutional reforms; if other factors are more influential, then policies might need to address those underlying determinants instead. This intellectual tension is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9), who both emphasize the importance of institutions but offer different perspectives on how these institutions come into being and evolve.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that political institutions play a crucial role in defining efficient property rights and ensuring effective enforcement, both of which are vital for economic growth. These perspectives converge on the importance of institutions but diverge on the mechanisms through which they influence long-run economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al.'s formulations. Austin suggests that European settler colonies benefited from institutional legacies that fostered economic growth, while non-settler colonies faced extractive institutions that hindered development.

This tension highlights the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic development. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for devising effective policies that can promote sustained economic growth.

North argues that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth through their impact on transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 exemplifies how institutional changes can dramatically reduce transaction costs. North and Weingast further illustrate this point with evidence from capital markets, showing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions in England highlights the importance of political factors that underpin economic growth. North and Weingast acknowledge that France's pre-revolutionary economic performance was comparable to Britain's, suggesting that institutional differences alone may not fully account for economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This implies that while institutions are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political commitment also play significant roles in shaping long-run economic growth.

This simplification obscures the complexity of institutional change over time. Austin and Nunn and Wantchekon share this claim (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade, for instance, is one such factor that has been shown to shape cultural attitudes towards trust within Africa, which in turn affects economic development. This historical legacy of the slave trade underscores how deeply rooted cultural factors can influence long-run economic outcomes. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on how well they align with local conditions and incentives. Nunn and Wantchekon's work complements this by showing that the slave trade's impact on trust is multifaceted, affecting both external environments and internal norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Austin observes that colonial administrations were not uniform; their actions depended on fiscal security and local incentives. This variability in institutional responses indicates that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth can be contingent on specific historical and cultural contexts. Austin's analysis suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis may overlook these nuances, leading to an oversimplified view of how institutions influence long-run economic growth.

This variability underscores the importance of considering both institutional frameworks and their implementation in understanding economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner's work (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) on historical economic performance in Africa reveals that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels surpassing subsistence. This finding challenges the notion of persistent poverty in Africa before 1950. Frankema and van Waijenburg corroborate this perspective (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), arguing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, as recent literature often emphasizes. This availability allows for more detailed comparisons with other regions, revealing that African economies exhibited growth patterns closely linked to foreign trade and export markets. These variations in growth were influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner's analysis shows that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly both between and within countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that the absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves key questions about long-term economic development unanswered. This highlights a need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the historical dynamics of African economic growth.

This evidence suggests that the variability in institutional responses and economic performance across Africa underscores the importance of local contextual factors in shaping long-run economic growth, which can be missed by broad generalizations.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that promoted investment and economic growth or extractive ones designed for rapid resource extraction. This variation in institutional design had profound implications for long-run economic development. This institutional framework shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, with those subject to extractive institutions experiencing slower development and lower income levels. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document how the interaction between colonial institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions. The institutional changes brought by colonialism were not consistent across all colonies; local conditions and the nature of colonial rule influenced how these institutions affected economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al.'s work underscores that the legacy of colonial institutions persists in shaping current economic performance. The extractive nature of many colonial institutions has left a lasting impact, hindering economic growth and development in former colonies. This institutional framework, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, continues to influence economic outcomes today. Acemoglu et al.'s findings highlight the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development in shaping current economic performance.

Hence, it is crucial to adapt institutions in response to technological advancements to maintain long-term economic growth. Kuznets elaborates on this by emphasizing that modern technology requires significant societal changes and institutional adaptations for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial, as institutions must evolve to support new technologies and manage the conflicts they introduce.

Mokyr further emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is essential for economic improvement. He suggests that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements might be limited or even reversed by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets supports this view, noting that resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully is vital for maintaining a strong political fabric within society and sustaining long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are necessary to harness the benefits of technological progress, they are not the primary cause but rather a facilitating factor.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. The mechanisms by which these institutions functioned can be traced through their impact on wealth distribution: in New World societies, for example, initial conditions led to extreme inequality in wealth and political power, shaping institutions that reinforced this disparity. This pattern is evident in the role of government policies that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie's analysis enhances this understanding by showing how these institutional structures were shaped by underlying conflicts over resource distribution (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of such institutions in pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and craft guilds, reflects the broader historical reality that distributional struggles often take precedence over efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency goals; instead, they are deeply rooted in the social dynamics of wealth and power distribution.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflects increased security of property rights, which are critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. Austin highlights the importance of secure private property rights as emphasized by Douglass North's institutionalist approach, reinforcing the idea that institutions facilitating market exchanges drive economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). The political delivery of secure private property rights underpins long-term economic growth.

These insights are further supported by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that credible commitments to enforce property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They demonstrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in securing these rights. The success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic transactions and growth.

The role of institutions goes beyond considerations of mere efficiency. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often take precedence over efficiency goals in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For example, European colonists established extractive states in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The historical evidence from former European colonies settles the disagreement over whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency goals or distributional conflicts. The reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of income per capita, which shows how institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.
