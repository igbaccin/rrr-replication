The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in understanding development trajectories across nations. At stake is whether the institutional frameworks that govern societies exert a causal influence on their prosperity over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around two main positions. One camp asserts that institutions designed to enforce property rights and rule of law are fundamental drivers of economic growth, while another posits that factors like geography or cultural beliefs play more significant roles in shaping long-term economic outcomes. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), while Ogilvie challenges the notion that efficiency drives core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The interplay between these perspectives is crucial for comprehending why some countries thrive economically over centuries, while others stagnate or decline.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the direct causal role of institutions in fostering long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. provide empirical evidence, showing that differences in European colonization strategies resulted in varied institutional frameworks, which significantly influenced current income per capita levels across former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In particular, extractive institutions were established where Europeans faced high mortality rates, leading to long-term economic underdevelopment. The findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. also highlight that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North's assertion that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

Another stream of scholarship offers a competing explanation, emphasizing the role of geography and cultural beliefs in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This view suggests that institutional frameworks are not the sole determinants of economic growth; instead, they interact with geographic factors and local conditions to produce varied outcomes. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations, instead highlighting the role of power dynamics and cultural beliefs. Austin's work underscores the complexity of institutional evolution, noting that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The interplay between these streams reveals a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. While some scholars emphasize the direct causal role of institutions in fostering prosperity, others highlight the importance of geographic factors and cultural beliefs. These perspectives are not mutually exclusive; rather, they offer complementary insights into the complex dynamics of economic development. The enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes underscores the significance of historical institutional frameworks.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the persistence of these institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. identify that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes, highlighting the importance of political power dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex. This nuanced understanding reveals that while colonial institutions significantly influenced current income per capita, their impact was mediated by complex political and economic dynamics.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the emergence of specific institutions can significantly lower transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional innovation was not merely an economic development but also a political one, emerging from the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War and reflecting a trade-off of certain rights to Parliament for revenues. This dynamic underscores how institutional changes can drive economic growth by reducing friction in markets. This suggests that the institutional changes in England, though significant, did not single-handedly determine economic growth but rather interacted with other factors to produce observed outcomes. The nuanced view is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, but their effects are contingent on broader political and economic contexts.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are central to understanding core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They propose that these conflicts shape institutional structures in ways that prioritize the interests of elites over efficiency. In pre-industrial Europe, institutions such as serfdom and guilds were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by power dynamics between different social groups. These institutions often served to maintain existing hierarchies rather than optimize productivity. This perspective is echoed in the context of New World societies by Sokoloff and Engerman, who show that institutions in societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of these inequalities over time highlights how institutional arrangements can be shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely economic efficiency. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, their impact on institutional development is mediated through broader historical and political contexts.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) posits that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is pivotal in economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights how innovations can transform economies, but it also acknowledges the role of institutions in facilitating these changes. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this point, illustrating that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments for efficient use and absorption by society (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial; without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be limited or even reversed. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes that coordination failures can impede economic improvement, underscoring the necessity of robust institutions to sustain long-term growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that the sovereign state plays a critical role in resolving conflicts arising from technological changes, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible without excessive costs or political instability. This suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth, it requires supportive institutional frameworks to fully realize its potential.

Historical factors, including colonial policies and the slave trade, have significantly influenced African economic development by shaping institutions and social norms (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that European settler presence and colonial administrations' fiscal security needs drove indigenous commodity production and institutional changes. This historical context shaped local trust levels, a critical factor in economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, which accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalized mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it can hinder cooperation and collective action necessary for growth. Thus, the legacy of colonialism in Africa is not merely a static constraint but an evolving dynamic that continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African economic history is more complex than conventional views suggest, with higher per capita incomes and real wages in the early twentieth century than previously thought. This re-evaluation highlights that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by this period, challenging the notion of persistent poverty as a historical constant. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability indicates that economic performance was influenced by specific conditions and policies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) support this view by demonstrating that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that the region's economic development was more dynamic than often portrayed. This gap underscores the need for further research to fully understand the historical determinants of Africa's economic performance and how they relate to contemporary development challenges.
