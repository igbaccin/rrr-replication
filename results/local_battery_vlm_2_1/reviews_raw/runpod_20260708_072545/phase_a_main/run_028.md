The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. Institutions are understood as the rules of the game that structure political and social interactions. At stake intellectually is whether institutional frameworks can be designed to promote sustained economic growth. Two positions dominate this debate: one argues that institutions directly influence economic performance, while another posits that institutions' effects on growth depend on their interaction with other factors such as geography or culture. The latter position holds that the impact of institutions is contingent and cannot be isolated from these other variables.

The causal role of institutions in long-run economic growth can be understood through the lens of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers implemented different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional arrangements on economic development. This perspective is complemented by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth and development because they provide secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The interaction between institutions and economic outcomes is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by Europeans in their colonies. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward; it involves complex interactions with other factors. North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs per transaction, but a growing proportion of GNP will be dedicated to the transactions sector as specialization and division of labor increase (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic underscores how institutional frameworks can influence economic growth by reducing barriers to market exchanges. Austin's work on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests that the impact of institutions is contingent on historical periods and paths, emphasizing that different institutional prescriptions were adopted in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective highlights how institutional changes can be shaped by local contexts, further complicating the relationship between institutions and economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that seventeenth-century England's institutional evolution fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of institutions such as the Bank of England in 1694, along with new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of long-term property rights. This reduction was pivotal for fostering a robust capital market and stimulating economic growth. The resultant increase in specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges, thereby expanding the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP. This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can facilitate market exchanges by reducing barriers, thus promoting economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism led to significant institutional changes that have enduring effects on current income per capita. The nature of these institutions varied widely depending on European mortality rates in the colonies. Where mortality was high, Europeans established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, rather than fostering local economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the institutional framework was tailored to maximize colonial rents rather than promoting long-term growth. Colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) argue that these institutional differences are a primary driver of current disparities in income per capita among former colonies. In particular, extractive institutions have persisted, locking many former colonies into poverty traps. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is related to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. For example, former colonies that had the chance to industrialize during this period were able to leverage their institutions more effectively for growth.

Distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key factor in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic structures of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. These scholars demonstrate that institutional arrangements are often the result of power struggles among different social groups rather than purely efficient responses to economic conditions. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that institutions like serfdom in pre-industrial Europe were maintained because they served the interests of powerful elites who could impose their will on society (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to New World societies, where greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic suggests that economic institutions in many historical contexts were shaped by distributional conflicts that favored certain groups at the expense of others.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) both show that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a positive impact on the development of formal education. This educational infrastructure helped elevate levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century in many Anglophone African economies. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that export production was tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports, which varied across countries due to factors like local endowments and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while British colonial rule generally promoted education, its specific impacts on economic development were mediated by local conditions.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Hopkins 2009, p.18)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) argue that African economic history reveals the critical role of institutions in shaping growth. This scholarship shows that colonial rule significantly impacted institutional development by codifying customary laws that often marginalized certain groups, such as women. For instance, under colonial rule, women were frequently relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor was reinforced through the legal system, where European courts operated alongside native courts governed by customary law. The codification of these laws under colonial rule entrenched patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to productive resources and economic opportunities. This institutional framework not only constrained economic growth but also perpetuated inequalities that persisted long after colonial rule ended. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that the invention of custom under colonial rule further solidified these patriarchal alliances, creating a legal framework that disadvantaged women. Despite these challenges, African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting their resilience and adaptability in the face of institutional barriers. This underscores the complex interplay between institutions, gender dynamics, and economic development in Africa.

The slave trade's legacy of mistrust remains a significant barrier to economic development in Africa, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust is not only interpersonal but also institutional, eroding the social capital necessary for collective action and economic growth. The internalisation of this mistrust is at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn argues that the long duration of slave trades in Africa, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had a profound and lasting impact on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19), shaping institutions and social norms in ways that persist today. This deep-seated mistrust hinders the formation of effective economic institutions by undermining cooperation and collective action. This suggests that while mistrust is a significant barrier, the presence of strong institutions can mitigate its effects.

Technological advancements are a potent driver of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both argue that the efficient use of innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. These adjustments facilitate the integration of new technologies into existing socio-economic frameworks, enabling sustained growth. For example, the development of modern technology necessitates changes in rural lifestyles, family structures, and societal attitudes towards nature (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Similarly, Mokyr highlights that coordination failures can hinder technological progress, underscoring the need for institutional reforms to support economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The path from technological innovation to sustained growth is not straightforward. Kuznets points out that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved without excessive costs or long-term political weakening of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, their benefits can only be fully realized through effective institutional management of associated social and political challenges. The sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that the societal fabric remains intact during periods of rapid economic change (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views on African economic history by arguing that poverty is neither structurally persistent nor solely the result of historical impediments. They posit that African poverty has been a recent phenomenon, driven by factors other than structural impediments such as colonial institutions. One specific mechanism in this cluster involves the role of indigenous commodity production and colonial government incentives. This adaptability suggests that colonial institutions were not rigidly imposed but rather evolved in response to local economic dynamics, contrary to the notion of static, growth-inhibiting structures. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further support this view by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies, showing variability over time (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This finding challenges the idea that poverty in Africa is a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), in their work on economic performance metrics, demonstrate the importance of integrating multiple factors beyond institutions. The skill premium is a critical measure of long-term economic performance, reflecting the remuneration for investing in human capital and the costs associated with training skilled workers (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This metric provides insights into the efficiency of economic systems by highlighting how investment in skills translates into higher wages over time. However, institutions that facilitate skill development are not always beneficial; they can also create barriers to entry and form local cartels, which may counteract their positive effects on intergenerational skill transfer (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dual nature of institutions underscores the complexity of their role in economic growth, suggesting that while they can foster human capital investment, they must be carefully managed to avoid negative consequences. The collaborative research efforts highlighted by Bolt and van Zanden emphasize the need for continuous improvement in measuring long-term economic performance (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). Their work shows how Maddison's pioneering approach has inspired scholars to refine our understanding of global economic development, but it also reveals the subjective nature of historical data and the margins of error involved. This underscores the importance of ongoing research and collaboration in achieving more accurate measurements of economic growth over time.

Broadberry and Gupta emphasize that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, focusing on the geographical patterns of economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). They argue that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This highlights the crucial role of the service sector in early modern economic growth, paralleling its importance in the modern period. Van Zanden cautions against assuming a direct link between economic development and improvements in living standards during this era. The lack of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 underscores the complexity of early modern economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1; van Zanden 1999, p.9). Broadberry and Gupta further note that the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in a warm climate reinforced this effect. The focus of economic development shifted to the countries bordering the North Sea, confirming that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs.

The Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Pamuk 2007, p.16). Pamuk's analysis demonstrates that institutional flexibility was pivotal in shaping these regional disparities. In particular, the political authorities' ability to adapt to new economic opportunities played a crucial role. For example, Holland's weak monopolies allowed for rapid urban growth and the flourishing of rural manufacturing. This institutional flexibility enabled regions like Holland to transform into advanced commercial hubs (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This process was not uniform across Europe; in Tuscany, efforts to strengthen existing monopolies stifled economic dynamism and hindered urban development. The divergence in economic trajectories between northern and southern Europe can be attributed to differences in institutional responses to the Black Death's demographic shocks. Regions with rigid monopolies experienced stagnation and a decline in urban rankings. The impact of these institutional differences was profound; by 1450, the wage gap between the northwest and other parts of Europe had widened significantly due to these long-term structural changes.

Peters (2004, p.19) and Stern (2004, p.1; 2004, p.10) show that competition, conflict, and environmental factors can significantly influence socio-economic processes. In sub-Saharan Africa, Peters shows how pervasive competition and conflict over land reveal exclusionary processes and deepening social divisions. These dynamics are influenced by perceptions of 'custom' as static, which can advantage groups seeking to evade official scrutiny while also fuelling agricultural development initiatives (Peters 2004, p.5). This interplay between competition for resources and institutional responses is critical in shaping socio-economic outcomes. The environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) hypothesis suggests that economic growth could eventually lead to environmental improvement. Dasgupta et al.'s critical review highlights various viewpoints on the emissions-income relation, underscoring the complexity of achieving sustainability without significant deviations from current practices (Stern 2004, p.10). This points to a nuanced understanding of how environmental factors can both drive and constrain economic growth. The interplay between competition, conflict, and environmental impacts reveals that while these factors shape socio-economic processes, their effects are mediated by institutional responses and the pursuit of sustainable development paths.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that influence economic actors' decisions. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms critical for market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). These findings converge on the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic development. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term economic growth.

The historical context of colonialism highlights how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes, which in turn influenced economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. note that European settlers adopted different approaches, ranging from setting up extractive states to establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional development is further illustrated by Austin's analysis, which shows how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The interplay between these historical factors and institutional responses underscores the complexity of economic growth pathways.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, shaping the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). North's work on the English financial revolution provides a historical example of how institutional changes can lead to significant economic transformations. The development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts within societies. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is complemented by Hopkins' emphasis on the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Englebert's findings show that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality; instead, societies can be either homogeneous or heterogeneous and still develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints on economic actors' decisions. There remains a disagreement over whether distributional conflicts or efficiency considerations primarily drive institutional development.

The specific historical evidence of colonial policies and their impact on income per capita settles this disagreement. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison highlights the enduring effects of institutional frameworks shaped by colonial policies.
