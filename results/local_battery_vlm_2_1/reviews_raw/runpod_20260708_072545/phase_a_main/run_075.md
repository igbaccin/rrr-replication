The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development studies. At stake intellectually is understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained economic prosperity over generations. Two key positions emerge in this debate. One position emphasises the causal primacy of institutions themselves as drivers of economic growth, arguing that certain institutional frameworks foster investment and productivity gains. The other position highlights the role of exogenous factors such as geography or cultural beliefs as antecedents to these institutions, suggesting that institutions are merely epiphenomena of deeper underlying causes. These positions conflict over whether institutions directly cause long-term economic outcomes or if they are secondary effects of other forces. Institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

The development of financial institutions and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). For instance, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a pivotal moment in this process. The evolution of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for fostering economic growth by facilitating market exchanges. North (1989, p.9) argues that this institutional development leads to a growing proportion of GNP being allocated to the transactions sector, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). They argue that the regime’s commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange is critical for long-term growth, as evidenced by capital market developments (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective does not account for all possible variations in economic performance across countries with similar institutions; North and Weingast (1989, p.29) acknowledge that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not hinder French economic growth. This suggests that while financial institutions play a pivotal role, other factors such as bureaucratic efficiency may also influence long-term economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The nature of these institutions varied widely depending on European mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in areas with low mortality, like the United States and New Zealand, settlers created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development, as evidenced by the persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document how population density and prosperity influenced European policies, leading to different institutional outcomes. In densely populated areas with high mortality rates, Europeans were more likely to establish extractive institutions that maximized their short-term gains. This pattern had profound implications for industrialization, as colonies with institutions conducive to investment saw greater economic development during the nineteenth century. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions might be extractive even if they do not promote economic development, highlighting the complex interplay between political power and institutional design. This suggests that the long-term impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is a function of their initial design as well as the evolving dynamics of power and resource extraction.

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further complicated by the fact that they are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term gains over long-run development. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how institutional changes introduced by colonialism continue to influence contemporary economies.

This influence is evident in the historical contexts that shaped institutional frameworks, which Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue have long-term economic implications. They posit that the varying degrees of inequality in New World societies were influenced by different factor endowments at colonization; these differences persisted over time, impacting institutions and development paths. Nunn elaborates on this mechanism through the lens of Africa's slave trades. He argues that the four slave trades spanning nearly 500 years significantly affected subsequent economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights how specific historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories. The impact of slavery on institutions is profound: it altered power dynamics and resource allocation in ways that hindered long-run growth. The influence of slave trades extends beyond mere economic factors; Nunn notes that sustained trade required centralized governance and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the institutional legacies of historical inequalities are complex, encompassing not just economic structures but also political and social frameworks. The enduring effects of these institutions on contemporary economies underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical contexts shape long-term development trajectories.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) reevaluated historical economic performance in Africa and Asia, finding that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels as early as the twentieth century, a period marked by significant institutional changes. These findings suggest a more complex picture of African economic history than previous characterizations of the region's economic stagnation. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share this perspective by challenging the notion that African poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They argue for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which can help assess future growth possibilities and the impact of specific historical or geographical conditions on long-term welfare development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) identify that patterns of economic growth in African countries were often tied to foreign trade and export markets. This suggests that institutions facilitating trade and market integration played a significant role in shaping long-run economic performance, even in the colonial era. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves critical questions about poverty persistence unanswered. This highlights the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the historical determinants of economic development in Africa and Asia.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie highlights the role of core institutions like serfdom and guilds, which were structured to manage disputes over resource distribution among different social groups. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions evolved primarily to enhance economic efficiency. Austin complements this by noting that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic actions, suggesting that policy outcomes reflected a balance of power rather than simple efficiency calculations. Colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged subjects to feel vested in maintaining stability (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior underscores the importance of political incentives in shaping institutions. Austin also notes that these adaptations were not uniform; Governor-General Clifford's decision for higher production equilibrium contradicts Nunn’s hypothesis, indicating variability in institutional responses across different contexts. The implication is that colonial economic policies were influenced by both local conditions and broader political strategies, reflecting a complex interplay of power dynamics rather than straightforward efficiency maximization. This nuanced view suggests that the study of institutions must consider multiple factors beyond mere economic efficiency to fully understand their impact on growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both agree that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. This stream proposes that the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is key to resolving conflicts induced by growth. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that the source of technological progress and the pace at which it advances differed over centuries and among regions, indicating a complex interplay between technology and institutions. Institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The growth of useful knowledge is not a straightforward process but requires complementary institutional developments to ensure long-term sustainability.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological progress and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than purely geographic factors. These institutions shaped the long-term development paths of these regions by influencing their ability to industrialize and adopt new technologies. North (1989, p.6) similarly emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. The creation of stable institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, which were critical for long-term capital markets and economic development.

The interaction between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial for sustained economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth. This is evident in the political factors governing how rules are enforced and changed, which are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that institutions that were apparently favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. This variability underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, showing that the adoption of efficient institutions requires specific historical and political conditions.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England provides a clear example of how institutional changes can foster economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of these institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations, but they must complement technological advancements to ensure sustained growth (Austin 2008, p.15). The reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, showing that the adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth is contingent on specific historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than long-run growth, which highlights the need for institutions that promote efficient use of innovations and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the debate on whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The measurement type used in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) shows that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than long-run growth. This is supported by the evidence from North and Weingast (1989, p.2), which highlights the importance of credible commitment underpinning growth.
