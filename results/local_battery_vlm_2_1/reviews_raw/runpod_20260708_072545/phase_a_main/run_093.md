The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake are the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty over centuries. One live disagreement concerns whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous forces like political decisions. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can persist and significantly affect long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Some scholars argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies results from institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). The debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on economic trajectories, which requires tracing both the historical roots and contemporary manifestations of these institutions.

The historical legacy of extractive institutions continues to influence current economic disparities, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). This effect is particularly pronounced when Europeans established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole and invest in local development. This institutional legacy persists and has profound implications for contemporary economic trajectories.

The mechanism through which these extractive institutions operate involves the establishment of political and economic systems that prioritize the extraction of resources over investment in local infrastructure and human capital. This suggests that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions. This institutional framework ensures that politically powerful groups maintain their control, often at the cost of broader economic development.

The downstream implication of this institutional legacy is a persistent gap in income per capita between regions with extractive institutions and those with more inclusive ones. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, show similar results to those reported in their study. This indicates that the quality of institutions matters significantly for economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions suggests that historical legacies can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes, shaping contemporary development trajectories in profound ways.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both historical factors and contemporary policies.

North and Weingast's work on seventeenth-century England highlights that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal for fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies this dynamic. This development led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. Moreover, North and Weingast emphasize that the institutional changes in England were not merely about the rules governing economic exchange but also about the institutions enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This enforcement mechanism is crucial because it ensures that the regime or sovereign remains committed to these rules. This commitment fosters an environment where economic growth can thrive without hindrance from bureaucratic structures. North's analysis of transaction costs in a growing economy further elucidates this point (North 1989, p.6). As political institutions specify and enforce efficient property rights, the development of economic institutions facilitates market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction. This dynamic is essential because it allows for increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. The institutional evolution in England secured property rights and laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth by creating a robust framework for market exchanges.

Building on this foundation, Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) both emphasize that distributional conflicts and government policies are pivotal in shaping economic institutions. They jointly establish that inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power can drive the evolution of institutions in ways that advantage elites. This dynamic is evident in New World societies where extreme inequality led to institutional arrangements that restricted competition. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) provide an example: differentials in factor endowments across colonies resulted in varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved.

Ogilvie (2007, p.3) further explains this process by advocating for a more nuanced approach to understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes. She suggests that distributional conflicts often influence institutional development, challenging the notion that efficiency alone drives these processes. This view is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies. They show that initial conditions of inequality led to government policies that restricted competition. The resulting institutional frameworks often prioritized the interests of elites over broader economic efficiency, leading to persistent inequality and limited economic growth.

Austin and Broadberry argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical economic development in Africa by compressing different periods into one narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Colonial policies played a critical role in shaping long-term economic outcomes, not inherent institutional factors. The argument hinges on the observation that in areas where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, indigenous commodity production was often encouraged by colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). In these regions, colonial administrations prioritized fiscal security and responded to local incentives, altering their institutional prescriptions accordingly. This dynamic highlights how colonial policies could adapt to local conditions and be uniformly detrimental. Broadberry and Gardner point out that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies was more nuanced than previously thought, with many regions achieving levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This pattern underscores the complexity of colonial influences on economic development, suggesting that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions all played significant roles. The variability in export production tied to foreign trade illustrates how multiple factors interacted to shape economic growth trajectories in Africa.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) shared finding that women's roles in African economic development have been significantly influenced by colonial institutions. Women's economic contributions were often marginalized under colonial rule, with customary law frequently codifying their subordinate status. This process was reinforced through the dual legal system imposed by colonial powers, which privileged men in access to land and resources crucial for cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The impact of these institutional changes extended beyond gender dynamics, affecting broader economic structures. For instance, the British influence on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa highlights how different colonial powers varied in their effects on African development. British rule fostered a more conducive environment for formal education compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). This variation suggests that the nature of colonial institutions played a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories, with some colonial legacies being more beneficial than others. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the quality of public goods provision is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but rather by the effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Thus, while colonial institutions undeniably shaped African economic development, their impact was mediated by local institutional dynamics and governance capabilities.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a profound and lasting impact on the continent's economic development, creating a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn highlights that this prolonged period of exploitation spanned nearly five centuries. The slave trade's enduring legacy includes economic exploitation and institutional degradation. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large a factor in perpetuating this legacy than external environmental factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. This mistrust, deeply ingrained over centuries, has significantly hindered Africa's economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation. Nunn argues that the slave trades had adverse effects on economic development beyond those attributed to colonialism or more recent events. The cultural legacy of the slave trade, which includes a pervasive mistrust of others, continues to shape African societies today. This mistrust is so deeply rooted that it affects interpersonal relationships and broader social and economic interactions. This culture of mistrust persists despite significant changes in governance and economic policies, highlighting its enduring impact on Africa's development trajectory.

This perspective emphasizes the importance of advancing knowledge in fostering long-term economic development. Technological innovations introduce new production methods and sectors, altering the economic landscape over centuries and across regions. The institutional adjustments required to efficiently use these innovations are crucial for sustained growth. Kuznets highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Therefore, societies must adapt their institutions and ideologies to accommodate technological changes. This adaptation process can generate conflicts within society, but resolving them peacefully is essential for achieving economic growth. The sovereign state plays a pivotal role in managing these growth-induced conflicts without damaging the societal fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates that coordination failures can impede economic progress if not addressed through institutional changes (Mokyr 2005, p.54). These adjustments ensure that technological advancements lead to sustainable economic improvements and are not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. The growth of economic reasonableness is less predictable and irreversible compared to technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54), indicating that while technology drives growth, institutions must evolve to harness its benefits effectively.

One of the most compelling arguments for how institutions shape long-run economic growth is through their role in establishing secure property rights and facilitating efficient market exchanges.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have also been pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies, particularly through their impact on industrialization. Acemoglu et al. show that population density and prosperity influenced European policies, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy is evident in how some colonies developed extractive institutions that maximized rents for European colonists and promoted long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, demonstrating how institutional changes can be responsive to economic incentives (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated through their impact on the development of financial markets and transaction costs. North's analysis of the English financial revolution shows that new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and contributed to economic growth by reducing transaction costs per exchange while increasing the aggregate volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6).

Institutional changes address efficiency and distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. support this perspective by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive because they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence on colonialism and its impact on institutional development provides a clear pathway for understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European colonization strategies, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions, have had lasting effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin's work further highlights the complexity of these institutional changes by showing how colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).

One underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by North’s emphasis on secure property rights and Acemoglu et al.’s analysis of extractive versus inclusive institutions (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutional changes were driven by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Austin's work shows that colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more nuanced interplay between efficiency and distributional factors (Austin 2008, p.15). This debate is settled by the empirical comparison of colonial strategies and their long-term effects on income per capita, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27).
