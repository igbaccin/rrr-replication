The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is the question of whether and how institutions influence economic development over extended periods. The debate hinges on two main positions; one argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for efficient markets and secure property rights, while the other posits that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper structural factors such as geography or culture. The disagreement centers around whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or exogenous drivers of it. This debate is not merely academic; understanding the causal role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth in developing regions. The crux of the matter lies in identifying how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, affect long-term economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely across different colonies, leading to distinct institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These colonial strategies resulted in either the establishment of inclusive institutions that promoted investment and growth or extractive ones focused on transferring resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The divergence in these institutional setups significantly impacted current income per capita. Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution over time (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective underscores the need to consider how institutions evolve in response to economic and political incentives. The implication is that a more nuanced understanding of institutional change can provide better insights into long-run economic growth.

North posits that institutions matter significantly in economic growth and development, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as primary determinants (North 1989, p.9). He argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs by increasing the security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie suggests that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective highlights how power dynamics shape institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The key takeaway is that the nature of institutions, whether they reflect efficiency or distributional conflicts, plays a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is complemented by Hopkins, who argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The shared finding is that institutional quality, particularly the presence of 'good' institutions, is crucial for providing incentives for investment and development.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional transformation was driven by the English financial revolution, including the creation of the Bank of England. The financial revolution dramatically reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of long-term capital markets. This development is critical to understanding how institutions can facilitate market exchanges and economic growth. The emergence of these institutions led to a growing proportion of the economy dedicated to transactions, reflecting increased specialization and division of labor.

The success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets. North and Weingast highlight that the commitment of the regime to enforce property rights was a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment underpinned economic growth by providing a stable framework for investment and development, reducing uncertainty, and encouraging long-term planning.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends beyond secure property rights. This suggests that while secure property rights are necessary for economic growth, they may not be sufficient on their own. The broader institutional framework, including political stability and effective governance, also plays a significant role. In conclusion, the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights can foster economic growth, but it is important to note that this process is influenced by a range of additional factors within the broader institutional landscape.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily shaped by efficiency concerns. The specific institutional arrangements of serfdom, the community, craft guilds and merchant guilds in Europe reflect the outcomes of power struggles between different social groups over resource allocation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These arrangements were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by the political and social dynamics of the time. This distributional conflict framework is supported by the institutional structures seen in New World societies, where inequality was entrenched through various government policies that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman point out that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped the institutions that evolved. This highlights how initial conditions of inequality can lock in institutional arrangements that perpetuate disparities over time. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes that while these distributional conflicts are crucial, they are just one part of a broader institutional landscape. She advocates for a more comprehensive approach to understanding institutions, suggesting that they should be seen as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian. This implies that while distributional conflicts are significant, they must be studied in conjunction with other factors to fully grasp their impact on economic growth and development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that fostered investment or extractive ones designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. This variation in colonial institutions had profound effects on long-run economic outcomes. This implies that the path dependency of colonial institutions is a critical factor in understanding the divergent economic trajectories of former colonies.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and impacts contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon show how the legacy of the slave trade has shaped African societies by fostering a pervasive environment of distrust, which remains influential in modern social dynamics. This mistrust is not merely a residual effect but a deeply ingrained norm that continues to affect interpersonal relationships and economic interactions. The slave trade's impact on trust is multifaceted, encompassing both external environmental factors such as the rule of law and internal norms of behavior. Nunn highlights how the long duration of Africa’s slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, outlasts the period of colonial rule by a significant margin (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extended historical influence suggests that the economic and social institutions shaped during this era were more enduring than those imposed during the comparatively shorter colonial period. Furthermore, Nunn's analysis indicates that sustained trade in Africa was contingent on the presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). The Kongo kingdom’s ability to support trade with Europeans underscores how institutional structures play a critical role in facilitating economic development. This points to a broader implication: the legacy of mistrust, coupled with the historical absence of robust institutions, has left Africa with a challenging foundation for contemporary economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule was more favorable than under other colonial powers. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing both African political leaders' macro-level decisions and families' micro-level cost-benefit calculations for their children's schooling (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift created a demand for missionary education that was shaped by these new structures. This institutional environment fostered a unique educational landscape that differentiated British Africa from its colonial counterparts. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that structural impediments imposed during the colonial period are not solely responsible for the persistence of poverty in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.1). While British rule may have facilitated formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, the broader economic development and welfare outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of historical and geographical factors.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating the framework within which economies operate. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates; in colonies with high mortality, extractive institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This historical context aligns with Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

The interaction between indigenous economic actions and colonial administrations further shaped these institutional changes. For instance, when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic illustrates that institutions are not merely imposed but also co-created through interaction with local economic actions.

North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development by specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported economic growth.

Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations. These institutions reflect the political dynamics and power struggles within societies.

The new economic history of Africa highlights how institutional reforms can inform poverty elimination among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Englebert shows that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments, indicating that effective institutions can be developed regardless of ethnic heterogeneity. This shared finding underscores the importance of understanding diverse historical contexts and paths that shaped African economies.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are critical in shaping long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions are formed and how they interact with local economic actions remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is countered by Austin's historical evidence, which shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local incentives, suggesting a more nuanced interaction between indigenous economic actions and institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the detailed analysis of colonial policies and their adaptation to local contexts. For example, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn’s hypothesis, illustrates how institutions were shaped by indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This historical evidence shows that institutional changes were not merely imposed but co-created through interaction with local economic dynamics, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
