The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, marked by a live disagreement between those who see institutions as the primary drivers and those who view them as secondary to other factors. At stake intellectually is understanding the fundamental mechanisms that propel economic development over extended periods. One side argues that institutions are critical because they establish rules and enforce property rights, thereby fostering investment and growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). On the other hand, there are those who contend that institutions alone cannot explain economic growth, as they must interact with various factors such as population density and prosperity to have an impact (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The causal mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth are varied. Institutions can shape economic outcomes by determining how resources are allocated and how conflicts are resolved. This is evident in the historical context of England, where the development of institutions that ensured secure property rights played a pivotal role in economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The evolution of these institutions was driven by the need to address fiscal crises and ensure the security of property rights, highlighting the interplay between political and economic factors in shaping institutional development.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the varied mortality rates of Europeans in different regions led to distinct colonization strategies and institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive states prioritized the interests of European colonists over long-term economic development in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, shaping their current income levels and overall prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by evidence showing that colonial policies influenced population density and prosperity in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical context underscores how European colonization strategies, driven by mortality rates and resource extraction goals, laid the groundwork for institutional frameworks that continue to impact economic growth today.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that secure property rights are pivotal for fostering economic growth. The development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates this dynamic. This security was crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. These factors include how rules governing economic exchange are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the key drivers of pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions. They focus on how these conflicts shaped institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in ways that were not solely driven by efficiency. Ogilvie explains that inefficient institutions persist due to power dynamics within societies, where dominant groups maintain institutions securing their own interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's work on colonial administrations shows how institutional changes are often responses to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, illustrating a more dynamic interplay between local actors and colonial powers. This perspective suggests that institutions evolve in response to historical contexts shaped by power relations. Colonial governments sometimes encouraged commodity production when it aligned with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.15). The role of distributional conflicts is nuanced; Austin notes that institutional changes can also be driven by the need for fiscal security and the desire to maintain social stability.

Frankema's work on colonial rule and educational development in Africa shows that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more significant impact on formal education than other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). His study reveals that the demand for missionary education was shaped by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, influenced by new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule. The role of institutions in this context is nuanced: while British rule facilitated educational development, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated, such as altering cost-benefit perceptions, were crucial. Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies further underscores the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in these societies was perpetuated through institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and hindering growth. The institutional legacy of colonial rule thus plays a pivotal role in shaping educational and economic outcomes, with enduring implications for long-term development trajectories.

Technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both underscore the pivotal role of technological progress in driving long-run economic growth. Technological progress is highlighted as the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutional changes must complement these advancements for sustained development. Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations by providing a framework that supports technological diffusion and adaptation (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Integrating new technologies into existing economies requires significant adjustments in institutions and ideology. These institutional adaptations are necessary to mitigate conflicts arising from economic growth and ensure its sustainability. Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving these growth-induced conflicts without weakening societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54), which could jeopardize long-term economic stability. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the complexity of economic development and highlights the need for a balanced approach that fosters both technological innovation and institutional adaptability. Institutions are essential in creating an environment conducive to the growth of useful knowledge, thereby sustaining long-run economic growth.

The slave trade has been argued to have had a direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) assert that the legacy of mistrust fostered by the slave trade is crucial in understanding persistent underdevelopment. This mistrust manifests as a deeply ingrained cultural trait that affects intergroup relationships, undermining economic cooperation and productivity. The mistrust developed through centuries of enslavement has profound implications for contemporary African societies, where it persists as a significant barrier to trust-based interactions necessary for economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further suggest that this internalized mistrust is at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. The historical legacy of the slave trade thus shapes current economic outcomes by fostering an environment of mistrust, which hinders the development of institutions necessary for sustainable economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that facilitate investment and market exchanges. North asserts that secure property rights are crucial for economic development, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law significantly impacts economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical legacy of colonialism illustrates this point: extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have had enduring effects on the economies of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The evolution of institutions involves distributional conflicts. Ogilvie contends that pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North’s view that institutions are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which may not always align with long-term growth (North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence from colonial administrations shows that institutional changes often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, further highlighting the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of trust within these institutional frameworks is also critical. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that trust and other social factors play a significant role in economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). This internalization of mistrust, as shown by Nunn, creates a self-reinforcing cycle where low trust levels perpetuate economic stagnation. The historical legacy of the slave trade thus shapes current economic outcomes by fostering an environment of mistrust, which hinders the development of institutions necessary for sustainable economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth through mechanisms such as securing property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement centers on whether these institutional changes are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth, but this view does not account for the complexity of historical evidence from capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This evidence shows that institutions per se were crucial in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England.
