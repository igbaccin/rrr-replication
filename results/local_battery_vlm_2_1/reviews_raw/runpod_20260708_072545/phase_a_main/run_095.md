The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and development studies. What is at stake intellectually is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors such as culture, geography, or external shocks play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions create a framework for secure property rights and efficient markets, fostering long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). Others posit that institutions are merely one piece of the puzzle, with cultural beliefs and historical legacies also shaping economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The debate is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and other factors. For instance, some researchers highlight how colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes in former colonies, influencing current income levels through the establishment of extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance, which persists even after accounting for geographic or cultural differences.

The nature of institutions matters significantly. Some scholars emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and markets function efficiently (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). This view suggests that effective enforcement mechanisms and stable political environments are crucial for sustained economic development. The causal relationship between institutions and economic growth can be complex. This perspective highlights how power dynamics and internal struggles shape institutional development and, consequently, economic performance.

The historical context also plays a critical role in understanding the impact of institutions on economic growth. For example, some scholars point out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This critique suggests that a more nuanced understanding of institutional change over time is necessary to fully grasp how institutions influence economic growth.

The role of trust within societies also emerges as a significant factor. Some studies indicate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores how historical events can shape cultural norms and institutions, affecting economic development over generations.

The interplay between institutions and other factors such as culture, geography, and historical legacies continues to shape our understanding of long-run economic growth. The debate surrounding the role of institutions in this process is far from settled, with ongoing research providing new insights into how these complex interactions influence economic trajectories over time.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. Acemoglu et al (2002) further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth. This dynamic is evident in colonial contexts where European settlers established institutions that varied widely in their impact on economic outcomes. The long-term effects of these institutional choices are profound, as they continue to influence current income per capita in former colonies. This highlights that the legacy of colonial institutions persists, shaping economic trajectories over centuries and underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the institutional changes in England during this period were pivotal, as they created a framework where credible commitments to property rights could be enforced. This development was not merely about establishing rules for economic exchange but also about creating institutions that ensured these rules were consistently enforced and could evolve over time. This complexity is evident in how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between institutions and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The implication is that the efficacy of institutions in promoting growth depends on their adaptability and responsiveness to changing circumstances. However, this adaptive capacity was not uniform across regions; for instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, showing how local conditions shaped institutional responses (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the nuanced role of institutions in economic growth, where their impact is mediated by historical and contextual factors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2); (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought. This perspective is shared by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who argue that the historical poverty of Africa has not been a persistent phenomenon due to structural impediments. Broadberry and Gardner highlight how recent work in African economic history has revealed large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, which had previously limited comparative studies on African economic growth and development. This availability of data allows us to reassess the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth in Africa, as they provide a more nuanced view of historical economic performance than earlier accounts. Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa to fully grasp the possibilities for future growth. The patterns of growing and shrinking economies were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors suggest that while economic performance was robust in certain areas at specific times, it was also susceptible to external shocks and institutional changes. This indicates that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and contingent on a variety of factors, including local conditions and historical contexts.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These institutions were shaped by power struggles between social groups rather than efficiency considerations. For instance, serfdom and guilds emerged as a result of elites securing their positions against potential threats from other classes. This mechanism is evident in the way that institutions such as craft guilds and merchant guilds were established to control access to markets and resources, thereby maintaining elite dominance. The persistence of these institutions can be seen as a strategy to preserve existing power structures rather than an effort to enhance economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illustrate this by showing how the factor endowments in New World societies led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites. This institutional framework restricted competition and limited opportunities for broader economic development, thereby perpetuating inequality. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions against viewing these institutions solely through an efficiency lens, emphasizing the need to consider their historical and social contexts. Institutions in pre-industrial Europe were not merely tools for economic growth but were deeply intertwined with the power dynamics of the time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that Africa's slave trade fostered a deep-seated culture of mistrust in the region (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust has persisted over centuries, significantly impacting economic development by undermining trust levels. Trust is crucial for economic growth as it facilitates cooperation and reduces transaction costs.

The mechanism through which this mistrust operates involves both internal norms of mistrust and the broader external environment (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrates that the slave trades in Africa lasted nearly 500 years, far outlasting colonial rule. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted social institutions and economic development, creating a legacy of mistrust that continues to affect African societies today.

The impact of this culture of mistrust on economic growth is not merely about reduced cooperation but also about the broader societal implications (Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trades undermined the development of centralized governments and well-developed markets, which are essential for sustaining trade and economic growth. This historical legacy has left African societies with weaker institutions that struggle to support modern economic activities. Consequently, the mistrust fostered by the slave trade has a profound and lasting effect on Africa's economic trajectory, shaping its long-run development in ways that are still being felt today.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional adjustments in facilitating the efficient use of innovations, highlighting how modern technology requires significant adaptations in societal structures to fully realize its economic potential (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Institutions play a critical role in managing conflicts arising from technological changes, ensuring that these transformations do not undermine the political and social fabric necessary for sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, is essential in resolving growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that coordination failures can impede economic progress, necessitating policies to rectify these issues and ensure sustainable improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, the role of institutions in facilitating technological advancements and managing associated conflicts is indispensable for long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern the allocation of resources and the enforcement of property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North's seminal work underscores how institutions reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.9). This perspective is complemented by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis, which highlights that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current income levels. Settler colonies with extractive states experienced different outcomes compared to those where Europeans settled and set up rule-of-law-institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical evidence from these studies shows that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for economic growth.

The role of institutional evolution in economic development is further elucidated by the work of North and Weingast, who emphasize the importance of credible commitments in sustaining economic progress. They argue that the political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were instrumental in creating secure property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Ogilvie's assertion that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs shape core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guild systems. These institutional frameworks reflect the underlying political dynamics and social norms of a society, which are essential for understanding long-term economic outcomes.

The historical evidence from these studies underscores the significance of institutional design in determining economic growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that extractive institutions can impede economic development by maximizing rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is supported by Austin's analysis, which challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and highlights how different historical paths and institutional prescriptions shaped economic outcomes in various regions. The shared finding that institutions matter is clear; however, a precise remaining disagreement concerns whether these institutions are primarily shaped by population density and prosperity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) or by political factors underpinning secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The empirical comparison of colonial settler colonies versus extractive states resolves this debate: it shows that the type of institutions established, particularly those governing property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial determinants of long-term economic growth.
