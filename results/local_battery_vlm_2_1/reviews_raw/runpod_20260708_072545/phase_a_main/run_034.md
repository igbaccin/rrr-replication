The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. The debate hinges on how institutions influence economic outcomes over extended periods, and what mechanisms underlie this influence. One key position argues that institutions matter fundamentally because they shape the incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). A rival view suggests that population density and prosperity are more critical determinants of economic policies pursued by colonial powers and their long-term effects on former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Meanwhile, some scholars propose that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The stakes are high as understanding the causal role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at promoting sustainable economic development.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in 17th-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). In particular, the English financial revolution of 1694, which included the creation of the Bank of England, exemplifies how political institutions can facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of long-term property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The emergence of these institutions reflects a broader trend where political stability and secure property rights create an environment conducive to market exchanges and economic development. North (1989, p.9) argues that efficient property rights, enforced by effective political institutions, lower transaction costs per exchange and increase the overall volume of transactions, thereby boosting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Institutional changes do not always yield immediate economic benefits; bureaucratic structures can impede growth, as seen in France. The evolution of these institutions in England highlights how political stability and secure property rights foster economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. illustrate how European settlers created extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, aiming to maximize rents for colonizers and not long-run growth. This pattern was not uniform; in regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states were established in areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic development.

Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that these institutional differences are a product of political and economic conditions at the time of colonization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). In densely populated and prosperous regions, Europeans were more likely to implement extractive institutions because they could exploit existing resources without investing in long-term development. This approach maximized immediate gains for colonizers but left lasting negative impacts on the economic trajectory of these regions.

The persistence of these extractive institutions can be attributed to the political power dynamics at play; powerful groups within colonies often resisted institutional changes that would reduce their rents or political influence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This resistance further entrenched extractive practices, preventing the adoption of growth-oriented policies. Therefore, it is clear that the legacy of colonialism continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies through the enduring impact of these institutions on current income per capita.

The institutional framework established during colonial times has profound implications for contemporary economic development strategies; understanding this historical context is crucial for policymakers aiming to foster growth in regions still grappling with the effects of extractive institutions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15) re-evaluate historical economic performance by examining specific cases of commodity production and educational development under different colonial administrations. Colonial institutions shaped the trajectory of economic growth through their impact on indigenous commodity production, particularly in regions where European settlers were absent. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial governments often encouraged local commodity production when it aligned with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political pressures, a mechanism that allowed for some degree of endogenous institutional change.

The development of formal education under colonial rule showed significant variation. This variation in educational policies had profound implications for long-term economic growth, as it influenced the skills and human capital available in post-colonial societies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this point by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions, educational development, and economic performance in shaping long-run growth trajectories.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and craft guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions emerged from power struggles among elites, influencing long-term economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) support this view by demonstrating how institutional structures in New World societies evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective highlights that the persistence of institutions shaped by distributional conflicts can lead to entrenched economic disparities. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that institutional structures evolve over time as shared beliefs within societies change, altering the dynamics of economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This evolution underscores the importance of understanding how institutions adapt in response to changing societal norms and power structures.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa had profound negative effects on economic development that persist to this day. Specifically, they highlight how the transatlantic slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, which developed over centuries of exploitation and violence, has had enduring consequences for social cohesion and economic interactions. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by noting that while colonial rule lasted around 75 years, the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, indicating a much longer period during which these detrimental effects could accumulate. This prolonged exposure to slavery disrupted local economies and social structures, creating lasting barriers to development. The mistrust generated by these historical experiences has been shown to significantly reduce levels of trust in contemporary African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). One downstream implication is that this lack of trust can hinder economic development by impeding cooperation and reducing the effectiveness of institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn's work suggests that mistrust impacts economic outcomes differently across regions. The legacy of slavery varies depending on local conditions such as pre-colonial political structures and market integration. For instance, regions with more centralized governments and developed trading networks, like the Kongo kingdom, were better equipped to engage in sustained trade despite the slave trades' disruptions (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Technological progress serves as the primary driver of economic growth, with institutions playing a crucial role in facilitating the efficient use and diffusion of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Institutions are vital for enabling the implementation of technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both highlight that the growth of useful knowledge is central to economic development, with institutions serving as catalysts for this process. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is evident in historical contexts where epochal innovations transformed economies.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) underscores that technological advances require complementary institutional developments to ensure sustainability. Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that the success of modern economic growth hinges on resolving conflicts arising from technological changes, necessitating robust institutions to manage these transitions peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This alignment between technological and institutional progress is crucial for sustained economic improvement. This variability in institutional development suggests that while technology drives economic growth, the effectiveness of institutions in supporting this process can fluctuate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they provide the framework within which economic activities operate and evolve. Institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives and constraints that govern individual and collective behavior. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that the creation of secure property rights, facilitated by institutional changes like the development of the Bank of England, was critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This underscores how institutions can foster an environment conducive to sustained economic improvement. Austin agrees with this view, adding that European settler colonies saw a decline in transaction costs due to secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (Austin 2008, p.2).

Institutions also play a crucial role in managing the complexities of long-run growth by adapting to changing economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and not promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth, as seen in the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This highlights how institutional frameworks can adapt to ensure economic stability and growth over time. Austin supports this by noting that colonial administrations sometimes adjusted their policies based on indigenous economic actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional responses (Austin 2008, p.15).
