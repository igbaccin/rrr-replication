The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science, as it addresses whether and how societal rules and structures influence prosperity over extended periods. Intellectually at stake is how much institutional frameworks determine economic trajectories, alongside other factors such as geography or culture. The debate revolves around two main positions: one emphasizes institutions as primary drivers of growth, while the other sees them as secondary factors influenced by deeper historical processes. A key disagreement lies in how institutions are conceptualized and measured. Some scholars argue that institutions, defined as formal rules and informal norms, directly shape economic outcomes by influencing investment, innovation, and productivity. Some scholars argue that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and social forces, making them a consequence of growth. This debate underscores the need to understand the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic development.

North (1989, p.9) highlights the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, with extractive institutions hindering long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of institutional evolution over time. This shared finding shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political changes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency, suggesting complex motivations behind institutional development. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth, as they are influenced by both economic and political factors.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. These developments were both economic and political, reflecting strategic trade-offs where certain rights were ceded to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises. This institutional evolution underscores that secure property rights are crucial for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. The development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement in England led to falling transaction costs and an expanding market sector (North 1989, p.6). The impact of these institutions on economic growth can be nuanced. This implies that while secure property rights are important, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political regimes can also influence long-term economic growth.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth trajectories of former colonies through their effects on property rights and governance structures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established diverse institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates; high mortality led to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources, while low mortality encouraged settler institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who designed them to maximize their own rents, often at the expense of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy highlights a critical mechanism through which colonialism influenced economic development: the enduring impact of property rights and governance on economic outcomes. The specific pathways through which these institutions operated varied across regions. This nuanced perspective underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic performance in colonial contexts.

Acemoglu et al.'s work on property rights and governance highlights a specific mechanism through which institutions shaped long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts underpinned key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie's analysis of serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds reveals that these institutions were shaped by struggles over resource allocation and power between different social groups. These conflicts led to institutional arrangements that often favored specific elites at the expense of broader economic efficiency. In a similar vein, Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate how New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that entrenched elite advantages through policies restricting competition. This dynamic highlights how distributional concerns can override efficiency considerations in shaping institutional frameworks. This interplay underscores the complexity of institutional development and its profound impact on long-run economic growth.

Austin argues that colonial governments in Africa influenced economic outcomes by shaping incentives and rents rather than directly causing long-run growth through institutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor. This dynamic highlights how colonial policies could create incentives for local economic activities, which in turn influenced the broader economic landscape. However, Hopkins shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but also by progressive institutional development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). These findings suggest that while colonial administrations played a significant role in shaping economic activities and rents, the long-term impact on institutions and growth was contingent upon indigenous responses to these policies. Frankema and van Waijenburg note that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective underscores the importance of examining historical economic data to grasp the structural effects on long-term African welfare development, which in turn informs current policy recommendations aimed at fostering economic growth. The interplay between colonial policies and indigenous responses reveals that while institutions are important, their impact is mediated by broader socio-economic dynamics that influence long-run economic outcomes.

The slave trade had a profound adverse effect on long-run economic development in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade's impact extends beyond mere population depletion, shaping cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks that persist to this day. This mistrust persists as a feature that continues to influence economic interactions and development trajectories. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, significantly outweighs the period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). This temporal disparity underscores the enduring legacy of the slave trade, suggesting that its impact on economic development is more deeply rooted than that of subsequent colonial policies. The long-term effects of the slave trade are further compounded by the cultural norms it engendered, particularly the erosion of trust within and between communities (Nunn 2008, p.19). This mistrust has implications for contemporary economic development, as it affects interactions in markets and political institutions. The legacy of mistrust, therefore, extends beyond immediate economic losses to encompass broader socio-economic dynamics that continue to shape African development.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are pivotal for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that modern technology requires societal adaptations to be effectively integrated into the economy. This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in historical contexts where technological changes have spurred economic growth by fostering new production methods and enhancing productivity. For instance, the Industrial Revolution was marked by significant technological innovations such as steam power and mechanization, which drove substantial economic growth across Europe. These technological advancements required institutional adaptations to mitigate conflicts arising from rapid changes in production processes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state played a crucial role in resolving these conflicts, ensuring that the political fabric of society remained intact and facilitating long-term economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by highlighting how technological progress could be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors if institutional frameworks fail to address coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of robust institutions in harnessing technological advancements for sustained economic development, as they provide the necessary stability and coordination to manage societal changes induced by new technologies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both argue that the institutions created by European colonizers significantly influenced long-term economic growth in their colonies. The creation of extractive states in some colonies, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, contrasts with the establishment of rule-of-law-enforcing institutions in others like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This difference shows that institutional frameworks shaped by colonial policies can either hinder or facilitate economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutional differences were tied to the policies pursued by Europeans, which influenced population density and prosperity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) supports this view by highlighting how specific institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security. This institutional development facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North's work complements Acemoglu et al.'s findings by demonstrating that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for sustained economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) also notes the importance of institutional evolution in understanding long-term economic patterns, emphasizing that secure private property rights are key to growth (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared focus on institutions as drivers of economic growth across different historical contexts underscores their fundamental role.

The role of institutions extends beyond economic efficiency to include distributional conflicts and political power dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) assertion that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Hopkins (2009, p.18) further illustrates how institutional reforms can inform contemporary development policies aimed at reducing poverty in Africa. The new economic history of Africa shows that good institutions provide essential incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is clear: institutions matter because they determine the allocation of power, resources, and opportunities within a society. North (1989, p.9) makes this explicit by showing how institutions specify property rights and enforce them to facilitate market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). There remains disagreement about whether these institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the latter is more influential, highlighting how pre-industrial European institutions were driven by power dynamics rather than economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence from colonial policies in Africa settles this disagreement. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, demonstrating the enduring impact of institutional frameworks shaped by power dynamics rather than efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
