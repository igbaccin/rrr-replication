The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians alike. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and secure economic exchanges, are crucial for fostering long-term economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that cultural beliefs or historical legacies may be more influential. Additionally, there is a debate on whether institutions evolve endogenously in response to economic needs or if they are exogenous impositions from colonial powers.

One key area of contention involves the nature of institutional change and its relationship with economic outcomes. Some scholars emphasize the importance of credible commitments by political regimes to enforce property rights and stable governance structures, while others point to distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs as primary drivers of institutional development. These debates highlight the complexity of institutional analysis and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic growth over time.

The debate on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous is central to this discussion. Some researchers argue that institutions evolve organically in response to local conditions, while others suggest that they are often imposed by external powers, such as colonial governments. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions emerge and how they adapt over time.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the role of credible commitments in institutional development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political factors underpinning economic growth are critical, particularly the degree to which regimes or sovereigns are committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that European settlers established different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and mortality rates shaped institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These studies highlight how credible commitments can foster secure property rights and stable governance, which in turn promote long-term economic growth. The role of institutional change is further illustrated by North's discussion of the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional shift reflected increased security of property rights, critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Together these sources show that institutions can shape economic outcomes by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights.

This perspective underscores the importance of understanding both the presence and the nature and design of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. The interplay between institutional design and economic development highlights the need to consider who benefits from these institutions, as this can significantly influence their impact on growth.

This institutional development is shared by Austin (2008, p.2). North and Weingast's analysis highlights a specific mechanism through which this occurred: the establishment of credible commitments to property rights, which allowed investors to engage in long-term projects with greater confidence. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 was pivotal in this regard, as it constrained the monarch's power and established parliamentary supremacy, thereby ensuring that property rights would be enforced and protected.

The institutional changes in England during this period involved creating rules for economic exchange as well as establishing institutions that governed how these rules were enforced and changed. This aspect of institutional design is crucial because it addresses the issue of credible commitment, which underpins long-run growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The political factors governing public choice ensured that the regime was bound by the rules, providing a stable environment for economic activity.

This perspective underscores the importance of considering how institutions evolve in response to political and economic pressures. Austin's work on colonial administrations offers a complementary view, showing how institutional prescriptions could change in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between institutions and societal forces highlights that the evolution of property rights is an ongoing process shaped by various factors. However, Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that this evolution can be complex and influenced by multiple historical paths and periods, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is multifaceted and context-dependent.

Mokyr highlights the role of the "growth of useful knowledge" in sustaining economic improvement. This process requires institutional adjustments to ensure that new technologies are efficiently integrated into society. Kuznets elaborates on this, noting that technological progress necessitates significant changes in social structures and ideologies. The pace and spread of technological advancements vary across different regions and periods, influencing the type of institutions required for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes that resolving conflicts arising from these technological changes is essential for maintaining political stability. Without effective conflict management, the benefits of technological progress can be undermined, leading to a weakening of the societal fabric. This underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and political tensions that accompany economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) supports this by noting that without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, sustainability would be limited. The growth of "economic reasonableness" is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, highlighting the need for continuous institutional adaptation to support long-term economic development.

British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered more robust educational institutions than other colonial powers, which influenced long-term economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that British administrative structures promoted formal education by altering the perceived benefits of acquiring skills through new social and political institutions. African leaders' cost-benefit calculations shifted, driving demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework was pivotal in shaping long-term economic development, as it laid the groundwork for human capital accumulation and skill development.

The positive impact varied across regions and was contingent on specific historical and geographical conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) underscore that British colonies experienced significant real wage growth during colonial rule, indicating improvements in living standards (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This economic progress, coupled with enhanced educational opportunities, suggests that institutional differences shaped by colonial rule had profound implications for long-term economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that this mistrust affects trust levels which are crucial for long-run economic growth. A direct implication is that the adverse effects on trust, through both internal norms and external environmental factors such as rule of law, significantly hinder African economies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings align with research highlighting the role of trust in various facets of development, including international trade and political institutions (Knack and Keefer 1997; Fafchamps 2006). Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that Africa's slave trades had a profound impact on economic development over nearly five centuries. This extended period suggests that the effects of the slave trade are deeply embedded in African societies, influencing contemporary institutions and economic outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.19). The adverse effect extends beyond trust to broader institutional frameworks, which further complicates long-run growth prospects.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping economic institutions during pre-industrial Europe and beyond (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that the driving force behind key economic institutions was not efficiency but distributional conflicts. She illustrates this through serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in Europe. These institutions often served to maintain power imbalances among elites rather than promote efficiency or productivity. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to colonial New World societies, where the initial inequality of factor endowments led to institutional frameworks that further entrenched elite advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern is illustrated by government policies that restricted competition in favor of elites. The persistence of these institutions over time had profound implications for economic development, as they stifled competition and innovation. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this downstream implication by noting that efficiency considerations did play a role in shaping some aspects of these institutions, albeit secondary to distributional concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between power dynamics and institutional design, suggesting that while distributional conflicts were primary, they did not entirely displace efficiency as an influencing factor.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to structure incentives and constrain behavior over time. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks, with extractive states in some colonies and rule-of-law-enforcing institutions in others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted and significantly influenced current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes.
