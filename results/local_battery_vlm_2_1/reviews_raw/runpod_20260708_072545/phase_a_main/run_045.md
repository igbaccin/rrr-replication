The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives sustained economic progress over generations. A key disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and how different types of institutional frameworks influence this process. One side argues that institutions, particularly those ensuring secure property rights and rule of law, are crucial for fostering long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another perspective suggests that factors such as cultural beliefs or geographic conditions also significantly shape economic outcomes (Author Year p.N). This debate highlights the need to delve into how institutions interact with these other variables and whether they can override them.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different types of institutions based on mortality rates in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In places where Europeans faced high mortality, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. In areas with low mortality, like the United States and Australia, institutions were implemented to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. This institutional framework directly influenced current income per capita levels. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). They document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and show that it was related to industrialization during the nineteenth century. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing property rights security (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs fostered economic growth through increased market exchanges and specialization. The political institutions that undergird these changes were crucial for securing property rights, which facilitated long-term capital markets and sustained economic development (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These institutional developments are particularly evident in the English financial revolution of the late seventeenth century.

The Bank of England's establishment was a direct response to fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, reflecting a trade-off between certain rights and revenues. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the success of these institutions is evident in capital market performance, indicating that institutional changes underpinned economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions govern economic exchange and adapt rules over time.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge persistent poverty characterizations in Africa by highlighting periods of growth tied to foreign trade and export markets. They argue that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, demonstrating significant economic development during the colonial era. The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores that institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping economic performance. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize the importance of real wage levels and trends in understanding long-term African welfare development, suggesting that historical data can reveal periods of economic growth that challenge assumptions of persistent poverty.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions significantly shaped the current income per capita in former colonies. They posit that European colonizers established extractive institutions in high-mortality regions to maximize resource extraction quickly, while in low-mortality areas they developed more inclusive institutions that fostered long-term growth. This institutional divergence, driven by mortality rates, led to a stark contrast in economic development trajectories among colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions was motivated by the colonizers' desire to maximize short-term gains at the expense of long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). European colonists designed these institutions for their own benefit, not local economic development, leading to persistent poverty in many former colonies. The persistence of extractive institutions is due to initial colonial policies and a lack of subsequent institutional reforms. The legacy of colonialism thus continues to affect economic growth in many regions today.

Institutional reforms are seen as pivotal for economic development in Africa, particularly in addressing poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that the new economic history of Africa can inform such institutional reforms. This approach is supported by Simson (2019, p.2), who highlights the significance of governance structures and industrial policy in shaping post-independence expenditure trends. These expenditures, influenced by factors like capital investments and military spending, have been crucial in determining the economic trajectories of African nations. For instance, Kenya's high subsidies and interest payments in the early 1990s displaced both capital and recurrent spending, illustrating how specific institutional policies can redirect financial resources away from development (Simson 2019, p.11). This pattern underscores the importance of targeted institutional reforms that can mitigate such redistributions and foster economic growth. Simson (2019, p.2) also notes that neopatrimonial tendencies in Africa often result in government resources being treated as private property, which can hinder broad-based public goods and services (Simson 2019, p.2). This qualification suggests that while institutional reforms are necessary, their effectiveness depends on the underlying governance structures and how resources are allocated.

Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade contributed to a lasting culture of mistrust within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). The historical trauma of the transatlantic slave trade has had profound effects on social cohesion. This mistrust is embedded in social norms, which persistently inhibit economic development by reducing cooperation and trust among individuals. Austin supports that this mistrust affects how communities interact with each other and with external institutions, complicating efforts to build robust governance structures that can foster growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the cultural legacy of slavery continues to shape economic outcomes by influencing social norms and institutional effectiveness. The impact of this mistrust on economic development varies across regions and communities in Africa. The variation in how different societies have adapted to these historical legacies can provide insights into potential pathways for overcoming these barriers (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

They posit that sustained long-run growth is fundamentally driven by advancements in useful knowledge. This perspective emphasizes the role of innovation in transforming economies over extended periods, suggesting that institutional adjustments are crucial for accommodating these innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) elaborates on this idea by highlighting that technological progress often requires significant societal changes to resolve conflicts between traditional modes of life and new economic realities; this includes the development of national states capable of managing growth-induced tensions. For example, Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the transition from rural lifestyles to industrialization necessitates institutional adaptations such as the establishment of modern national states.

Mokyr (2005, p.49; 2005, p.54) further supports this perspective by illustrating how technological progress is complemented by institutional developments. He argues that without these complementary institutions, the benefits of economic growth could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. Mokyr (2005, p.49) specifically mentions policies aimed at reducing access costs to knowledge as essential for sustaining economic improvement. This implies that while technology drives growth, it is the supporting institutional framework that ensures this growth is sustainable over time. Mokyr also acknowledges that the progress of economic reasonableness is less predictable than technological advancements.

Technological advancements often necessitate institutional reforms that facilitate the efficient use of new innovations, thereby shaping long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are crucial in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. These conflicts arise from inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power, which influence the development of institutions that either sustain or mitigate these disparities. The persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutional frameworks that favored elites. These institutions restricted competition and maintained elite dominance through policies that limited access to resources and opportunities for other groups (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in the contrasting development paths of societies with differing levels of initial inequality, where institutional arrangements either reinforced or challenged existing power structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes that institutions are deeply rooted in the social and political contexts of their time. The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding long-run economic growth, as it suggests that addressing distributional conflicts is essential for creating more equitable and sustainable institutional frameworks.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and allocate resources. Acemoglu et al. show how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach differs from settlements like the United States, where institutions enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). North similarly argues that economic growth is intrinsically linked to the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement. He highlights the English financial revolution as a key moment where transaction costs fell dramatically due to increased security in property rights, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). These findings underscore the critical role of institutional frameworks in determining economic outcomes over extended periods.

The historical context in which institutions develop is also crucial. Ogilvie emphasizes that distributional conflicts shape institutions, not purely efficiency-driven motives. She argues that core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were products of these conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin further supports this view, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who argues that understanding Africa's economic record requires recognizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment. He notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments, suggesting that institutional development can occur even in heterogeneous societies (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Institutions evolve in response to political and economic pressures. This evolution is driven by the need to address distributional conflicts and provide stable frameworks for economic activity.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement lies in the measurement of long-term economic growth outcomes, which show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had lasting effects on relative incomes among former colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate this through their analysis of how extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists inhibited long-run growth in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The comparative study of different colonial experiences provides clear evidence that the type of institutions established during colonization has had a profound and lasting impact on economic development.
