The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question for understanding the trajectory of societies over time. Institutions can be understood as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, including political systems, legal frameworks, and cultural practices. The live disagreement centres around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological innovation, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that inclusive institutions, which enforce property rights and encourage investment, are crucial for sustained growth. Others contend that extractive institutions, designed to benefit a narrow elite, can hinder economic progress by creating barriers to investment and innovation. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies aimed at fostering economic growth in both developed and developing nations.

The centrality of institutions in economic growth is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that colonial institutions significantly influenced current income per capita by shaping long-term economic outcomes. The authors highlight how European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states in places like the Congo to more inclusive systems in regions such as the United States and Australia (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional variation is crucial because it directly affects the incentives for investment and economic activity. Similarly, North emphasizes that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth. He argues that institutions like the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and facilitating long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). These insights underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering an environment conducive to economic development.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is further explored by Acemoglu et al. They argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective aligns with North's view that efficient property rights and effective enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization is highlighted as a key factor in the long-term development of these colonies. Furthermore, Austin challenges the oversimplification of causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis needs to account for the complexity of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced view suggests that understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires a detailed examination of how these institutions evolved over time and interacted with other factors.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights. This financial revolution was crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. North further argues that the evolution of these institutions facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization and division of labor, which multiplied aggregate volume of transactions. This institutional framework was essential for economic growth.

The evidence from capital markets supports this perspective: the success of British financial institutions highlights the importance of political factors underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that a critical factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules, suggesting that credible commitment underpins growth. This institutional framework governed economic exchange and enforced how these rules were changed over time. North and Weingast's perspective on the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England underscores that the development of secure property rights through credible commitment is essential for long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are a more fundamental driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They establish this claim by examining the institutional frameworks that governed economic exchange. Ogilvie posits that the institutions of serfdom, community, craft guilds and merchant guilds were rooted in distributional conflicts rather than efficient resource allocation. She notes that while shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure, they are not the primary driver. Instead, she suggests that these institutions arose from power dynamics between different societal groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this point by examining New World societies, where institutions often advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these institutions highlights how distributional conflicts can shape economic growth over the long run. However, Ogilvie qualifies this perspective by noting that while efficiency may not be the primary driver, it still plays a role in shaping institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies by introducing institutional changes that often favored extractive practices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. illustrate this point through their analysis of European colonization strategies, which varied widely depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. The persistence of these extractive institutions highlights how colonial legacies can have profound and lasting impacts on economic trajectories.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that African governments' quality of public goods is shaped by institutional frameworks and ethnographic fragmentation. Institutions are crucial in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This emphasis on institutions aligns with a broader shift in developmental policy towards 'governance' and the recognition that 'good' institutions matter significantly (Hopkins 2009, p.8). They note that a better grasp of historical and geographical conditions can reveal whether specific factors have had temporary or structural effects on African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also caution about the persistence of poverty in Africa due to structural impediments, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their impact may be mitigated by other historical and geographical factors. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of African economic development, where institutional quality is a necessary but not sufficient condition for sustained growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting social and economic dynamics (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon's work with Austin's  shows that the legacy of African slavery is not just an historical fact but a persistent cultural force, one that continues to shape contemporary attitudes toward trust. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that this mistrust has two channels: internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin's findings reveal that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labor. This suggests that while institutions may have been shaped by historical events, they also adapted in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon's notion of external channels influencing trust. Austin's work highlights that institutional prescriptions varied based on local conditions. This variation underscores the complexity of institutional adaptation and its impact on economic development, showing that while institutions are crucial, their effects can be mitigated by other historical and geographical factors (Austin 2008, p.15).

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge fuels technological advancements, which in turn propel long-term economic growth. This process requires institutional adjustments to efficiently integrate new technologies into the economy. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the diffusion of these innovations necessitates significant institutional and ideological changes, as modern technology often clashes with traditional societal structures (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements may not be fully realized or sustained (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interdependence suggests that while institutions are essential for harnessing the benefits of technological progress, they play a supportive rather than a primary role in driving economic growth. Institutions must adapt to facilitate these technological changes; otherwise, the potential gains from innovation may be lost due to coordination failures or rent-seeking behaviors. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional flexibility in response to technological advancements, as rigid institutions can impede the full realization of economic growth potentials inherent in new technologies.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to innovation and investment. The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is critical for economic development. North shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework reduced the cost of transacting and enhanced the security of property rights over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interaction between political and economic institutions is crucial for sustaining long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, which resulted in varied institutional outcomes. In some colonies, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Other colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast established extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These differences in institutional frameworks significantly affected current income per capita.

The historical record demonstrates that institutions must adapt to technological advancements to maximize economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al.'s formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the dynamic nature of institutions over time. Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa by providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

North's work on transaction costs shows that as political institutions specify efficient property rights, the costs per transaction fall, leading to increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6).
