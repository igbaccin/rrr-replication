The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake intellectually is the identification and prioritization of causal mechanisms that drive sustained economic development. The debate centers around two primary positions: one emphasizes the centrality of institutions in determining economic outcomes, while another highlights alternative factors such as geography or cultural beliefs. Some scholars argue that institutions are pivotal because they shape incentives for investment, innovation, and overall economic activity by establishing rules governing property rights and enforcement mechanisms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Other researchers assert that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights also significantly impact institutional effectiveness. This disagreement underscores the need to disentangle the specific ways in which institutions influence long-term growth, particularly when considering the historical contexts of colonization and post-colonial development.

North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), while Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how different European colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks. Some colonies were characterized by rule-of-law enforcement and investment encouragement, whereas others were marked by extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These varying approaches had profound implications for current income per capita in former colonies. Acemoglu et al.'s findings highlight that differences in European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, which in turn affected long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The institutional framework set by colonial powers significantly shaped the economic trajectories of their former colonies. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al.'s formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its interaction with local economic and political dynamics. For example, colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating how institutions can adapt over time based on local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the historical development of financial instruments and property rights. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The persistence of extractive institutions can be attributed to the influence of politically powerful groups who may not benefit directly from institutional development or fear losing their political power if there is institutional change. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions are likely designed to maximize rents for European colonists, which is a focus on long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores how the interests of colonial powers shaped the institutions established in their colonies, with enduring effects on economic development.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast highlight that the success of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The degree to which a regime is committed to or bound by rules governing economic exchange plays a critical role in economic development.

The causal mechanisms through which institutions shape long-run economic growth are multifaceted and deeply intertwined with historical contexts. Institutions established during colonial periods, shaped by European mortality rates and local economic conditions, have had lasting impacts on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The evolution of financial instruments and property rights further illustrates how institutional frameworks can facilitate or hinder economic growth over time. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for comprehending the broader dynamics of long-term economic development.

The institutional changes that occurred during this period were instrumental in creating a stable environment conducive to market exchanges and long-term capital investment, which are essential for sustained economic growth.

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a significant milestone in this process. North (1989, p.9) describes how these new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This development was crucial for economic growth as it facilitated the emergence of a long-term capital market. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this institutional transformation by necessitating trade-offs between certain rights and revenues. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that these political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should manifest in lower transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6). The evidence from capital markets underscores the significance of institutional changes in England during this period, highlighting how secure property rights were instrumental in driving economic growth. This is evident in the striking improvements in capital market performance, which North and Weingast (1989, p.29) attribute to the political commitment to enforce these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional framework established during this era laid a robust foundation for economic development by ensuring that property rights were secure and that markets could operate efficiently. This framework facilitated market exchanges and promoted long-term capital investment, both of which are essential for sustained economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. Acemoglu et al. show that these institutional differences persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these colonial-era institutions highlights the enduring influence of historical factors on contemporary economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These conflicts led to the creation of core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that when Europeans established colonies in the New World, factor endowments varied significantly between societies, leading to different degrees of inequality from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This variation in factor endowment influenced the evolution of institutions. For example, some societies evolved institutions that concentrated wealth and political power in the hands of elites, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutional differences persisted over time and significantly impacted economic development trajectories. This finding is especially relevant for understanding how historical factors can shape contemporary economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, challenging the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had profound and lasting effects on African economic development. The slave trades disrupted social and political structures, leading to weakened institutions and impeded economic growth. These disruptions were particularly severe because they targeted human capital, which is crucial for economic development. This perspective emphasizes that the legacy of slavery is both an institutional issue and a demographic one, as it depleted Africa's population and hindered its ability to develop economically. The slave trades' impact varied across Africa, with some regions experiencing more severe effects due to varying levels of political centralization (Nunn 2008, p.19). This variation suggests that while the slave trades were a significant factor in African underdevelopment, their impact was mediated by local institutional and economic conditions. This nuanced view highlights the importance of understanding both historical shocks and local adaptive mechanisms in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa positively influenced the development of formal education, as Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) share. This influence was mediated by local demand for missionary education driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro (political leaders) and micro (families) levels; these educational institutions in turn shaped the perceived benefits of formal education, such as access to new economic opportunities. The implementation of these new social and political institutions altered local perceptions of the value of education, creating a positive feedback loop that reinforced demand for it (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Broadberry and Gardner's work on Anglophone African economies shows that by the early twentieth century, many of these economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence, suggesting that the educational developments under British rule may have contributed to broader economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner note that growth patterns varied significantly between countries due to factors such as local endowments and political institutions. This variation implies that while British rule facilitated educational development, the long-term economic impact depended on how these new institutions interacted with existing local conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes the centrality of technological progress in economic development. This perspective highlights the importance of innovation and knowledge diffusion in driving sustained economic growth over long periods. Kuznets (1973, p.1) reinforces this idea by noting that epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, spread to a substantial part of the world. These advancements necessitate institutional adjustments for their efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this point, emphasizing the role of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by robust institutions to ensure sustainable economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic activity and influence outcomes over extended periods. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or set up extractive states, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North emphasizes the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and providing secure property rights, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This alignment with institutional development is further supported by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where political factors underpinning economic growth included credible commitment to rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The historical evolution of institutions shows that they adapt over time in response to economic and political pressures. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive if designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which is a focus on long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the development of financial instruments during England's financial revolution, which reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth by securing property rights over time (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth has its complexities. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other factors over time. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter significantly for long-run economic growth because they govern the rules governing economic exchange. Austin's critique shows that institutional development can be influenced by historical context and distributional conflicts, which can lead to different outcomes depending on the specific circumstances (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie suggests that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The remaining disagreement centers around whether institutional development is primarily driven by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of politically powerful groups in shaping institutions to maximize rents rather than promoting long-run growth, highlighting the importance of political factors in institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement comes from the analysis of colonial institutions and their impact on current income per capita, which shows that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect economic outcomes over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
