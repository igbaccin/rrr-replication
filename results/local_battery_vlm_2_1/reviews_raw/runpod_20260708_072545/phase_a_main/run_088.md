The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that engages economists and historians alike. At stake is the ability to understand and predict economic development trajectories over centuries, which has profound implications for policy-making today. The disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or initial endowments play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that geographical factors or initial conditions set by colonialism have a more decisive influence on long-run economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

There is also debate about whether institutions are shaped primarily by efficiency concerns or by distributional conflicts. Some scholars posit that institutions evolve to maximize economic efficiency, while others argue that they are the result of power struggles and political interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This debate has significant implications for understanding why some countries develop strong institutions that support growth, while others do not. The question of whether institutions cause economic growth or if they merely reflect other underlying factors is a live one.

The first stream of scholarship emphasizes the causal role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, shaped the long-term economic trajectories of these regions by influencing investment and productivity. North similarly argues that institutions matter for economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms, reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The second stream of scholarship proposes a rival cause to the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth: geography. This perspective shifts the focus from institutions to other factors such as climate, disease environment, and natural resources. Austin's argument is supported by Hopkins' work, which highlights the importance of understanding Africa's economic history to inform institutional reforms and eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The third stream of scholarship develops the claim that institutions shape long-run economic growth through different phrasing or as a more specific instance. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective emphasizes the role of initial conditions set by colonialism in shaping long-term economic outcomes and highlights the specific institutional mechanisms through which these outcomes are achieved. Austin's work supports this perspective by showing that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs through the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century. The creation of the Bank of England and other financial innovations dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was a product of fiscal crises that necessitated trade-offs between certain rights and revenues, as well as a response to economic needs. This dynamic fostered economic growth by facilitating market exchanges through reduced transaction costs. The evidence from capital markets supports this, showing that these institutions provided a stable framework for economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also emphasize the importance of credible commitment by the regime to these rules, suggesting that the stability and enforcement of property rights were critical factors in sustaining long-run growth.

Colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns; in regions with high mortality, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This pattern was a deliberate policy choice that had lasting economic consequences, not merely a response to geographic factors. Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by their analysis of various institutional measures, including constraints on the executive and property rights indices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The extractive nature of these institutions meant that they did not encourage investment or economic development. This institutional framework persisted over time, influencing current income per capita and development trajectories. The interaction between institutions and industrialization played a central role in this long-run development.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis fails to account for the complex interplay between institutions and economic development over time. One specific mechanism highlighted is the role of European settlers in shaping colonial institutions. This dynamic suggests that colonial institutions evolved in response to local conditions, challenging the notion that they were uniformly extractive. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this view by emphasizing the enduring impact of historical events such as the slave trade on contemporary economic outcomes. They find that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly affects trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal norm of mistrust, shaped by historical legacies, continues to influence economic development, highlighting how past institutional frameworks can have long-lasting effects on contemporary economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share a finding that African economic development has been significantly influenced by the historical legacy of colonial institutions. These scholars do not directly address how specific institutional frameworks fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores the importance of public goods provision in African economies, emphasizing that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional. This aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) exploration of real wage levels across British Africa. They find that understanding long-term economic development is crucial for assessing future growth prospects, which includes considering historical conditions' effects on African welfare.

This perspective highlights how institutions shape incentives for investment and development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of real wage divergence across British Africa, noting that this knowledge will help assess future growth possibilities. Their analysis shows that while historical conditions have influenced African welfare, these effects are not necessarily permanent; instead, they can be temporary or structural.

This literature highlights how colonial institutions have shaped contemporary economic outcomes in Africa by influencing public goods provision and real wage levels. This underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutional frameworks can have long-lasting effects on contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

The enduring impact of colonial legacies on African economies suggests that addressing poverty requires a nuanced approach to institutional reforms.

Technological progress is the primary driver of long-term economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological advances, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, spur economic expansion. This process involves innovation as well as the institutional adjustments necessary to efficiently integrate these innovations into society. The pace at which technology progresses is pivotal for growth; however, this progress must be complemented by suitable institutional frameworks.

Technological advancements necessitate changes in societal structures and ideologies. Kuznets highlights that modern technology often conflicts with traditional ways of life, such as rural living patterns and extended family systems. These conflicts can only be resolved through institutional adjustments to accommodate technological changes. The state plays a crucial role in this process by ensuring peaceful transitions during periods of rapid growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements may not be sustained, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode economic improvements over time. This underscores the importance of coordinated efforts between technological and institutional developments in driving long-term growth.

However, this perspective does not imply that institutions are irrelevant to economic growth; rather, it emphasizes that technological progress is the primary driver with institutions playing a supporting role. Kuznets notes that economic growth requires resolving conflicts arising from technological changes without weakening societal cohesion. This suggests that while technology drives growth, institutional stability ensures that growth remains sustainable over time (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Therefore, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for achieving sustained economic development.

This theme of technological progress as a key driver of economic growth underscores the necessity of understanding how institutions can facilitate or hinder this process.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were shaped primarily by efficiency concerns. Instead, Ogilvie contends that "shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, " highlighting how cultural and political factors can influence institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this idea to New World societies, where they show that initial conditions of inequality led to institutions that favored elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality by shaping institutions that protected elite interests over broader economic efficiency (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie's argument is particularly compelling because it shows how the same institutional structures can arise from different underlying motivations, depending on the prevailing distributional conflicts. The roots of institutional development are deeply intertwined with societal power dynamics, not solely driven by economic efficiency.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their capacity to influence property rights and transaction costs. North emphasizes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects increased security in property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth itself. Austin agrees with this emphasis on institutions but also highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are shown to be crucial for fostering an environment conducive to economic growth, as specialized labor and increased market exchanges multiply aggregate transactions. This institutional framework is essential for reducing costs per transaction, thereby promoting long-run economic development (North 1989, p.6).

Austin further argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He shows how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation underscores that institutions evolve based on societal dynamics, which is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

The persistence of certain institutions can be attributed to the interests of politically powerful groups, who may resist changes that could threaten their rents or power. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions often maximize rents for these powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts significantly shape core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), which were not primarily driven by efficiency but by the interests of dominant groups. The persistence of these institutions highlights how societal power dynamics influence long-run economic growth.

The historical evidence shows that colonial policies played a crucial role in shaping institutions that either promoted or hindered economic development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varied institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some colonies, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in others, institutions enforcing the rule of law encouraged investment. This variation in institutional development directly influenced current income per capita, illustrating how institutions shaped by colonial policies continue to affect economic growth today.

The precise historical evidence that settles these disagreements is that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The measurement type used, such as income per capita and the presence of extractive states versus rule-of-law institutions, shows that institutions shaped by colonial policies directly influenced long-run economic growth. This empirical comparison highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping economic development, reinforcing the underlying point of agreement that institutions matter significantly in long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9).
