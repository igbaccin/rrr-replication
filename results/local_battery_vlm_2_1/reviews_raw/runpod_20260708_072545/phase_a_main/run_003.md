The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a fundamental question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of why some regions have prospered while others have lagged behind. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geographical endowments or cultural traits, play a more significant role. Institutions can be defined broadly to include political structures, legal frameworks and social norms that govern behavior in societies. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current economic outcomes. This debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote sustainable economic growth, as it informs strategies for institutional reform and development aid. The tension between institutional determinism and other explanatory frameworks underscores the complexity of economic growth dynamics.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the direct causal impact of institutions on long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for fostering economic development. He highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating long-term capital markets. This institutional framework is seen as foundational for sustained economic growth. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth, emphasizing the importance of credible commitment to rules governing economic exchange. They argue that the success of institutions in seventeenth-century England was remarkable, as evidenced by developments in capital markets. The evolution of these institutions fostered secure property rights and provided a stable framework for economic activity, highlighting how institutional changes can drive long-term economic performance.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes is another key area of inquiry. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, as seen in the United States and Australia, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive institutions, prevalent in colonies like the Congo, aimed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutional differences have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, but nonetheless acknowledges the importance of institutional evolution in shaping economic outcomes. This stream underscores how colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic development, with inclusive institutions fostering growth while extractive ones hinder it.

Institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty are also a critical focus for scholars who study Africa's economic history. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The emphasis on 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment essential for development, reflects a broader consensus on the role of effective governance structures. Austin (2008, p.15) further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic highlights how local context can shape institutional outcomes and influence long-run economic growth.

The relationship between institutions, trust, and economic development is another crucial area of study. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy has significant implications for economic development, as trust is essential for fostering cooperation and investment. Their findings complement broader studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring impact of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes underscores the long-term effects of institutions and social norms.

Institutional quality and its relationship to public goods provision are further explored in the context of Africa's economic challenges. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional performance; societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet still dysfunctional or heterogeneous and capable of upholding the rule of law while developing progressive institutions. This perspective highlights how the quality of institutions is crucial for providing public goods and fostering economic development. The interaction between ethnic diversity and institutional effectiveness underscores the complexity of factors influencing long-run economic growth.

The stream on the impact of colonial institutions, trust, and public goods provision converges to show that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic outcomes. From secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms to inclusive governance structures and cultural norms of trust, institutions are fundamental for fostering sustained economic development. Understanding these dynamics is essential for policymakers seeking to promote growth and reduce poverty.

North illustrates this dynamic through the creation of the Bank of England, which introduced new financial instruments that dramatically lowered transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The establishment of secure property rights is pivotal as it facilitates long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework fosters an environment where market exchanges can flourish, leading to increased specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplies the volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al (2001) observe that European colonization strategies varied widely. In some colonies, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment; however, extractive states were set up in other places with the goal of rapidly transferring resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional legacy of colonialism persists today and significantly affects current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than fostering long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had lasting implications for the economic trajectories of their former colonies, shaping their institutional frameworks and development paths.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the political foundations of economic growth are rooted in institutions governing public choice. Specifically, secure property rights were crucial for fostering long-run economic development.

This institutional framework was essential for the emergence of capital markets. North and Weingast's research highlights how England's political stability, underpinned by institutions like the Glorious Revolution, created an environment where property rights were secure. This led to increased investment and economic growth. The credibility of these institutions allowed investors to trust that their investments would be protected, thereby fostering a robust capital market. This focus on institutional credibility also suggests that the political stability required for such commitment may not always align with short-term economic gains. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight that societies with extreme inequality, such as those in Latin America, often lacked the institutions necessary to protect property rights effectively. This inequality restricted competition and hindered long-run growth, illustrating how initial conditions can shape institutional frameworks. North and Weingast's findings on England suggest that the quality of public choice institutions is a key determinant of economic success.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African poverty has not been an enduring historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Both authors find that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous narratives that emphasized persistent economic stagnation in Africa. This finding is supported by the availability of extensive economic data from the colonial era, which has enabled recent work in African economic history to reconstruct and compare real wage levels across different colonies (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The pattern of growth in export production varied significantly between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further highlight that these patterns were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. This suggests that the economic performance of African economies during colonial times was influenced by a complex interplay of external and internal factors, including the quality of political institutions and the integration into global markets. The extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development remains an open question, necessitating further research into the interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within African societies, which has persisted over time. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights the prolonged duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, compared to a much shorter period of colonial rule. The impact was particularly severe in regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom, which were better positioned to support trade but were nonetheless devastated by the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). This disruption underscores how the legacy of the slave trades continues to shape economic outcomes.

Mokyr and Kuznets shared finding that technological advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). They argue that useful knowledge accumulation is pivotal for sustained growth. Mokyr highlights that while technological progress is essential, it must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure long-term sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that the efficient use of innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments, which can vary significantly across regions and time periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts without weakening a society's political fabric. For instance, modern economic growth demands substantial changes in societal structures, such as transitioning from rural lifestyles to urbanization and adapting family patterns to accommodate new technologies Kuznets. Kuznets also warns that these adjustments must be managed carefully to avoid excessive costs or long-term political instability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state plays a crucial role in this process by ensuring that conflicts arising from economic growth are resolved peacefully. Thus, while technological advancements propel economic growth, the institutional environment must adapt and support these changes to achieve sustained development.

The importance of institutions is further underscored by Kuznets' observation that modern economic growth necessitates significant societal adjustments. These adjustments include technological shifts as well as ideological transformations that align with new economic realities (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions highlights the complex nature of economic growth, where both factors must evolve in tandem to support long-term development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to adapt and support societal changes, including technological shifts and ideological transformations. The establishment of secure property rights through political institutions has been crucial for fostering economic development by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges and encourages investment, which is essential for sustained growth.

Institutional evolution is further illuminated by the historical context of colonialism. European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, have had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. This dynamic underscores how initial institutional setups can persist and shape future economic trajectories.

The complexity of institutional change is also evident in the interplay between political power and economic outcomes. Institutions that enforce secure property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This political dimension is crucial because it determines the commitment to rules governing economic exchange, which in turn influences long-term growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Moreover, the historical evidence shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have been pivotal in determining the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. These changes highlight how institutions can either support or hinder economic development, depending on their design and enforcement (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North 1989, p.9). The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions.

The historical evidence also indicates that institutional reforms can inform policy changes aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people. This is particularly relevant for Africa, where poor institutions have been linked to dismal economic records (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North 1989, p.6). Effective governance and strong institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development.

The specific historical evidence that settles these disagreements lies in the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes, as documented in former colonies, provide a clear empirical comparison of how different institutional setups have led to varying economic outcomes. The measurement of income per capita and the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century are crucial for understanding long-run development (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
