The question of what role institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding development trajectories across nations and epochs. At stake intellectually are the mechanisms that drive sustained economic prosperity versus stagnation, and the extent to which these mechanisms can be influenced by policy. Institutions have been posited as a crucial variable in this process, but disagreement arises over their precise role: some scholars emphasize institutions' capacity for credible commitment (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while others focus on the extractive nature of institutional frameworks that can hinder growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The interplay between these perspectives shapes our understanding of how institutions influence long-term economic outcomes.

The development of new financial instruments in England during the 17th century significantly lowered transaction costs, as argued by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and subsequent innovations reduced the cost of transacting, a key factor in fostering long-run economic growth. This decline reflected increased security in property rights over time, which is crucial for both capital markets and overall economic expansion. These institutional changes were part of a broader political evolution that ensured secure property rights by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions was evident in the development of capital markets, highlighting the importance of credible commitment to enforceable rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights. However, they qualify this by noting that if England and France were almost at parity in economic performance before the French Revolution, it suggests that while institutional changes were significant, they were not revolutionary in isolation (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This underscores the complex interplay between political institutions and economic outcomes, where the specific nature of these institutions and their enforcement mechanisms play a critical role.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by establishing extractive states in some regions and investment-friendly institutions in others. This divergence was driven by differential mortality rates among Europeans, which led to distinct colonization strategies. In high-mortality areas, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted over time, shaping economic outcomes for centuries. The persistence of these institutions is further explored by Acemoglu et al., who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not conducive to long-run growth if they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern suggests that the initial institutional frameworks set during colonial periods had lasting impacts on economic development trajectories. However, it is important to recognize that these institutions were shaped by the political dynamics of the time and not necessarily by long-term growth considerations. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2001, p.19) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the interaction between colonial institutions and industrialization opportunities, highlighting the interplay between historical institutional frameworks and economic development pathways.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the view that institutional reforms in Africa are pivotal for poverty reduction. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa has highlighted how specific interventions can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa can provide insights into long-term African welfare development, which is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This suggests that while institutional reforms are targeted at poverty reduction, they also have broader implications for economic development and long-run growth trajectories.

For example, Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes the shift in development policy towards incorporating governance and institutions, which enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists. This institutional focus is crucial because 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, a key driver of development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This highlights the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand how historical conditions have influenced long-term economic development in Africa. The interplay between institutional reforms and economic growth is complex, requiring nuanced analysis and targeted interventions that consider both immediate poverty reduction goals and broader economic development objectives.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective highlights that advancements in technology create new opportunities for productivity gains, which in turn stimulate economic expansion. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. He argues that the accumulation of knowledge leads to innovations that enhance productivity and foster economic development. This process is not linear; it involves coordination failures that require institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores this point by noting that technological changes often conflict with existing social structures, necessitating adjustments in institutions to resolve these conflicts peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Without complementary institutional advancements, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and other inefficiencies (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This qualification underscores the importance of a balanced approach that integrates both technological and institutional developments to achieve long-term economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) highlight that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This persistent mistrust significantly impacts contemporary trust levels. The mechanism by which this historical legacy operates involves both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust within African societies. These norms were forged during the slave trade era, where communities learned to mistrust one another due to the violent and exploitative nature of the trade. This mistrust has since become deeply ingrained in social interactions, affecting various aspects of economic and political life. The external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, plays a role but is less influential than the internal norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not competing for African labor. This historical context further complicates the institutional landscape, as colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions. Thus, the legacy of mistrust is compounded by the enduring effects of colonial institutions, which continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue. These conflicts over resource distribution drove the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds. The mechanism is that elite groups, seeking to maintain their advantages, implemented policies that restricted competition and preserved inequality. This dynamic was particularly evident in New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These inequalities were reinforced through institutional frameworks that advantaged elites, thereby perpetuating the status quo. The enduring legacy of these institutions results from both historical economic conditions and ongoing power struggles and elite interests.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either facilitate or hinder development. Shared beliefs about property rights influence institutional structures and thus affect long-term capital markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which means equilibrium institutions may not always foster economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further illuminated by the impact of colonialism. European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies receiving extractive institutions that prioritized resource transfer to the metropole over local investment and growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional legacy persists today, significantly affecting current income per capita. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is critical for economic growth, as seen in the development of financial instruments like those introduced by the Bank of England (North 1989, p.9).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism over geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists have enduring effects on economic outcomes.

Institutional frameworks are also shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These power struggles and elite interests reinforce institutional structures that perpetuate existing inequalities. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is critical for economic growth, as seen in the development of financial instruments like those introduced by the Bank of England (North 1989, p.9).

The enduring legacy of institutions results from both historical economic conditions and ongoing power struggles and elite interests. This is evident in the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, where initial factor endowments led to extreme wealth disparities that were reinforced through institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
