The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political economy. At stake is understanding whether the institutional frameworks that govern societies are fundamental drivers of prosperity or mere byproducts of other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements. A significant disagreement exists between scholars who argue that institutions directly influence economic outcomes and those who posit that other variables, such as population density or colonial policies, play a more decisive role. The former group emphasizes the importance of property rights, rule of law, and political stability in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Some scholars emphasize how historical events like colonization strategies and their institutional legacies affect current economic conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This debate underscores the need to disentangle the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in determining long-term economic performance. The central question remains: how do institutions shape economic growth over extended periods?

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9), (North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice in 17th-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight the English financial revolution as a critical institutional shift. This security was crucial for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North elaborates on this by noting that evolving political institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, leading to falling transaction costs per exchange as specialization increases (North 1989, p.6) . This institutional evolution underscores the broader impact of credible commitment in shaping economic outcomes. North and Weingast's focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth goes beyond mere rules governing economic exchange; it emphasizes institutions that enforce these rules and facilitate their change (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implication is that England's institutional changes, such as the financial revolution, fundamentally altered the trajectory of economic development. This suggests that while institutional changes in England were significant, they were not the sole determinant of economic growth; other factors also played crucial roles.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers varied widely based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In high-mortality areas, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to their home countries. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists and not promote long-term economic growth, persisted even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continues to influence current income levels. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is deeply rooted in the institutional changes introduced during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Politically powerful groups shape these institutions and may prioritize short-term gains over long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while extractive institutions have had a lasting impact on economic development, their persistence and effects can be moderated by changes in political power dynamics or shifts in institutional design.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers behind many core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. For instance, serfdom and community structures were shaped by the power dynamics between landowners and laborers. These institutions were primarily mechanisms to maintain social control and extract resources from the lower classes. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were efficient responses to economic conditions. Rather, they were rooted in distributional conflicts over wealth and power. This can be seen in the persistence of serfdom in regions where landowners had political influence despite its potential inefficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) note that while these institutions persisted due to distributional conflicts, their specific forms were also influenced by the unique factor endowments of different regions. In Latin America, for example, the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. This inequality was perpetuated through institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. While these institutions were designed to maintain elite control, they also had significant implications for long-run economic growth by stifling innovation and entrepreneurship.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that Africa's underdevelopment is primarily attributed to the impact of slave trades rather than institutions. The slave trades disrupted African societies by depleting human capital and undermining social cohesion, which hindered economic development over centuries. This disruption was exacerbated by the lack of centralized governance in many African regions, making it difficult for these societies to recover from the depredations wrought by the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). For example, sustained trade with Europeans did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets. This suggests that without strong institutions, African economies struggled to develop despite external opportunities. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Nunn 2008, p.2) caution against overemphasizing historical impediments, noting that the persistence of poverty in Africa is influenced by more recent events such as colonial rule and post-colonial policies. This implies that while slave trades played a significant role in shaping African economic development, other factors, including institutional changes during colonialism, have also been influential.

Frankema and Broadberry and Gardner argue that British rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, which was particularly conducive to economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2)(Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial administration implemented new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift increased demand for missionary education at both macro and micro levels, driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families alike (Frankema 2012, p.15). These educational advancements were not uniform across Africa; they varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). While British rule did promote education in some regions, its impact was nuanced. For example, Nunn and Wantchekon found that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust also influenced educational outcomes. The internal norms of mistrust, which were at least twice as large an influence as external factors like the rule of law, played a significant role in shaping trust levels within African communities (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Thus, while British colonial education policies had positive effects on formal education, they operated within a complex web of historical and social factors that shaped long-run economic growth.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that the accumulation and application of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic advancement. Kuznets highlights that technological progress requires institutional adjustments to facilitate its efficient use. He notes that major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which he terms epochal innovations, have significantly contributed to long-term economic growth across various regions (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These innovations necessitate corresponding changes in societal structures and ideologies to resolve conflicts arising from modernization. Mokyr further elaborates on this idea by underscoring the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements. He suggests that without such institutional adjustments, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is crucial for long-run economic growth. Kuznets points out that these conflicts must be resolved peacefully to avoid weakening society's political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Thus, the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress and managing societal changes remains pivotal for sustained economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, as seen through the lens of various scholars who emphasize different aspects of institutional impact. Acemoglu et al. highlight that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to distinct institutional frameworks in colonies. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These findings underscore the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in driving long-term economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice, as North and Weingast demonstrate, was crucial for securing these property rights and facilitating market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had profound effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists significantly affected long-run development outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is complemented by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond mere efficiency considerations. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, which were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups rather than purely economic efficiencies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This aligns with North's contention that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists, not to foster long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Thus, the political and distributional aspects of institutional design are critical factors in understanding long-run economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether the evolution of institutions is primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth, suggesting that efficient property rights enforcement is crucial (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts are a more accurate explanation for institutional design in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This disagreement can be settled by examining the historical evidence of institutional changes in colonial contexts. The measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic outcomes in former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al., provides a clear empirical comparison that supports the view that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists significantly affected long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
