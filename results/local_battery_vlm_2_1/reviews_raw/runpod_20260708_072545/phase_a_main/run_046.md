The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics and economic history. At stake is how to understand the divergent trajectories of economies over centuries. One live disagreement concerns whether colonial institutions have lasting effects on contemporary economic outcomes. The debate pits scholars who argue for the primacy of colonial institutions against those who emphasize other factors, such as endogenous cultural beliefs or the specific historical contexts of institutional changes. Additionally, some scholars question the efficacy of existing measures of institutions and their quantitative applicability to explaining long-term growth.

The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is not new; it has been a central theme in economic thought for decades. One key position is that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers adopted different strategies depending on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In areas with high mortality, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, while in areas with low mortality, settler colonies developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This argument highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on current economic conditions.

The importance of institutions is further underscored by North (1989, p.9) who contends that secure property rights are critical for long-term economic growth. He notes that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported economic growth by lowering costs per transaction while increasing the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North's emphasis on secure property rights aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that institutions enforcing the rule of law are conducive to economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth is not limited to colonial contexts. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions determined current economic outcomes, oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the institutional evolution of older industrial economies should be understood in the context of patterns and paths going back to the original industrialization of Europe. Austin (2008, p.15) further notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change.

The debate over institutions extends to the pre-industrial period as well. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) advocates for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. She argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were designed for efficiency and instead suggests that they were shaped by power dynamics and distributional struggles. Ogilvie's argument complements Acemoglu et al.'s thesis on extractive institutions, as both highlight the role of power in shaping institutional outcomes.

The enduring impact of colonial institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions were established and how they continue to influence economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights the significance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. He argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins's emphasis on the role of institutions in promoting economic growth aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that institutions enforcing the rule of law are conducive to economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also notes that ethnic homogeneity does not prevent dysfunctional societies and that ethnic heterogeneity does not necessarily hinder progressive institutions' development. This perspective challenges the notion that ethnic fragmentation is a barrier to institutional development.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth underscores the complexity of this issue. While some scholars emphasize the enduring impact of colonial institutions, others highlight the dynamic nature of institutional change and the importance of understanding historical contexts. The role of power dynamics and distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes is also a crucial aspect of this debate. Ultimately, the literature suggests that institutions play a significant role in shaping long-term economic growth, but their effects are mediated by a range of factors including historical context, power dynamics, and endogenous cultural beliefs.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth and development. They highlight the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms as key factors enabling economic transactions, which are essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic expansion. This reduction in transaction costs reflects increased security in property rights over time, which is critical for fostering economic growth. This institutional framework is crucial for sustaining economic development by providing a stable environment for investment and innovation.

The evolution of seventeenth-century England's public choice institutions highlights the importance of political factors. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the success of these institutions depends on the regime's commitment to enforcing rules and property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is a critical factor in fostering economic growth, as it ensures that the rules governing economic exchange are stable and predictable. The evidence from capital markets supports this view, indicating that institutional changes can significantly impact economic performance. The implication is that while secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth, the political context in which these institutions operate also matters greatly.

Acemoglu et al. contend that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The differential colonization strategies adopted by Europeans resulted in varied institutional frameworks across colonies. In settler colonies like the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas in regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These colonial policies, which prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic development, had lasting effects on institutional quality. Acemoglu et al. argue that these initial institutions shaped by European colonists persisted and significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies ( (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19)), demonstrating the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize population density and prosperity as key factors shaping European policies, suggesting that these influenced the type of institutions established (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This nuanced view indicates that while extractive institutions hindered long-run growth, the interaction between institutional frameworks and local conditions played a crucial role in determining economic trajectories.

This perspective is echoed by Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49), both argue that technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are key drivers in long-term economic growth. These technological advancements necessitate institutional adjustments to efficiently integrate new technologies into the economy. Kuznets further notes that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to be possible, underscoring the need for a robust political framework (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr qualifies this by noting that institutional progress complements technological advancements but is not always monotonic or irreversible. This suggests that while institutions are important, their role is secondary to the primary driver of economic growth: technological progress and useful knowledge accumulation (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Thus, although institutions play a supporting role in facilitating technological integration, they do not fundamentally cause long-term economic growth.

This is evident in the Kongo kingdom, which had a national currency and trading networks that supported trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). These factors highlight pre-colonial institutions' importance and underscore the slave trades' enduring legacy on Africa's economic trajectory.

Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) find that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. A key mechanism underlying this process is the security of property rights, which was often skewed in favor of the elite classes. This institutional framework created barriers to entry for lower-income individuals, preventing them from accumulating wealth or gaining political influence. The result was a self-reinforcing cycle where elites maintained their dominance by shaping institutions that protected their interests, while also limiting opportunities for upward mobility among the general population. The persistence of inequality is further compounded by the lack of investment in public infrastructure and social capital, which are crucial for economic development. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds nuance to this perspective by showing how colonial rule could alter the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities created by institutional changes. This suggests that while inequality can persist through elite-favoring institutions, there may be pathways for change when external factors, such as colonial policies, introduce new incentives and opportunities.

This interplay is further illustrated by the literature on African economic development and colonial impact, which highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) illustrates this dynamic with the argument that new economic history can inform institutional reforms. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This understanding must go beyond historical analysis; it requires exploring how these determinants relate to other parts of colonial Africa. The implications of such an approach are significant, as they suggest that specific historical or geographical conditions may have cast a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development rather than a structural one. This nuanced view challenges the notion of persistent poverty and opens avenues for future growth by highlighting the potential for institutional reforms to overcome historical impediments.

While there is agreement that institutions matter, the precise mechanisms through which they influence growth remain a subject of debate. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of income per capita and its correlation with institutional quality.
