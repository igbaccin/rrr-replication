Institutions are often credited as the cornerstone of economic growth. They influence long-term development by creating incentives and constraints that affect investment decisions, resource allocation, and productivity. The central debate revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions exert this influence and whether they act independently or in conjunction with other factors like geography or culture. Some scholars argue that institutions are key to securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. Others propose that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within a society shape these institutions, while still others consider that institutional changes themselves can be pivotal.

The disagreement hinges on the causal mechanisms by which institutions affect growth. The role of institutional change in fostering economic development is evident in how secure property rights and effective enforcement reduce transaction costs. This view underscores the significance of political factors in shaping markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth due to their role in reducing transaction costs and fostering investment. North 1989's case study of the Bank of England illustrates how new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, enhancing long-term capital markets. The Bank of England's establishment reflects a political trade-off: certain rights exchanged for revenues during fiscal crises (North 1989, p.6). This institution-building process is critical because it underpins economic growth by securing property rights over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) echo this emphasis on institutions, highlighting how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional frameworks in European colonies. These institutional differences significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The role of political power is evident in both cases: North 1989's fiscal crises and Acemoglu et al.'s colonial strategies show that powerful groups shape institutions to maximize their rents, even if this does not always align with long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to benefit politically powerful groups rather than promote development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This nuance underscores the complex interplay between political power and institutional design in economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are critical for long-run economic growth, as they foster investment and innovation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal; these institutions ensured that rulers were committed to enforcing property rights. This credible commitment was crucial because it provided a stable environment where economic actors could plan for the future with confidence, thereby stimulating growth (credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2)). Ogilvie supports this perspective by emphasizing the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. She argues that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for understanding long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), and she notes that the distribution of political power influences how institutions evolve. Ogilvie also points out that shared beliefs within a society can significantly impact institutional design and economic performance. This underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth; while institutions governing public choice are vital for fostering property rights, the interplay between power dynamics and shared beliefs can either reinforce or undermine these institutions' effectiveness.

Institutions in societies marked by greater inequality tend to entrench elite advantages through policies that restrict competition and sustain inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the initial factor endowments of New World colonies led to extreme wealth, human capital, and political power disparities. These inequalities persisted over time, shaping institutional evolution in ways that favored elites and hindered broad-based economic growth. This pattern is evident in the specific focus on how different colonial environments resulted in societies with varying degrees of inequality, which in turn influenced the development of institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these inequalities is further illustrated by Simson's analysis of East Africa's post-independence expenditure trends, where military spending and debt service payments were significant factors. These expenditures often displaced capital investments, highlighting how institutional policies can divert resources away from growth-enhancing activities. However, the extent to which these patterns hold universally is qualified by variations in specific contexts, such as Kenya's peak subsidies and interest rates in the early 1990s (Simson 2019, p.11). This suggests that while inequality-driven institutional policies generally restrict competition, their impact on economic growth can vary based on local conditions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) posit that Africa's slave trades caused a culture of mistrust that continues to impact the continent today. This prolonged exposure to slave trades likely had profound and lasting effects on social institutions. The legacy of mistrust affects economic development through its impact on trust levels, which are crucial for various aspects of growth, including trade and political stability (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). However, Nunn also points out that sustained trade in Africa only began with the Portuguese finding the Kongo kingdom, highlighting how centralized governance and developed markets facilitated trade. This suggests that while mistrust is a significant factor, the presence of strong institutions can mitigate some of its negative effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The legacy of colonial rule in Africa is marked by a duality of constraints and opportunities for economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial governance influenced African economic trajectories through its effects on women's roles in agriculture, as well as the development of formal education systems. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production, a dynamic reinforced by customary law under colonial rule. This gendered division of labor had significant implications for agricultural productivity and economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner suggest that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, highlighting the potential for economic development despite colonial constraints (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of this growth varied across regions due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These findings indicate that while colonial rule presented challenges, it also created opportunities for economic advancement through education and agricultural productivity gains (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and its implications for African development highlight the complexities of historical paths and institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, arguing that the economic historiography of industrial economies should consider patterns and paths of institutional evolution going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe. This perspective is important because it underscores how colonial rule's impact on Africa varies depending on whether European settlers were present or absent, and how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The emphasis on 'good' institutions is crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the presence of poor institutions does not solely determine Africa's economic record; ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, while heterogeneous ones can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced understanding suggests that institutional reforms in Africa must consider both historical contexts and contemporary political realities.

Institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing rules that govern social interactions and economic transactions. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This effect is not merely about initial conditions but also about how these institutions persisted over time, reflecting the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes.

North argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). He emphasizes that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is essential for reducing transaction costs. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which highlight how extractive institutions can hinder long-term growth by not encouraging investment or innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

However, North's work shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional framework is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The specific historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This empirical comparison highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in shaping long-run economic growth.
