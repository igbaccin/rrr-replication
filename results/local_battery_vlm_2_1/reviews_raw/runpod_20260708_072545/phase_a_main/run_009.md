The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies, as it addresses how societies can foster prosperity over extended periods. Institutions, defined as rules governing human interaction, influence investment, innovation, and resource allocation to facilitate or hinder economic progress. A key disagreement centers around whether institutions are primarily shaped by geographical factors, cultural beliefs, or political dynamics. Some scholars argue that geography and natural endowments play a decisive role in determining institutional trajectories, while others emphasize the importance of historical contingencies and political choices. Additionally, there is a debate over whether institutions evolve to maximize efficiency or are driven by distributional conflicts. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for devising policies that promote sustainable economic growth.

One stream of scholarship examines how colonial legacies have shaped institutions and long-term economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, characterized by secure property rights and effective governance, fostered economic growth in regions like the United States and Australia. Extractive institutions, designed for rapid resource exploitation, hindered development in areas like the Congo and the Gold Coast. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more complex interplay between local conditions and colonial policies.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by North's seminal work, which emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North (1989, p.9) contends that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term investments and market exchanges, which are essential for sustained economic development. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore that credible commitment to property rights is a critical political factor underpinning economic growth. The success of capital markets in seventeenth-century England illustrates how institutions governing public choice can foster secure property rights and promote economic development.

Another stream of scholarship focuses on the evolution of institutions over time and their impact on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism caused a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. They emphasize that these institutional changes interacted with the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, playing a central role in long-run development. This perspective aligns with North (1989, p.6), who argues that as economies grow, political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling transaction costs and increased market exchanges. The size of the transactions sector grows proportionally to GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) advocates for incorporating institutions into long-term research programs, highlighting their importance in understanding economic outcomes. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. These insights suggest that institutional design is influenced by power dynamics and distributional conflicts, which can shape economic outcomes in significant ways.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They contend that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. The political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement were instrumental in this process. This dynamic underscores the interconnectedness between political and economic institutions in driving growth. The extent to which these institutional changes were revolutionary remains debated; some scholars argue that bureaucratic structures inherited from previous regimes, such as those under Louis XIV, did not significantly hinder economic growth. This perspective suggests that while institutional changes were important, their impact on economic development was more nuanced than initially thought.

These nuanced impacts of institutional changes are further exemplified by the significant influence of colonialism on long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that these institutions were extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. These institutional structures had a profound impact on economic development by shaping property rights, rule of law, and governance frameworks. The divergent paths of institutional development can be traced back to the varying colonization strategies employed by Europeans. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled in the colonies and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states rapidly transferred resources to the metropole. The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to affect current income per capita across former colonies.

This interplay highlights how initial institutional frameworks set by colonial powers could either facilitate or hinder subsequent economic growth, depending on whether they fostered investment and innovation or merely extracted resources. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies underscores the enduring effects of these historical institutional changes. Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) analysis, using various measures of institutions such as constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, consistently shows that these factors are crucial in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The robustness of these findings across different institutional metrics reinforces the significance of colonial-era institutions in shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share the view that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the interests of elites who benefited from maintaining them, even if they were not necessarily efficient. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate how the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies influenced the evolution of institutions that perpetuated these disparities. This dynamic underscores how elites' interests can drive institutional development in ways that maintain their advantage while potentially hindering broader economic growth. Despite this, institutions shaped by distributional conflicts may still have enduring effects on long-run economic trajectories.

The divergence between Europe and Asia's economic trajectories before 1800 illustrates how historical economic performance can be misunderstood due to the lack of comprehensive data, which obscures the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that regional economic performances, such as those in Anglophone African economies and Asia, were more complex than previously thought. The evidence suggests that historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa has been limited, which has led to misconceptions about the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta note that productivity in Europe’s traded goods sector was driven by merchant distribution and finance, highlighting how service sectors played a crucial role in early modern growth. This underscores that living standards were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled workers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The complexity of this economic landscape is further illustrated by the varying timing and scale of export production growth across countries, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These dynamics suggest that historical divergences in economic performance were deeply intertwined with institutional frameworks and market structures (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta's analysis complicates this picture by arguing that high productivity in southern Indian agriculture resulted from significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries as well as geographical factors. This investment led to both high levels of economic development and low silver wages in India, challenging the notion that institutional change alone drove European agricultural productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, involving complex interactions between geography, investment, and market structures.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn contends that the African continent experienced four simultaneous slave trades over nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. The adverse effects of these slave trades are evident in their disruption of societal structures and economic systems. Nunn points out that sustained trade with Europeans was only possible when centralized governments like those found in the Kongo kingdom supported it with well-developed markets and trading networks, underscoring the importance of pre-existing political and economic institutions (Nunn 2008, p.2). Austin challenges the notion that the reversal of fortune thesis adequately explains Africa's underdevelopment by highlighting how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political actions. This qualification suggests that the impact of slave trades and subsequent colonial institutions was more nuanced than simply detrimental, as it depended on the specific economic and political contexts of different regions in Africa.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-run economic growth. This perspective highlights the role of epochal innovations in propelling sustained growth over extended periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This process requires institutional adjustments to efficiently harness these innovations. The sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving conflicts that arise from technological progress and ensuring the peaceful integration of new technologies into society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technology is a primary driver, institutions are necessary to sustain economic growth over the long term (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Thus, the relationship between knowledge and institutions in driving economic growth is complex and interdependent.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. Austin notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labor (Austin 2008, p.15), indicating that institutions can adapt to local conditions to promote economic activity. These findings highlight how institutions shape the economic environment by reducing transaction costs and ensuring property rights, thereby fostering growth.

Institutions also play a crucial role in mediating distributional conflicts and rent-seeking behaviors that can hinder economic development. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), emphasizing the importance of political institutions that enforce rules and commit to them. Acemoglu et al. point out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that while institutions are essential for economic development, their effectiveness depends on how they manage distributional conflicts and prevent rent-seeking behaviors. The historical evidence of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England and the colonial policies in Africa illustrate this dynamic.

The interplay between technology and institutions further underscores their interdependent role in driving long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. note that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements can be limited or even reversed by rent-seeking behaviors (North 1989, p.9). This implies that while technology is a primary driver of growth, institutions are necessary to sustain it over the long term. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), suggesting that institutions often evolve to manage these conflicts. The historical evidence from colonial policies and technological advancements in Europe shows that institutions shape how societies integrate new technologies, ensuring their beneficial impact on long-run growth.

The specific historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies settles this disagreement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This empirical comparison shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were central to the long-term development of these regions. The measurement of income per capita and the analysis of colonial policies show that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had significant economic consequences.
