The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue among economists and historians. The debate centers around the extent to which institutional frameworks determine economic trajectories over extended periods. One position holds that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges, thereby driving economic growth. This perspective is grounded in the idea that efficient institutions reduce transaction costs, promote investment, and encourage innovation. A competing explanation suggests that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs significantly influence core economic institutions, with efficiency considerations playing a supporting role. These differing views underscore the importance of understanding how institutions influence long-run economic growth.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. assert that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. These institutional frameworks significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin challenges this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin suggests that European settlers' presence or absence, along with colonial governments' fiscal security interests, shaped institutional prescriptions differently from what Acemoglu et al. propose (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the complexity of institutional development in colonies and the need to consider multiple factors beyond mortality rates.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also explored through the lens of financial developments. North emphasizes that institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments were critical for reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast build on this by arguing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. This political commitment to rules underpinned economic growth and market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The discussion around institutions and economic growth often revolves around the idea of credible commitments. North argues that the creation of efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitates economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is shared by North and Weingast, who highlight that political regimes committed to these rules foster a stable environment conducive to economic development. In this context, institutions provide the necessary framework for long-term investment and market exchanges.

Another stream of scholarship focuses on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and merchant associations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily designed to maximize economic efficiency. Instead, it suggests that these institutions often reflect the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. Ogilvie's work underscores the importance of considering the political and social contexts in which institutions operate.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled. While some scholars emphasize the importance of efficient property rights and market exchanges, others point to distributional conflicts and shared beliefs as key factors. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions influence economic trajectories over extended periods.

The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of innovative financial tools were pivotal in this regard. This perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights as a dimension fundamental to both capital markets and sustained economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate on this by emphasizing the role of political institutions in specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms. Their research shows that the evolution of these institutions was crucial for economic growth, particularly in the context of 17th-century England. The success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs.

This commitment underpins economic growth by ensuring that the rules governing economic exchange are enforced and can be changed in a predictable manner. The development of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms is thus essential for long-run economic growth, as it fosters an environment conducive to market exchanges and reduces transaction costs.

The implications of these institutional developments extend beyond transaction costs, influencing the broader economic landscape by shaping capital markets. This highlights the enduring impact of secure property rights on economic performance, with England's experience serving as a historical precedent for understanding the role of institutions in long-run economic growth over time.

Colonial institutions established by European powers played a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic outcomes across former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks depending on the mortality rates they faced. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. These initial institutional choices had lasting effects on economic development.

The persistence of these colonial-era institutions is crucial for understanding current economic disparities. Colonial powers' institutional frameworks influenced immediate economic outcomes and shaped long-term development trajectories. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote growth in the colonies themselves, created a legacy of underdevelopment that persists today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The institutional impacts of slavery and inequality highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the New World societies with greater inequality were characterized by extreme disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by examining how Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, have significantly influenced the continent’s economic trajectory. This prolonged period of exploitation led to deep-seated institutional weaknesses that continue to hinder contemporary African economies (Nunn 2008, p.2). The legacy of slavery is historical and manifests in persistent mistrust and underdeveloped social capital, which are critical for economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today. This mistrust significantly impedes trust-based interactions essential for economic development, such as those required in international trade and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust stems from internal norms deeply ingrained over centuries of exploitation as well as external environmental issues (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The persistence of these institutional legacies underscores the enduring impact of historical inequalities on long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, as African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that favored educational investment. This shift was driven by the introduction of missionary education, which created new opportunities for Africans to acquire skills valued in the emerging colonial economy (Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this point by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic development was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, which in turn were influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial policies and local conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) caution that British rule facilitated economic development but did not uniformly benefit all regions or social groups. African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon solely due to structural impediments; rather, it was shaped by the specific historical and geographical conditions of colonial rule.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) together argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). The mechanism through which this distributional conflict operates is the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This pattern suggests that institutions were imposed, negotiated, and adapted based on local conditions. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were solely designed to maximize productivity. Colonial policies had varied outcomes depending on the specific historical and geographical conditions of colonial rule.

Technological progress and useful knowledge accumulation are primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) assert that technological advancements require institutional adjustments for efficient use. Technological progress involves new inventions and the diffusion of innovations across societies, which requires coordination to overcome access barriers and resolve conflicts arising from these changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr’s argument highlights that without complementary institutional progress, economic gains from technological advancements are unsustainable and can be eroded by rent-seeking behavior. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges that technological progress can be more monotonic and irreversible than institutional progress. This distinction suggests that although technology drives growth, institutions play a critical role in ensuring that this growth is sustainable over the long term.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North (1989, p.9) argues that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement lowers transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that European settlers established extractive states in high-mortality colonies to maximize rents rather than foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), highlighting the importance of institutions in determining economic outcomes. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The historical context of colonialism further illustrates the impact of institutions on long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, rather than purely geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by Austin's analysis, which contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation and that different historical periods and paths must be considered. For example, colonial administrations encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for labor, demonstrating how institutional changes can adapt to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive capacity of institutions is crucial for understanding their role in economic growth.

The evolution of public choice institutions also plays a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the success of England's capital markets was underpinned by political factors, including credible commitment to rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework ensured that the regime or sovereign was bound by these rules, which is essential for long-term economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how different institutional arrangements can shape economic outcomes over time.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles disagreements about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is found in the contrasting experiences of former European colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, as described by Acemoglu et al., versus the development of more inclusive institutions in low-mortality colonies shows how institutional differences can lead to divergent economic paths (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This empirical comparison demonstrates that while technology drives growth, institutions are crucial for ensuring sustainable long-term economic development.
