The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake is understanding whether and how the formal rules and informal norms that govern societal interactions influence sustained economic prosperity over generations. The debate centers on whether institutions are primary drivers of economic growth, or if they merely facilitate other key factors such as geography or culture. One position emphasizes the direct causal role of institutions in fostering economic growth through mechanisms like secure property rights and efficient governance. A rival position argues that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent upon other factors such as population density and prosperity. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are autonomous drivers of long-run economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with broader historical and geographical contexts.

One stream of scholarship directly links institutional quality to long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonizers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive ones that encouraged investment to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. The type of institution set up correlated with contemporary income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic development. This perspective is complemented by North and Weingast (1989, p.2) who argue that credible commitment underpins growth. They contend that the political factors governing how rules are enforced and changed are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. These findings collectively underscore the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic trajectories, as they provide the framework within which economic activities occur and thrive.

Another stream of scholarship examines how specific institutional arrangements influence economic development over time. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe. This view is supported by North (1989, p.9) who posits that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. Austin (2008, p.15) further notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, indicating a dynamic interplay between institutions and local contexts. This stream of scholarship highlights how specific institutional arrangements can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth depending on historical and contextual factors.

Institutional quality is not the only determinant of economic development; distributional conflicts also play a significant role. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, influencing how institutions are formed and function. For instance, North and his co-authors ascribe economic failures in Latin America to a system of shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, which has blocked the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) maintain that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth. These findings underscore how distributional conflicts can shape institutional outcomes and economic development, as they influence the incentives and constraints under which institutions operate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and complex. While institutions provide the framework within which economic activities occur, their impact is contingent upon broader historical, geographical, and cultural contexts. Understanding this interplay is crucial for fostering sustainable economic development over generations.

North identifies institutions as critical determinants of economic growth, highlighting that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional evolution was pivotal in fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, dramatically lowered the cost of transacting. These developments reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is essential for both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further emphasize that these institutions were about governing economic exchange and enforcing rules and ensuring commitment to them, which was crucial for economic development.

The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalysed a trade-off where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, leading to the establishment of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement. This process reduced transaction costs per exchange, even as the volume of transactions increased due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The institutional framework in England thus facilitated market exchanges by lowering the costs associated with each transaction, thereby promoting economic growth. This nuanced view underscores that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is contingent upon broader historical and political contexts.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie contends that institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by struggles over resource distribution, challenging the notion that these institutions emerged solely from efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutional structures advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implications of these findings are significant: if institutions are primarily shaped by distributional conflicts, then efforts to promote economic growth through institutional reform must address underlying power dynamics and inequalities. Ogilvie cautions that this approach should be integrated into a broader research program that considers the multifaceted nature of institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and extractive incentives, which persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This framework highlights the role of initial conditions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. These institutions, characterized by weak property rights and lack of rule of law, hindered investment and economic development.

This finding underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic trajectories. The persistence of these institutions in former colonies suggests a complex interplay between power dynamics and institutional design. This pattern illustrates how initial colonial policies can create path-dependent trajectories, where early institutional choices constrain future economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African economic performance under colonial rule has been misunderstood. They show that many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous narratives about persistent poverty. This finding is supported by historical national accounting data, which reveals significant economic activity and growth in export production tied to foreign trade (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This pattern indicates that colonial rule did not uniformly impede African economies. The timing and scale of growth varied due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, factors that influenced the market for exports and thus shaped economic trajectories.

This variation illustrates how institutional factors can mediate economic outcomes even within a shared colonial context. Frankema and van Waijenburg note that the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about long-term African welfare development unanswered (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This gap highlights the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the impact of institutions on economic growth in Africa.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon found that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust. This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative endeavors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin notes that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, which suggests that local agency could mitigate some institutional legacies of the slave trade. The deep-seated mistrust fostered by centuries of exploitation persists, influencing contemporary African societies' ability to build trust-based institutions essential for economic growth. This enduring legacy highlights a critical mechanism through which historical institutions continue to shape long-run economic outcomes in Africa.

Mokyr's work, shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggests that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This perspective underscores how technological progress requires institutional adjustments to manage conflicts arising from modernization, which Kuznets notes can disrupt societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Thus, the interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained growth. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is generally irreversible, institutional improvements must be equally robust to prevent erosion by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while institutions are not the primary drivers of economic growth, they play a critical role in sustaining the benefits of technological advancements.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and complex, as demonstrated by various studies that highlight different aspects of institutional influence. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacts current income per capita, with extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies persisting and affecting long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding is supported by North's argument that institutions matter greatly for economic growth and development, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms critical for market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The historical context of institutional evolution is crucial. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast illustrate how the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, highlighting a critical political factor: the regime's commitment to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, demonstrating that institutions can evolve to support economic growth under the right conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). North argues that the creation of financial instruments during England's financial revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Institutional change is influenced by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, showing how these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maximize rents and promote long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to colonial powers and support long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement between these views is the evolution of institutional frameworks in response to local economic conditions, as demonstrated by the adaptation of policies by colonial administrations. This shows that institutions can evolve to support growth when they are responsive to local needs and pressures, rather than being static or extractive (Austin 2008, p.15).
