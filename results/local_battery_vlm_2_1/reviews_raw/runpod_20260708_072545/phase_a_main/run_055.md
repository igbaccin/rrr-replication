The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The central debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, play more significant roles. One side argues that institutions, by defining property rights and enforcing contracts, create an environment conducive to investment and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North (1989, p.9) contends that the creation of secure property rights through effective political institutions is critical for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6), whereas Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) assert that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income levels in former colonies, regardless of geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests that distributional conflicts are more important than efficiency in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary cause or a secondary effect of economic growth. This question is pivotal for understanding the mechanisms behind sustained economic development and the effectiveness of policy interventions aimed at fostering growth.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes is central to this discussion. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonists established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, with extractive states in high-mortality areas and more inclusive institutions in low-mortality regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences persisted over time and had a lasting impact on the economic performance of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial policies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) illustrate how secure property rights fostered by effective political institutions were crucial for economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The legacy of colonialism extends beyond direct institutional frameworks. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) identify that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust within Africa, which has persisted and significantly impacts current levels of trust and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural dimension complements the institutional framework by showing how historical events shape societal norms and attitudes toward economic interactions. The interplay between institutions and cultural factors underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic growth, where both formal rules and informal norms play critical roles.

The importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth cannot be overstated. This commitment to institutional frameworks ensures stability and predictability, which are essential for long-term investment and growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. Institutions define the rules governing economic interactions and shape cultural norms and societal attitudes toward trust and cooperation. The enduring effects of colonial policies and historical events highlight the importance of understanding the long-term impacts of institutional frameworks on economic development. This interplay between institutions, culture, and economic growth underscores the need for comprehensive policies that address both formal rules and informal norms to foster sustained economic development.

The Bank of England's creation in 1694 exemplifies this dynamic. This financial institution, along with new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs. They also increased the security of property rights over time. This institutional development fostered a long-term capital market and spurred economic growth. The result is an expanding transaction sector within the economy, as specialization and division of labor increase aggregate exchanges.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that this institutional evolution was driven by credible commitments from the English regime. This political factor underpinned economic growth and market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The commitment to enforce property rights established a stable environment for economic activity, which in turn enhanced market efficiency and overall economic performance. This underscores the importance of institutions governing public choice and their role in ensuring that rules are enforced effectively.

In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic performance. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the robustness of these findings holds across various measures of institutional quality, including constraints on executive power and property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This suggests that while colonialism introduced extractive institutions in many regions, the specific form these institutions took varied based on local conditions and European strategic interests.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that economic institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns alone. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions evolve primarily to enhance economic performance. For example, the persistence of serfdom can be attributed to the ability of landlords to maintain their control over labor rather than any inherent efficiency of the system. These authors demonstrate how differential factor endowments, such as land abundance relative to labor, led to extreme wealth inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped institutions that restricted competition and maintained elite dominance. The persistence of these unequal institutions had a lasting impact on economic development. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering both institutional and non-institutional factors in understanding long-run economic growth. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design highlights the complex dynamics at work in shaping economic performance over time, suggesting that institutions are not merely tools for efficiency but also arenas for power struggles.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought. The two papers demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, contra previous characterizations of the region's economic performance.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight that patterns of growing and shrinking in African economies were tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This pattern suggests that external trade played a crucial role in shaping economic growth during the colonial period. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) support this view by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965). Their findings indicate that real wages were higher than previously estimated, challenging the notion of persistent poverty in Africa due to structural impediments.

This reevaluation of historical economic performance in Africa underscores the importance of considering multiple factors beyond institutions alone when assessing long-run economic growth. The data-driven approach employed by these scholars not only corrects previous misconceptions but also provides a foundation for understanding the complex interplay between economic, social, and political factors that shape development trajectories over time.

The studies do not directly address institutions' role in long-run economic growth. This gap leaves room for further exploration into how institutional frameworks interacted with economic data to influence African economies during this period.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within African societies, which remains prevalent and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The prolonged duration of the slave trades, far exceeding the period of official colonial rule, highlights their profound and lasting impact on African economic development. This suggests that the legacy of mistrust and social fragmentation caused by the slave trade continues to hinder economic progress by eroding trust within communities and between different ethnic groups. The implications are profound: while institutions may play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, the enduring effects of historical traumas like the slave trade create additional barriers that must be addressed for sustainable development.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that the accumulation of technological advancements and knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who posits that epochal innovations, major breakthroughs in human knowledge, have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These innovations have spread to a substantial part of the world, driving technological progress and necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby ensuring modern economic growth is possible without excessive costs or long-term weakening of society's political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the adoption and diffusion of technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that while technological progress can be irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic. This implies that institutional progress must complement technological progress to ensure sustainable economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without such institutional support, the benefits of technological advancements could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the necessity of robust institutions in harnessing knowledge-driven economic growth for long-term development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that encourage investment and secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutional structures are fundamental to fostering an environment where economic transactions can occur efficiently. North highlights the significance of secure property rights, noting how the creation of institutions like the Bank of England reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is complemented by Acemoglu et al.'s observation that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups in colonies, influencing long-term economic outcomes. For example, colonies with extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole faced distinct challenges compared to those where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence shows that these institutional differences significantly impacted current income per capita, underscoring the enduring influence of early colonial policies on economic development.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further illustrated by their impact on technological progress and economic reasonableness. While technological advancements can drive growth, they are not sufficient on their own. Institutional support is crucial for harnessing these advancements effectively. Without robust institutions, the benefits of technology can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the necessity of complementary institutional progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical record shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth have often been detrimental to economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is evident in former colonies where extractive institutions, established during colonial rule, persisted and hindered economic progress. Regions with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment experienced more sustained economic growth.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement between the role of technology versus institutions lies in the case studies of former European colonies. These studies show that while technological advancements can drive short-term growth, long-run economic development is contingent on the presence of robust institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). For example, the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors or initial endowments. This historical evidence underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth and development, reinforcing the need for policies that foster secure property rights and enforce the rule of law.
