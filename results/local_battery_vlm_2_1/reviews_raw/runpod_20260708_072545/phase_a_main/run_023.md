The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some societies thrive economically over extended periods while others stagnate. This debate pits those who emphasize the primacy of institutional factors against those who attribute economic outcomes to other variables, such as geography or cultural beliefs. The disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors.

One stream of scholarship focuses on how colonial institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes, which in turn significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These authors highlight that where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, economic growth was fostered. Extractive states were established in regions with high mortality rates, resulting in enduring underdevelopment. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9), who emphasizes that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security in property rights, which are essential for both a robust capital market and sustained economic growth. This institutional framework provides a clear pathway through which colonial policies directly influenced economic outcomes.

However, Austin (2008, p.2) offers a nuanced view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood within patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone determine economic growth, highlighting the complexity of historical processes and the need for a more nuanced understanding of how different factors interact over time.

Another stream of scholarship proposes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective suggests that institutional outcomes are often driven by power dynamics and the interests of politically powerful groups. Acemoglu et al (2002) support this view by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their control and extract resources, illustrating how institutional design can be influenced by the interests of dominant elites. This stream underscores the importance of understanding power dynamics in shaping economic institutions.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice has been crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth include not only the rules governing economic exchange but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their success in seventeenth-century England shows that credible commitment to property rights is essential for sustained economic development. This perspective highlights the importance of institutional design in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic growth, emphasizing the role of political factors in shaping economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. While colonial institutions have had a significant impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies, distributional conflicts and power dynamics also play crucial roles in institutional design. The evolution of public choice institutions further underscores the importance of political factors in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Understanding these dynamics is essential for comprehending why some societies thrive economically over extended periods while others do not.

This institutional evolution was pivotal for fostering economic growth, as it facilitated market exchanges and supported long-term capital markets. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) elaborates on how the decline in transaction costs reflects increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and overall economic growth. This dynamic was driven by political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms.

The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, particularly those governing public choice, played a crucial role in this process. This institutional framework was essential for economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for market exchanges and long-term investments.

The political factors driving economic growth extend beyond the rules themselves to include how these rules are enforced and modified, emphasizing credible commitments' role in maintaining long-term economic development.

The emergence of financial institutions like the Bank of England exemplifies a broader pattern where institutional changes, driven by political dynamics, can significantly impact economic outcomes.

The establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions, such as the Congo and Gold Coast, versus the settlement colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, exemplifies how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. These differences were driven by European settlers' mortality rates and their subsequent policies toward economic development. The settlers who faced lower mortality rates established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). High-mortality areas featured extractive states intended to swiftly transfer resources back to Europe, thereby hindering long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The persistence of these colonial institutions over time underscores their enduring impact on economic development. These institutional legacies are evident in current income disparities, as regions with extractive colonial institutions continue to lag behind those with more inclusive ones. The mechanisms through which these institutions operate include the distribution of political power and the incentives for economic development. For instance, politically powerful groups may resist changes that could threaten their rents or political influence, thereby maintaining extractive institutions even when they do not maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic illustrates how institutional design can be shaped by short-term interests rather than long-term economic benefits.

Acemoglu et al. argue that in the context of former colonies, equilibrium institutions were likely designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-term growth. This perspective is crucial because it highlights the importance of understanding the historical origins and political motivations behind institutional design. The legacy of these colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes today, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn 2008 both argue that the legacy of the slave trade continues to influence contemporary African societies, impacting economic outcomes through various channels. One key mechanism is the erosion of social trust within affected communities. This mistrust persists today and significantly impedes economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, left deep-seated impacts on African economies. Colonial rule lasted approximately 75 years, indicating that the detrimental effects of the slave trade are more profound and enduring (Nunn 2008, p.2). The historical context reveals that centralized governments and well-developed trading networks facilitated sustained trade with Europeans, highlighting how institutional frameworks shaped economic interactions during this period (Nunn 2008, p.19).

This disruption had long-lasting effects on African economies, making it difficult for them to recover and develop sustainably. Nunn's analysis shows that Africa's slave trades were a significant factor in shaping its subsequent economic development.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) assert that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. The mechanism through which technology drives growth involves the efficient use of innovations, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that major breakthroughs in human knowledge, or epochal innovations, constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, necessitating societal adjustments. This suggests that while institutions may facilitate economic growth, they are not the primary drivers; rather, they adapt to technological progress.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvement's sustainability would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions play a crucial role in coordinating efforts and reducing access costs. In this sense, institutions are not merely reactive but also proactive in supporting the growth process.

This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions highlights the complex nature of long-run economic growth, where technological advancements set the pace while institutional frameworks provide necessary support.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) demonstrate that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by a combination of missionary efforts and the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African leaders and families. The demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, with new institutional frameworks enhancing the value of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions, particularly those introduced by British rule, played a pivotal role in shaping educational outcomes. The persistence of inequality in other colonial contexts highlights that while institutional changes can drive educational development, they do not uniformly benefit all societal segments. Sokoloff and Engerman note that the factor endowments and initial conditions in New World societies led to extreme inequalities, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This finding suggests that the benefits of colonial education were often constrained by pre-existing social structures, limiting the broader impact on economic development.

This perspective is shared with Ogilvie, who underscores the importance of considering institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe as distinct phenomena rather than merely efficient responses to economic conditions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Austin focuses on colonial contexts where European settlers were absent, showing that indigenous commodity production could align with colonial interests and influence institutional prescriptions. This dynamic is evident in cases like Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn’s hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). Institutional arrangements, Ogilvie argues, were shaped by distributional conflicts in addition to efficiency considerations alone, providing nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective qualifies the idea that institutional changes always drive broad-based economic development; instead, these changes often reflect and reinforce existing inequalities.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented across historical and contemporary contexts. North argues that institutions are critical for economic development because they define the rules governing economic exchange (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, which significantly influenced current income per capita in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These frameworks facilitated or hindered long-term economic growth by determining the nature of property rights and their enforcement.

The evolution of institutions is also closely tied to political dynamics. North and Weingast highlight that credible commitments from political regimes are essential for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This view is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that the institutional changes introduced during colonialism were pivotal in determining the long-term development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These studies collectively underscore that institutions are not merely economic constructs but are deeply intertwined with political structures and power dynamics.

Institutional arrangements can also reflect underlying social conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie argues that pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom or craft guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is reinforced by Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which simplifies causation and compresses different historical periods into a single narrative. Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the complex interplay between institutions and local contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced understanding of institutions challenges the notion that they always drive broad-based economic development; instead, these changes often reflect and reinforce existing inequalities.
