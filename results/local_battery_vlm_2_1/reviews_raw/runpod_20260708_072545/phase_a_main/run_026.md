The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economics, as it challenges the primacy of neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geopolitical conditions, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for sustained growth, while others contend that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs shape core economic institutions. The debate extends to whether institutional changes are revolutionary or merely incremental in their impact on economic performance. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North's emphasis on the institutional framework aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how different colonization strategies led to varied institution-building efforts across European colonies, with some establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up systems that supported economic development by promoting investment. This institutional framework was pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for economic activities.

North (1989, p.9) also notes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). These developments reflect increased security in property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth. North’s analysis underscores how political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement can lead to falling transaction costs per exchange, thereby facilitating market exchanges and boosting overall economic activity (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) build on this by emphasizing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that the long-term impact of institutions on economic growth can be profound, shaping a colony's trajectory decades after initial settlement. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also point out that these institutions are often shaped to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the quality and intentions behind institutional design significantly influence economic outcomes.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the development of credible commitment mechanisms within these institutions allowed markets to flourish by providing a stable framework for economic exchange. This institutional change was critical because it provided a secure environment for investment and capital accumulation. However, the impact of these institutions extended beyond economic stability; they also shaped societal structures by influencing how power was distributed and exercised. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that in New World societies with greater inequality, elite groups often used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their privileged status (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic is significant because it illustrates how the institutions governing public choice can have far-reaching effects on economic development. The persistence of inequality in these societies suggests that institutional design can either foster inclusive growth or entrench existing power structures, depending on the specific mechanisms in place. Economic historians have long recognized the importance of factor endowments and geographic factors in shaping development paths (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). However, the role of institutions in mediating these influences underscores their significance in determining economic outcomes over the long run.

Van Zanden and Pamuk both focus on Europe's economic development during specific historical periods (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). They show that the relationship between economic development and structural changes is complex. Structural changes in economic development can occur without a corresponding improvement in living standards over long periods. For instance, van Zanden shows that there was no clear relationship between economic growth and improvements in living standards in Europe from 1500 to 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This period saw significant shifts in economic activity, with the focus moving from southern European countries like Italy to northern regions such as Holland and England. These changes had profound effects on relative prices and the cost of basic foodstuffs, reflecting broader structural transformations within Europe's economies (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Pamuk further elaborates that these structural changes were not uniform across Europe. He shows that by 1450, a wage gap emerged between northwestern Europe and other regions due to long-term effects initiated by the Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland allowed for rapid responses to new economic opportunities. This was in stark contrast to regions such as Tuscany, where existing monopolies were strengthened, limiting adaptability. These findings underscore that structural changes in Europe’s economy were influenced not only by exogenous shocks but also by the underlying institutions and their ability to foster or hinder adaptive responses (Pamuk 2007, p.16).

British colonial education policies played a pivotal role in shaping early twentieth-century economic performance in Anglophone African economies (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that these regions achieved per capita income levels above subsistence. This economic development was driven by the establishment of formal education systems, which were more extensively implemented under British rule compared to other colonial powers. Frankema (2012, p.15) demonstrates that demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, indicating a strategic adaptation to new social and political institutions. The implementation of these educational systems altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were tied to new economic opportunities. These educational policies created institutional frameworks that fostered adaptive responses to changing economic conditions, thereby facilitating growth. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further note that the timing and scale of this growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability underscores the conditional nature of institutional impact on economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) share the view that the reversal of fortune among former European colonies is not simply due to institutional legacies from colonial rule. This perspective acknowledges that economic outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including indigenous economic actions and political responses within the colonial framework. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local incentives created by indigenous actions (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could align with local interests. This adaptive behavior suggests that institutional changes under colonial rule were not monolithic but rather responsive to the economic and political dynamics of the colonies themselves. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates on this by noting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, but also by broader institutional capabilities (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view underscores that while colonial institutions played a role, they did so within a context shaped by local conditions and adaptive responses.

Ogilvie and Nunn and Wantchekon argue that distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Ogilvie's analysis suggests that the institutions of serfdom, community governance, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by underlying power struggles rather than efficiency concerns. This focus on distributional conflicts highlights how economic institutions are not merely products of rational design but are deeply influenced by social dynamics and power imbalances. Nunn and Wantchekon extend this perspective to Africa where the slave trade created a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting current levels of intergroup trust (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This enduring effect demonstrates how historical distributional conflicts can have lasting impacts on institutional development and economic outcomes. Ogilvie's call for institutions to become an indispensable tool in economic history underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of these dynamics, emphasizing that institutions are not just structural elements but are embedded within broader social contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Nunn and Wantchekon further elaborate on this by showing how internal norms of mistrust, shaped by historical events like the slave trade, play a significant role in economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional formation suggests that understanding the roots of contemporary economic challenges requires delving into the historical power dynamics that shaped institutions.

The growth of useful knowledge is pivotal in driving economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) establish. They argue that technological advancements, which are rooted in the accumulation of practical knowledge, fuel long-term economic development. This perspective highlights a specific mechanism: the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments. Technological innovations require corresponding changes in institutions to be efficiently utilized, as Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.54) elaborates on the point that modern technology is incompatible with traditional social structures; thus, economic growth necessitates both technological advancements and institutional adaptations. Mokyr also points out coordination failures that can hinder sustained economic improvement if not addressed by institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay suggests that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic sustainability. This dynamic underscores the importance of both technological innovation and institutional evolution in shaping economic growth trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing how resources are allocated and how incentives are structured (North 1989, p.6). North contends that institutions matter because they affect transaction costs, which in turn influence the efficiency of resource allocation. This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted long-term economic outcomes. The English financial revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and enhance economic growth (North 1989, p.9). In contrast to neoclassical theories, which focus on population and savings as key determinants, North emphasizes that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of institutions is further illustrated by the divergent paths taken by European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks, with some encouraging investment and rule of law while others established extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design has had lasting effects on current income per capita levels across these regions. Austin's work adds nuance by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, showing how different historical periods and paths must be considered to fully understand institutional impacts (Austin 2008, p.2). The development of financial instruments and institutions in England during the 17th century is a clear example of how secure property rights can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs. This historical evidence supports North's argument that institutions are fundamental to long-term economic success.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is also significant, as Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of extractive institutions in some colonies can be attributed to the interests of politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo rather than fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. further show that equilibrium institutions may prioritize the extraction of rents over long-term growth, highlighting how institutional design reflects the power dynamics of the time (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial policies and their lasting effects on economic development underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Hopkins emphasizes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with North's view that effective property rights and enforcement mechanisms are key to economic growth. The historical experience of Africa shows that institutional quality matters more than ethnic fragmentation in determining the provision of public goods and the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
