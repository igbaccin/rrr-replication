The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and political science, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At stake intellectually are competing explanations for why some countries thrive while others stagnate. One live disagreement concerns whether institutions that support secure property rights and contract enforcement are necessary conditions for sustained economic development. A second debate hinges on the sources of institutional change: are they driven by distributional conflicts or by external factors such as colonial policies? A third question is whether institutions operate independently from other variables, such as geography, or if their impact is mediated through these variables. This review will consider how institutions shape long-run economic growth by examining different streams of scholarship that address these debates.

The first stream of literature emphasizes the critical role of institutional quality in determining economic outcomes. North argues that institutions matter for economic growth and development by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which lowers transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. also highlight the importance of institutions, arguing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The quality of institutions is not only about property rights but also about how they are enforced, as emphasized by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency all correlate with economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This stream underscores that institutional quality is a key determinant of long-run economic growth.

The second stream focuses on the role of colonial policies in shaping institutions and economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be traced back to historical processes of colonization and exploitation. Similarly, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). This stream demonstrates that colonial policies played a significant role in shaping institutions and, consequently, long-run economic growth.

The third stream examines the relationship between distributional conflicts and institutional change. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and struggles over resources rather than purely economic considerations. This stream shows that institutional change is driven by underlying power dynamics and struggles over resources.

The final stream explores the potential of institutional reforms in addressing poverty, particularly in Africa. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective highlights the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. By providing insights into how institutions can be reformed to promote development, this stream offers a pathway for addressing global poverty through institutional change.

The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. The reviewed literature underscores that institutions play a crucial role in determining economic outcomes, whether through their quality, historical origins, or the dynamics of distributional conflicts. Understanding these mechanisms can inform policy efforts to promote sustainable development and reduce global poverty.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional development facilitated long-term capital markets and spurred economic growth through increased efficiency in market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further argue that the success of these institutions was underpinned by a credible commitment to rules governing public choice, which bound the regime or sovereign to enforce these rules effectively. This commitment ensured that economic growth was not hindered by political instability or arbitrary rule changes, thereby fostering a stable environment for market development.

However, this perspective is nuanced by evidence from capital markets in England and France, suggesting that while institutional changes were significant, they did not solely account for differences in economic performance (North 1989, p.9). The elaboration of bureaucratic structures inherited from Louis XIV in France indicates that institutions alone are insufficient to explain growth disparities; other factors such as market integration and technological advancements also played a role. This finding underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where institutional changes interact with broader socio-economic conditions to drive long-run development.

The institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, but it did not operate in isolation from other factors influencing market development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) find that societies characterized by high levels of inequality tend to develop institutions that favor elites. This dynamic is evident in the New World colonies where extreme wealth disparities led to political structures that reinforced elite dominance, thereby restricting economic opportunities for the broader population. Kuznets (1973, p.6) underscores how institutional changes are necessary for technological advancements to drive growth efficiently. While this suggests that institutions can adapt to facilitate growth, Sokoloff and Engerman's findings indicate that in societies with entrenched inequality, such adaptations may not occur evenly or equitably. This disparity is evident in the persistence of inequality over time, as elite-controlled institutions often prioritize policies that maintain their advantages rather than fostering widespread economic development. The implication is that without addressing underlying inequalities, institutional reforms aimed at promoting growth may be limited in their effectiveness.

Acemoglu et al.'s work shows that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific mechanism through which these institutions operated was the differential colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. In regions where European settlers faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, rather than fostering local economic development.

The impact of these institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. This finding underscores the enduring influence of colonial-era policies on long-term economic trajectories. This qualification highlights that the impact of institutions on economic development can vary based on the interests of the politically powerful groups that shape them.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision and institutional change driven by both African initiatives and exogenous causes (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation. Ethnic homogeneity does not guarantee functional institutions, nor does heterogeneity preclude them; rather, the capacity to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions varies independently of ethnic composition (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British colonial rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa by altering the perceived benefits of education through new social and political institutions. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were influenced by a range of factors including industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. These expenditures often displaced public goods provision, as resources were directed towards consumption rather than broad-based services (Simson 2019, p.11). This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between colonial legacies and post-independence policies in shaping African economic development.

The slave trades in Africa have been argued to have significantly impeded economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) both demonstrate that the legacy of these slave trades extends beyond mere historical trauma, shaping contemporary socioeconomic dynamics. This detrimental impact manifests through a culture of mistrust, which has persisted over centuries and continues to affect intergroup relations. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that the duration of the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, far outlasting the period of official colonial rule. This historical context underscores the profound and enduring influence of the slave trades on African economic development. The trust deficit arising from these historical injustices has tangible economic consequences as well as being a social issue. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) assert that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the deep-seated nature of this problem. The pervasive lack of trust hinders economic development by undermining social capital, which is crucial for fostering cooperation and collective action. This dynamic is particularly detrimental in regions where strong institutions are necessary to support economic growth. Moreover, the impact of the slave trades extends beyond social trust to encompass broader institutional frameworks. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many African societies hindered their ability to engage in sustained trade with Europeans. This historical pattern suggests that the legacy of the slave trades has left a lasting imprint on Africa's economic institutions, shaping contemporary development trajectories. The enduring impact of these historical events underscores the need for policies that address both immediate economic challenges and the deeper structural issues rooted in historical injustices.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) show that colonial administrations adopted institutional prescriptions that were responsive to local economic and political actions, suggesting a dynamic interplay between institutions and their environment. This dynamic is evident in Austin's (2008) analysis of colonial governance where the Governor-General Clifford opted for higher production equilibrium contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This underscores how institutional responses can be shaped by local conditions and economic incentives, rather than being static or externally imposed. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates on this dynamic by arguing that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, often drove the formation of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the underlying motivations and power dynamics within societies when examining institutional impacts. Ogilvie's call underscores the need for a more nuanced approach to studying institutions, one that recognizes their complex and multifaceted roles in shaping economic outcomes. This nuance is crucial for appreciating how specific institutional arrangements can influence economic growth over time, even if they do not directly address the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding historical development trajectories. Institutions are pivotal in determining how resources and opportunities are distributed within societies, thereby influencing economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had lasting impacts on current income levels by affecting property rights and investment incentives. This perspective is supported by North's argument that secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The institutional framework set up by colonizers in different regions led to varying degrees of economic development, with extractive institutions hindering growth while inclusive ones fostering it. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-term economic performance. Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often adapted their policies based on local conditions and indigenous economic actions, further highlighting the dynamic role of institutions in economic development.

The evolution of institutions over time is influenced by both internal and external factors, including political dynamics and economic pressures. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional change reflects a broader pattern where political stability and secure property rights create an environment conducive to long-term investment and development. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting growth. This dynamic is evident in the colonial context, where European settlers established institutions that served their interests, often at the expense of long-term economic development in the colonies.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes is another critical aspect. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by North's emphasis on the political dimension of institutional change, where powerful groups influence the design and enforcement of property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence from colonial periods shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often hindered long-term growth in the colonies. This highlights the importance of understanding how power dynamics shape institutional outcomes.

The precise role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions remains a subject of debate, with some scholars emphasizing efficiency while others focus on political and social factors. The shared finding is that institutions matter fundamentally (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). There is disagreement over whether these institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by the interests of powerful groups.
