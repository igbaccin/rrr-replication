The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is understanding how enduring patterns of prosperity and poverty emerge over centuries. One live disagreement concerns the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that specific types of institutions, those that enforce property rights, ensure rule of law, and promote investment, are critical for sustained growth. Others contend that distributional conflicts and political power dynamics are more decisive factors in shaping institutional structures and their economic impacts. Additionally, there is debate over whether the effects of institutions are direct or mediated through other variables such as population density, geographic factors, or cultural beliefs. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for developing effective policies aimed at fostering long-term economic development.

The causal role of institutions in promoting long-run growth is central to Acemoglu et al.'s thesis that extractive colonial institutions significantly affected current income levels by maximizing rents to European colonists rather than encouraging long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with North's argument that the creation of efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic development. North illustrates this through the example of the Bank of England, whose establishment led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and reflected increased security of property rights, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These scholars share a focus on how institutional frameworks shape economic outcomes by influencing the security and efficiency of transactions.

Austin challenges the simplistic view that institutions alone determine economic trajectories. He argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Instead, he emphasizes the importance of considering the evolution of institutions over time and their interaction with other factors such as policy experience and investment patterns. This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who highlights that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. He notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily hinder the provision of public goods or the development of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the establishment of robust institutions is pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement. This financial revolution was a direct result of political decisions that traded certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which were driven by fiscal crises (North 1989, p.9). The development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and lowered costs per transaction, even as the size of the transactions sector grew (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast further highlight that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their success is evidenced by the development of capital markets, which shows that institutional changes were revolutionary in their impact on economic growth. The political factors underpinning this growth include a regime committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment ensures that institutions provide credible commitments, which are essential for economic development.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. highlight that Europeans established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In areas like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled permanently, institutions were designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence was pivotal in shaping long-term economic trajectories; extractive institutions stifled growth by discouraging investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. further explain that these institutions often persisted due to political power dynamics, where ruling groups benefited from maintaining the status quo rather than fostering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions can be seen in how European settlers who faced low mortality rates established more inclusive and growth-promoting institutions; conversely, high mortality rates led to extractive institutions that maximized short-term rents for colonizers at the expense of long-term economic gains. Acemoglu et al. also note that these institutional frameworks were not immutable; changes in political dynamics or external shocks could alter their trajectory (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Therefore, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence contemporary economic development by shaping current institutional frameworks and governance structures.

Institutional inequality in New World societies shaped economic outcomes by creating and maintaining policies that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and reinforcing the persistence of inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) posit that the distinct factor endowments of these colonies led to extreme disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception. These initial inequalities were perpetuated through institutions that concentrated economic gains among a small elite, limiting broader socio-economic development. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions against overemphasizing the role of institutions alone, arguing instead for a more nuanced understanding that includes shared beliefs and cultural factors. This perspective suggests that while institutions are crucial, they operate within a broader societal context shaped by deeply ingrained norms and values. The interplay between institutional frameworks and underlying cultural beliefs thus offers a richer explanation for economic development trajectories in these societies.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight how technology drives economic growth. Kuznets underscores that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments for efficient use; otherwise, conflicts between technological changes and societal structures can impede sustained growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that the sustainability of economic improvement relies on complementary institutional progress to mitigate coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The interplay between technological advancements and institutions is thus crucial. This dynamic underscores that while technological progress is a primary driver, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that can adapt and evolve alongside new innovations. The integration of technological advancements and adaptive institutions creates a robust environment for sustained economic growth.

However, the role of institutions in this context is not merely supportive but also dynamic. This highlights an important condition: the effectiveness of institutions in facilitating growth depends on their ability to manage and resolve societal tensions arising from technological progress. The success of economic growth thus hinges on both the generation of useful knowledge and the adaptive capacity of institutions to integrate this knowledge into society without causing undue disruptions.

Colonial rule in Africa fostered institutional change through both negotiated and imposed governance structures (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. These institutions were shaped by colonial policies that influenced the provision of public goods and the quality of governance. This educational infrastructure was crucial as it altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule thus created a framework where formal education could thrive.

The effectiveness of these institutional changes varied significantly across different regions and ethnic groups. This discrepancy suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was mediated by local contexts and adaptive capacities. The legacy of colonial education systems continues to influence contemporary African societies, shaping their institutional frameworks and economic trajectories. As a result, understanding the role of colonial institutions in Africa requires a nuanced examination of both the imposed structures and the negotiated adaptations that occurred within them.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that this mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experience of slavery. The paper finds that the adverse effects of the slave trade on trust are not merely a result of external environmental factors but are also driven by internal norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this idea, showing that Africa's economic development was significantly hindered by these slave trades. The paper argues that the adverse impact of slavery on African societies is not confined to historical periods but extends into contemporary times through enduring social and institutional structures (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the legacy of mistrust fostered by the slave trade continues to shape Africa's economic trajectory, influencing everything from individual behaviors to broader societal dynamics.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They show that African economic development is not solely shaped by colonial institutions but by a complex interplay of indigenous economic actions, political incentives, and policy responses. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates how colonial administrations tailored their institutional prescriptions based on local economic conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, in areas where European settlers were absent, the expansion of indigenous commodity production aligned with colonial interests. This finding underscores that African development is not a monolithic outcome of colonial policies but a dynamic process influenced by both external and internal factors. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further elucidate this complexity by reconstructing real wage levels in British Africa, revealing that poverty was not always persistent (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, they were just one piece of the puzzle. The broader implication is that understanding African development requires examining multiple historical paths and their interactions with local dynamics.

North (1989, p.9) illustrates this through the creation of the Bank of England, which significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. The persistence of extractive institutions in certain colonies highlights how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic development paths. This is evident in the differential outcomes between colonies with settler institutions and those without, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). For example, the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.
