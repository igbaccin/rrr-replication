Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but scholars disagree on how this influence manifests and which institutions matter most. One key debate centers around whether the primary driver of economic growth is the establishment of secure property rights through effective political institutions. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth, while other scholars suggest that the role of institutions may be more complex and multifaceted. For example, Austin (2008, p.2) proposes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The stakes are high: understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at fostering sustained development. This literature review synthesises scholarship on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal pathways that link institutional frameworks to economic outcomes.

The institutional changes in England during the 17th century, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), illustrate how secure property rights can be a pivotal outcome of evolving public choice institutions. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional evolution was not merely about establishing rules for economic exchange but also about creating mechanisms for enforcing these rules and ensuring that they could be altered when necessary (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of this institutional framework is evident in the robust performance of England's capital markets, which contrasts with traditional interpretations that overlook the role of institutions. However, North (1989, p.9) notes that as economies grow and specialize, the transaction sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP, indicating that while costs per transaction may fall, the overall volume of exchanges increases. This implies that the benefits of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms extend beyond immediate economic gains, fostering sustained growth over the long term.

The influence of distributional conflicts on economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies is evident through the work of Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These authors show that the persistence of inequality in these societies was maintained through government policies that favoured elites, which restricted competition. This mechanism operated as a response to distributional pressures faced by the elite, who sought to preserve their wealth and power. The institutions that emerged from this process were designed to maintain existing inequalities rather than promote efficiency or growth.

The specific mechanism of maintaining inequality through institutional design was exemplified in New World societies with greater initial inequality, where elites implemented policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional setup not only preserved the elite's advantages but also limited opportunities for broader economic development. This means that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional design is mediated by broader societal norms and values.

Colonial rule in Africa had multifaceted impacts on economic development, including contributions by women and the role of education under British administration (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Hopkins emphasizes that colonial institutions altered African societies' incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This institutional impact was not uniform across colonial powers; Frankema highlights how British rule fostered formal education more than other colonial regimes did. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule changed the perceived benefits of formal education at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). However, this educational push had a gender dimension: Akyeampong and Fofack reveal that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crops. This codification of customary law under colonial rule further marginalized women's economic roles (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dynamic suggests that while British rule may have fostered education, it also entrenched gender inequalities that limited broader economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon's findings on the slave trade in Africa suggest that historical exploitation can lead to a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2), which has long-lasting effects on economic development. This mistrust is not merely an artifact of historical events but rather a persistent social norm that has shaped contemporary African economies. The internalization of this mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon argue is at least twice as large as the effect stemming from external environmental factors such as rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), highlights how deeply ingrained these norms are within societies. This suggests that while institutions may shape economic growth, they must contend with entrenched social attitudes that can either reinforce or undermine their effectiveness. Nunn further elaborates on this dynamic by noting that the duration and intensity of Africa's slave trades were far more prolonged than colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This extended period of exploitation disrupted traditional economic institutions, making it challenging for subsequent development efforts to take hold. In fact, Nunn notes that sustained trade did not occur until regions with centralized governments and developed markets emerged (Nunn 2008, p.19), indicating that the slave trades' adverse effects were amplified in areas lacking strong institutional frameworks. This interplay between historical exploitation and institutional development underscores the complexity of Africa's economic trajectory, suggesting that addressing contemporary challenges requires acknowledging both immediate and deeply rooted issues.

Mokyr's insights into technological progress and economic growth underscore the importance of expanding useful knowledge for sustained development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective suggests that the accumulation of technological advancements and intellectual discoveries drives economic improvements more fundamentally than institutional frameworks. Kuznets supports this view, noting that epochal innovations constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These breakthroughs not only propel economic progress but also necessitate institutional adjustments to harness their full potential effectively. Mokyr further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements could be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This dynamic underscores the importance of aligning institutional developments with technological progress to ensure long-term economic sustainability.

Acemoglu et al. assert that colonial institutions set up by Europeans in their colonies were central to determining long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This thesis is shared with Austin who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, with institutions varying from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is supported by Austin’s findings that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating a dynamic interplay between local conditions and colonial policies (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The downstream implication of these institutions is significant: Acemoglu et al. show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact was mediated by complex historical processes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al. who highlight that European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those enforcing the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences significantly influenced current income per capita. North elaborates on this point, asserting that institutions matter because they determine transaction costs and the enforcement of property rights, both critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6).

Institutional frameworks involve distributional conflicts as well as efficiency. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Austin, who notes that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a complex interplay between local conditions and institutional design (Austin 2008, p.15).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.
