The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods; this knowledge can inform policymaking and development strategies. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they shape incentives for investment and economic activity, while others posit that the efficiency of institutions is secondary to their distributional effects. Still others consider that cultural beliefs and values underpin institutional structures. The interplay between these perspectives is crucial for understanding the mechanisms through which societies achieve sustained economic development. The debate extends into how different historical periods and paths have influenced current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the institutional developments in England during the 17th century played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically lowered the cost of transactions, which was crucial for long-run economic growth. This reduction in transaction costs reflected an increased security of property rights over time, a critical factor for both capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms would lead to falling costs per transaction as the economy grows (North 1989, p.6). The size of the transactions sector, as a proportion of GNP, expands due to increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) underscore that the success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets, highlighting the role of political factors underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implications of these institutional developments vary across different contexts. Recent research has challenged traditional interpretations by suggesting that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was closer to England's than previously thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This nuance indicates that while institutions significantly influenced economic growth in England, other factors may also play a substantial role in different historical and geographical settings.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the varying mortality rates among Europeans in different colonial settings led to distinct institutional frameworks. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This pattern is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, thereby locking these regions into a path of underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. also note that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development, as they are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term gains over long-term prosperity.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This simplification overlooks the nuances of how institutions evolve over time and interact with local political dynamics. Austin's work underscores that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which means that institutions were not static but rather dynamic entities shaped by local contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate the importance of trust as a fundamental factor for economic development, highlighting how historical events such as the slave trade have left lasting impacts on social norms and institutional frameworks within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internalisation of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, has significantly hindered the ability to foster institutions conducive to growth. This dynamic interaction between local and colonial forces shows that institutional outcomes are contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they operate. The complexity of these interactions underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth, particularly in regions with diverse historical experiences such as Africa.

The literature on African economic development and colonial legacies explores how institutional changes influenced public goods provision but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, suggesting that the quality of institutions may have a more critical impact on African economies than previously understood. The role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This focus on wages and living standards does not directly address the broader question of how institutions shape long-term economic growth. The literature on African economic development has made significant strides in understanding the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, but more work is needed to fully understand their impact on long-run economic growth. This underscores the need for further research that specifically addresses the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-term economic performance in Africa.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that these distributional conflicts were more instrumental than efficiency considerations in the development of institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. The persistence of these institutions can be traced to the power dynamics between different social groups, where elites leveraged their influence to maintain structures that favoured their interests. This dynamic is particularly evident in the context of New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Sokoloff and Engerman elaborate on how these inequalities were perpetuated through institutions that restricted competition and advantaged elites. Ogilvie further qualifies this by noting that the persistence of these institutions was due to shared beliefs within society (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This highlights that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the broader social context in which these conflicts occur also plays a significant role in institutional development. The interplay between power dynamics and societal norms creates a complex landscape where institutions evolve from economic necessity and deeply ingrained social structures.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North argues that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, which are essential for economic development. He shows how secure property rights can lead to lower transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. This is evidenced by the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. similarly emphasize that institutions shape long-run economic growth by determining how resources are allocated and who benefits from them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Ogilvie notes that distributional conflicts within society are crucial in institutional development. She argues that shared beliefs and societal norms play a significant role in shaping institutions, which often persist due to these social structures rather than solely economic efficiency (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. For example, the institutions established in colonies were often aimed at transferring resources back to the metropole rather than fostering local economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of societal norms and shared beliefs is further underscored by North's work on transaction costs, which shows how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can reduce costs per transaction. This reduction in transaction costs is crucial for economic growth as it encourages investment and market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Austin adds to this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth hinges on whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency or distributional conflicts. North shows that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth, while Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence of colonial institutions in Africa settles this disagreement by showing how extractive institutions were established to transfer resources back to the metropole rather than promote local economic development, highlighting the role of distributional conflicts in shaping long-run economic growth.
