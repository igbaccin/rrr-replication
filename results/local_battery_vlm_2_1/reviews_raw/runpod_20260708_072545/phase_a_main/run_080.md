The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, as it addresses how societies create and maintain the conditions for sustained prosperity. Institutions are the rules that govern human interactions, encompassing formal laws, informal norms, and organizational structures. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars emphasize the direct causal impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, while others argue for the importance of historical context and path dependency in shaping institutional evolution. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. The stakes are high: understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth can inform policy design and economic development strategies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2) both argue that European colonial strategies led to different institutional outcomes in colonies, with settler colonies establishing more growth-enhancing institutions than extractive ones. These scholars highlight the enduring impact of colonial institutions on current economic performance. Austin (2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He points out that in some cases, colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting a more complex interplay between institutions and local conditions. The interaction between colonial policies and local responses reveals the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) both emphasize the critical role of institutions in economic growth by highlighting how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts as well as efficiency-driven considerations, which can lead to outcomes that prioritize rent-seeking over economic development. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics underlying institutional design.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) (North 1989, p.6) both argue that colonial institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting how initial conditions can shape institutional outcomes. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not conducive to economic development, as they are shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maintain their rents or political power. This perspective underscores how institutional changes can have profound long-term effects on economic performance.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (Austin 2008, p.15) both highlight that a credible commitment to property rights is essential for sustained economic growth, emphasizing the political factors underpinning this commitment. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, involving complex interactions between historical context, political dynamics, and local responses. Understanding these interactions is crucial for designing effective policies that promote sustained prosperity.

The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security in the time dimension of property rights. This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) explains that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This political exchange set a precedent for future institutional developments, illustrating how public choice institutions can shape property rights and, consequently, economic outcomes.

The role of credible commitments in this process is crucial; North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the regime's commitment to enforce these rules is a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment underpins the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which are essential for sustained economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, where secure property rights fostered a stable environment for investment and economic activity.

This pattern suggests that while institutions are pivotal for long-run economic growth, the immediate effects of institutional changes can be complex and varied.

This influence was mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions. The persistence of these extractive institutions, which did not encourage investment or enforce the rule of law, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19), a pattern that is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) finding that "Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies," which resulted in vastly different institutions. These findings suggest that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not merely a function of their immediate effects but also of their enduring influence on the institutional landscape of former colonies, which in turn shapes long-run economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) along with Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight how historical institutions shaped by inequality have influenced economic outcomes in Africa. These scholars show that the legacy of colonialism and slavery has had profound effects on institutional development, which in turn shapes long-run economic growth. The impact of these historical institutions is evident in the varying degrees of inequality they fostered; for instance, New World societies with greater inequality saw elites benefit from various government policies that restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This mechanism of elite capture and policy manipulation has been a significant barrier to inclusive economic development. The long-term effects of these institutional changes are evident in the persistent inequalities that continue to shape economic outcomes today. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this view by demonstrating how Africa's slave trades have had an adverse effect on subsequent economic development, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic conditions. However, Nunn also notes that the presence of a centralized government and well-developed trading networks in certain African kingdoms facilitated trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19), suggesting that not all institutional legacies are uniformly detrimental to growth. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between historical institutions and long-run economic outcomes, where specific institutional features can either hinder or support development depending on their interaction with local conditions.

The cluster of research around Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenges the notion that African poverty is an enduring historical phenomenon by examining economic performance in Africa before 1950. This work demonstrates that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more complex economic trajectory than previously thought. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era has enabled recent studies to reconstruct and compare real wage levels across British African colonies from approximately 1880 to 1965 (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This period saw significant variations in export production tied to foreign trade, local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors influenced the timing and scale of economic growth within and between countries. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner emphasize that economic patterns were tightly linked to external markets for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This focus on export markets highlights a specific mechanism through which historical trade networks shaped long-run economic outcomes in Africa. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has limited our understanding of these dynamics (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2), suggesting that further research is needed to fully grasp the long-term picture of African economic development.

According to Austin, the institutionalist approach in economic history, which emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, faces challenges in quantitative testing. Furthermore, this theory struggles to explain why institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others. This critique aligns with Ogilvie's examination of pre-industrial European economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds, which she argues are better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency. By focusing on the specific mechanisms through which colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic actions, Austin shows that these responses were shaped by fiscal security incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests a nuanced view of institutional change where local contexts play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' (1973) observations on epochal innovations, which highlights how major technological breakthroughs have historically driven long periods of economic growth. These innovations necessitate institutional adjustments to be effectively integrated into the economy. The role of institutions in this context is to facilitate the efficient use and diffusion of new technologies, not drive growth directly. This suggests that while institutions are important, their significance lies more in adapting to and supporting technological progress than being the primary drivers of economic growth. This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between technology, institutions, and economic development, indicating that while technological progress is fundamental, institutional factors can either enhance or hinder its impact on economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with historical contexts and political dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that European colonies established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, which significantly influenced current income levels. This highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.15) demonstrate how specific political institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth through their effects on property rights and transaction costs.

The evolution of financial institutions also played a crucial role in economic development. North (1989, p.9) notes that the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional change was pivotal for long-term capital markets and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) builds on this by showing how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, illustrating that institutions can evolve to support growth when aligned with local incentives.

The dynamics of distributional conflicts also shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues for a more nuanced understanding of pre-industrial European institutions, suggesting that they were shaped by distributional struggles rather than purely efficient considerations. This aligns with North and Weingast's emphasis on the role of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Both perspectives underscore how institutional frameworks are deeply embedded in political and social structures.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth involve complex interactions between historical legacies, political power, and economic incentives. North's argument that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights (North 1989, p.9) aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding is that the nature of institutional frameworks, whether supportive or extractive, is a critical determinant of economic growth. The remaining disagreement centers on the specific historical evidence used to measure these effects. Austin shows that colonial administrations could adapt their policies in response to indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15), whereas Acemoglu et al. emphasize the persistence of extractive institutions established during colonization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This discrepancy is settled by empirical comparisons of former colonies, which reveal that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were central to the reversal in relative incomes among these regions.
