The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a focal point for scholars seeking to understand the trajectory of nations' development paths. At stake is whether institutions are merely reflections of deeper structural factors such as geography and culture, or whether they possess causal power that can propel or hinder economic progress. This debate hinges on two primary positions: one emphasises the independent role of institutions in driving growth, while the other posits that institutions are epiphenomenal to more fundamental determinants. The disagreement centres around whether institutions themselves are the engines of long-term economic change. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions is pivotal in lowering transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies this process, as it facilitated secure property rights and reduced transaction costs significantly. This financial revolution was both an economic phenomenon and a political one, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further underscore this point, highlighting that the success of these institutional changes is evident in capital markets. The commitment to rules by the regime or sovereign is a critical political factor in this context. The aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies due to increasing specialization and division of labor, resulting in a growing proportion of GNP being dedicated to transactions (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional development in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that these institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income per capita. The nature of these institutions varied widely depending on European mortality rates in the colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe, whereas low mortality rates facilitated the development of institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for long-run economic growth. For instance, colonies with extractive states often failed to develop robust legal systems or effective governance structures, which hindered economic progress by increasing transaction costs and discouraging investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific pattern of colonization influenced both the initial development and long-term trajectory of these economies.

A key mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped economic growth was their impact on property rights and legal enforcement. This institutional framework discouraged innovation and efficient resource allocation, as individuals had little incentive to invest in long-term projects when the returns could be expropriated by colonial powers or local elites. These extractive institutions persisted over time, ensuring that negative effects on economic growth were deeply ingrained in the economic fabric of these societies.

The long-term implications of colonial institutional legacies are evident in the current economic performance of former colonies. Colonies that developed extractive states struggled to industrialize and modernize their economies, leading to persistent poverty and underdevelopment. Colonies with more inclusive institutions experienced higher levels of economic growth and development over time. This pattern underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that institutions shaped by inequality and the slave trade significantly influenced economic development in specific regions. These scholars argue that societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating disparities. This dynamic is evident in colonial Latin America, where elite-dominated institutions impeded broad-based economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) extend this argument by demonstrating how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. This institutional legacy persists today, affecting contemporary economic outcomes by reducing social capital and undermining the rule of law. The long-term effects of these institutions are profound: they have shaped regional economies by limiting opportunities for broad-based development and reinforcing existing inequalities.

Colonial rule in Africa had specific economic impacts that shaped long-run growth trajectories, particularly through gendered institutions and differential educational outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) note that colonial rule altered the status of women and the development of formal education, both central to African economies. The codification of customary law under colonial rule marginalized women's access to land for cash crops, which had significant economic implications. These educational shifts were tied to broader institutional changes that affected economic growth through human capital development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show how export markets influenced economic growth patterns across Africa, highlighting the role of political institutions in shaping these outcomes. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions, gendered access to resources, and educational development in driving long-run economic growth in Africa.

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 focuses primarily on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This body of work does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Instead it provides an empirical foundation to understand the conditions under which Europe's economy developed during this period. This finding is significant because it challenges the notion of an 'industrious revolution' in Western Europe during this time. Pamuk (2007, p.23) builds on this by arguing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, leading to a wage gap between northwestern and southern Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This divergence highlights how regional economic shifts influenced living standards across different parts of Europe. These studies collectively underscore the complex interplay of economic development and structural changes in shaping European growth patterns. However, Pamuk (2007, p.23) also notes that institutional flexibility played a crucial role in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This nuanced view suggests that while wage data and urbanization rates are important indicators, the institutional environment is pivotal in facilitating or hindering economic development.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently harnessing technological advancements to drive long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that the introduction of new technologies necessitates corresponding changes in institutions and ideologies, as modern technology often conflicts with traditional societal structures. This adjustment process is pivotal for sustaining economic growth over extended periods. Mokyr (2005, p.49) builds on this by emphasizing the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, which is essential for technological progress. These institutions facilitate the diffusion of innovations and help prevent rent-seeking behaviors that could erode economic gains. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) also highlights that these adjustments must be managed carefully to avoid excessive social conflict or political instability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving such conflicts peacefully, ensuring the sustainability of modern economic growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation underscores the multifaceted nature of long-run economic development, where efficient use of innovations requires both technological advancements and complementary institutional changes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that institutional theories often oversimplify causal mechanisms by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin maintains that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks these nuances, which are crucial for understanding economic growth in older industrial economies. This simplistic approach fails to account for why certain institutions favourable for growth were adopted in some countries but not others. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe further supports this critique by demonstrating that distributional conflicts provide a more robust explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency-based theories. She argues that the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds can be better understood through the lens of power dynamics and social negotiations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This implies that institutional theories must incorporate these historical complexities to accurately predict long-term economic growth.

Austin's critique extends beyond theoretical oversimplification, highlighting specific instances where colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous actions. For example, colonial governments often encouraged commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labour, demonstrating a dynamic interplay between local incentives and institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability challenges the static view of institutions as rigid structures, instead portraying them as responsive to changing economic and political conditions. Ogilvie's emphasis on distributional conflicts aligns with this perspective, suggesting that institutions are shaped by power struggles and negotiated settlements. Together, these insights underscore the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional change, one that acknowledges the role of historical contexts and social dynamics in shaping long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Institutions shape long-run economic growth through their role in establishing and enforcing property rights, which are critical for fostering investment and economic development (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's call to transform institutions into indispensable tools for economic historians, emphasizing their role in long-term research programs. By specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, political institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These insights highlight the importance of institutional frameworks that ensure secure property rights and encourage investment.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by Acemoglu et al.'s work on colonial institutions. They demonstrate that European colonists established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive, depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic development of these regions, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive ones fostering it. The persistence of these institutional legacies underscores how initial conditions can shape long-term economic trajectories.

Austin challenges simplistic views of institutional change by emphasizing the dynamic nature of colonial administrations' responses to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). He argues that colonial institutions evolved in response to indigenous economic activities and political pressures. This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's emphasis on distributional conflicts, suggesting that institutions are shaped by power struggles and negotiated settlements (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15). Together, these insights highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional change, one that acknowledges the role of historical contexts and social dynamics in shaping long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by North's argument that secure property rights are crucial for fostering investment and economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al.'s findings on the impact of colonial institutions, which show how initial institutional designs can have lasting effects on economic growth. North and Weingast's work on seventeenth-century England provides evidence that credible commitment underpins growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges.

The precise remaining disagreement centres on whether the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods. Austin argues that this thesis does not fully capture the complexity of institutional evolution and the dynamic responses of colonial administrations to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's emphasis on distributional conflicts, suggesting that institutions are shaped by power struggles and negotiated settlements. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional designs established by European colonists in their colonies, which had lasting effects on long-term economic trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15).
