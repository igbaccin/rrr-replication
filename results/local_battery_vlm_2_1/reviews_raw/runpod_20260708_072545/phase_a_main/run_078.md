The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development. Scholars disagree over the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some argue that institutions shape economic growth primarily by enforcing secure property rights and encouraging investment. Others contend that the impact of institutions is more nuanced, influenced by historical contexts and power dynamics. The debate centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. This discussion is crucial for determining how policies can be designed to promote economic development effectively.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) provide compelling evidence that institutions established during colonial periods have had lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These studies highlight how institutions can either foster economic growth through secure property rights and investment incentives or hinder it through exploitative practices. The findings suggest that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has had a persistent impact on long-run economic development, underscoring the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elucidate the role of institutions by emphasizing their critical function in reducing transaction costs and enforcing property rights. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic efficiency (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast extend this argument by highlighting how political institutions governing public choice played a crucial role in securing property rights and fostering economic development in seventeenth-century England. They note that the success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Their work underscores the importance of credible commitment in institutional design, showing how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) presents a different perspective by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie contends that shared beliefs within societies influence institutional structures, which in turn shape economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily designed to maximize economic efficiency and instead suggests that they often reflect power dynamics and distributional struggles. The implications of this view are significant for understanding how institutions evolve and their impact on long-run economic growth. Ogilvie's argument complements the findings of Acemoglu et al. by highlighting the complex interplay between historical contexts, power dynamics, and institutional design in shaping economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) further complicates the picture by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin suggests that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood within the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of considering long-term institutional trajectories and their interaction with other factors such as geography and culture. Austin's work challenges the notion that institutions are the sole drivers of economic growth and instead highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how various factors interact to shape long-run economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex and multifaceted, influenced by historical contexts, power dynamics, and interactions with other factors.

This dynamic was driven by greater specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the number of transactions in the economy. The commitment to secure property rights was essential for fostering economic growth as it provided a stable environment for investment and market development. This institutional framework not only reduced transaction costs but also ensured that the rules governing economic exchanges were consistently enforced, thereby creating an environment conducive to long-term economic prosperity in England.

Institutional frameworks in societies with high inequality have historically favored elites, shaping economic policies that restrict competition and perpetuate disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). High levels of inequality can lead to institutional arrangements that concentrate power and resources among the elite. This concentration often results in policies designed to maintain their dominance rather than foster broad-based economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show how New World societies with greater inequality established institutions that favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The slave trade’s negative impact on trust is particularly significant, as it undermines the social capital necessary for economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, significantly impeding economic growth. This suggests that the legacy of inequality and mistrust can have long-lasting effects on institutional development and economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of colonial institutions established was directly tied to the mortality rates Europeans experienced in these regions. This pattern is evident in the stark differences between colonies like the United States and Australia, which developed strong property rights and rule of law, and those like the Congo or Gold Coast, where extractive states were established. The persistence of these institutional structures long after colonial rule ended has had profound effects on economic development. This nuance suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, the specific nature of these institutions and their interaction with local contexts is critical. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic development trajectories in former colonies today.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa. These institutions created opportunities for Africans to engage with new economic activities, such as wage labor and trade, which in turn increased demand for skills that could be acquired through education. The introduction of missionary schools further facilitated this process by providing accessible educational infrastructure. This institutional framework laid the groundwork for economic growth by enhancing human capital development, a critical factor in long-run economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that while British rule may have been more conducive to education than other colonial powers, it did not uniformly benefit all regions or groups within Africa.

Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, contrary to Nunn’s hypothesis of lower production equilibrium. This suggests that local responses and adaptations played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes under colonial rule. The complexity of these interactions means that simple efficiency-based explanations do not fully capture how institutions evolve.

Mokyr's work, alongside Kuznets' findings (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1), argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective highlights technological progress as a pivotal factor in long-run economic development, suggesting that advancements in technology necessitate corresponding institutional adjustments to facilitate efficient use and diffusion. Kuznets' analysis indicates that technological changes often conflict with traditional social structures, such as rural lifestyles or extended family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These conflicts must be resolved through institutional reforms for sustained growth. Mokyr further elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge and fostering an environment conducive to economic reasonableness (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr's work also underscores that without these institutional adjustments, technological progress alone may not be sufficient to sustain long-term economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' observation that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, thereby ensuring the political stability necessary for modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). However, Mokyr acknowledges that institutional progress can be less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, highlighting the complex interplay between knowledge growth and institutional development. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, their impact on economic growth is contingent upon the adaptive capacity of institutions to support and integrate these innovations. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intertwined with the broader dynamics of technological change and societal adaptation.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework within which economic activity occurs. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, determine how resources are allocated and how investments are encouraged or discouraged. North argues that secure property rights, enforced through effective political institutions, reduce transaction costs and facilitate economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The evolution of institutions over time is crucial for understanding their impact on economic development. North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This concept underscores that stable and predictable institutional frameworks are essential for long-term economic prosperity. The historical evidence from seventeenth-century England, where political factors underpinned the enforcement of property rights, illustrates this point clearly.

The path to institutional development is not always linear or irreversible. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often drive the establishment of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and the interests of politically dominant groups, which can lead to the creation of extractive rather than growth-promoting institutions. For example, Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established different types of institutions in their colonies based on the expected benefits and costs (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights how institutional outcomes are influenced by both immediate economic incentives and long-term development considerations.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between technological change and societal adaptation. Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This indicates that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent upon a variety of factors, including the adaptive capacity of societies to integrate new technologies and institutional changes. The historical evidence from colonial administrations illustrates how indigenous economic and political action can influence institutional prescriptions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional development.
