The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, as it addresses how political, social, and legal frameworks influence the trajectory of societies over extended periods. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. One position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial for economic development. This view is supported by scholars who emphasize the importance of credible commitment mechanisms in fostering long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another perspective posits that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had profound effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. This stream highlights how extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth, have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interplay between these perspectives reveals that the study of institutions in economic growth has real-world implications for policy-making and development strategies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) highlights the English financial revolution as a key example, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs. This reduction was facilitated by increased security in property rights, which is crucial for both capital markets and overall economic growth. North's discussion underscores that the political model underlying these changes involved trading certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during fiscal crises, such as those stemming from the Hundred Years' War.

North also emphasizes that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are critical (North 1989, p.6). As economies grow, political institutions evolve to facilitate market exchanges, leading to a decline in costs per transaction. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that this process involves complexities. They suggest that the commitment of regimes or sovereigns to these rules is a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). If a regime is bound by these rules, it fosters an environment conducive to economic growth and market development. The role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights thus becomes a foundational element for sustained economic progress.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts were fundamental in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutional efficiency is the primary driver. Instead, it posits that these institutions evolved as a result of power struggles between different social groups. A key mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced economic institutions was the creation and enforcement of rules that favored elites. Ogilvie suggests that institutions like serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were efficient responses to economic conditions and shaped by the interests of dominant groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing how government policies in these regions often restricted competition and maintained inequality. The persistence of inequality in these societies can thus be attributed to the institutional frameworks that emerged from these conflicts. Ogilvie cautions against reducing institutions to mere efficiency tools; she advocates for a nuanced understanding that incorporates both efficiency and distributional aspects (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of considering power dynamics in the study of economic institutions, suggesting that future research should delve deeper into how these conflicts continue to shape modern economic systems.

Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied greatly, leading to either the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or extractive states designed for rapid resource extraction. This variation in institutional frameworks was driven by differences in European mortality rates, which influenced the type of colonial governance implemented (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who maximized rents and fostered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism explains how extractive institutions persisted and continued to affect economic development in former colonies. These findings are robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al.'s research highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes, demonstrating that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had profound effects on income per capita.

British colonial rule in Africa influenced education development in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema and Broadberry and Gardner argue that the impact of British colonialism was more nuanced than often portrayed. They show that formal education was developed under British colonial rule through demand driven by African political leaders and families making cost-benefit calculations (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). These developments were tied to new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education in Africa. The implementation of these institutions created a shift that fostered the development of formal education systems across sub-Saharan Africa. This process highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth by influencing human capital development through education. Frankema and van Waijenburg's work on real wages in British African colonies qualifies the extent to which this growth was sustained or hindered. They demonstrate that poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while colonial institutions may have initially fostered economic development through education, the long-term impact of these institutions on economic growth was more complex and varied across different regions and periods. The varying patterns of growth in export production further underscore the multifaceted nature of colonial impacts on African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that has persisted over centuries. This persistent mistrust is rooted in both external environmental factors such as the rule of law and internal norms of mistrust, with the latter being at least twice as large as the former (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.2) elaborates on this by showing how Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, significantly impacted economic development. Nunn notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the institutional framework of pre-colonial Africa played a crucial role in shaping its economic trajectory, with the slave trades exacerbating the negative effects on development.

van Zanden emphasizes that measuring long-term economic performance requires accurate data and understanding of predictors like the skill premium (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Skill acquisition is essential for economic development; van Zanden demonstrates that in western Europe, guilds played a pivotal role by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills. These institutions guaranteed fair execution of apprentice contracts and thus helped to reduce barriers to entry into trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The skill premium, which reflects the returns on investing in human capital, is influenced by various factors including training costs, institutional barriers, interest rates, and demographic considerations. This framework highlights how institutions can shape economic outcomes by influencing the supply of skilled labor (van Zanden 2009, p.3). Bolt and van Zanden caution that historical data for national accounts are often subject to significant margins of error, which can affect the accuracy of long-term economic performance measurements (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This underscores the need for collaborative research to improve the robustness of these estimates. The measurement challenges in long-term economic performance highlight the broader complexity of understanding and predicting economic growth, even when institutions facilitate skill acquisition.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological advancements can independently fuel long-run economic growth without necessarily relying on institutional adjustments. This perspective is supported by the idea that epochal innovations in technology are essential for sustained growth over extended periods. The pace of such technological progress, along with its impact on various production sectors and regions, varies significantly across different historical epochs (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this mechanism through a detailed analysis of the formal institutions that were established to reduce access costs to knowledge. For example, he highlights the significance of scholarly societies in Europe between 1600 and 1799, noting how these institutions played a crucial role in facilitating the dissemination of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This institutional support for technological progress involved creating new knowledge and ensuring its efficient use. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress to support technological advancements, economic improvements would be unsustainable and vulnerable to erosion by rent-seeking activities (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver, its effective integration into the economy depends on institutional adjustments that mitigate potential disruptions (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) re-evaluate historical economic theories and their impact on Africa, offering nuanced perspectives on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the role of colonial administrations in shaping African economies. For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, revealing how local incentives shaped institutional responses despite the broader colonial context. Governor-General Clifford's decision illustrates this dynamic; he opted for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). The complex interplay between colonial administrations and local economic actions highlights that institutions evolved in response to various incentives. Furthermore, Hopkins underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing investment incentives essential for development, suggesting that understanding institutional evolution is crucial for eliminating poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin's critique indicates that the new institutionalist approach has limitations in explaining why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2), highlighting the need for further research into the specific conditions under which institutions foster economic growth. This nuanced view of institutional dynamics challenges simplistic narratives about African economic history and underscores the importance of contextual factors in shaping long-term economic performance, particularly when comparing different historical trajectories.

Institutional differences significantly shape long-term economic growth by influencing investment and productivity patterns. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates have persisted (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions prioritize resource extraction over local development, leading to lower income per capita today. Similarly, North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), thereby facilitating economic growth by securing property rights over time. This dynamic illustrates how specific institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder long-term economic performance.

Austin acknowledges that institutional approaches have limitations in explaining why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not others (Austin 2008, p.2). He argues that colonial administrations often adapted their policies to encourage indigenous commodity production, indicating a more nuanced relationship between institutions and economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by Hopkins's assertion that the quality of public goods provided by African governments cannot be solely attributed to ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Instead, effective institutions can develop in both homogeneous and heterogeneous societies, suggesting that institutional effectiveness is context-dependent.

Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), highlighting the role of power dynamics in shaping institutions. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth, particularly in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that institutional design is often influenced by the interests of dominant groups, which can lead to institutions that prioritize short-term gains over sustainable development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is grounded in their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs. North argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that extractive institutions, which do not encourage investment or development, can persist due to the interests of politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence of colonial policies and their long-term economic impacts settles the disagreement by showing how institutional frameworks designed for resource extraction have led to persistent economic disparities.
