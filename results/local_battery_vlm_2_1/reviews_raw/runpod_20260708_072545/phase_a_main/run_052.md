The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics and political science, with scholars disagreeing over the mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. At stake is understanding how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One position posits that institutions are central to sustained economic development; another argues that other factors, such as geography or culture, play more decisive roles. Institutions can facilitate growth through secure property rights and efficient enforcement of contracts (North 1989, p.6), while other scholars argue that the specific historical paths taken by different societies cannot be reduced to a single causal variable like institutions. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in tandem with other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic conditions. This question is crucial for understanding why some nations thrive economically while others stagnate. Institutions can be shaped by politically powerful groups to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), which complicates the picture further.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs. This institutional evolution fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This development was crucial for both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. The evolution of these institutions ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing rules, which underpinned economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also suggest that institutions impact economic growth in ways that are comparable to other factors such as bureaucratic structures. This nuanced view is supported by evidence from capital markets, which indicates that while institutional changes were significant, they were not the sole determinants of economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) also highlights how the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement reduced transaction costs. This reduction facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

Distributional conflicts were central to shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie's argument (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) that efficiency considerations are insufficient for explaining these institutions is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman who argue that the persistence of inequality in New World societies resulted from elites using government policies to restrict competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic played out through various mechanisms, including the control over land and labor resources. For example, Ogilvie's work on serfdom shows how these institutions were rooted in power dynamics that maintained social hierarchies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this logic to New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality was perpetuated through institutional frameworks that favored the elite. These institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, had long-lasting effects on economic development. Ogilvie argues that these institutions were rooted in power dynamics that maintained social hierarchies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding long-run economic growth, as it suggests that institutional changes driven by distributional conflicts can have enduring effects on economic performance.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by Europeans' settlement patterns and mortality rates, which determined whether they set up extractive or inclusive institutions. In areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. In regions with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had profound effects on economic development: colonies with extractive institutions experienced slower growth due to the lack of incentives for long-term investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing consistent results across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, reinforcing the idea that institutional quality is crucial for economic performance.

The persistence of these colonial-era institutions into the modern period underscores their enduring impact on economic growth. This perspective highlights how institutional design can prioritize short-term gains over sustainable development, a pattern observed in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) qualify this by emphasizing that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping long-run development trajectories. This suggests that while initial institutional frameworks were crucial, subsequent economic policies and global dynamics also influenced growth outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), together with Frankema and van Waijenburg (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), show that African economic performance during the colonial era has been underestimated. This underestimation is partly due to a lack of historical data, which has limited comparative analyses of Africa's economic growth and development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner point out that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2), indicating a more robust economic performance than previously thought. This finding is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies, which reveals that poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The variation in growth patterns across different regions was influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note the tight linkage between economic growth and foreign trade markets for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10), suggesting that external economic dynamics played a significant role in shaping African economies during this period. Frankema and van Waijenburg's emphasis on understanding long-term economic development and income distribution implies that internal factors also contributed to Africa's economic trajectory.

The interplay between these local and global forces underscores the complexity of African economic development under colonial rule, highlighting that multiple determinants shaped growth outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that this mistrust is deeply ingrained and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, which could have influenced the development of these norms over time (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that while colonial institutions may have been imposed externally, the resulting mistrust was internalized and became a persistent feature of African societies. The legacy of the slave trade thus continues to shape contemporary social dynamics, influencing economic development by affecting trust in both internal and external environments.

The significance of this mistrust extends beyond social interactions, impacting broader economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) note that trust is crucial for various aspects of economic development, including international trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This implies that the persistence of mistrust due to the slave trade may have hindered Africa's integration into global markets, further complicating its economic trajectory.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) (Kuznets 1973, p.1) propose that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) complements this view by noting that advancements in technology necessitate institutional adjustments for efficient use and integration into society. For instance, modern technology may conflict with traditional rural lifestyles or family structures, requiring societal adaptations to accommodate these changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). Without such institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvements risk being unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors that erode gains over time. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) also acknowledges the importance of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them, indicating that while technological progress is crucial, it must be accompanied by institutional developments to ensure long-term sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Thus, the interplay between technology, knowledge, and institutions is complex, with each influencing economic growth in interconnected ways.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce effective governance structures. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive versus inclusive institutions during colonization significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions specifying efficient property rights are critical for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6).

Institutional frameworks that ensure credible commitment to property rights also play a pivotal role in economic development. North and Weingast highlight the importance of public choice institutions that bind rulers to commitments, which is essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, showing how extractive institutions can persist and affect long-term economic outcomes by maximizing rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The interplay between technological advancements and institutional development is crucial. Austin challenges the oversimplification of causation in the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, emphasizing that different historical periods and paths must be considered to understand how institutions influence economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). This aligns with North's argument that the creation of new financial instruments and the Bank of England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The historical evidence shows that credible commitment underpins growth. Austin emphasizes that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political action, illustrating how institutions can evolve in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie underscores the importance of understanding institutions as an indispensable tool for economic historians, highlighting the need to incorporate them into long-term research programs to better explain historical economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The specific historical evidence that settles disagreements between these perspectives is found in comparative analyses of colonial outcomes. Acemoglu et al.'s analysis shows that differences in European mortality rates led to distinct colonization strategies and associated institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This comparative approach, grounded in empirical data on institutional differences and their long-term economic impacts, provides a clear framework for understanding how institutions shape economic growth.
