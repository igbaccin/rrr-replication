The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in understanding the dynamics of economic development over time. At stake intellectually is whether institutions are fundamental drivers that can explain divergent economic trajectories across different regions and epochs, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technology play more decisive roles. The debate hinges on several key positions: some scholars argue that specific institutional frameworks, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are essential for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others posit that institutions are shaped by broader historical and cultural contexts, making their impact on economic growth contingent on these underlying factors. This disagreement underscores the need to examine how institutions interact with other variables over extended periods. The evolution of institutional frameworks themselves is subject to scrutiny: whether they emerge organically or are deliberately crafted to foster economic development remains a contentious issue (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.18).

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal for economic growth by establishing frameworks that facilitate market exchanges and secure property rights. The English financial revolution exemplifies this process; the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this mechanism by demonstrating that European colonization strategies varied widely: in some colonies, Europeans established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment; in others, they set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation had profound implications for long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this pattern by highlighting that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not promote long-term growth, underscoring the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence and impact of these colonial-era institutions on current income per capita illustrate the enduring effects of historical institutional frameworks.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth highlights how a regime's commitment to enforcing rules can drive market development. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) expands on this by emphasizing the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programmes, suggesting that understanding institutional evolution requires a nuanced approach that goes beyond mere efficiency considerations. This perspective underscores how secure property rights, facilitated by robust institutional frameworks, can catalyze economic growth by providing stability and predictability for market participants. The interplay between these factors suggests that while secure property rights are essential, they must be supported by institutions that address broader social dynamics to sustain long-term growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This was achieved through the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutional changes created incentives for Africans to pursue formal education, thereby contributing to human capital development in the region. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) observe that the timing and scale of economic growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability suggests that while British colonial rule had a positive impact on education, its effects were not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa. Nevertheless, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the differential outcomes observed under varying institutional frameworks.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa persists today as a culture of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue impacts contemporary levels of trust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is not primarily explained by its impact on the external environment such as rule of law but rather through internal norms of mistrust. This suggests that the legacy of slavery has deeply ingrained a culture of distrust within African societies, which hinders social cohesion and economic development. This prolonged period of exploitation likely entrenched mistrust as a fundamental aspect of social interactions. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that sustained trade and economic development were facilitated in regions with centralized governance and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom. This implies that while mistrust is a significant barrier to trust-based transactions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), it can be mitigated by strong institutions that support trade and economic activity. The presence of a national currency, developed trading networks and well-organized markets in the Kongo region indicates that effective governance structures can help overcome the adverse effects of mistrust on economic development, thus highlighting the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the new economic history of Africa both argue that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty. This shared claim establishes a mechanism whereby institutions shape long-run growth through their role in delivering secure private property rights. Hopkins (2009, p.18) elaborates on this, asserting that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This mechanism suggests that the quality of institutions, specifically their ability to ensure secure private property rights and provide incentives for investment, directly influences economic growth over the long run. Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests a conditional pattern where the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their adaptability to local contexts and incentives. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by pointing out that while ethnically homogeneous societies can be dysfunctional, heterogeneous ones can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This indicates that institutional quality is determined by broader socio-political factors as well as ethnic composition.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005; Kuznets 1973). Mokyr's argument aligns with Kuznets' observation that technological progress and institutional adjustments are intertwined in shaping long-run economic growth. The interplay between these elements is crucial: technological advancements necessitate corresponding adaptations in institutions to harness their full potential for growth. This dynamic ensures that the benefits of technological innovations are effectively integrated into the economy, preventing conflicts that could hinder sustained development (Kuznets 1973; (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.1)). Mokyr points out that while institutional progress is essential, it does not follow a linear path and can be subject to reversals. This implies that the sustainability of economic growth driven by technological advancements hinges on continuous institutional adaptation (Mokyr 2005; (Mokyr 2005, p.54)). In this sense, although institutions are indispensable for translating technological progress into sustained growth, they do not act as a standalone driver but rather as a complementary force.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue that has been extensively studied by economists and historians alike. Institutions matter significantly to economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). They are critical because they specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement, which leads to falling transaction costs as the economy grows (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This is evident in historical contexts such as the creation of the Bank of England, which facilitated a decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth. The institutional framework that underpins property rights and their enforcement is pivotal for long-term economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) point out that institutions are dynamic forces shaped by politically powerful groups. They can be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic nature is evident in the varying institutional strategies adopted by European colonizers. For instance, in regions like the United States and Australia, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas in areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These varying institutional strategies had profound impacts on current income per capita. The divergence in economic outcomes across former European colonies underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that the wealthiest regions in 1500 became the poorest by 2000 and vice versa, is a contentious issue. This perspective argues that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping long-run economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, it also acknowledges that this reversal was not solely due to geographic factors but rather the result of complex interactions between institutions and opportunities for industrialization during the nineteenth century. This perspective highlights how initial conditions and institutional frameworks set by colonial powers shaped subsequent economic development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions provides another lens through which to understand long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These conflicts can better explain the persistence of certain institutions, such as serfdom or craft guilds, than attributing their existence solely to efficiency considerations. This perspective is crucial because it underscores how power dynamics and distributional struggles within societies shape institutional outcomes.

The new economic history of Africa offers insights into how understanding historical contexts can inform contemporary institutional reforms (Hopkins 2009, p.18). By examining the impact of ethno-linguistic fragmentation on public goods provision, this perspective shows that societal homogeneity does not guarantee effective governance or progressive institutions. The quality of institutions in Africa is crucial for providing incentives for investment and driving development.

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). They are dynamic entities shaped by political forces, and their nature, whether extractive or growth-promoting, significantly impacts economic outcomes. The remaining disagreement revolves around the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. For instance, some studies emphasize the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while others focus on the impact of colonial policies and their long-term effects on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This disagreement is settled by examining historical evidence from former European colonies, which shows that extractive institutions established during colonial periods had lasting impacts on economic development.
