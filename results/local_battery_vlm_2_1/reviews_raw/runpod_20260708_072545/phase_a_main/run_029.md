The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development and historical scholarship. The stakes are high because the answer determines how to interpret centuries of economic divergence and convergence among nations and regions. Institutions can serve as both enablers and constraints on economic activity, influencing everything from investment decisions to technological innovation. A core disagreement revolves around whether institutions that promote growth are inherently efficient or if they arise primarily due to distributional conflicts. Some scholars emphasize the efficiency-driven nature of institutional development, arguing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering long-term economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). However, others contend that the core institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional struggles than by efficiency considerations. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies, as understanding the underlying mechanisms of institutional change can guide efforts to promote economic growth in both developed and developing economies. The resolution of this dispute hinges on whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency concerns or by power dynamics that prioritize the interests of dominant groups. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions is pivotal in long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework fostered long-term capital markets, which are critical for economic expansion. North (1989, p.9) elaborates that as economies grow, political institutions specifying efficient property rights emerge to facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction despite an increasing volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6). This commitment to enforcing property rights and ensuring secure markets is a critical factor in economic development, as it provides stability for investment and long-term planning (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implication is that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as those in seventeenth-century England, plays an essential role in fostering secure property rights. Institutional development is a gradual process with long-term impacts on the economy, as seen in the historical comparison between England and France.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) challenge conventional views that institutions were shaped by efficiency concerns. They argue instead that distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping these institutions. In pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the power dynamics between different social groups. These institutions often served to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of elites at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Distributional conflicts are not merely a byproduct but rather a fundamental driver of institutional development, shaping the economic landscape in ways that reflect power imbalances.

The impact of these distributional conflicts is evident in the persistence of institutions that restricted competition and maintained inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how these institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. For example, in New World societies with greater inequality, institutional frameworks often included restrictions on land ownership and labor mobility. These policies ensured that wealth and power remained concentrated among a few, thereby limiting economic growth opportunities for the broader population. This dynamic is not limited to pre-industrial Europe but extends to other historical contexts as well, where institutional frameworks reflect underlying power structures and distributional conflicts. The persistence of these institutions underscores the long-term impact of distributional conflicts on economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied greatly, leading to the establishment of either extractive institutions or those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These differences in colonial strategies were driven by mortality rates among Europeans: high mortality led to extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer, while low mortality facilitated the development of more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development. For instance, colonies with extractive institutions experienced stagnation or even decline in economic growth due to the lack of incentives for investment and productivity improvements. Acemoglu et al.'s work emphasizes that these institutional legacies are not merely historical artifacts but continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on long-term development trajectories. This dynamic is further supported by evidence showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to industrialization opportunities and the interaction between institutions and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutional frameworks underscores how historical events can have lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory, shaping its current income per capita through enduring institutional legacies.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is often proposed as a means to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28)). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, in contrast with recent literature emphasizing such impediments. This perspective is anchored in the idea that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the efficacy of these reforms is contingent on the specific context and historical conditions of each African country.

The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. Englebert concludes that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced understanding highlights that institutional reforms must consider the unique socio-political dynamics of each country rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms that can truly address poverty in Africa.

Technological advancements are pivotal for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight that the growth of useful knowledge drives economic growth. They identify technological progress as an essential element in understanding long-run economic development. This perspective is supported by the observation that significant breakthroughs in human knowledge have historically constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over extended periods and across regions (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further argues that the growth of economic reasonableness, which complements technological progress, is crucial for sustaining economic improvement. Without institutional progress to support technological advancements, the benefits of these innovations could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54) and coordination failures. Kuznets emphasizes that modern technology requires substantial adjustments in institutions and ideologies to integrate efficiently into growing economies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is critical for resolving growth-induced conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Technological advancements necessitate a concomitant evolution in social structures to ensure that economic growth remains sustainable.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This cultural legacy has significantly impacted trust levels within African societies, which is crucial for long-run economic growth. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment where interpersonal trust was eroded, creating lasting effects on societal norms and behaviors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) elaborates that the institutional prescriptions adopted by colonial governments often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, which could either reinforce or mitigate these cultural legacies. For instance, colonial administrations seeking fiscal security might have encouraged material interests in the status quo, potentially altering the trajectory of trust within societies (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that while the slave trade's impact on trust is profound, the institutional frameworks imposed during and after colonial rule could either exacerbate or alleviate the mistrust fostered by the slave trade. Thus, the interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional adaptations shapes the long-term economic prospects of African nations.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives for investment and the efficiency of resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents rather than promote growth, experienced persistent low incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin explains that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional policies in response to local economic and political actions, suggesting a dynamic interplay between external impositions and indigenous developments (Austin 2008, p.15).

The nature of institutions matters significantly. North argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is essential for fostering an environment conducive to long-term capital investment and economic development. Ogilvie emphasizes that institutions are not merely efficiency-enhancing tools but are shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutional legacies from colonial rule continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins highlights that understanding these historical institutional frameworks can inform current policy reforms aimed at eliminating poverty in Africa and other regions. He shows that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident across various contexts. Shared finding: Institutions are crucial for economic growth as they reduce transaction costs, facilitate market exchanges, and provide incentives for investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, there remains a debate on whether the persistence of low incomes in former colonies is due to extractive institutions or other factors. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the varying institutional responses to local economic actions during colonial rule, as documented by Austin (2008, p.15). This empirical comparison shows that while extractive institutions were prevalent, they were not uniformly implemented or maintained across all colonies.
