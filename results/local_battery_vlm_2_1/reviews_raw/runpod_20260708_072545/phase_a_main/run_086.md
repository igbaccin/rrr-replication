The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. Institutions are widely recognized as critical factors influencing economic outcomes by structuring incentives and constraints that govern human interactions. The live disagreement centres on the causal mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, arguing that secure property rights and effective governance foster growth. Another position focuses on how historical and political contexts shape institutional development, suggesting that the evolution of institutions is path-dependent and influenced by power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Additionally, there are rival explanations that highlight geographical factors or cultural beliefs as primary drivers of economic growth.

A shared finding among scholars is that the quality of institutions significantly influences long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The institutional framework determines the security of property rights and the efficiency of market exchanges, which in turn affect investment decisions and economic growth. For example, North argues that the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during the English financial revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was crucial for fostering economic growth by providing a stable environment for long-term capital markets. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are essential for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth are still debated. Some scholars argue that institutional quality is shaped by historical and political contexts, leading to path-dependent outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. posit that European colonization strategies resulted in the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The presence of European settlers often led to the creation of inclusive institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment. Areas without significant settler populations tended to develop extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction for the benefit of the colonial power. This historical context shaped long-run economic outcomes by determining the type of institutions that were established.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is another critical aspect of this debate. Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-based explanations of institutional development, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She contends that institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by power dynamics and the interests of dominant groups. This perspective highlights how historical power structures influence institutional development and, consequently, long-run economic outcomes. Austin also notes the importance of indigenous economic and political action in shaping colonial institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives and actions, indicating that institutional development results from complex interactions between different actors.

The role of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth is another important aspect. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Credible commitment underpins long-run growth by providing a stable framework for investment and market exchanges. This institutional development was driven by political factors, including the degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to enforcing these rules.

Their work highlights how the development of financial instruments and institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs. This institutional framework was driven by political factors, particularly the commitment of the regime to enforce these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which indicates a broader economic transformation. North notes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement would lead to falling transaction costs per exchange (North 1989, p.6). North's argument is supported by evidence from capital markets showing how institutional changes facilitated economic growth. This highlights how secure property rights, underpinned by credible commitment, fostered a stable framework for investment and market exchanges.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by introducing varied institutional frameworks that significantly influenced the development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutional structures in their colonies. This variation in institutional design was crucial as it determined the economic outcomes for these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, where Europeans faced low mortality and settled permanently, they established inclusive institutions that fostered investment and economic growth. These institutional differences persisted over time, influencing current income levels in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is largely attributable to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism.

Distributional conflicts were the primary driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts. These institutions included serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, all of which were influenced more by power dynamics than by efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The persistence of these institutions can be understood through the lens of inequality and elite control over resources. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implications of these distributional conflicts are far-reaching; they shaped institutional frameworks and influenced long-term economic development by reinforcing power structures. This nuance underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of how distributional conflicts and institutional design interact to shape long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) both assert that historical factors like slave trades significantly influenced African economic development by shaping the institutional landscape in ways that persist to this day (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights how it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The impact of slave trades on Africa was profound, with Nunn noting that these trades spanned nearly 500 years (Nunn 2008, p.2). This duration allowed for deep institutional changes; the slave trades altered economic and social structures in ways that were not easily reversed. For instance, regions affected by high levels of slave exports often saw a decline in population and economic activity, which in turn weakened institutions and hindered development. These adaptations often involved changes in production equilibria and economic strategies, reflecting a more nuanced interaction between historical factors and institutional development. This cultural dimension is crucial because it underscores how historical factors can have lasting effects on institutions beyond immediate economic impacts.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) jointly establish that economic performance in colonial Africa was more varied than often depicted. They show that many African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early 20th century, challenging previous notions of persistent poverty. This variation is tied to local endowments and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The role of foreign trade and export markets is crucial; growth in these sectors was influenced by factors such as transport costs and colonial policies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize that understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including income distribution, is essential for assessing future growth potential and the impact of historical conditions on African welfare. This highlights how local factors shaped economic outcomes during colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The role of institutions in shaping these outcomes remains a critical area for further investigation.

The relationship between foreign trade and export markets and long-term economic development underscores the importance of understanding immediate economic impacts and historical factors that shape institutional frameworks over time.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both underscore that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that the diffusion of innovations requires institutional adjustments to facilitate economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies a reciprocal relationship where institutions must adapt to accommodate technological progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by suggesting that coordination failures can impede economic sustainability if not addressed through institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the necessity of institutional adaptations in sustaining long-term economic growth driven by technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that resolving conflicts arising from technological changes is crucial for maintaining political stability, which in turn supports economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that although institutions are essential for harnessing the benefits of technological advancements, their evolution may not always align seamlessly with economic growth trajectories.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that long-term structural changes initiated the great divergence between Europe and Asia well before 1800. Pamuk (2007, p.23) posits that the Black Death led to significant structural changes in Europe by 1450, with northwest Europe experiencing rapid urbanisation and institutional flexibility (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This institutional flexibility enabled regions like Holland to capitalise on new economic opportunities more effectively than areas with stronger monopolies. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further elaborate that the service sector, including merchant distribution and finance, was instrumental in driving economic growth during the early modern period, a pattern that continued into the modern era (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed, with skilled labourers experiencing greater surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled workers. Pamuk (2007, p.23) qualifies this by highlighting that high productivity in agriculture was not solely due to geographical factors but also resulted from significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These findings underscore the importance of considering broader economic conditions and structural changes when examining long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern property rights and market exchanges. North's seminal work underscores the significance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions determining "efficient" property rights are crucial for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and lower costs per transaction (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The evolution of these institutions, particularly in early modern England, was instrumental in fostering economic development. North and Weingast highlight how the political factors underpinning economic growth included the rules governing exchange and the mechanisms for enforcing and changing those rules, which were critical to securing property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of colonial institutions on current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies, with some experiencing rapid resource extraction while others developed more sustainable economic frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their enduring impact on economic development. Austin contends that European settlers' presence and competition for African labour were pivotal in shaping colonial policies, which in turn influenced indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2).

The interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth is also evident in the historical context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts drove the core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were solely designed for economic efficiency, instead highlighting their role in managing social and political power dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9).

The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement is found in the reversal of fortune thesis, which shows how institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to a divergence in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, leading to different institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Austin 2008, p.15).
