The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics and history. Scholars disagree over whether institutions drive growth directly or are merely a symptom of deeper causal factors. Some argue that the quality of institutions determines a country's economic trajectory by influencing investment and productivity. Others posit that institutions are shaped by geographical factors, cultural beliefs, or political power dynamics. The crux of this debate lies in understanding how much agency institutions have in promoting growth versus being passive outcomes of other forces. This question is pivotal for development economics because it informs policy recommendations: should policymakers prioritize institutional reform or focus on other areas? A key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions can be actively shaped to foster growth, or whether they are merely reflective of underlying social and political structures. This issue remains unresolved as scholars continue to explore the complex interplay between institutions and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. and North both emphasise the pivotal role of institutions in long-run economic growth, but they differ in their focus on specific institutional mechanisms and historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, significantly influenced current income per capita by shaping investment and economic activity. North emphasizes that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6), which are vital for economic growth. North’s argument is that institutions matter because they lower transaction costs and provide a stable framework for economic activity. This perspective underscores the idea that institutional quality directly impacts economic performance by fostering an environment conducive to investment and productivity gains.

Austin and Ogilvie both contribute to the debate on institutions but focus on different aspects of their role in economic development. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He suggests that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change. Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-based explanations for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, arguing instead that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). She posits that institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by power struggles as well as purely economic efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's perspective adds nuance to the debate by emphasizing the role of political dynamics in shaping institutions, which can either support or hinder long-run growth depending on the distribution of power within society. This view underscores that institutions are not static entities but evolve in response to social and political forces.

Acemoglu et al. and Hopkins both address the impact of colonialism on institutional development, highlighting how historical legacies shape current economic outcomes. They emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, illustrating how colonial legacies can have enduring effects on economic development. Hopkins discusses how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). He argues that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective underscores that understanding historical institutional legacies is key to designing effective policies for economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This institutional evolution reflected an increased security of property rights, which in turn facilitated market exchanges. The regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing public choice was crucial for securing property rights, thereby fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that while these institutional changes were significant, they interacted with broader economic conditions. This interaction underscores the complexity of institutional change in driving long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, which determined the extractive nature of colonial rule and thus shaped long-term economic outcomes. This institutional framework persisted even after independence, affecting current income per capita in these regions.

Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on this mechanism by highlighting that European colonizers were motivated by the prospect of extracting maximum rents from their colonies rather than fostering long-term economic development. These institutions were designed to maximize benefits for European colonists, which often meant creating systems that did not promote growth in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic is crucial because it underscores how institutional design can be deeply influenced by the immediate interests of colonizers, leading to lasting economic impacts. Acemoglu et al. also note that while population density and prosperity influenced European policies, the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century played a central role in shaping the long-run development trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

This dynamic highlights that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress and advancing knowledge, which require institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets highlights that the interplay between technological changes and economic growth necessitates specific institutional and ideological adjustments. This dynamic is crucial because it underscores how technological advancements can only be fully realized when supported by complementary institutions. Mokyr complements this view, emphasizing that coordination failures in the absence of institutional progress can undermine the sustainability of economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while institutions are essential for harnessing technological gains, they are not the primary drivers but rather facilitators of growth. Kuznets notes that modern economic growth is possible only if conflicts arising from technological changes are resolved without excessive costs to the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that even though technology is the primary driver, institutions play a critical role in managing societal disruptions and ensuring long-term stability.

Distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping key economic institutions during pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, are the fundamental drivers behind the development of core institutions. The primary mechanism for this is the influence of elites in maintaining their power through policies that restrict competition, which in turn affects long-term economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) support this by showing how New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, thereby perpetuating inequality through policies that restricted competition. This suggests that economic outcomes are deeply intertwined with the social and political structures that govern resource distribution as well as being a product of technological advancements.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The thesis compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, failing to account for the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Austin argues that this simplification can obscure the nuanced ways in which colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local conditions and indigenous actions. For instance, colonial governments often encouraged commodity production even when European settlers were not present (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that institutional changes resulted from interactions between colonial powers and local populations as well as being imposed. Nunn and Wantchekon reinforce this idea by showing that the slave trade's impact on trust was multifaceted, with both internal and external channels playing significant roles in shaping contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding highlights how historical legacies can have enduring effects on institutions and economic development, even when direct institutional continuities are not apparent.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, emphasizing that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment and essential for development. The role of women in African economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods has been marginalized. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate how patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This points to a specific mechanism where institutional changes, such as those in land access and education policies, had multifaceted impacts on economic development.

This nuanced view is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), who challenge conventional views of African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes in Anglophone Africa before the mid-20th century. They show that the region's economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding that contradicts previous characterizations and suggests a more nuanced understanding of African economic performance. The growth patterns in these economies were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, indicating that external factors played a substantial role in shaping economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) extend this analysis by reconstructing real wage levels across British African colonies. Their work highlights the variability in economic conditions within Africa and underscores the importance of understanding long-term trends to assess future growth prospects. This approach reveals that poverty was not a persistent structural feature but rather a product of specific historical circumstances (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg's findings imply that addressing contemporary African economic challenges requires recognizing the region's diverse economic histories, which may offer insights into effective policy interventions.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 was marked by regional shifts. Van Zanden's analysis of wage data across twenty European cities reveals no straightforward correlation between urbanization rates and improvements in living standards, challenging the notion of a linear 'industrious revolution' during this era. The absence of a clear relationship suggests that economic development was influenced by complex factors beyond simple measures of urban growth. Pamuk's analysis (Pamuk 2007, p.16) extends this view by linking structural changes in Europe to the Black Death, which initiated long-term shifts that diverged regional economies. By 1450, these divergences created a wage gap between the northwest and other parts of Europe. This pattern aligns with van Zanden's findings, showing that economic development was not uniform but varied significantly across regions. Pamuk identifies institutional flexibility as a key factor in enabling regions like Holland to rapidly adapt to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This regional variability implies that while institutions played a role in shaping long-run economic growth, their impact was contingent on local conditions and historical events such as the Black Death, which disrupted traditional economic patterns. This dynamic interplay between institutions and external shocks highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development in Europe during this period.

Long-run economic growth is significantly influenced by institutions that shape the economic environment and provide incentives for investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Extractive institutions were established in regions where Europeans faced high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita by limiting economic opportunities and discouraging investment. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic development.

The role of institutions is further underscored by North's work, which emphasizes that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security in property rights, which in turn facilitates long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework is essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting market exchanges.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, better explain core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She shows that beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the development of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This perspective highlights how shared beliefs within a society shape its institutional structure, influencing long-term economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. also demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). They show that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, leading to different developmental trajectories for the colonies. This interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in shaping long-term development.

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter significantly in economic growth and development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). There remains disagreement over whether extractive institutions are an equilibrium outcome or a result of specific historical events. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive because they maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence of colonial institutions in regions with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality rates, as documented by Acemoglu et al., shows the impact of extractive institutions on long-term economic development.
