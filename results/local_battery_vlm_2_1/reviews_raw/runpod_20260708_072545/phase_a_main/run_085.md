The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The stakes are high because institutions are understood to be the rules of the game that structure political, social, and economic interactions; thus, they can either facilitate or hinder economic development. One live disagreement concerns the extent to which historical legacies, such as colonial institutions, have enduring effects on contemporary economic performance. Scholars argue that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers continue to stifle growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Furthermore, there is a debate over whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns or distributional conflicts; some scholars assert that institutions arise from the need to manage power struggles and allocate resources among different groups. This disagreement underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolve and their impact on economic outcomes.

Institutions have been identified as pivotal in fostering economic growth, particularly through the enforcement of property rights and the reduction of transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that institutions create an environment where investments are protected and transactions are efficient, thereby promoting economic activity. The historical context shaping these institutions can significantly influence their effectiveness. For instance, colonial administrations often introduced institutions that prioritized resource extraction over local development, leading to persistent economic underperformance (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and historical legacies, where the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies is a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth. These institutions are often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups; they promote long-term development less frequently (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The legacy of colonial rule has left many countries with institutional frameworks that hinder investment and economic growth. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical legacies on contemporary economic performance, where initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to influence institutional design and economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts play a significant role in shaping core economic institutions, particularly in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were efficient responses to economic needs; they also resulted from power struggles and resource allocation among different groups. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how institutions evolve from distributional conflicts; it focuses on efficiency concerns less frequently. By examining the historical context in which these institutions developed, scholars can gain insights into how power dynamics shape institutional design and economic outcomes.

The interaction between institutions and industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions interact with other factors, such as industrialization, to shape economic growth. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which interacted with opportunities for industrialization during this period. This highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other economic factors in shaping long-run development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, involving historical legacies, efficiency concerns, distributional conflicts, and interactions with other economic factors. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing effective policies to promote economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This claim is supported by North's analysis of the English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England (1694) and new financial instruments (North 1989, p.9). These institutional developments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This security was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. North and Weingast's argument is further supported by their analysis of the political factors underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Specifically, they highlight that a regime committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange is essential for fostering secure property rights. This commitment was pivotal in seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice played a central role in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast's claim about the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes is qualified by their observation that France's pre-revolutionary economic performance was almost at parity with Britain (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that while institutional changes were significant, they were not the sole determinant of economic growth. The implication is that other factors, such as bureaucratic structures and market development, also played a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, indicating that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutions persisted over time and had lasting effects on economic development. The pattern of colonial settlement and institution-building was not uniform; it varied based on factors such as European mortality rates and the density of indigenous populations. This variation led to different institutional outcomes, with some colonies receiving more productive institutions than others (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis suggests that these institutional differences are a key factor in explaining the divergence in income levels among former colonies today. The implication is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic performance long after decolonization, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique is shared with Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), who also argue that the thesis overlooks the complexity of African historical experiences. A key mechanism in this oversimplification is the assumption that institutional changes occurred uniformly across colonies, regardless of local conditions or indigenous economic activity. For example, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments might encourage indigenous commodity production due to fiscal interests. This adaptive behaviour suggests that institutional outcomes were shaped by local dynamics and the economic actions of colonised populations as well as being imposed. The implication is that while institutional adaptations occurred, their effectiveness was contingent upon broader socio-economic contexts and long-term welfare development.

This is particularly evident in how technological advancements require complementary changes in institutions to be fully leveraged for sustained economic improvement. This perspective underscores the importance of 'epochal innovations', breakthroughs that significantly alter the trajectory of economic growth over long periods and across regions (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that institutional progress complements technological advancements, preventing the erosion of gains due to rent-seeking behaviors. Without such institutional support, even substantial technological improvements could be unsustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets emphasizes that modern economic growth necessitates resolving conflicts arising from technological changes through effective institutions. This is crucial for maintaining societal cohesion and ensuring that growth does not weaken the political fabric of society. The sovereign state plays a pivotal role in managing these transitions, highlighting the interdependence between institutional stability and economic progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Kuznets also warns that excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric can impede modern economic growth; he suggests that while institutions facilitate technological progress, they must be robust enough to manage the associated challenges. This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions is central to understanding long-run economic growth, as it illustrates how institutional frameworks adapt to and support technological advancements over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa had profound and lasting negative impacts on economic development, influencing cultural norms of mistrust that persist today (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust through both internal norms and external environments, with internal norms being at least twice as impactful. This mistrust is an independent outcome that directly hinders economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of these cultural norms continues to affect modern economies by reducing intergroup trust and impeding collective action, which are crucial for sustained growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights how the absence of centralized governments and well-developed markets in many African societies during the colonial period further exacerbated this issue, as these institutions were necessary for facilitating foreign trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This underscores that while the slave trade laid the groundwork for mistrust, the lack of robust institutional frameworks during colonial rule prevented Africa from overcoming this legacy.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) posit that distributional conflicts were the driving force behind core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They argue that these institutions, such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were shaped by power struggles rather than efficiency considerations. The unequal distribution of wealth and political power in colonial societies influenced institutional development, with elites leveraging government policies to maintain their dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective suggests that institutions were instruments used by powerful groups to preserve their status; they were tools for economic efficiency less frequently. This view does not negate the role of exogenous factors such as factor endowments and cultural beliefs in shaping these institutions. Ogilvie highlights how shared beliefs can also form part of a society's institutional structure (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in their ability to facilitate market exchanges and secure property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, with some colonies adopting extractive states while others developed institutions enforcing the rule of law. This variation underscores how initial institutional setups can persist and significantly influence current economic outcomes. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) demonstrates that the emergence of political institutions specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth.

Institutional frameworks are also shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were driven more by distributional conflicts than efficiency considerations. This perspective aligns with North's notion that shared beliefs are part of a society's institutional structure, influencing the adoption and enforcement of property rights (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) further highlights how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, reflecting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution.

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is also evident in the reversal of fortunes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and opportunities for development during the nineteenth century. This finding is supported by Hopkins, who emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment and essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies.

The shared belief across these studies is that institutions matter significantly for economic growth. For example, North (1989, p.9) argues that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic development, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies due to institutional changes. A key disagreement remains regarding the extent to which exogenous factors, such as factor endowments and cultural beliefs, influence institutional development. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local economic actions, indicating a more dynamic interaction between institutions and external factors. The specific historical evidence of colonial policy adaptations in response to indigenous economic action settles this disagreement by illustrating how exogenous factors can shape institutional frameworks over time.
