The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with key disagreements revolving around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if they merely complement other factors. One camp emphasizes the foundational role of institutions in securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges, arguing that these mechanisms reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Another perspective posits that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, suggesting that pre-industrial European institutions like serfdom and craft guilds were products of power struggles rather than economic optimality. The debate hinges on whether the institutional framework is the primary engine of growth or if it merely supports other key factors such as population density and savings rates (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs. This institutional evolution reflects increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This process was driven by political factors that underpinned economic growth and market development; these institutions specified "efficient" property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this institutional change, as certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues. This political model underscores how institutions can emerge from specific historical contexts and shape economic outcomes over the long run.

The evolution of these institutions had profound implications for England's capital markets. For instance, recent research has challenged traditional interpretations by showing that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was comparable to Britain’s. This comparative perspective highlights that while institutions are crucial for economic development, their impact can be nuanced and contingent on broader historical contexts. The bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not significantly hinder French economic growth, illustrating the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

This development was concurrent with an increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. The emergence of efficient property rights and their enforcement facilitated market exchanges, driving economic growth by reducing transaction costs.

The institutional framework in England during this period was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, illustrating how institutions can shape long-run economic outcomes through specific mechanisms such as reduced transaction costs and enhanced market efficiency.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to extractive states in high-mortality areas and more investment-friendly institutions elsewhere. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. For instance, colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand benefited from institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences persisted over time, shaping long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on this by noting that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions, shaped by the interests of the colonizers, could hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions post-colonialism meant that former colonies with extractive states faced significant barriers to economic growth, a pattern that continues to affect their development trajectories today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth underscores the critical role of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a significant adverse effect on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon contend that the transatlantic slave trade has left a legacy of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust is deeply rooted and persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The impact of these historical events extends beyond mere economic factors; they have shaped societal norms and cultural attitudes in profound ways that continue to influence contemporary African societies. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, suggesting instead that historical legacies like those left by the slave trade play a critical role in shaping current economic conditions. This legacy underscores how deeply historical events can shape long-term economic outcomes, even when the immediate causes are no longer present.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are pivotal for economic growth, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both argue. Kuznets posits that technological advancements necessitate institutional changes to resolve conflicts arising from economic growth; these changes include the modernization of the state structure and societal norms, all of which are crucial for sustaining long-term economic development. Mokyr's work complements Kuznets’s insights by highlighting how institutional changes enable the diffusion of knowledge and technology, thus fostering sustained economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge requires complementary institutional progress to mitigate coordination failures and prevent rent-seeking behaviors that could undermine economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is evident in historical contexts where societies that successfully adapted their institutions reaped the benefits of new technologies, while those that failed to do so lagged behind. Kuznets's emphasis on the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts suggests that political stability and effective governance are essential for harnessing technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates that without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvements may not be sustained over the long term; this implies that institutions play a critical role in maintaining the momentum of economic development. The interplay between technology and institutions is not merely additive but rather synergistic, where one reinforces the other to drive economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that African economies in the early twentieth century exhibited periods of growth linked to foreign trade and export markets. These growth patterns were not uniform across countries but were influenced by various factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize that understanding the long-term economic development in Africa requires a deeper examination of real wage trends. This approach helps assess future growth prospects and understand the structural effects on African welfare development over time.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Their analysis focuses on the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity gains in Europe, highlighting how institutional changes facilitated investment in agriculture and manufacturing. This perspective underscores a broader pattern where early modern economic growth was driven by structural shifts and institutional adaptations rather than technological innovation alone.

The implications of this divergence are evident in the varying standards of living across regions. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that skilled laborers in northwest Europe saw significant gains, while unskilled laborers did not experience comparable benefits (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This disparity suggests that economic growth during this period was unevenly distributed, with certain sectors and groups benefiting more than others. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) supports this view by noting the shifting focus of economic development to regions bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These shifts confirm that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a complex interplay between economic growth and living standards.

However, van Zanden (1999, p.1) also points out that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This nuanced perspective challenges overly optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth. The absence of a straightforward correlation suggests that while institutional changes and structural shifts were crucial, they did not uniformly improve living standards across all regions. This finding underscores the importance of considering both institutional factors and regional variations in assessing long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share that colonial governments' interests in indigenous commodity production significantly shaped institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Their work highlights how European settlers' presence influenced the expansion of local commodity production, which was often aligned with colonial administrations' fiscal goals. This dynamic shows that institutions evolved in response to both external pressures from colonial powers and internal economic actions by the colonized populations.

The colonial administrations' institutional adjustments reflected broader patterns of responding to economic and political conditions. For example, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium illustrates how colonial governance adapted to local economic realities (Austin 2008, p.15). These adaptations were not merely reactive but also proactive, as colonial authorities sought to create a stable environment that served their interests while accommodating indigenous economic activities. This adaptive institutional evolution was pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as it influenced the development of property rights, fiscal policies, and public infrastructure.

While Austin (2008, p.2) focuses on the dynamic interplay between colonial governance and local economic actions, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize how initial factor endowments shaped institutional differences across New World societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Their analysis underscores that variations in land, labor, and natural resource distributions led to distinct levels of inequality, which in turn influenced the development of institutions. These initial conditions set the stage for long-term economic trajectories by determining the distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power. This perspective aligns with Austin's (2008, p.2) argument that colonial governments adapted their institutional prescriptions based on local economic actions, highlighting a shared recognition of the importance of adaptive institutional frameworks in shaping economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to determine the distribution of wealth and human capital. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established distinct institutional frameworks based on local conditions. In colonies where settlers faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, settlers created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). This variation in institutional design directly influenced long-term economic trajectories. North highlights that secure property rights are essential for economic growth, noting that the development of financial instruments like those introduced by the Bank of England dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast show that in seventeenth-century England, the political factors underpinning economic growth included credible commitments to rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These commitments were essential for the development of capital markets and the enforcement of property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that political institutions are stable and enforceable, providing a foundation for sustained economic development. This perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was related to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not just geographic factors.
