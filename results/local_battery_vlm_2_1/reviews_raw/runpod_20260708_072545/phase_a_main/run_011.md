The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development theory. At stake is understanding why some societies prosper while others stagnate over extended periods. There are two competing positions on this topic. On one side, scholars argue that institutions play a pivotal role in determining long-term economic outcomes by influencing property rights and transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). On the other hand, there is the perspective that the impact of institutions has been overstated and that factors such as geography or culture may have more significant effects. This disagreement underscores the need to clarify how institutional frameworks influence economic growth trajectories. The debate hinges on whether institutions are primary drivers or secondary factors in economic development.

The first stream of scholarship emphasizes that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which had lasting impacts on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al argue that Europeans adopted different approaches to colonization depending on mortality rates and other local conditions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This resulted in some colonies receiving institutions that enforced property rights and encouraged investment while others were subjected to extractive states designed for resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). Austin acknowledges the complexity of institutional evolution and its relationship with economic growth, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of how different historical periods and paths influence current economic conditions. This perspective emphasizes that the impact of institutions on economic growth varies based on historical context and specific colonial policies (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6).

North and Weingast argue that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The emergence of this bank and new financial instruments dramatically lowered transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time. North and Weingast's work underscores that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights is crucial for facilitating market exchanges. These institutions lower costs per transaction while increasing overall market activity, as specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges.

The historical evidence from England demonstrates that these institutional changes were pivotal in transforming the economy, leading to sustained growth over centuries (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of this transformation highlights how effective enforcement mechanisms can maintain secure property rights. Credible commitment by the regime or sovereign is essential for sustaining economic growth; it underpins the enforcement of property rights and the broader institutional framework that supports economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the type of institutions established by Europeans varied greatly depending on factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions. These institutional differences had profound effects on economic development. This approach differed from settlements like the United States and Australia, where Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.

The persistence of these institutional structures over time highlights their enduring impact on economic outcomes. This institutional legacy is crucial for understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, where geographic factors alone cannot account for these disparities. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-term development trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional framework affected economic growth and influenced broader social and political dynamics within these societies.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights technological progress as a key driver of long-term economic development, contrasting with views that prioritize institutions. The process of technological advancement requires the generation of new ideas and their efficient diffusion and adoption. This is where institutional frameworks play a crucial role. Kuznets' work underscores this point by noting that technological progress often necessitates significant adjustments in societal structures and ideologies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are essential for integrating new technologies into existing economic systems without causing disruptive conflicts. Mokyr further elaborates on the importance of institutional support, arguing that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements may not be sustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while useful knowledge is the mainspring of growth, institutions facilitate its effective deployment and sustained impact. Kuznets emphasizes the role of the sovereign state in resolving conflicts arising from economic growth, highlighting the importance of a stable political environment for sustaining technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Therefore, the interplay between technological innovation and institutional frameworks is vital for long-term economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa created a legacy of mistrust that has persisted and continues to affect economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon's study shows that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust is an internal norm that has become deeply ingrained (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further argues that Africa's slave trades had lasting adverse effects on economic development, suggesting that these trades disrupted social and political institutions in ways that hindered subsequent growth. The impact of the slave trade on African economies extended into broader institutional frameworks beyond direct losses from human capital depletion. Nunn (2008, p.2) cites Vansina's account of how local coastal societies lacked the economic and social institutions necessary for sustained foreign trade, highlighting the importance of centralized governance in facilitating trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while external factors such as colonialism may have played a role, the internal institutional damage caused by the slave trades was significant. The long-term effects of the slave trade on African development are complex and multifaceted; however, one key implication is the need for comprehensive strategies to rebuild trust and strengthen institutions, which can foster economic growth in the region.

The literature on economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa highlights that the development of formal education and the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule played pivotal roles in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous narratives of uniform economic stagnation. This finding is supported by Frankema's (2012, p.15) analysis, which posits that British colonial rule fostered a more conducive environment for formal education compared to other colonial powers. These institutional changes were not merely educational; they also influenced broader economic patterns. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that growth in export production was closely tied to foreign trade and political institutions, with variations between countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political stability (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The specific mechanisms through which these institutional changes translated into long-term economic growth remain complex. Frankema (2012, p.15) points out that the demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African leaders and families, suggesting that the effectiveness of colonial institutions depended on local adaptations and perceptions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks interact with local contexts to drive economic development. The historical evolution of these institutions in sub-Saharan Africa provides valuable insights into the broader role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in regions with diverse colonial legacies.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by examining historical patterns that do not attribute African poverty to long-term structural impediments. They argue that this thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, thereby ignoring the complexities of institutional evolution in Africa. Austin (2008, p.2) identifies a mechanism where colonial administrations adapted their policies based on local economic conditions and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with fiscal interests. This dynamic highlights how institutions evolved in response to local incentives and were not imposed uniformly across colonies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered about long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This points to a need for more granular data on economic trends in Africa to fully understand the interplay between institutions and growth.

A central theme across institutional scholarship is that institutions matter for economic growth through their role in shaping incentives and reducing uncertainty (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth by providing secure property rights and predictable enforcement mechanisms. This is evident in the political factors underpinning economic growth, where regimes committed to rules foster market development and lower transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights. This historical evidence highlights that secure property rights are a precondition for growth and a product of political institutions that enforce and adapt rules over time.

Institutional differences across colonies stem from varying European settlement patterns and mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The diverse colonization strategies led to different institutional frameworks, with some colonies establishing extractive states while others developed rule-of-law institutions. This variation in institutional outcomes is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth, as it shows how initial conditions shaped by colonial policies persisted over time. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the difference between extractive and inclusive institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions underscores their role in determining economic trajectories, with extractive institutions often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between local incentives and colonial policies. Colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with fiscal interests (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This dynamic highlights how institutions evolved in response to local conditions and were not uniformly imposed. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For example, colonial administrations adapted their policies based on indigenous economic actions, illustrating a more nuanced relationship between institutions and growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The evidence from these studies shows that institutions evolve in response to local incentives and political dynamics.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of disagreement. All agree that institutions matter, as shown by the influence of secure property rights on economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). The remaining point of contention is whether these institutional differences are primarily driven by initial conditions set during colonial rule or by subsequent adaptations to local incentives and political dynamics. Austin shows that colonial administrators changed their policies in response to indigenous actions, illustrating the role of local adaptation over time. This historical evidence contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s focus on the persistence of extractive institutions from colonial times (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional change and adaptation over time, as shown by Austin's analysis of colonial policies in response to indigenous economic actions.
