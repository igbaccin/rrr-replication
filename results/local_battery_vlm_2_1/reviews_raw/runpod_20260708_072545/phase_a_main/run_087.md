The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is understanding whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors play a more significant role. One side argues that the institutional framework, particularly the presence of secure property rights and effective governance, directly influences economic outcomes over the long term. This view posits that institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs and ensuring the enforcement of contracts. The opposing camp contends that institutions are merely one factor among many and that other variables such as geography, culture, or exogenous shocks can have a more profound impact on economic growth. A shared finding is that institutions matter (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of financial institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically lowered transaction costs. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement reflects a growing economy. This development is evidenced by falling costs per transaction, despite an increasing proportion of GNP dedicated to the transactions sector due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North's work highlights how secure property rights enable long-term investments, which are essential for sustained economic growth. The evolution of these institutions in England fostered a stable environment conducive to market exchanges and capital accumulation. This institutional framework facilitated economic transactions and ensured regime commitment to enforcing these rules, providing credible commitments to investors (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast's findings are further supported by evidence from capital markets, which show that England's economic performance was significantly influenced by its institutional changes. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political stability also play a role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Their work demonstrates that Europeans established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates and colonization strategies, with extractive institutions prevalent in high-mortality areas and more inclusive ones in low-mortality regions. These institutional differences had lasting effects on economic development, as evidenced by the persistence of these institutions into the modern era (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanisms through which these institutions operate are multifaceted; for example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive because they maximize rents to colonial powers rather than promoting long-run growth. This focus on rent extraction aligns with the broader literature on institutional economics, which highlights how institutions can be designed to benefit specific groups at the expense of overall economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this by noting that these extractive institutions do not necessarily encourage economic development, suggesting a complex relationship between colonial policies and long-term growth. One downstream implication is that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin argues that institutions evolve over time, shaping and being shaped by economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). Similarly, Ogilvie posits that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were deeply rooted in distributional conflicts, which are themselves a product of historical and political contexts. This perspective suggests that the institutional arrangements observed during this period were outcomes of complex social negotiations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's work highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local incentives and actions, indicating a dynamic relationship between institutions and economic behavior. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to initial hypotheses, demonstrating the adaptability of institutional frameworks to indigenous economic activities (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie further elaborates on this by suggesting that institutions evolve in response to changing social and political dynamics. This evolution can lead to significant changes in economic outcomes over time, as seen in the shift from serfdom to more market-oriented systems. Austin notes that while institutions adapt, they also create path dependencies that influence future economic trajectories.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) show that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This phenomenon is rooted in the unique institutional frameworks established by British colonizers, which incentivized both political leaders and families to invest in formal education. British institutions altered the perceived benefits of education at both macro and micro levels, leading African political leaders and families to engage in cost-benefit calculations that favored educational investment (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was influenced by the specific institutional context created by British colonial rule, which differed significantly from other colonial powers. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) caution that these institutions promoted education but entrenched inequalities through policies that advantaged elites, restricting competition and shaping long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that while British colonial institutions had a positive impact on educational development, their effects were not uniformly beneficial for all segments of society.

The slave trades in Africa had profound negative impacts on economic development, shaping contemporary African societies through enduring cultural and institutional legacies (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, they suggest, persists to this day and significantly impedes economic development. The mechanism through which the slave trades influenced economic trajectories is complex; it is not solely about the loss of labor or human capital but also about the erosion of social cohesion and trust.

For instance, Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until centralized governments with well-developed markets and trading networks emerged. This indicates that the absence of such institutions due to the slave trades likely hindered economic development. Furthermore, Nunn and Wantchekon's finding that mistrust is a significant factor in reduced trust levels today suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to affect contemporary African economies through reduced social capital.

This legacy interacts with other factors such as colonial rule and post-colonial policies. The impact of the slave trade on economic development must be understood within the broader context of Africa's history, including the role of colonial institutions and more recent political and economic events. This nuanced view acknowledges that while the slave trades had adverse effects, their influence was not isolated from other historical and contemporary factors shaping African economies.

This perspective highlights technological innovation as a primary engine of long-run economic growth. Mokyr elaborates on this idea, emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets complements this view by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustaining long-term economic development. However, Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress can be relatively irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness, essentially the institutional progress required to support new technologies, is far less monotonic (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advances may propel economic growth, institutions must continuously adapt to ensure these gains are sustained. The complexity of this interplay underscores the importance of both technological innovation and institutional flexibility in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress is further highlighted by Kuznets' observation that sovereign states play a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This indicates that while technological innovation drives growth, the institutional framework must be robust enough to manage the social and political challenges that arise from these advancements. Without such institutional support, the benefits of technological progress could be undermined by societal disruptions or rent-seeking behaviors. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is dynamic; institutions facilitate and shape the trajectory of long-term economic development through their role in supporting technological innovation.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating and sustaining technological innovation. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions can either encourage investment and enforce the rule of law or create extractive states that transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly emphasizes that secure property rights, which are specified and enforced by political institutions, are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This shows that the presence of robust institutions is essential for managing the social and political challenges arising from technological advancements.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the role of transaction costs. North highlights how the creation of financial instruments like the Bank of England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). Ogilvie also emphasizes that institutions are deeply rooted in distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies. This perspective underscores that institutions must align with societal values and power structures to effectively support economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical evolution of institutions provides insights into their impact on long-run economic development. North and Weingast show that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the degree to which a regime is committed to rules governing property rights and market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Austin challenges the notion that European mortality rates alone determined institutional outcomes in colonies, arguing that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation shows how institutions can evolve dynamically based on local conditions and interactions with existing power structures (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the evidence from capital markets. The success of England's financial revolution, as documented by North (1989, p.9), illustrates how credible commitments to secure property rights can foster economic development. This historical evidence contrasts with Ogilvie’s argument that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The empirical comparison of capital markets shows that institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary, as they led to significant economic growth despite similar initial conditions in France.
