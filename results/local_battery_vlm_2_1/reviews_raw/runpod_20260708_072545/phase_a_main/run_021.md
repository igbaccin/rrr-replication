The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. Institutions are defined as the rules of the game in society, including both formal and informal constraints that shape human interaction. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can guide policy interventions aimed at fostering sustained economic growth. A live disagreement centers on whether institutions matter primarily through their impact on economic efficiency or through their influence on distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs, while others contend that they are shaped by and in turn shape power dynamics within societies. This debate underscores the complexity of institutional design and its implications for long-term economic outcomes. The key to unlocking sustained growth may lie in understanding how different types of institutions interact with economic factors over time.

One stream of scholarship highlights the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in certain colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This perspective is supported by North's assertion that secure property rights are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin (2008, p.15), however, posits that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between institutions and local contexts. These findings collectively illustrate how the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today.

Another stream emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) argues that efficient property rights and their enforcement are crucial for lowering transaction costs, which in turn facilitates economic growth. This view is complemented by North and Weingast's (1989, p.2) argument that credible commitment underpins growth. The development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, for example, was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3), however, suggests that the role of institutions should be examined more critically, advocating for a deeper understanding of how they are incorporated into long-term research programs. This stream underscores the importance of institutional design in creating an environment conducive to economic development.

This institutional evolution in England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as North and Weingast, along with North (1989, p.9) (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), argue. This institutional development can be traced through significant events such as the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. The institutional changes in England also reflected a broader political model where certain rights were traded with Parliament in exchange for revenues, addressing fiscal crises that had plagued the country during earlier conflicts. The institutional framework not only governed economic exchanges but also ensured that these rules were enforced and could be changed as needed, highlighting the critical role of credible commitment in fostering growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast's analysis suggests that the political factors underpinning economic growth are more complex than just the rules governing exchange; they involve a regime committed to enforcing these rules. This institutional framework was crucial for the development of markets and securing property rights, which in turn drove long-term economic growth. However, recent research challenging traditional interpretations of British and French economic history indicates that institutions alone may not have been as revolutionary as initially thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)). The type of institutions established by colonial powers varied greatly depending on factors such as mortality rates and the settler population. Where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they tended to establish extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)) rather than fostering local economic development. This institutional framework was designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)). Where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, these colonies experienced more robust economic development. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. One implication of this institutional legacy is that countries with a history of extractive institutions tend to have lower current income per capita compared to those with institutions that encourage investment and enforce property rights (Acemoglu et al., (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19)). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a crucial role in shaping long-run development outcomes. Nevertheless, the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform across all former colonies; variations in local conditions and post-colonial policies can mitigate or exacerbate these institutional legacies.

The persistence of colonial-era institutions is a critical factor in understanding the divergent economic paths of former colonies, but it is also important to consider how these institutions interact with other factors such as geography and post-colonial policies.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The institutions of serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were shaped by the interests of elites seeking to maintain their power and wealth rather than by any inherent drive for economic efficiency. In these societies, initial conditions of extreme inequality led to the establishment of institutions that reinforced elite dominance through various government policies restricting competition. These institutions persisted over time, contributing to the enduring inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern highlights a critical mechanism: distributional conflicts can lock in institutional arrangements that favor elites, even if these arrangements are not economically efficient. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes that institutions are not merely tools for efficiency but are deeply rooted in the social and political fabric of societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This insight underscores that the persistence of colonial-era institutions is not just a matter of historical inertia but also a reflection of ongoing power dynamics.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had distinct impacts on education systems, which were shaped by both local demand and the institutional framework imposed by colonial administrators. This approach fostered an environment where formal education could be more readily adopted as it became aligned with new economic incentives and political stability (Frankema 2012, p.15). This pattern varied across countries due to factors such as transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth is thus contingent upon the specific context in which they operate, suggesting that the legacy of British rule in sub-Saharan Africa is more nuanced than a uniform negative impact on education systems.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that Africa's slave trades have had lasting adverse effects on economic development. The transatlantic slave trade in particular has been shown to have long-lasting effects on the cultural attitudes of African populations, with a profound impact on trust within communities. Trust is important for economic development and growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade fostered mistrust between different ethnic groups, which persists even today; this enduring mistrust is one of the primary mechanisms through which the slave trade negatively impacts long-run economic outcomes in Africa.

Moreover, Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that the slave trade disrupted the development of local institutions and markets. The Kongo kingdom, for instance, had well-developed trading networks but was unable to sustain trade due to the impact of the slave trade. This disruption hindered the ability of African societies to engage in sustained economic activities, further stunting growth. The extent to which this institutional disruption can be directly attributed to the slave trade versus other historical factors remains a topic of debate. The legacy of the slave trade on Africa's long-run development is thus complex and multifaceted, involving both cultural and institutional dimensions.

Technological and intellectual advancements are fundamental drivers of long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets agrees, noting that technological progress requires institutional adjustments for efficient use and diffusion through economies. Together, they suggest that while institutions may facilitate or hinder growth, it is the advancement of technology and knowledge that ultimately drives long-term economic development. For instance, epochal innovations have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These technological breakthroughs are not merely isolated events but are embedded in broader socio-economic contexts. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting how coordination failures can impede the sustainability of economic improvements unless complemented by institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets also underscores that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully to ensure modern economic growth, emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in managing these transitions. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in facilitating growth, their effectiveness is contingent on the underlying technological and intellectual advancements.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce contracts, which are crucial for fostering economic development. North highlights that the evolution of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9), thereby enhancing economic efficiency and growth. This institutional framework provided a stable environment where long-term capital markets could flourish. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth in former colonies by failing to encourage investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings are consistent with the argument that institutional quality directly influences economic outcomes, as seen in the varying trajectories of different regions.

The relationship between technological advancements and institutional development is also pivotal. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), suggesting that institutions must adapt to new technological paradigms to sustain economic progress. This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's observation that shared beliefs, which are part of the institutional structure, can either facilitate or impede economic development depending on their alignment with technological changes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutions in adapting to changing circumstances (Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run growth are further illuminated by the interplay between property rights and transaction costs. North demonstrates that efficient property rights and effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering a growing economy where specialization and division of labor thrive (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. build on this by showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can stifle long-run growth by failing to promote private property rights and contract enforcement (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their ability to balance the interests of various stakeholders, ensuring that property rights are secure and transaction costs are minimized. The historical evidence from capital markets shows that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in establishing a framework that supported long-term economic development, as seen in the success of the Bank of England.
