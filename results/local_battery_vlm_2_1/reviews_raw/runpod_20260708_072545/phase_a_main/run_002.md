The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that cuts to the core of economic development and historical trajectories. At stake intellectually is understanding why some economies thrive while others stagnate over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. One position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are essential for sustained economic development. Another perspective posits that while institutions matter, they are not the sole determinants; instead, a complex interplay of factors, including cultural beliefs and historical contexts, shapes long-run economic growth.

This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development in underdeveloped regions. If institutions are indeed the primary drivers, then policies should focus on institutional reforms that promote secure property rights and effective governance. If other factors exert greater influence, a broader approach that includes cultural changes and technological advancements may be necessary.

Some scholars assert that institutions designed to enforce property rights and rule of law are critical for economic growth. For instance, North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, leading to varying levels of economic development. In colonies with low mortality rates, settlers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in high-mortality areas, extractive states were established to transfer resources rapidly (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions significantly influenced current income per capita.

However, some scholars argue that institutions are not always designed for efficiency but rather shaped by distributional conflicts. For instance, Ogilvie contends that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were driven by power struggles (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective suggests that institutional designs may be more about distributing power and resources among different groups than maximizing economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional change was driven by fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated trades between certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. The creation of these institutions reflects a broader pattern where political developments underpin economic growth and market exchanges. This implies that while institutional changes were critical, their impact on economic growth was also influenced by broader economic dynamics.

These findings underscore the significance of political institutions in shaping long-run economic growth through secure property rights, but they also highlight the complex interplay between institutional changes and economic developments.

Distributional conflicts are highlighted as a key factor in shaping economic institutions, challenging efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the distributional interests of powerful groups often led to the creation of institutions that served their particular needs.

These institutions often restricted competition and innovation, thereby limiting economic growth. For example, craft and merchant guilds, while providing some benefits such as quality control and training, also acted as barriers to entry for new competitors, thus stifling potential economic dynamism (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These findings underscore how institutional structures shaped by distributional conflicts can have lasting impacts on economic trajectories.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. 2001 and 2002 argue that European colonizers adopted distinct strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. These extractive institutions, which prioritized the interests of the colonial power over local economic development, persisted long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The legacy of these extractive institutions has had profound implications for economic development in former colonies, as they often hindered investment and constrained local economic dynamism (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional path dependency means that the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to shape economic outcomes long after decolonization. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutions evolved in response to political and economic pressures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). For example, the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century played a central role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, suggesting that institutional development can occur independently of ethnic homogeneity. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) complement this by noting that understanding historical determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is essential to assessing future growth potential. The distribution of income and long-term economic development are critical to evaluating the structural effects of colonial rule on African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight a significant gap in pre-1950 GDP estimates for Africa, which limits our ability to fully understand these historical determinants. This lacuna underscores the need for more comprehensive data to better grasp the long-term economic development of African countries.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) posit that the primary driver of economic growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge rather than institutional frameworks. This perspective highlights technological progress as a pivotal force in shaping long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by noting that modern technology necessitates significant institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use and integration into society (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also recognizes the potential for conflicts arising from these changes; such conflicts must be resolved peacefully to maintain economic progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Economic reasonableness and technological progress are intertwined in a way that requires careful management of social and political dynamics to ensure long-term growth.

The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This enduring mistrust is rooted in the historical legacy of slavery, which has shaped social norms and intergroup relations. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that this internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The persistence of these norms indicates that the legacy of mistrust continues to influence contemporary social dynamics in profound ways. This mistrust has far-reaching implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative behaviour and efficient market functioning (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The cultural legacy of slavery in Africa has a profound impact on contemporary trust levels and economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and incentives that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonial strategies varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment while others were subjected to extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks has had enduring effects on current income per capita, highlighting the critical role of institutions in economic development. Austin complements this view by emphasizing that understanding these historical patterns is essential for grasping how contemporary trust levels and economic development are influenced by past institutional legacies (Austin 2008, p.2).

The creation of secure property rights is a key mechanism through which institutions foster long-term economic growth. North argues that the establishment of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These institutional developments reflect a broader trend where political institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, leading to falling transaction costs and increased economic activity (North 1989, p.6).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is also significant. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the persistence of certain institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. further illustrate this point by showing how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that this reversal was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, which interacted with opportunities for industrialization to shape long-term development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the importance of historical institutional frameworks in determining contemporary economic outcomes.

The legacy of mistrust and weak institutions has profound implications for economic development in Africa. Austin highlights how the cultural legacy of slavery complicates efforts to establish secure private property rights and foster long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins builds on this by showing that poor institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The shared finding that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth is grounded in the historical evidence of colonial strategies and their enduring effects on contemporary economic outcomes. The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate, with some scholars emphasizing the role of secure property rights and others focusing on distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise measurement of institutional quality, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al., shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups have had lasting negative effects on economic development.
