The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. This question matters intellectually because it addresses how and why some economies thrive while others stagnate over extended periods. The debate centres around two key positions. One position argues that institutions are crucial determinants of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of property rights, rule of law, and governance structures. Another position suggests that institutions alone cannot fully explain long-term economic performance; other factors such as geography, culture, and historical contingencies also play significant roles. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions drive economic outcomes versus the influence of these alternative factors. The question is how much institutions matter relative to other variables. This debate has profound implications for policy-making and economic theory, as understanding the causal mechanisms behind economic growth can inform strategies for development and poverty reduction. A clear resolution would require a nuanced examination of both institutional frameworks and broader contextual factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the advent of innovative financial tools marked a pivotal moment, described as the English financial revolution. This revolution was instrumental in securing property rights over time, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9). They argue that these institutional changes fostered secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate on the political factors underpinning this economic growth, noting that a regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange is critical for market development  (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). A study by North (1989, p.9) suggests that these institutional changes were significant and did not occur in isolation. The emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement reduced transaction costs per exchange. This reduction was paralleled by an increase in the aggregate volume of exchanges due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). As a result, the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP, indicating that institutional changes were pivotal and interconnected with broader economic developments.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns, which had lasting impacts on economic development. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutional differences had profound long-term effects on economic outcomes. This is significant because it highlights how the initial institutional frameworks set by colonial powers could lock former colonies into paths of underdevelopment, making it challenging for them to achieve sustained economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this by highlighting how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines social capital and cooperation necessary for growth. The institutional impacts of inequality and slave trades on economic development highlight how specific historical factors shaped institutions in ways that either advantaged elites or hindered overall economic growth. For example, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to societies where institutions were not conducive to broad-based development. This institutional framework restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, creating a vicious cycle where elites maintained their dominance through policies that favored their interests over those of the broader population. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, which is consistent with Sokoloff and Engerman’s emphasis on how unequal societies develop institutions that favor elites. Therefore, understanding the long-term effects of historical inequalities and their institutional legacies is crucial for comprehending contemporary economic disparities.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently using innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress necessitates significant institutional changes to resolve conflicts arising from economic growth. He notes that the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully managing these growth-induced conflicts, ensuring modern economic growth is possible without weakening societal cohesion. Mokyr (2005, p.49) builds on this idea by emphasizing that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of technological advancements would be limited and could eventually erode due to rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that technological innovations alone are insufficient for long-term growth; institutions must adapt to facilitate the efficient use of these innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also acknowledges that the process of becoming economically reasonable is not linear or irreversible, indicating that while institutional adjustments are necessary, their effectiveness can be hindered by various factors. This underscores the complexity of achieving sustained economic growth through technological advancements and institutional adaptations.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge conventional views of African economic performance by highlighting periods of growth and development before 1950. This challenges the notion that poverty was an inevitable outcome for Africa's pre-independence economies. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more dynamic economic landscape than previously thought. These patterns of growing and shrinking were tied to foreign trade and the market for exports; the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that colonial-era African economies experienced fluctuations driven by external trade dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) support this view, noting the importance of reconstructing real wage levels to understand the historical trajectory of African economic development. This underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic divergence across colonial Africa, highlighting the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15); (Simson 2019, p.2) both argue that colonial education systems had a significant impact on post-colonial economic trajectories in Africa. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the development of formal education under British rule was more conducive than other colonial powers, with local African leaders and families making cost-benefit calculations regarding the benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational infrastructure laid a foundation for post-independence economic growth by fostering human capital. Simson (2019, p.2) notes that post-independence expenditure trends were influenced by various factors such as industrial policy, military spending, and debt service payments, which often diverted resources from education (Simson 2019, p.11). These expenditures can impede the long-term benefits of colonial-era educational investments, highlighting the complex interplay between institutional legacies and contemporary economic policies. This pattern suggests that while colonial institutions may have laid some groundwork for growth, the efficacy of these institutions is contingent on post-colonial economic policies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women's historical contributions to economic growth have been integral to the continent's development trajectory. They demonstrate that during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, women played pivotal roles in agricultural production. For example, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production under colonial rule. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by the codification of customary law, which privileged men's access to land and resources. Hopkins (2009, p.8) adds that the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is crucial, as "good" institutions provide incentives for investment essential for development. This institutional framework, however, often marginalized women's contributions by reinforcing patriarchal structures through customary law. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further note that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system, with European courts based on common and civil law coexisting with native courts. This legal bifurcation exacerbated gender inequalities by codifying women's subordinate status in customary law. The implication is that institutional reforms aimed at fostering economic growth must address these historical gender disparities to ensure inclusive development.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's critique is particularly relevant because it highlights how simplistic causal frameworks can obscure the nuances of historical institutional development. By emphasizing the importance of understanding different historical periods and paths, Austin shows that institutional change was deeply influenced by political dynamics. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions arose solely from efficiency considerations, instead suggesting they were products of power struggles among different societal groups. Austin's analysis underscores how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, illustrating a more nuanced relationship between institutional change and local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15) . This insight is crucial because it indicates that the adoption of institutions favorable for growth varied based on specific historical contexts and responses to local conditions.

The variability in institutional outcomes across different regions underscores the importance of understanding how local economic actions influence institutional development. This relationship is further supported by North's argument that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which in turn drive economic growth (North 1989, p.6).
