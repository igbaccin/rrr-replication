The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks determine a nation's prosperity trajectory over centuries. Two positions are live in this debate. One position holds that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing how secure property rights and effective governance foster investment, innovation, and productivity. This view posits that countries with robust institutions experience sustained economic development, while those with weak or extractive institutions remain stagnant or underdeveloped. The other position argues that institutional frameworks are influenced by broader socio-economic factors such as cultural beliefs, colonial legacies, and geographic conditions (Author Year, p.N). Proponents of this view contend that understanding long-term growth requires a holistic approach that considers these interrelated elements. This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it informs strategies aimed at fostering economic development through institutional reform or broader structural changes. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes involves both immediate impact and enduring legacies over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) share a common thread: both highlight the critical importance of institutions in driving long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to vastly different institutional outcomes, with some colonies benefiting from rule-of-law-enforcing institutions while others were burdened by extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. These differing institutions had a profound impact on current income per capita. Both perspectives underscore that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, plays a pivotal role in determining long-run economic performance. Institutions serve as the backbone for economic activity, influencing everything from investment decisions to technological advancements.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.3) highlight that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex than simple cause-and-effect models suggest. Austin (2008, p.2), for example, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective suggests that institutional evolution is shaped by a multitude of factors over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) similarly emphasizes the need to move beyond superficial analyses of institutions, advocating for a deeper understanding of how they function within broader socio-economic contexts. Institutions are dynamic systems influenced by historical legacies and contemporary forces.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) both underscore the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that various measures of institutions, including constraints on executive power and property rights, consistently show similar results in their impact on economic outcomes. This consistency highlights the robustness of institutional frameworks in driving long-term growth. Credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that institutions are established and enforced effectively over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and North (1989, p.9) both explore how extractive institutions can impede long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9), similarly, notes how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for economic growth. Extractive institutions, by contrast, create barriers that hinder investment and innovation.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical for economic growth. This development was part of a broader evolution of political institutions that specified 'efficient' property rights and provided effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9). The implication involves both institutional differences and the regime's commitment to these rules. Moreover, North (1989, p.6) elaborates on how transaction costs are integral to this process; as the economy grows, efficient property rights and their effective enforcement lead to falling per-transaction costs but an increasing proportion of GNP dedicated to transactions. This pattern underscores the role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth through secure property rights.

Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers established different institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or Gold Coast, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. The legacy of these extractive institutions persists today in lower income per capita and poorer economic outcomes. These institutions facilitated long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges and property rights. The differential impact of colonial institutions on economic development is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate how population density and prosperity influenced European policies during colonization, leading to varied institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutional legacies varies across all former colonies. For instance, some colonies with extractive institutions have managed to transition towards more inclusive institutions over time, although this process is often fraught with challenges and requires significant political will and economic reforms.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the formation and persistence of these institutions, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were driven by power struggles among different social groups rather than efficiency considerations. For instance, in regions with high inequality, elites often used their political influence to implement policies that restricted competition and maintained their privileged status. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates how these distributional conflicts manifested through shared beliefs within societies, which became integral parts of the institutional framework. This dynamic highlights how economic institutions are deeply intertwined with power dynamics and social structures. The downstream implication is that any attempt to understand or reform institutions must account for these underlying distributional conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) point out that while factor endowments initially drove inequality, the persistence of these inequalities over time was reinforced through institutional mechanisms that favored elites, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality and institutional stagnation. This suggests that addressing distributional conflicts requires long-term structural reforms to disrupt this cycle.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth extends beyond their immediate impact on efficiency and productivity; they are deeply embedded in the social fabric, reflecting and reinforcing power dynamics within societies.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and other scholars (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) show that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a distinct impact on education. This impact was more positive than the broader narrative of colonialism's detrimental effects on economic institutions would suggest. These institutional changes altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African families and leaders. This dynamic is evident in the growth patterns of export production, which were tightly linked to foreign trade and influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, adapting their institutional prescriptions accordingly. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations based on purely exploitative motives (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that while British rule could foster education, its impact was contingent on local conditions and responses. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further underscore this point by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This economic resilience indicates that colonial institutions interacted with local factors in complex ways.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a significant adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that this impact is mediated through trust. A culture of mistrust developed within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it hinders cooperation and social capital, both critical for growth. Nunn (2008, p.19) further illustrates how centralized governance and well-developed markets facilitated trade, suggesting that the absence of such institutions due to the disruptive effects of the slave trades likely impeded economic progress. This institutional disintegration, coupled with persistent mistrust, creates a challenging environment for long-term economic development.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49), a perspective shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize that technological progress and the advancement of human knowledge are pivotal for sustained long-term growth. This technological progress requires institutional adjustments to facilitate its efficient use, as institutions can either enhance or hinder the diffusion of new technologies. Moreover, Mokyr highlights that economic reasonableness, an understanding of how economic systems function, is critical for sustaining technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets complements this view by noting that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures; thus, institutional changes are necessary to accommodate technological progress. Mokyr cautions that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while institutions may not drive growth directly, they play a crucial role in enabling and sustaining it.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme that binds various streams of scholarship together. Institutions can be understood as the rules and norms governing human interactions, and their impact on economic outcomes is profound. North highlights that institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic activity, specifying property rights and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and foster a stable environment for investment (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions is further underscored by the historical evidence from colonial settings. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those with rule of law and investment encouragement (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design directly influenced economic outcomes, with some colonies experiencing rapid resource extraction while others developed more sustainable economic systems. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring influence on long-run economic growth. North and Weingast illustrate this by showing how the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, which were critical for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These developments led to a more prosperous and stable economy in regions with inclusive institutions.

The role of institutions extends beyond their direct impact on economic growth. They also play a crucial part in enabling technological advancements and accommodating societal changes. Acemoglu et al. note that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This implies that institutions are guardians of economic progress, ensuring that technological gains are translated into sustainable growth. Austin challenges the notion that institutions are always efficient, arguing that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights that institutions can be shaped by power dynamics and social structures, not just efficiency considerations.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions drive growth directly or indirectly. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North contends that institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic activity, specifying property rights and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the development of financial instruments in seventeenth-century England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This shows that institutions can directly influence economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
