The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development studies. Institutions are widely understood to be the rules that shape human interaction and influence economic outcomes. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions cause growth directly or indirectly through other factors such as geographic endowments, cultural beliefs, or colonial policies. One stream of research argues that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are key to long-term economic development. Another stream posits that the efficacy of these institutions is contingent upon specific historical contexts and political incentives. A further stream suggests that distributional conflicts within societies play a significant role in shaping core economic institutions, offering an alternative explanation for institutional outcomes. The interaction between different institutional frameworks and their impact on economic growth remains a contentious issue.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2), Austin (2008, p.15) argue that the historical context of colonialism significantly influenced the types of institutions established in colonies, with extractive institutions often imposed to maximize resource extraction rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective underscores the role of political incentives and power dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes, highlighting that institutions are not merely neutral frameworks but products of specific historical and political contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote economic development. This view aligns with the idea that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts within societies. The shared belief among scholars is that understanding the historical and political contexts in which institutions emerge is crucial for comprehending their impact on long-run economic growth.

The interplay between institutions, power dynamics, and economic outcomes highlights a complex relationship that goes beyond simple causal mechanisms. Institutions are not static entities but evolve in response to changing political and economic conditions. The historical context of colonialism provides a rich case study for understanding how institutional frameworks can be shaped by external forces and internal power struggles. This interplay underscores the need for a nuanced approach to studying institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth, one that considers the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its interaction with broader historical and political contexts.

North argues that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9); this fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War had earlier prompted a trade-off of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues; this political maneuver reflects how institutions evolved in response to economic pressures. This evolution involved more than establishing rules; it also created mechanisms for enforcement and modification. North and Weingast advise against overestimating the revolutionary nature of these changes when comparing England's economic performance with France's. They argue that while England's institutions were pivotal, they did not fundamentally differ from those in other European countries like France (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This nuanced view suggests that while institutional development was critical, it is essential to consider the broader historical and political contexts in which these institutions operated.

Acemoglu et al. show that the type of colonial institution imposed was determined by the Europeans' settlement strategy and mortality rates, which influenced their approach to resource extraction versus investment. This finding is supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that the persistence of these extractive institutions significantly impacted current income per capita in former colonies. The legacy of these institutions continues to influence economic outcomes, as they were designed to benefit the colonizers rather than foster local development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The implications are profound: the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers created a pathway dependency that has been difficult to alter. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on institutional quality and economic performance in former colonies today.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Austin questions whether the institutionalist approach to economic growth fully accounts for why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not others. This limitation is particularly relevant when considering the diverse experiences of African colonies, where European settlers' presence or absence significantly shaped colonial policies. For instance, in regions without settler competition for labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). These policies illustrate how local economic and political actions influenced institutional prescriptions. Austin's analysis underscores that colonial administrations adapted their approaches based on the incentives created by indigenous activities, highlighting a more dynamic interaction between local contexts and institutional frameworks than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests. Nunn and Wantchekon complement this view by showing that historical legacies such as the slave trade have fostered enduring cultural norms, including mistrust, which continue to impact contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin's work demonstrates that institutional changes were not always top-down; local actions could influence them as well. This nuanced understanding of institutional evolution challenges the notion that institutions are the sole determinant of long-run economic growth and suggests a more complex interplay between historical legacies, local contexts, and institutional frameworks.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, indicating a more complex interplay between institutional frameworks and historical legacies. For instance, societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view is complemented by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They emphasize the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which relates to other parts of colonial Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective underscores the need for a deeper grasp of long-term economic development and its distribution to assess future growth possibilities. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves critical questions unanswered. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) address this gap by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This detailed analysis provides a more comprehensive understanding of the historical context that shaped contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.6) argue that technological advancements necessitate institutional adjustments. This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for sustained economic growth.

Technological progress often requires significant changes in societal structures, as Kuznets points out, noting that modern technology may not align well with traditional rural lifestyles or family patterns. These conflicts must be resolved peacefully to avoid long-term political instability (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr extends this idea by highlighting the role of useful knowledge in economic growth and the importance of institutions in reducing access costs to this knowledge. He notes that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements could become unsustainable due to rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

This interplay between technology and institutions is particularly evident in periods of industrialization, where new technologies disrupt existing social and economic structures. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional adaptability in harnessing technological advancements for long-term economic development.

Distributional conflicts are fundamental in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie and Sokoloff & Engerman argue that the core institutions of serfdom, community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were driven by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns. These conflicts arose from power imbalances within societies, where elites sought to maintain their dominance through institutional arrangements that restricted competition and preserved their privileged status (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For instance, in the context of New World societies, Sokoloff & Engerman show that differentials in factor endowments led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie warns against overemphasizing the role of efficiency in explaining these institutions. Distributional conflicts often result in suboptimal outcomes because they prioritize the interests of powerful groups over overall economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the complex interplay between power dynamics and institutional development, suggesting that a focus on distributional conflicts provides deeper insights into how economic institutions evolve.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of power and resources within societies. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to extractive states in high-mortality colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach highlights how institutions can perpetuate inequality and hinder economic development when they are designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is crucial. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by North's argument that institutions matter because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2).

The complex interplay between power dynamics and institutional development is evident in the historical context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This aligns with Austin's observation that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the role of power dynamics in shaping institutions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional outcomes across different colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that colonies with high mortality rates established extractive institutions, while those with low mortality rates developed more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison demonstrates how power dynamics and distributional conflicts shaped institutional development and, consequently, long-run economic growth. Austin's analysis of the Nigerian case further supports this by showing how colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local conditions and indigenous actions (Austin 2008, p.15), thus providing a concrete example of the historical evidence that settles the disagreement.
