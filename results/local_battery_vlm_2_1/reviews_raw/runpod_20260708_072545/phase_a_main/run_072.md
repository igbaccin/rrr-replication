The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake intellectually is the understanding of why some societies prosper while others stagnate, and how persistent institutional frameworks influence this trajectory. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or culture, or if they emerge endogenously through political and economic processes. One position emphasizes that institutions are critical for growth because they provide the stable framework necessary for investment and innovation. Another position argues that institutions themselves are products of deeper cultural or geographic determinants, which in turn influence economic outcomes.

The causal role of institutions in economic development is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. who argue that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups in colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast similarly highlight the importance of political institutions in securing property rights, which are essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective underscores that credible commitment underpins growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity.

North's work on institutions highlights their pivotal role in shaping economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions are fundamental to economic growth. They emphasize that the creation of financial instruments, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time. This development fostered a stable environment for long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. They note that the success of these political factors is evident in capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated market development. North and Weingast also recognize that institutional changes alone do not always drive revolutionary outcomes; other factors, such as bureaucratic structures, can influence economic results (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This nuanced view suggests that while institutions play a critical role, their impact on economic growth is contingent upon broader political and structural conditions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies were instrumental in influencing long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This pattern is evident in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where institutional frameworks were less conducive to long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita by stifling investment and economic growth. Regions with lower mortality rates saw the establishment of more inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These differences in institutional frameworks highlight how colonial policies laid the groundwork for divergent economic trajectories in former colonies.

The impact of these colonial institutions is further underscored by Acemoglu et al.'s analysis of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. They argue that this reversal was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective emphasizes the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic development, suggesting that the legacies of these policies continue to shape economic outcomes long after independence. However, Acemoglu et al.'s work also notes that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth.

Mokyr suggests that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective contrasts with views emphasizing institutions as primary drivers, instead highlighting technological advancements as pivotal. Kuznets elaborates on this idea, noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Economic growth is contingent upon resolving conflicts arising from technological changes without excessive costs, underscoring the role of the sovereign state in managing these transitions peacefully. This aligns with Mokyr's notion that coordination failures can impede economic improvement, suggesting that institutional progress complements technological advancements to sustain long-term growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Kuznets highlights that while technological progress is often irreversible, the growth of "economic reasonableness", a proxy for institutional adaptability, is not necessarily monotonic or irreversible. This qualification suggests that while institutions may support economic growth by facilitating technological adoption, they can also hinder it if they fail to adapt effectively (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today. The persistence of this mistrust is particularly significant because it impedes social cohesion and cooperation, both of which are crucial for sustainable development. This finding underscores the long-term impact of historical events on economic trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) also found that both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors played significant roles in shaping trust levels, with internal norms being at least twice as important as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that while the slave trade had a profound impact on African development through social institutions, its effects are mediated by complex interactions between individual attitudes and broader societal structures. The institutional legacy of mistrust created by the slave trades continues to influence economic performance in Africa today.

The Black Death in the 14th century triggered long-term structural changes that led to significant economic divergences within Europe by 1450 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that these changes were driven by institutional flexibility in northwestern Europe, which allowed for rapid adaptation to new economic opportunities. Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights how Holland's weak monopolies and political authorities enabled the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing, fostering a highly urbanised and commercialised region. This institutional environment contrasts with that of Tuscany, where existing monopolies were strengthened, leading to stagnation. Broadberry and Gupta further elucidate this divergence by noting that high productivity in northwestern Europe's traded goods sector was facilitated not only by production but also by merchant distribution and finance. The service sector thus played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period as it does today. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) also point out that the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates may have reinforced these economic disparities. This suggests that while institutional changes were pivotal, geographical factors also played a role in shaping long-term economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.16).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) show that the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth varied significantly across different contexts. British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, as evidenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders on the macro level and African families on the micro level. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the highly unequal distribution of wealth in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and contributing to persistent inequality. The varying impacts of colonial institutions on economic growth highlight the importance of specific institutional arrangements and their interaction with local contexts.

The historical economic performance of Africa has been misunderstood in previous characterizations that highlight persistent poverty. Broadberry and Gardner argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg's finding that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These scholars challenge previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance, which have often relied on limited data. Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates are lacking in the literature; they reconstruct real wage levels, showing significant economic activity during colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). These findings suggest that export production played a crucial role in shaping Africa's economic growth trajectories. Broadberry and Gardner note how economic trends were tied to foreign trade and local endowments, illustrating that external factors could drive domestic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This points to the importance of understanding both internal and external conditions when examining Africa’s long-term economic performance.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are the key drivers of pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). She contends that institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by power struggles between different social groups. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions emerged to maximize economic efficiency. Austin (2008, p.2) extends this analysis, demonstrating how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial governments often encouraged commodity production when settlers were absent, illustrating how local dynamics influenced institutional outcomes. This dynamic highlights the role of power relations in shaping institutions that could otherwise be seen as purely efficiency-driven.

Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that colonial governments sought fiscal security and stability, which sometimes led to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts helps explain why certain institutional forms persisted despite their apparent inefficiency. For instance, serfdom and guilds remained prevalent in pre-industrial Europe because they benefited powerful elites at the expense of less privileged groups. This power dynamic underscores how institutions can be designed to maintain social hierarchies. The implication is that understanding the distributional aspects of institutional design is crucial for grasping long-term economic growth patterns.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of financial instruments and the development of secure property rights, as seen with the Bank of England, were pivotal for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework reduced transaction costs, fostering a more efficient marketplace. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to extractive states in some colonies and growth-oriented institutions in others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These divergent paths illustrate how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic trajectories.

Distributional conflicts also play a crucial role in the persistence of certain institutions. Ogilvie contends that pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were maintained because they benefited powerful elites (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Austin's observation that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions based on the interests of indigenous economic and political actors (Austin 2008, p.15), further emphasizing the role of power dynamics in shaping institutions. The implication is clear: understanding who benefits from existing institutions is essential for grasping long-term economic growth patterns.

Institutional design significantly impacts economic outcomes by influencing property rights and enforcement mechanisms. North and Weingast demonstrate that credible commitment underpins growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England where political factors fostered secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. further support this by showing how equilibrium institutions can be extractive if designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding underscores that institutional frameworks are not neutral; they are shaped by the interests of those in power and, in turn, shape economic performance.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for long-term economic growth. North shows how secure property rights and financial instruments fostered market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The disagreement lies in whether these institutions arose organically or were designed to benefit specific groups. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted even when they hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial policies and the empirical comparison of former colonies with high and low mortality rates settle this disagreement. Colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic and political action, showing that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15).
