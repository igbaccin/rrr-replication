The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that has spurred extensive debate among economists and historians. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of prosperity and development across different regions and historical periods. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that specific institutional frameworks can foster secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). Others contend that institutions are shaped by underlying political dynamics and power structures, which in turn influence their effectiveness in driving growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

Institutions matter fundamentally because they create the framework within which economic activities occur. North's seminal work underscores how institutions reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights, both of which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by the findings that colonial institutions, influenced by mortality rates in European colonies, significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The implications are clear: institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups may not necessarily promote long-run economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) together with North (1989, p.6), argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This period saw significant institutional changes, notably the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). These developments reflected an increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The establishment of these institutions aimed to create rules for economic exchange and ensure their effective enforcement. This commitment to the rule of law is crucial because it provides a stable framework within which economic activities can thrive, reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The political institutions that emerged were committed to upholding these rules, thereby fostering a stable environment conducive to economic growth. This dynamic appears in the development of capital markets and the financial revolution that occurred in England, supporting economic growth and underscoring the importance of secure property rights in sustaining long-term economic development.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping the institutional frameworks that either fostered or hindered development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on local conditions, notably mortality rates. Where mortality was high, colonists set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional framework persisted over time, locking former colonies into a path of underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. further support the persistence of extractive institutions by emphasizing their design to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism highlights how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic trajectories. Despite this, the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies underscores the profound impact of colonial institutions on their economic futures.

Distributional conflicts shaped economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For example, the persistence of serfdom can be attributed to landowners' interests in maintaining a labor force dependent on their estates. This institutional framework restricted social mobility and suppressed technological innovation, as those at the bottom lacked incentives or means to adopt new methods and those at the top saw little benefit in promoting change that might challenge their power (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The distributional conflict mechanism is also evident in New World societies where elites used institutions to maintain control over resources. For instance, policies favoring landowners restricted competition from emerging entrepreneurs, thereby perpetuating inequality and stunting economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The role of institutions in maintaining elite dominance varied across regions; some areas experienced stagnation due to entrenched power structures while others saw dynamic shifts driven by changes in factor endowments and political dynamics.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence, challenging previous assumptions about the region's economic underdevelopment. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) similarly demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. The shared finding of early economic development in regions often overlooked is a testament to the complex interplay of institutions, trade, and local endowments.

The role of foreign trade and export markets was pivotal in shaping these economies' growth trajectories; Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight that patterns of economic expansion were closely tied to external market dynamics. These findings underscore the importance of global economic integration, suggesting that access to international markets facilitated economic development by stimulating local production and distribution networks. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) caution against overgeneralizing these effects: trade can spur growth but its benefits are unevenly distributed due to variations in local institutions and endowments.

This pattern of uneven growth has significant implications for understanding the long-run economic performance of regions. Institutions play a crucial role in shaping the overall trajectory of economic development and the distribution of its benefits within societies.

Nunn attributes Africa's underdevelopment primarily to its slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.2); Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). The slave trade led to political disintegration and the destruction of social capital, which had adverse effects on economic development. Nunn highlights that the slave trades lasted for nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, significantly longer than the period of colonial rule (approximately 75 years). This long-term disruption hindered the formation of stable political structures and social institutions conducive to economic growth. Austin notes that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15), suggesting that local economic incentives could counteract some of the negative effects of the slave trades. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The authors reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965), providing evidence that economic development was influenced by factors beyond just the slave trades. This long-term perspective highlights how different historical periods and paths shaped Africa's economic trajectory, revealing a more complex picture than one solely focused on institutions or the slave trade.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective underscores that advancements in technology and intellectual capital are more fundamental to long-run economic growth than institutional frameworks. Kuznets elaborates on this point by noting that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations, highlighting the interplay between technological changes and societal adaptations (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts arising from modern economic growth.

This mechanism suggests that while institutional progress complements technological advancements, it is not solely responsible for sustained economic improvement. Kuznets reinforces this idea by emphasizing that without these institutional adjustments, the benefits of technological progress could be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Economic improvements may ultimately be eroded if institutions do not evolve to support technological advancements.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and their effective enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who highlight how European settlers established institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional frameworks that North describes and those implemented by European colonists both demonstrate the significance of property rights and contractual enforcement in fostering economic growth.

The effectiveness of these institutions depends on their adaptability to changing economic conditions. Austin points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This adaptability is crucial because it ensures that technological progress translates into tangible economic benefits rather than being dissipated by inefficiencies or conflicts. Furthermore, North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth, as evidenced by the political factors governing how rules are enforced and changed in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in this period was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The historical evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of institutional effectiveness. North and Weingast show that recent research significantly upgrading France's economic performance before the French Revolution has led to an overhauling of traditional interpretations of British as well as French economic history (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that the institutional changes described were revolutionary. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth.

The disagreement over whether institutional change is primarily driven by efficiency or distributional conflicts is settled by empirical comparisons of institutional frameworks across different regions and periods. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In contrast, Ogilvie contends that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, which can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the contrasting outcomes in colonies with high versus low mortality rates. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions persisted in high-mortality colonies, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), while North and Weingast demonstrate that credible commitment to enforceable property rights fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The measurement of institutional effectiveness through long-term economic outcomes provides clear evidence for the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.
