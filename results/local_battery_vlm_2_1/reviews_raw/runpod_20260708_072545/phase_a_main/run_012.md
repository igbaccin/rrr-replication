The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and political science. Institutions are defined as the rules of the game that structure social interactions and shape incentives for individuals and organizations. The intellectual stakes hinge on understanding whether and how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder sustained economic development. This debate is centered around two main positions: one arguing that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial drivers of long-term growth, while the other posits that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic determinants, may be more influential. The core disagreement lies in whether institutional quality is a primary determinant of economic success or merely an ancillary factor. Institutions incentivize investment and innovation while reflecting deeper structural issues within societies. This discussion underscores the need to understand how institutions evolve and interact with other socio-economic factors over time, particularly in the context of colonial legacies and post-colonial development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and marked the beginning of the English financial revolution. The emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement facilitated market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction while increasing specialization and division of labor in the economy. As a result, the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP, multiplying the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, where England's performance indicates that institutional changes were revolutionary in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

This highlights the importance of credible commitment in sustaining long-run economic growth.

The institutions established during colonial periods significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The European policies that governed colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks. In high-mortality regions, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to their home countries. These institutions often lacked mechanisms for enforcing property rights and encouraging investment. In contrast, in regions with lower mortality rates, settlers established more inclusive institutions that fostered economic growth by promoting secure property rights and investment (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19). This divergence in institutional frameworks was driven by the differing colonization strategies adopted by Europeans, which were influenced by factors such as disease environments and settler mortality rates. The persistence of these institutions over time has had lasting effects on economic outcomes, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) qualify this pattern by noting that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that even if institutions are designed to maximize rents for European colonists, they may not necessarily promote economic development. The implications of this divergence in institutional design are profound, as it underscores how initial conditions set by colonial powers can have enduring impacts on economic trajectories.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This educational development was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by new social and political institutions introduced during British rule. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). British rule's economic policies also supported export production, which was tied to growing foreign trade and markets for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This development in education and export production laid a foundation for economic growth in Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century. While these findings suggest a positive impact of British colonial rule on African economic development, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that the long-term effects of this growth must be understood in relation to other parts of colonial Africa.

The literature on African economic development and institutional reforms highlights that women played significant roles in shaping long-run growth dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) underscore that during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, women made substantial contributions to economic growth. This perspective underscores that the role of institutions is crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The extent to which these contributions were sustained or hindered by colonial institutions remains a subject of ongoing inquiry.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Pamuk supports this view by noting that structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to significant wage gaps between northwestern Europe and other regions by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This early divergence was driven by institutional differences as well as factors such as merchant distribution, finance, and climate. These merchants played a pivotal role in economic development, similar to their influence in modern economies. During the early modern period, the service sector's contribution enabled living standards to rise through falling relative prices of manufactured goods (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This uneven distribution of economic benefits highlights how structural changes, such as those driven by merchant activities and climate conditions, influenced long-term economic growth. Pamuk cautions that institutional flexibility also played a key role in enabling regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities, while regions like Tuscany saw stagnation (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The divergence in urbanisation rates between northwestern Europe and southern Europe further underscores these structural changes. This pattern of early economic divergence suggests that while institutions are important, they operate within a broader context shaped by historical events and environmental factors.

Van Zanden and other scholars (van Zanden 1999, p.1; van Zanden 2009, p.19) argue that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during 1500-1800. This perspective challenges optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth. In particular, guilds played a crucial role by guaranteeing fair execution of apprentice contracts. This institutional framework facilitated skill acquisition but also created monopolies that restricted market access to maintain high wages (van Zanden 2009, p.3). The implications are significant: while guilds fostered skill development and long-term economic performance, their restrictive practices could impede broader economic growth by limiting competition. Van Zanden's analysis of wage data across European cities further underscores the complex relationship between economic development and living standards. He found that urbanisation did not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These findings suggest that while institutions like guilds were pivotal in shaping economic outcomes, their effects were nuanced and depended on specific historical and regional contexts.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For instance, institutional differences in New World societies were driven by the unequal distribution of power and resources, which advantaged elites through government policies. These policies restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts shaped institutions to maintain elite interests (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In Europe, economic institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were tools used by elites to consolidate their power. Ogilvie suggests that these institutions emerged from conflicts over resource distribution, with elites manipulating them to maintain control (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of inequality in New World societies underscores how institutional frameworks can be shaped by distributional conflicts, reinforcing the idea that efficiency is not always the primary driver. Sokoloff and Engerman qualify this perspective by noting that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific historical context and factor endowments also play significant roles. This suggests that while institutions may be shaped by distributional conflicts, their impact on economic growth can vary depending on broader contextual factors. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional frameworks thus offers a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2), which persists today and affects economic outcomes. This mistrust is a persistent cultural trait that influences contemporary economic behavior, including trust in institutions and fellow citizens. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the legacy of colonialism and the slave trade has left deep imprints on African societies, shaping their institutional frameworks and economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.15). This mistrust can hinder economic development by reducing cooperation and investment in public goods. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that this cultural mistrust is a significant factor in understanding Africa's economic underperformance relative to other regions. The internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as the external factors such as rule of law and trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), play a crucial role in perpetuating this cycle. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions when examining the long-run effects of historical events on economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either facilitate or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that European colonial strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies with high mortality rates versus those with low mortality. Extractive institutions were established where colonists faced high mortality, resulting in the transfer of resources back to Europe rather than investing in local economic development. In regions like the United States and Australia, settlers implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.15) (Hopkins 2009, p.8) further illustrates that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic actions, highlighting a more dynamic interplay between local factors and colonial policies. The legacy of these institutions persists in current economic disparities among former colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that the creation of financial instruments and institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional framework provided the security needed for long-term capital markets to thrive. The English financial revolution exemplifies how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.9). Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) emphasizes that distributional conflicts shape institutions, which can lead to the persistence of seemingly inefficient structures like serfdom or guilds. These conflicting interests often result in institutions that maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-term growth.

The historical evidence shows that colonial institutions have had a lasting impact on economic development trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to institutional changes introduced during colonialism. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who argues that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. The new economic history of Africa underscores the importance of understanding these institutional legacies in order to inform effective reforms and eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This historical comparison settles the disagreement over whether colonial institutions are extractive or growth-oriented by showing that their design maximized rents for European colonists, which inhibited long-term economic development.
