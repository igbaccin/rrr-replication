The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question for understanding development trajectories across societies and historical periods. At stake intellectually is the identification of causal mechanisms that underpin persistent economic disparities between regions. A live disagreement exists between scholars who argue for the primacy of institutional factors, such as secure property rights and effective governance, versus those who emphasize other determinants like geographic endowments or cultural factors. These competing explanations offer different pathways to understanding why some economies thrive while others stagnate.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes how institutions influence long-run economic growth by shaping incentives and constraints on economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast similarly highlight the role of credible commitments underpinning growth through secure property rights and effective governance (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective underscores that institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development by influencing the distribution of resources, investment patterns, and overall productivity. Institutions matter because they create the rules of the game that shape economic behavior over the long term.

North highlights that the development of financial institutions and instruments was pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a significant milestone in this regard. By establishing secure property rights through political institutions that enforced efficient property rights, these developments facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction. The political factors underpinning these institutions are crucial for ensuring that the regime is committed to the rules. This commitment is reflected in the remarkable success of capital markets, which indicates the effectiveness of the institutional changes implemented during this period (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). However North and Weingast caution that while financial institutions played a significant role, it is essential to consider other factors as well, such as the broader economic performance. The implication here is that while financial institutions are important, they are not the sole determinant of economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The European settlers' mortality rates influenced the type of institutions they established in their colonies: high mortality led to extractive institutions, while low mortality fostered more inclusive ones. This difference in institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding the varying economic trajectories of former colonies. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole, hindering local development. This institutional divergence, driven by mortality rates, has had lasting effects on income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) work further underscores that these institutions are designed to maximize rents for the colonizers, not necessarily for long-run growth. This perspective is qualified by the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also note. The interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in the long-run development of these colonies, suggesting that institutional changes introduced by colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes today.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This approach underscores the need for deeper understanding of historical economic paths beyond structural impediments. The shared claim that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments shifts attention toward the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth. In addition, Austin (2008, p.15) shows that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This suggests that the presence or absence of settler populations influenced the institutional prescriptions adopted by colonial administrations. Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, thereby altering their institutional responses based on indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic indicates that local economic conditions played a crucial role in shaping colonial institutions. The interplay between settler presence, indigenous economic activity, and colonial governance underscores how historical contexts shaped long-term economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that Africa's slave trade had long-lasting negative impacts on economic development by fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust. This mistrust was not merely a product of the slave trade's effects on the external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, but also deeply rooted in individuals' internal norms. This prolonged period of exploitation left deep scars on African societies, shaping their institutions and economic trajectories in profound ways. The long-term effects of the slave trade extend beyond economic stagnation to social structures that perpetuate mistrust. The cultural legacy of the slave trade thus persists today by affecting contemporary levels of intergroup trust and undermining economic growth.

Distributional conflicts explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies better than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe can be attributed to landowners' ability to leverage their political power. This enabled them to resist reforms that would have enhanced agricultural productivity but threatened their control over labor (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). In regions with abundant land and scarce labor, such as North America, institutions evolved to promote broader economic participation. Areas with limited land and abundant labor, like Latin America, saw institutions that concentrated power in the hands of elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern of institutional development had profound implications for long-run growth trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that distributional conflicts are crucial; they do not operate in isolation from other factors such as cultural beliefs and technological change. These additional influences can either amplify or mitigate the effects of distributional struggles on institutional outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that this process of knowledge accumulation involves the generation of new ideas and the creation of institutions that facilitate their dissemination. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This means that as societies develop technologically, they must also adapt their social structures to accommodate these changes. For instance, the rise of industrialization in Europe necessitated the formation of new economic institutions that could manage and coordinate complex production processes. Mokyr (2005, p.49) cautions that while technological progress can be rapid and transformative, institutional progress is often more gradual and subject to various social and political constraints (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that even if a society experiences significant technological advancements, the benefits of these innovations may not be fully realized unless accompanied by commensurate institutional changes. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further emphasizes this point by highlighting the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby ensuring that economic progress is sustainable (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological and institutional advancements underscores the complex nature of long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce efficient transactions. North shows that the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution was critical for reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflected an increased security of property rights, which is fundamental to both capital markets and long-term economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrating how institutional frameworks can significantly influence economic outcomes.

The historical evidence from capital markets further highlights the importance of institutions. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This political commitment to enforcing rules underpins long-term economic growth, as seen in the remarkable success of English capital markets during this period. Austin argues that European settlers' presence and their establishment of colonial governments shaped institutional prescriptions, which influenced indigenous commodity production and overall economic development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that while European colonies experienced significant technological changes, the benefits were not fully realized without commensurate institutional changes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in mediating the impact of technological progress on economic development. North also emphasizes that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which are critical for long-term growth (North 1989, p.6).

The underlying agreement across these sources is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth by providing secure property rights and enforcing efficient transactions (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate. Austin argues that the institutional prescriptions of colonial governments were influenced by indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the role of local factors in shaping institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s focus on European settlers' strategies and their establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in colonial policies across different regions, which shows how both local factors and broader institutional frameworks influenced economic development. This variation can be measured through comparative analyses of colonial policies and their long-term economic impacts on former colonies.
