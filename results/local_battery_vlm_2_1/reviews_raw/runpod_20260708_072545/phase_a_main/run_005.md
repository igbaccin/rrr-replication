The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and development studies. At stake is an understanding of why some societies thrive while others stagnate, and how policy can influence these outcomes. Institutions are the rules of the game that shape political, economic, and social interactions. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-term economic development, while others posit that they are shaped by underlying cultural, geographical, or historical factors. A third group views distributional conflicts as a better explanation for core economic institutions. The question hinges on whether institutions are best understood as causes or effects in the process of economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the long-term economic performance of their colonies. Extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources back to the metropole, hindered development in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast. These findings suggest that colonial policies left a lasting impact on institutional frameworks, which in turn shaped economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9) who emphasizes how secure property rights and effective enforcement of these rights are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments illustrate how efficient property rights can facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) establish that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a pivotal moment, as it led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and can be described as "the English financial revolution." This institution reflected increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The process was driven by the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War.

The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should result in falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This is because increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP. They highlight that the success of these institutions is evident from capital markets, underpinning economic growth and market development. This focus on political factors extends beyond rules governing economic exchange to include how these rules are enforced and changed, with the regime or sovereign's commitment to these rules being a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional changes in England reflect a broader pattern of how secure property rights can be established through effective enforcement mechanisms, which in turn fosters long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al., along with other studies like Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33), argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The key mechanism involves the differential mortality rates of European colonizers, which led to varying institutional outcomes in colonies. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where colonial powers implemented institutions that maximized short-term extraction at the expense of sustainable development. In colonies where mortality rates were lower, such as the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional divergence has had lasting effects on economic outcomes, with former extractive colonies continuing to lag behind those with more growth-oriented institutions.

These differential institutional impacts highlight a broader pattern: the nature of colonial institutions can significantly alter long-run economic trajectories. The persistence of these extractive institutions has been a critical factor in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, with those burdened by such institutions failing to industrialize and develop as rapidly as their counterparts (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy underscores how colonial policies continue to shape economic disparities today.

Colonial policies in Africa significantly shaped institutional frameworks and influenced long-term development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa underscores how colonial institutions impacted African governance. This impact is further illustrated by Frankema (2012, p.15), who argues that British rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers, altering the perceived benefits of education for local populations (Frankema 2012, p.15). The role of institutions in economic growth is evident in how they provided incentives for investment and development. This dynamic is reflected in Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), who demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous views on Africa's colonial-era economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The availability of substantial economic data for the colonial era, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), supports detailed historical national accounting analyses. These findings are crucial because they reveal that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors including transport costs and political institutions. This variation highlights how colonial policies shaped immediate economic outcomes and set the stage for long-term institutional legacies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Historical inequalities shaped by institutions have led to persistent economic disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the institutional frameworks in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition, thereby perpetuating the existing power structures and hindering broader economic development. This dynamic is evident when factor endowments are considered: while all colonies started with abundant land and natural resources relative to labor, their institutions shaped by extreme wealth disparities ensured that most remained highly unequal (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon provide a complementary perspective on how historical inequalities have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. They show that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust reduces trustworthiness, which is a critical factor for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The institutional legacies of colonial rule shaped immediate economic outcomes and set the stage for long-term disparities in trust and economic performance.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced by 1800, with productivity and development patterns differing significantly (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta show that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector was driven by production as well as merchant distribution and finance. This highlights the role of services in economic growth during this period. The uneven distribution of early modern development benefits is evident in the wages of skilled versus unskilled laborers, with skilled workers enjoying a larger surplus over basic food needs (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Van Zanden's analysis supports this divergence by revealing that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards across Europe from 1500 to 1800. This challenges optimistic views of early modern growth, suggesting instead that the benefits were unevenly distributed across regions (van Zanden 1999, p.1). Van Zanden also notes a shift in economic focus to North Sea countries like Holland and England by the eighteenth century, confirming that economic development raised relative prices for basic foodstuffs. In England after 1740, wheat prices were highest, indicating a more expensive standard of living compared to other European regions (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This pattern underscores the complex interplay between productivity gains, regional specialization, and living standards in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for efficiently harnessing technological innovations to drive economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the integration of modern technology necessitates significant institutional changes, as it conflicts with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures. This process requires a cohesive political system, the sovereign state, to manage these transitions without disrupting society's fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological and institutional progress ensures sustainable economic improvement. Without complementary institutional development, economic gains could be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Moreover, Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that these adjustments are not uniform across regions or periods; they vary based on the specific innovations introduced and the sectors affected. This variability underscores the importance of adaptable institutions in facilitating long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) assert that institutional theories often oversimplify historical paths by compressing different periods into a single narrative. Austin's critique highlights how colonial administrations adjusted their policies based on indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating that institutional changes were dynamic responses to local incentives. For instance, Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium despite Nunn’s hypothesis, demonstrating the complexity of these interactions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie extends this critique by arguing that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights drive institutional changes. She posits that European institutions were shaped by cultural values such as diligence, thrift, tenacity, honesty, and tolerance (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of considering cultural factors alongside economic incentives when examining institutional development. Austin further argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by ignoring the distinct historical paths of different regions, thereby missing critical nuances in institutional evolution. The distributional conflicts Ogilvie highlights illustrate how economic institutions are products of power struggles and cultural beliefs as well as efficient responses to market conditions. This interplay between historical specificity and distributional conflicts is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

Distinct historical paths and institutional evolution are key to understanding long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation was driven by mortality rates and the presence of European settlers, which shaped whether extractive or inclusive institutions were established. These findings highlight how the specific historical context of colonial rule directly influenced long-run economic development. Austin builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis over-simplifies causation (Austin 2008, p.2). He emphasizes the importance of recognizing distinct historical paths and the nuances in institutional evolution, which are often overlooked when focusing solely on broad patterns.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's work. North argues that institutions matter because they shape property rights and enforcement mechanisms, which are crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.9). He shows that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework provided the security needed for long-term capital markets to develop, illustrating how institutions can drive economic progress.

Ogilvie's work complements these insights by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). She argues that these institutions are also products of power struggles and cultural beliefs. This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional development, where economic incentives are just one part of a broader social and political dynamic. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate this by showing how extractive institutions can persist even when they do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They argue that such institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term rents over long-run growth.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is particularly relevant here. He argues that this thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, missing critical nuances in institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15). By examining specific cases, such as colonial administrations encouraging indigenous commodity production, Austin demonstrates how economic institutions can adapt to local incentives and political actions. Hopkins extends this idea by showing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He argues that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing investment incentives, which are essential for development.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that variations in European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). The remaining disagreement lies in how to interpret the specific historical paths and nuances in institutional evolution. Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by ignoring these distinct historical paths (North 1989, p.6), which is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Historical evidence from colonial administrations shows that institutions adapted to local incentives, illustrating how specific historical contexts shaped institutional outcomes and, consequently, long-run economic development.
