The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is understanding how the structure and functioning of societies influence their economic trajectories over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or culture, or whether they are endogenous, evolving in response to internal political and economic dynamics. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutional design in determining economic outcomes, arguing that certain institutions can foster growth while others inhibit it. This view is often paired with historical analyses showing how specific institutional arrangements have led to sustained economic development or stagnation. Another position posits distributional conflicts as a more fundamental driver of institutional change.

The centrality of institutions in economic growth is underscored by North's seminal work, which argues that institutions matter because they define the incentives and constraints within which individuals operate (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al. who highlight how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, long-run economic growth was more likely. In extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, economic stagnation prevailed (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is further illustrated by the example of the Bank of England, whose creation and development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also examined through the lens of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional arrangements on long-run economic outcomes, highlighting how institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not foster long-term growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the development trajectories of these colonies (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This historical analysis is complemented by Austin's work, which emphasizes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that European settlers often competed for African labor, influencing colonial institutions and economic outcomes in complex ways (Austin 2008, p.15).

The debate over institutions and economic growth also engages with rival explanations that focus on distributional conflicts. Ogilvie challenges the notion that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, she argues that these institutions were shaped by power struggles and the need to manage distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is illustrated by the example of guilds, whose rules were motivated by Catholic beliefs about the afterlife (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This view suggests that understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires examining the underlying power dynamics and cultural factors that shape institutional design.

This is because Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates and established varied institutional frameworks. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This approach contrasts with colonies like the United States and Australia where Europeans settled and established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment.

The differential outcomes across colonies highlight how initial institutional frameworks set during colonization periods can have lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al.'s findings are supported by the analysis of various measures of institutions, including constraints on executive power and property rights indices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). It is important to note that these institutions do not always encourage economic development; they often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic growth in colonial contexts.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress is a key factor in sustained economic development. This progress requires institutional adjustments for efficient use, such as reducing access costs to knowledge. These adjustments include the establishment of formal institutions like scholarly societies (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that modern technology demands significant societal changes; these changes are often accompanied by conflicts that must be resolved peacefully to ensure long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further notes that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This implies that while technology drives growth, institutions play a critical role in sustaining it. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) also highlights that these conflicts can weaken societal cohesion if not managed properly. This underscores the importance of strong political frameworks in supporting long-term economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also highlight the critical role of institutional development, noting how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) explains that this process often began as a response to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, where specific rights were traded for revenues. This highlights how political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights can lead to falling transaction costs, even as the volume of exchanges increases due to specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). They point out that while institutions like the Bank of England were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, similar institutional changes did not necessarily lead to equivalent economic outcomes across different countries. This implies that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be moderated by broader political and historical contexts.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) share that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping Europe’s economic institutions before industrialisation. The distributional struggles between different classes and groups often led to the creation of institutions like serfdom and guilds, which served to maintain power balances. Colonial governments often adjusted their policies based on indigenous actions, highlighting how power struggles shaped institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective challenges conventional views by suggesting that institutions are products of social and political negotiations.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) along with Nunn (2008, p.2), argue that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition. They show that the initial factor endowments of these societies, such as abundant land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme wealth, human capital, and political power inequalities from the outset. This disparity in factor endowment shaped institutional evolution by creating incentives for elites to establish policies that maintained their advantages. These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and constrained economic opportunities for non-elite groups, perpetuating inequality over time. Nunn (2008, p.19) further qualifies this pattern by showing how the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, facilitated trade with Europeans. This suggests that the specific institutional arrangements in New World societies were crucial for determining their economic trajectories. Thus, the persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the institutions that evolved from initial conditions of extreme inequality, which restricted competition and limited economic mobility for non-elite groups.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) demonstrate that institutional reforms in Africa have been pivotal for poverty reduction. They show that institutions can be transformed to enhance economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores how institutional quality can drive economic growth by fostering a conducive environment for investment, ultimately leading to poverty reduction. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further emphasize that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth potential (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that institutional reforms aimed at addressing historical economic disparities can pave the way for sustained economic development in Africa. Hopkins's observation that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions qualifies this pattern (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that while institutional reforms are key to poverty reduction, their success is contingent on broader social and political factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in how different institutional frameworks have historically influenced economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists, experienced persistently lower income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, such as those established by the Bank of England, were crucial for economic growth in Europe. This highlights how institutions that facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs are pivotal for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.9).

Moreover, Austin notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional policies based on local economic and political actions, which could lead to different development paths even within the same colonial framework (Austin 2008, p.15). This underscores that institutions are not static; they evolve in response to broader social and political contexts. Hopkins reinforces this by demonstrating how societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions, thereby fostering economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The historical evidence shows that institutional reforms aimed at addressing economic disparities can pave the way for sustained development. Ogilvie asserts that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that understanding the underlying social dynamics is crucial for effective institutional design. The case of Africa provides a compelling example: Hopkins shows that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is vital for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether institutions are shaped by efficiency or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial institutions settles this disagreement: the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This highlights that institutions are shaped by both efficiency considerations and distributional conflicts, with the latter often dominating in extractive contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).
