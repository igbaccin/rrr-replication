The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, as it addresses whether and how the rules and norms governing societies can drive sustained economic progress. At stake intellectually are two competing views on causation. One view prioritizes the impact of economic factors such as population density and savings rates. The other view emphasizes the role of institutions in securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. This disagreement reflects a broader tension between neoclassical theories, which focus on resource allocation and efficiency, and institutionalist approaches, which highlight the importance of political and social structures in shaping economic outcomes. A key point of contention is whether institutions are merely responsive to economic conditions or if they actively shape long-term growth trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North posits that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing increasingly effective enforcement should be reflected in the emergence of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. This perspective underscores how institutional changes can lower per-transaction costs, thereby promoting economic activity. Austin (2008, p.2) shares this emphasis on institutional evolution but also highlights the complexity of historical paths and the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin notes that while economists often view the behaviour of older industrial economies within patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience, the challenge lies in explaining why certain institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not others. This suggests that the dynamics of institutional adoption are influenced by a range of factors beyond simple efficiency considerations.

The role of credible commitment in fostering long-term economic growth is another critical aspect highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.2). They argue that the degree to which a regime or sovereign is committed to rules governing economic exchange is essential for sustained growth. This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on secure property rights, but it also introduces the idea of political commitment as a necessary condition for institutional effectiveness. The success of England in fostering economic growth through credible commitments contrasts with France's experience, where similar levels of economic performance suggest that institutions alone may not be sufficient to drive significant growth differences. This nuanced view underscores the importance of both institutional design and political context in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North (1989, p.9) describes how the creation of the Bank of England and the emergence of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. The Bank of England was established during fiscal crises, leading to increased security of property rights over time. This development reflects a broader political model where certain rights were traded for revenues, illustrating the critical role of institutions in economic growth.

They highlight that the success of England's political institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets. The degree to which a regime is committed to these rules is a critical factor in fostering economic growth. This commitment underpins the effectiveness of institutions in reducing transaction costs.

The implication is that institutions alone may not be sufficient to drive significant growth differences; political commitment and the enforcement of property rights are equally important. This nuanced view underscores the importance of both institutional design and political context in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

As specialization and division of labor increase, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP. This dynamic illustrates how institutional changes can have far-reaching economic implications by reducing transaction costs and fostering growth over the long run.

Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. European colonizers established different institutions depending on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality, they set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole; this was the case in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). On the other hand, in regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound economic consequences. The extractive institutions hindered long-term economic development by failing to encourage investment or protect property rights (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Colonies that had more inclusive institutions saw sustained economic growth. These findings underscore how colonial institutions shaped economic trajectories through their impact on property rights and investment incentives. This dynamic highlights how institutional legacies from colonialism continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today.

In contrast to these findings, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts rather than efficiency are key to understanding pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) posits that institutions such as serfdom, the community, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by power struggles over resources between different social groups. These institutions served as tools used by elites to maintain their dominance, which often resulted in suboptimal economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations adapted institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic where power relations influenced institutional design and implementation. For example, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production, highlighting how local conditions and power dynamics shaped institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15). These adaptations reflected the distributional conflicts of the time. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights also played a role in shaping institutions, which could block the development of property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores how institutional legacies from colonialism continue to influence economic outcomes today, as seen in the enduring effects of serfdom and guild systems on modern economic structures.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust is substantial, with internal norms of mistrust playing a more significant role than external factors. This mistrust, rooted in historical exploitation, continues to shape contemporary social dynamics and economic interactions. One specific mechanism through which this mistrust operates is the erosion of cooperative behaviors essential for economic development. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that sustained trade was only possible when centralized governments and well-developed markets were present, highlighting how institutional structures can mitigate some of the adverse effects of historical mistrust. The pervasive nature of this mistrust means that even in regions with strong institutions, the legacy of the slave trade continues to hinder economic progress. This is evident in the enduring effects on trust levels, which are crucial for various aspects of economic development, including international trade and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistent culture of mistrust, therefore, creates a significant barrier to long-run economic growth by undermining the social capital necessary for effective cooperation and development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) re-evaluate historical economic performance in Africa, challenging the notion of persistent poverty. They reveal that many African economies experienced growth periods tied to foreign trade and export markets. Broadberry and Gardner note patterns of growing and shrinking economies closely linked to external trade dynamics (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These fluctuations highlight the role of market forces in shaping economic trajectories across different regions. This historical perspective underscores the importance of considering both internal and external influences on long-term economic growth.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are both crucial drivers of long-run economic growth, as Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue. Kuznets argues that technological innovations require complementary adjustments in institutions and ideologies to be efficiently utilized. This interplay is essential for sustained economic growth over extended periods. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the role of useful knowledge as the primary driver of economic growth. Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the dissemination and application of this knowledge, thereby enhancing productivity and economic development. For instance, Kuznets notes that modern technology often conflicts with traditional modes of life, necessitating institutional changes to resolve these conflicts peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The sovereign state is pivotal in managing such growth-induced conflicts without weakening societal cohesion (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further emphasizes the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements. Without this institutional support, economic improvements may be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of economic reasonableness is not as linear or irreversible as technological progress, indicating that while institutions are necessary for harnessing technological innovations, their effectiveness can vary over time.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and productivity of economies through secure property rights and enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North argues that institutions matter because they define the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement, which is critical for fostering economic growth. The English financial revolution, for example, showcases how the creation of new financial instruments reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights (North 1989, p.9).

The role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further underscored by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. They demonstrate that credible commitments to secure property rights were pivotal for economic growth; this was achieved through the evolution of institutions governing public choice, which bound the sovereign to rules that encouraged investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The case of England shows how institutional changes can lead to long-term economic benefits. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical processes by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative, which overlooks the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). He highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions, indicating that local contexts matter significantly.

The interaction between population density, prosperity, and European policies is crucial for understanding long-run development. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not encourage long-term growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie's argument supports this perspective by stating distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in fostering technological advancements and economic growth is evident. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies with associated institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). These findings are consistent with North's argument that institutions defining property rights and their enforcement are critical for long-term economic growth.

Shared finding: Institutions are central to shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and productivity of economies through secure property rights and enforcement mechanisms, as well as fostering technological advancements (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The disagreement lies in whether institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists can encourage long-term growth. This is settled by Austin's analysis showing that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic actions, indicating the significance of local contexts and the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15).
