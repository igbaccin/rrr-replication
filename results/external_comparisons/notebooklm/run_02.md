The proposition that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth has become a dominant paradigm in modern economic history and development economics. This literature review examines the theoretical foundations of this hypothesis, the empirical evidence supporting it—particularly through the lens of colonial history—and the significant critical debates surrounding its methodology and conclusions.

### The Theoretical Foundation: Transaction Costs and Credible Commitment

The institutional hypothesis defines institutions as the “rules of the game in a society” or the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction. At its core, the theory posits that societies providing incentives and opportunities for investment will prosper, while those failing to do so will stagnate.

Douglass North argues that modern economic growth is rooted in the transition from personal to impersonal exchange. In personal exchange, dense social networks keep transaction costs low through reputation and repeat dealings. However, the productivity gains of the modern world require a vast division of labor and impersonal exchange across time and space. This transition necessitates institutional structures that reduce the uncertainties of transacting with strangers.

A central dilemma in this framework is the role of the state. Because the state has a comparative advantage in coercion, it is often tempted to use violence to extract surplus. For growth to occur, the government must not only establish property rights but also make a “credible commitment” to uphold them, ensuring that the sovereign will not arbitrarily revise rules for their own benefit. North and Weingast (1989) famously illustrate this through the Glorious Revolution of 1688 in England. They argue that the institutional redesign—creating a balance of power between the Crown and Parliament and an independent judiciary—allowed the government to commit credibly to private rights, which in turn fostered capital market development and long-run success.

### Colonial Origins and the Empirical Case for Institutions

The most influential empirical support for the institutional hypothesis comes from the work of Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (AJR). They argue that the large differences in income per capita today are rooted in the different types of institutions established during European colonization.

AJR’s theory rests on three premises:

- Colonization Strategy: Europeans adopted different strategies depending on the environment. In “Neo-Europes” like the United States and Australia, they settled and established institutions of private property. In other areas, they set up “extractive states” designed to transfer resources to the metropole, with little protection for property rights or checks against government power.

- The Feasibility of Settlement: The choice of strategy was influenced by the disease environment. In places where European settler mortality was high (due to diseases like malaria and yellow fever), Europeans were unlikely to settle and thus more likely to establish extractive institutions.

- Institutional Persistence: These early institutions persisted even after independence.

Using settler mortality as an exogenous instrument for current institutions, AJR estimate a large effect of institutions on GDP per capita. Remarkably, they find that once the effect of institutions is controlled for, geographic factors like distance from the equator or being in Africa are no longer statistically significant determinants of poverty.

This argument is further bolstered by the “Reversal of Fortune” thesis. AJR document that among former colonies, those that were relatively prosperous in 1500 (such as the Aztec or Inca empires) are now relatively poor, while those that were sparsely populated and less developed (like North America) are now the richest. They attribute this to an “institutional reversal”: Europeans were more likely to introduce extractive institutions in already prosperous, densely populated areas where they could take over existing tax systems or use forced labor. Conversely, in sparsely settled areas, they were induced to create institutions encouraging investment to attract settlers. This reversal accelerated during the 19th century as societies with good institutions were better able to take advantage of the opportunity to industrialize.

### Factor Endowments and Inequality

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000) provide a complementary view, focusing on how initial factor endowments—climate, soil quality, and native population density—shaped the long-run path of institutional development in the Americas.

They argue that environments suited for sugar and slave plantations (like the Caribbean) or those rich in minerals and native labor (like Mexico and Peru) led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This initial inequality allowed small elites to establish legal frameworks that protected their own interests at the expense of the broad population. In contrast, the northern United States and Canada, with climates suited for small-scale grain farming, began with greater equality and institutionalized broader access to economic opportunities.

### Mechanisms of Institutional Persistence

A key question in the literature is why dysfunctional, extractive institutions persist for centuries. Proponents suggest several economic mechanisms:

- Sunk Costs: Setting up restricted, extractive institutions is costly, and once in place, elites find it more profitable to exploit existing structures than to pay for reform.

- Elite Incentives: When a ruling elite is small, each member captures a larger share of the rents from extraction.

- Complementary Investments: Agents make irreversible investments (in physical or human capital) that are tailored to existing institutions, making them resistant to change.

- Political Conflict: Institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may fear losing their power or rents if institutions were improved to benefit the majority.

### Critical Perspectives and Nuance

Despite its popularity, the institutional hypothesis has faced significant critiques.

The "Efficiency" vs. "Conflict" Debate: Ogilvie (2007) argues that economic history is too often dominated by the belief that institutions arise because they are economically efficient solutions. She contends that many institutions—such as serfdom or craft guilds—existed not to maximize aggregate welfare but to resolve distributional conflicts in favor of powerful groups. In this view, "bad" institutions persist not just through inertia but because they successfully redistribute resources to those with coercive power.

Historical Nuance and the "Compression of History": Austin (2008) critiqued AJR for "compressing" history, arguing that their binary classification of settler vs. non-settler colonies ignores the diversity within continents. In Africa, for instance, even non-settler colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous enterprise (like cocoa in Ghana) to capitalize on comparative advantage, rather than relying solely on extraction. Austin also notes that economic rent and growth are not always opposites; coercion and growth often went hand-in-hand in pre-colonial and colonial history.

Alternative Explanations: Ideas and Culture: Other scholars emphasize that institutions themselves may be secondary to useful knowledge and ideas. Mokyr (2005) suggests that the rapid growth of the West was driven primarily by the Enlightenment and the systematic deployment of scientific knowledge in production. Similarly, McCloskey (2015) argues that "sweet talk"—ideological changes and the "commitment to open access"—was the true driver of the shift in the North Atlantic, rather than a mere material calculation of institutional costs and benefits.

### Extensions of the Hypothesis

Recent work has expanded the scope of institutional analysis:

- Economic "Shrinking": Broadberry and Gardner (2022) suggest that African underperformance is not just about a lack of growth, but a high frequency of "shrinking". They argue that "open access" societies are more resilient to shocks because more individuals have agency to protect their interests.

- Social Capital and Trust: Nunn and Wantchekon (2011) link current levels of low trust in Africa to the historical shock of the slave trade, which encouraged individuals to turn against one another, creating a persistent culture of mistrust that weakens modern institutions.

### Conclusion

The literature establishes that institutions are a primary determinant of long-run economic growth by providing the framework for property rights, contract enforcement, and investment incentives. While the AJR framework provides a powerful empirical model linking colonial history to modern prosperity, critics like Ogilvie and Austin remind us that institutions are often the product of political struggle and distributional conflict. Ultimately, the study of institutions remains a central, albeit complex, field for understanding why some nations succeed while others fail.
