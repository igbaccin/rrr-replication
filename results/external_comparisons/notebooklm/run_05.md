The thesis that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth represents a paradigm shift in economic history, moving the focus away from traditional factors like geography, climate, and simple resource accumulation to the humanly-devised constraints that structure social, political, and economic interaction [140, p. 998; 289, p. 1319; 340, p. 650]. This review examines the core arguments of the institutionalist perspective, the empirical evidence provided by the colonial natural experiment, and the critical debates regarding institutional efficiency versus distributional conflict.

### The Theoretical Framework: Rules of the Game

Institutions are defined as the "rules of the game" in a society, encompassing formal constraints like constitutions and laws, as well as informal norms and their enforcement characteristics [206, p. 161; 298, p. 1321]. The central claim of the "institutions hypothesis" is that societies providing incentives and opportunities for investment through secure property rights and checks on government power will prosper, while those that do not will stagnate [78, p. 1235; 87, p. 1262].

A critical component of successful institutions is credible commitment [261, p. 804]. For growth to occur, a government must not only establish property rights but also credibly commit to honoring them so that the sovereign cannot arbitrarily expropriate the wealth of its citizens [261, p. 804]. North and Weingast (1989) illustrate this through the Glorious Revolution of 1688 in England, which redesigned fiscal and governmental institutions to limit the Crown’s power [260, p. 803]. By creating a balance of power between the Crown and Parliament and ensuring an independent judiciary, England established institutions that lowered transaction costs and allowed for the development of modern capital markets [262, p. 805; 312, p. 1325]. In contrast, absolutist states like early modern Spain, which lacked such constraints, suffered from economic retardation [273, p. 808].

### The Colonial Natural Experiment and Reversal of Fortune

The most prominent empirical support for the primacy of institutions comes from the study of European colonization [8, p. 1369]. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) argue that Europeans adopted different colonization policies based on the feasibility of settlement [11, p. 1370]. In places with high settler mortality due to diseases like malaria and yellow fever, Europeans could not settle and instead established extractive institutions designed to transfer resources to the metropole [8, p. 1369; 12, p. 1370]. In healthy environments, they settled in large numbers and created "Neo-Europes" (e.g., the United States, Canada, Australia), replicating European institutions that protected property rights and checked government power [11, p. 1370; 64, p. 1395].

These early institutional choices have persisted to the present, explaining a large portion of the current world income distribution [12, p. 1370; 24, p. 1376]. For instance, it is estimated that improving Nigeria’s institutions to the level of Chile’s could lead to a seven-fold increase in Nigeria’s income in the long run [15, p. 1371]. Furthermore, these findings suggest that once the effect of institutions is controlled for, geographic factors like distance from the equator do not have a significant independent effect on economic performance [8, p. 1369; 45, p. 1386].

A corollary to this theory is the "Reversal of Fortune": among former colonies, those that were relatively rich in 1500 are now relatively poor [76, p. 1231]. Institutionalists argue this reflects an "institutional reversal" [76, p. 1231]. In prosperous and densely populated areas (e.g., the Aztec or Inca empires), it was more profitable for colonizers to introduce extractive institutions to force the local population to work in mines and plantations [79, p. 1235; 93, p. 1264]. In contrast, sparsely populated and poorer areas induced the creation of institutions of private property to encourage settlement and commerce [79, p. 1235; 118, p. 1278]. This reversal became most pronounced during the Industrial Revolution, as societies with good institutions were better positioned to take advantage of new technologies that required broad-based economic participation [81, p. 1236; 107, p. 1273].

### Factor Endowments and Inequality

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000) provide a nuanced view by linking institutions to initial factor endowments [399, p. 218]. They argue that environments suited for sugar production (e.g., the Caribbean) led to large-scale slave plantations and extreme inequality [401, p. 220]. This inequality allowed elites to establish legal frameworks that concentrated political power and reproduced high inequality over time, hindering long-run growth [402, p. 221]. Conversely, the grain-based economies of North America, characterized by small-scale farming and more equal distributions of wealth, developed institutions that were more conducive to broad-based economic growth [401, p. 220].

### Critiques: The Efficiency vs. Conflict View

A major debate within institutional history centers on why institutions arise and persist. The "efficiency view," pioneered by early institutionalists, suggests that institutions emerge as beneficial solutions to address economic needs and reduce transaction costs [343, p. 652]. However, Ogilvie (2007) critiques this "whatever is, is right" perspective, arguing that institutions often survive despite being inefficient for the aggregate economy [339, p. 649; 346, p. 655].

Instead, Ogilvie proposes a "conflict view", where institutions are the outcome of struggles over the distribution of resources [358, p. 661]. In this framework, "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive and growth-retarding because they increase the rents captured by the groups holding political power [78, p. 1235; 91, p. 1263]. For example, institutions like serfdom or craft guilds persisted for centuries not because they were efficient for the whole society, but because they distributed a large share of a limited "economic pie" to powerful elites while excluding others [339, p. 649; 361, p. 664].

### Alternative Drivers: Ideas, Culture, and History

Some scholars argue that the institutionalist focus overlooks the role of ideas and culture. McCloskey (2015) contends that ideas and ideologies, rather than interests or institutions, were the primary drivers of the rise of northwestern Europe [246, p. 65]. From this perspective, the shift toward a "business-respecting civilization" was a cultural and intellectual change that preceded and enabled institutional reform [246, p. 65].

Furthermore, historical critics warn against the "compression of history" [131, p. 996]. Austin (2008) argues that institutionalist models often ignore the agency of indigenous populations and the complexities of the colonial era, such as the fact that colonial rule was often negotiated rather than simply imposed [132, p. 996; 226, p. 170]. For instance, in West Africa, indigenous property rights systems were often supportive of growth even under colonial rule, and the decline of slavery was driven by domestic economic demand rather than just European policy [146, p. 1002; 148, p. 1004].

Nunn (2008) also adds a layer of deep history by showing that the Atlantic slave trade had a fundamental effect on the evolution of institutions in Africa, leading to ethnic fragmentation and the weakening of pre-colonial state structures, which continue to hinder post-colonial growth [145, p. 1001; 335, p. 166].

### Institutions and Modern Performance

Modern research also examines how institutions affect long-run performance beyond just incentives for growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022) suggest that institutions are critical for resilience to economic shrinking [184, p. 6]. "Open access" societies, where access to economic rents is broad-based, allow for better conflict resolution and more effective delivery of public goods, making economies more resilient to negative shocks [184, p. 6].

In mature economies, Abramovitz (1986) warns that past institutional commitments can become obstacles, leading to "institutional sclerosis" or arthritis that limits growth as countries reach the technological frontier [2, p. 402; 6, p. 405]. This suggests that "social capability"—the ability to adjust institutions to new technological opportunities—is as important as the institutions themselves [2, p. 402; 419, p. 297].

### Conclusion

The sources confirm that institutions are a fundamental determinant of long-run economic growth, primarily by providing the security of property rights and the credible commitment necessary for investment [8, p. 1369; 261, p. 804]. While the colonial natural experiment provides robust evidence for this link, the literature also emphasizes that institutions are often the product of distributional conflict rather than pure efficiency [358, p. 661]. Understanding long-run growth thus requires a multivariate approach that recognizes the interplay between formal rules, deep historical shocks, and the evolving social capabilities of a society [365, p. 666; 419, p. 297].
