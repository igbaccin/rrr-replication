The mechanisms through which institutions systematically shape long-term economic growth trajectories hinge on how they structure incentives, commit individuals to enduring commitments, and embed themselves in systemic frameworks that resist backsliding. The intellectual stakes lie in disentangling the conditions under which institutional design, not merely exogenous shocks or cultural predispositions, creates self-sustaining pathways of cumulative advantage, while the live disagreement centers on whether these effects emerge primarily through the enforcement of secure property rights, the institutionalization of distributional stability, or the contingent interaction of both with broader structural legacies. The tension arises when scholars either emphasize the foundational role of institutional architecture in public choice systems or when others highlight how historical path dependence and cultural determinants shape the very frameworks that institutions rely upon.

Shared finding (Austin 2008, p.24; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) underscores that institutions are neither purely deterministic nor exogenous forces; their influence depends on how they are embedded in regional contexts where differential historical interactions shape their efficacy. Austin (2008, p.24) demonstrates that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, while capturing a broad trend, oversimplifies by ignoring how divergent institutional legacies, such as those in Africa, create path-dependent constraints and opportunities that persist across generations. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) extend this by showing how colonialism did not merely redistribute resources but systematically inverted institutional outcomes, favoring extractive structures in once-flourishing regions while fostering property rights in newly settled areas, thereby locking in divergent growth trajectories through institutional reversal. These findings reveal that the long-term impact of institutions depends on their ability to endure and adapt to evolving conditions, not just their initial design.

The next section turns to how institutions emerge from and reinforce broader frameworks of social and economic organization.

The constitutional stability and enforceable property rights embedded within political systems, as demonstrated by North and Weingast (1989, p.28; 1989, p.2), serve as the foundational mechanisms by which institutions sustain long-term economic commitment. Institutions like representative government and common law courts do not merely codify rules but also institutionalize mechanisms that prevent opportunistic extraction, thereby fostering environments where private investment and long-term planning thrive. North’s (1989, p.9) analysis (North 1989, p.9) reveals how the English financial revolution, driven by the Bank of England and declining transaction costs, reflected deeper institutional security in property rights, illustrating how such stability reduces uncertainty and incentivizes capital formation. The Bank of England’s establishment, for instance, symbolized a shift toward a governance framework where temporal security of property rights became a critical enabler, reinforcing the idea that institutions must not only be stable but also adapt to new challenges, such as fiscal crises, to sustain their growth-promoting effects. This adaptation, as North notes (North 1989, p.6), remains an open question, yet the historical record suggests that institutional resilience, where property rights and constitutional frameworks endure despite pressures, directly underpins sustained economic momentum. The implication is that institutional endurance is less about rigid permanence than the capacity to evolve in ways that protect both present and future economic interests, ensuring that growth trajectories remain anchored in durable, adaptive structures.

Austin’s analysis highlights how regionally contingent path-dependent institutional transformations and entrepreneurial agency collectively determine long-term economic trajectories, transcending static institutional effects (Austin 2008, p.24; Austin 2007, p.2). The evidence from Ghana’s 19th-century cocoa boom demonstrates how a shift from land scarcity to labor abundance triggered deep institutional adaptations, such as the emergence of land-use markets and hired labor, through which African entrepreneurs navigated structural changes to redirect resource flows toward sustained economic transformation. These mechanisms illustrate how entrepreneurial agency, acting within preexisting institutional frameworks, refracted external shocks into opportunities for institutional differentiation, thereby embedding regional growth dynamics in path-dependent trajectories.

To refine this dynamic, the institutional consequences of cocoa’s economic pressure were not merely a result of market forces but of how chiefs and local elites responded to rising land scarcity by commodifying land rights and labor, creating new incentives for voluntary migration and wage labor (Austin 2007, p.23). The paper underscores that this institutional shift was not an isolated event but a response to broader regional labor mobility patterns, where labor scarcity in the savannah drove demand for forest laborers, thereby eroding traditional hierarchies of slavery and debt bondage. The downstream implication is that such transformations often exposed preexisting inequalities, as chiefs retained control over land leasing while indigenous subjects gained access to labor-intensive land, illustrating how institutional differentiation could both exacerbate and redistribute economic benefits in ways that sustained long-term growth momentum. This case highlights how entrepreneurial agency, constrained by existing institutions, could reconfigure resource allocation to create new growth pathways.

The interplay between path dependence and entrepreneurial agency also suggests that economic transformations are not linear but emerge from iterative adaptations to external pressures, where institutions act as both constraints and accelerators for growth. The African experience reaffirms that institutions are not inert but evolve in response to structural shifts, allowing for the emergence of novel institutional configurations that shape future trajectories. This suggests that long-term growth is less about initial institutional conditions than the capacity to institutionalize adaptive mechanisms that sustain cumulative advantage over time.

The 15th-century erosion of skill premiums and wage-price divergence in Europe’s institutional frameworks illustrates how persistent labor-market and economic governance constraints shaped divergent long-term growth outcomes. Van Zanden’s evidence demonstrates that formal institutions, like guilds, initially fostered intergenerational skill transfer but later exacerbated skill bottlenecks through restrictive entry policies, which (van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutional constraints created an environment where even skilled labor faced rigid wage structures, undermining the broader accumulation of human capital. Allen’s data further reveals that by midcentury, wage-price divergence had already locked in divergent income dynamics, where leading economies sustained stable wages while peripheral regions experienced prolonged stagnation, showing how uneven institutional enforcement of contracts perpetuated structural inequality. The critical mechanism here lies in the tension between institutional guarantees of apprenticeship and cartel-like restrictions, which (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Allen 2001, p.1) highlight as defining the early modern period’s economic fault lines. These conditions not only constrained productivity growth during the Great Divergence but also laid the groundwork for the divergent wage structures that would later feed into divergent economic outcomes. Evidence from this stream underscores that long-term growth is less about the initial stability of institutions than the capacity to navigate and adapt institutional rigidities over time.

Eurasian pre-modern societies’ structural inequality embedded in elite-dominated legal and economic frameworks constrained institutional adaptability, ensuring reforms remained narrowly controlled and growth-enabling. Bryant (2006, p.8) demonstrates that the European pre-industrial order’s institutional rigidity, rooted in patrimonial and mercantile hierarchies, prevented cumulative innovation by excluding non-elite actors from decision-making, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12) reveal how elite exclusivity in the Americas preserved rigid, unequal paths that suppressed broad-based opportunity. These sources jointly highlight that institutional rigidity did not emerge de novo but was fundamentally shaped by pre-existing structural divides, where elite dominance set the terms of institutional design, excluding potential agents of change.

Bryant’s critique of the “late and lucky” narrative underscores how institutional structures in Eurasia were not merely passive reflections of broader economic conditions but were actively shaped by elite interests, particularly through legal and commercial charters that reinforced elite privileges. The paper’s emphasis on the “convenient abundance of English coal” and colonial resources as mere fortuitous outcomes obscures the deeper mechanism: institutional designs were deliberately structured to favor elites, ensuring that external advantages were contingent on maintaining elite control over institutional evolution. Sokoloff and Engerman’s focus on New World societies further qualifies this by showing that even where external factors like colonial plunder were present, the absence of inclusive institutions meant that such resources were not translated into sustained growth but instead reinforced elite dominance. Together, these sources suggest that institutional adaptability depended not on external shocks but on the capacity of elites to institutionalize mechanisms that excluded broader participation, creating a feedback loop where inequality reinforced institutional rigidity.

Downstream of this, the exclusionary institutional frameworks created a feedback loop where elite control over land, credit, and governance prevented the emergence of entrepreneurial networks capable of driving long-term growth. Bryant notes that even in regions with comparable economic conditions, institutional structures like the Mughal Sultanate’s patrimonial order restricted capitalistic development, while Sokoloff and Engerman’s evidence from the Americas shows that persistent inequality in access to economic opportunities preserved rigid, elite-dominated institutions. The implication is that long-term growth trajectories were not determined by initial institutional stability but by the capacity to disrupt entrenched hierarchies, a disruption that required both institutional and structural conditions to enable broad-based participation. This tension between elite dominance and institutional adaptability thus becomes the defining challenge in understanding how societies transitioned, or failed to transition, into modernizing trajectories.

European pre-industrial institutions arose from persistent distributional conflicts, shaping everything from labor systems to credit frameworks rather than abstract economic efficiency. Distributional tensions, rather than neutral efficiency concerns, anchored structures like serfdom and guilds, which Ogilvie and Pamuk demonstrate were neither random nor exogenous but deeply embedded in the political and social interdependencies of local economies. Ogilvie’s framework highlights how the broader institutional framework, including kin-based welfare systems, credit markets, and labor markets, functioned as a cohesive whole, where each component reinforced rather than isolated the others, ensuring that rigid hierarchies persisted even as economic conditions evolved (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These systems did not function as isolated institutions but as interlocking networks that reinforced unequal access to resources and opportunities, embedding long-term disadvantages in specific regions.

Pamuk’s analysis complements this by tracing how exogenous shocks, such as the Black Death, accelerated the divergence of economic trajectories by preserving wage structures in regions like England and the Low Countries, where flexible institutional adaptation allowed for capital-intensive industries to thrive. The Black Death acted as a catalyst for institutional "creative destruction, " reshaping labor markets and economic incentives in ways that favored regions with pre-existing institutional resilience, those that could adapt without dismantling entrenched hierarchies (Pamuk 2007, p.2). This resilience was not merely a byproduct of efficiency but a deliberate outcome of institutions that balanced distributional stability with structural flexibility, ensuring that early modern transformations did not erode existing power structures but instead fortified them in new forms. The downstream implication of this interplay is that long-term economic divergence was not an inevitable outcome of demographic shocks but a product of how societies managed the tension between stability and adaptation, with distributional conflicts acting as the primary driver of institutional design.

These findings reinforce that pre-industrial institutions were not neutral frameworks but politically constructed systems that reflected and reinforced existing power dynamics. The shared finding that institutions like guilds and communes were shaped by cultural values, such as Putnam’s "social capital", further underscores how non-economic norms influenced economic outcomes. Putnam’s theory shows that societies with strong civic cultures, characterized by trust and reciprocity, developed institutions that fostered both economic growth and political accountability, while regions lacking these cultural foundations struggled to develop comparable frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that the capacity for institutions to evolve was not a function of external efficiency but of the ability to internalize and institutionalize collective norms that balanced distributional equity with structural stability.

Colonial extractive institutions did not merely disrupt pre-existing structures but *systematically reversed* institutional trajectories in high-productivity regions by embedding long-term property-rights and governance constraints in former indigenous settlements. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrates that extractive institutions, rooted in direct exploitation networks rather than inclusive frameworks, were deliberately cultivated in the Global South’s most prosperous areas, while European settlers in sparsely settled regions inadvertently adopted inclusive, property-rights-enhancing institutions. This institutional reversal, driven by settler mortality risks and malaria prevalence, created a feedback loop where extractive institutions persisted despite later reforms, particularly in historically dense, resource-rich regions. Their institutional legacy, however, was not absolute: in regions like the Americas and Australasia, settler-driven institutional reforms later mitigated these effects, though not fully reversing the foundational constraints imposed by colonial rule.

The mechanisms by which extractive institutions stifled growth were partly structural, relying on expropriation risks and weak property rights enforcement to suppress entrepreneurial activity. These mortality-driven institutional choices did not occur randomly but reflected *equilibrium outcomes* tied to broader colonial logics, where resource extraction took precedence over local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.14). This structural tension meant that even as European settlers established legal frameworks, the underlying incentive structures, driven by extractive governance, persisted, creating a lasting institutional bias toward inequality. The implication was that colonial-era institutional design was not merely a one-time disruption but an *embedded constraint* on long-term economic dynamism, particularly in regions where extractive institutions persisted despite later reforms. This legacy continues to shape modern income disparities, as extractive institutions in formerly wealthy colonies remain entrenched, while inclusive institutions in former sparsely settled areas fostered sustained economic growth.

African institutions reflect not just colonial imposition but the enduring *institutional gaps* between precolonial state-building and postcolonial governance failures that persist in contemporary governance structures. Hopkins (2019, p.11) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) jointly demonstrate that these gaps, rooted in systemic undervaluing of precolonial economic and social systems, create structural constraints on inclusive growth. The paper highlights how African history’s fragmentation into rigid, extractive governance frameworks, rather than ethnic fragmentation alone, underpins contemporary governance failures, particularly in how state-citizen relations remain embedded in colonial-era extractive logics. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further notes that the new institutional economics’ neglect of cliometric innovations, such as the 1960s cliemetric revolution, has obscured how precolonial economic structures, once dismissed as irrelevant, still inform contemporary capitalism.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) expand this analysis by tracing how colonial policies *systematically suppressed women’s economic agency* through patriarchal alliances with chiefs, codifying exclusionary norms across Africa. Their evidence shows that precolonial trade, agriculture, and leadership roles were erased under colonialism, leaving institutional gaps where women’s contributions, critical to early capitalism, were marginalized. The authors’ layered historical approach reveals that these exclusions created a feedback loop: codified patriarchy reduced women’s economic mobility, reinforcing extractive governance norms that continue to stifle inclusive growth. The implication is that contemporary governance failures in Africa are not merely the result of colonial legacies but the *permanent institutionalization* of those legacies, where extractive structures persist even as formal institutions change. This double mechanism, extractive governance and gender exclusion, undermines the potential for inclusive economic development, as both reinforce a structural bias toward inequality.

The downstream implication is that African economic trajectories are constrained by an *institutional mismatch* between precolonial production systems and postcolonial governance frameworks, where the latter’s extractive foundations persist despite formal reforms.

Ideological frameworks underpinning scientific and industrial innovation distinguish themselves from prior structural institutionalism, actively shaping long-term growth through their own mechanisms. Mokyr’s work highlights how Enlightenment-era optimism in human reason and Baconian empiricism did not merely enable technological diffusion but created the institutional conditions for sustained *coordination failures* to be addressed, ensuring innovations persisted beyond their initial phase (Mokyr 2005, p.41; Mokyr 2005, p.54). The paper underscores that the "enlightened" policies of the Industrial Enlightenment, such as state-backed scientific experimentation and market-friendly institutional reforms, outperformed institutional inertia by fostering a feedback loop between knowledge production and economic expansion. This mechanism contrasts with Kuznets’s emphasis on institutions as mere enablers of technological deployment, arguing instead that ideologies shaped the *priorities* and *persistence* of those institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.4). The shared finding that technological progress alone is insufficient to sustain growth, without complementary institutional and ideological support, reveals how Enlightenment ideologies *actively structured* the conditions for industrialization by framing science as a public good rather than a rent-seeking tool.

For example, the state’s role in funding scientific inquiry and the diffusion of industrial techniques was not merely instrumental but *ideologically grounded*, reflecting a belief in human progress through systematic inquiry. This institutional-ideological alignment ensured that technological innovations were not merely adopted but *embedded* in economic and social structures, sustaining momentum over generations. The lesson for long-term growth is that ideologies do not merely accompany institutions but *define their durability and adaptability*.

Endogenous barriers in catch-up economies, such as trade rivalry and shifting social priorities, actively reshape institutional priorities, creating self-reinforcing constraints that dampen long-term growth momentum. Abramovitz’s work highlights how the *objects of expenditure* and *structural incentives* differ across nations, creating unintended institutional lock-in that disrupts the convergence of productivity hierarchies. These dynamics are not merely exogenous shocks but *endogenous wild cards*, institutions adapt imperfectly to technological pressures, generating persistent divergence even as economies struggle to realize their growth potential. Nunn’s evidence reinforces this by illustrating how the *domestic violence and slave production* of precolonial Africa, though geographically distant from colonial trade hubs, left a legacy of weakened institutional resilience that later constrained wage growth. The implication: institutional underdevelopment in catch-up settings is not a single cause but a *multi-stage process* where early trade rivalries and elite-driven social hierarchies embed barriers that persist beyond formal institutional reforms.

Frankema and van Waijenburg’s findings demonstrate how *income inequality and colonial settler dominance* distorted long-term wage dynamics in British colonies, undermining the structural conditions for sustained growth. Their Kenyan case shows that even where colonialism initially imposed extractive institutions, *substantial wage improvements* among non-elite groups, evident in post-1920s trajectories, did not translate into broader economic mobility due to entrenched inequality. This suggests that while colonialism’s extractive legacy remains a critical factor, *institutional underdevelopment in catch-up economies* often stems from *internal social divisions* that prevent inclusive economic reforms from taking root. The shared insight from Abramovitz and Frankema and van Waijenburg is that catch-up growth depends not just on technological adoption but on *how institutions internalize trade rivalries and elite priorities* as structural constraints, shaping trajectories where growth stagnation becomes self-perpetuating. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) reports, “The objects of expenditure, the structures of transfers and taxes, and people's responses to both differ from country to country.” Nunn (2008, p.3) reports, “I ﬁnd that the data are consistent with these channels.”

The primary mechanism through which institutions systematically influence long-term economic growth trajectories is their role in embedding *secure property rights* as a foundational condition for sustained private investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate how the institutional commitment to enforce these rights, through mechanisms such as veto points in governance, creates stability that enables long-term commitments to capital accumulation, a core driver of sustained economic growth. Their analysis underscores that this stability is not merely about formal rules but the *political will* to uphold them over generations, as evidenced by the success of Britain’s constitutional evolution after the Hundred Years’ War. Such institutional stability also fosters a *decentralized institutional framework*, where transaction costs decline and markets expand, as North (1989, p.9) argues, citing the English financial revolution as a case where falling costs directly accelerated growth.

Beyond formal rights, the structural conditions of *institutional interdependencies* further shape divergent growth paths. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights how pre-industrial European institutions, from guilds to welfare systems, were intricately linked, with one institution’s collapse or reform often triggering cascading effects. Shared findings (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) suggest that institutions like guilds or communal welfare systems did not operate in isolation; their efficiency depended on broader cultural values, such as trust and reciprocity, which Putnam’s framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) later formalized. These interdependencies mean that stagnation in one sector, such as labor markets or credit systems, can systematically hinder growth in others, reinforcing self-perpetuating inefficiencies. For example, the Black Death’s uneven institutional adaptation in northwest Europe preserved wages in regions like England, creating lasting structural advantages (Pamuk 2007, p.23), while southern Europe’s rigid institutions failed to adapt, locking in stagnation.

A second mechanism is the *institutional reversal* imposed by colonialism, where extractive institutions replaced pre-existing prosperity-enabling ones. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.34) show that European colonization systematically undermined institutions in wealthier regions, such as the Americas, while fostering favorable ones in poorer areas, thereby reversing historical income disparities. This structural shift, where institutions were not merely “better” but *systematically redefined*, explain modern income gaps, as their regression analysis (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) confirms. Their findings align with Austin’s (2008, p.24) critique (Austin 2008, p.4), which reframes the “reversal of fortune” thesis as a product of these path-dependent institutional changes rather than a simple reversal of opportunity.

The final stream emerges from the tension between *institutional continuity* and *creative destruction*. Pamuk (2007, p.2) argues that the Black Death triggered institutional innovation, preserving wages and reshaping capital-intensive industries in northwest Europe, which later drove long-term divergence. While North and Weingast (1989, p.28) emphasize the *political commitment* needed to sustain such reforms, Ogilvie’s work (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) suggests that institutional change also depends on broader cultural and social capital, where values like trust or civic norms directly shape institutional design. The empirical resolution to this debate lies in the cross-country comparisons of Acemoglu et al. which demonstrate that institutional reversal, whether through colonialism or endogenous shocks, determines whether growth trajectories become self-reinforcing or stagnant, with measurement of institutional quality (e.g. property rights, extractive governance) as the defining variable.
