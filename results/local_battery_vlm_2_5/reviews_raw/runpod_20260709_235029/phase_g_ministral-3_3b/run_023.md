The core question driving scholarly inquiry into long-term economic growth is how institutions, defined broadly as the rules, norms, and structures governing human interaction, systematically shape trajectories of prosperity over generations. Institutions do not merely reflect economic conditions; they actively construct them through mechanisms of enforcement, incentives, and path dependency, altering the very conditions that sustain them. This intellectual tension centers on identifying which institutional features persist, adapt, or collapse to determine whether growth becomes self-sustaining or trapped in decline. The most contested claim in this debate is that growth outcomes hinge less on exogenous factors like technology or geography than on the *endogenous evolution* of institutional systems, which institutions themselves create, reinforce, or break. Intellectual stakes lie in separating enduring causal mechanisms from contingent historical accidents; for example, whether divergence in growth paths is best explained by institutions that secure property rights, institutions that govern distribution, or institutions that emerge as byproducts of cultural or demographic shocks.

The most direct evidence for institutions as primary drivers comes from the *reversal of fortune* thesis advanced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), which posits that colonialism did not merely transfer resources but *rewrote institutional frameworks* to favor extraction over investment, thereby altering long-term development paths. Shared findings across these studies reveal that institutions that constrain elites’ opportunism, such as those enforcing secure property rights, often precede, rather than follow, growth. Yet this claim is not without critique, as Austin (2008, p.24) highlights how the thesis’s cross-sectional regressions obscure regional interactions where institutions operate through *interdependent networks* of laws, norms, and social practices. The tension arises over whether institutions act as isolated variables or as part of a cohesive, adaptive framework where one change triggers cascading institutional reforms. This debate underpins the next stream’s focus on how historical shocks, like pandemics or wars, temporarily disrupt but later reshape institutional trajectories to perpetuate divergence.

The enduring institutional legacy of colonialism, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue, is not merely a matter of delayed reforms but a foundational erosion of the structures that once enabled endogenous growth in resource-rich regions. These scholars demonstrate that European colonialism systematically prioritized extractive institutions, such as property rights enforcement tied to resource extraction or tax collection, over those that fostered inclusive economic participation, embedding a persistent divergence in institutional quality that outlasts direct extractive pressures. The paper’s data reveal that regions previously endowed with wealth and diverse economic activities were more likely to adopt oppressive governance under colonial rule, while poorer, sparsely settled areas received institutional setups that, though initially exploitative, later evolved into more stable private property frameworks in places like North America and Australia.

The mechanism driving this divergence lies in the interplay between European mortality risks and settlement patterns, which shaped institutional design in ways that reinforced inequality. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that settler mortality influenced where Europeans established colonies, favoring regions with lower malaria risks (e.g. high-altitude areas like Bogotá) where institutions could be more extractive but also more resilient. Meanwhile, their 2002 work highlights that extractive institutions, such as those enforcing expropriation risks, diminished long-term productivity by undermining trust and innovation, even as later reforms in some colonies failed to fully rectify these foundational disruptions. This suggests that colonial institutions did not merely impose temporary shocks but rewired local institutional ecosystems to prioritize short-term extraction over sustainable growth trajectories.

The implication here is that institutional design under colonialism did not simply reflect power dynamics but actively suppressed the very conditions that enabled cumulative economic progress, such as inclusive property rights and robust legal frameworks. The study’s findings underscore that the persistence of extractive institutions, whether through direct expropriation risks or indirect barriers to investment, continues to constrain modern economic performance in former colonies, illustrating how historical institutional design becomes a self-reinforcing barrier to long-term growth. Hopkins’s critique of the new historiography of Africa and Nunn’s empirical tracing of the slave trade reveal how entrenched social and economic institutions in postcolonial Africa are fundamentally shaped by the erosion of precolonial cohesion and the institutional fragmentation induced by colonial extraction. Hopkins (2009, p.22) notes that the study of history in this context should prioritize understanding how evolving social institutions, particularly those governing group cohesion, economic interests, and religious affiliations, systematically reinforce governance failures by undermining trust and collective action. Nunn and Wantchekon’s (2011, p.4) work builds on this by demonstrating that the slave trade did not merely disrupt institutions externally but embedded enduring cultural norms of mistrust, where mistrust evolved as a "rule-of-thumb" response to insecurity, perpetuating a feedback loop between historical trauma and contemporary underdevelopment. Together, these sources establish that the institutional collapse of precolonial societies and the institutional patchwork of colonial rule created a structural environment where extractive governance became the dominant norm, limiting the capacity for inclusive economic policies.

The institutional legacies Hopkins (2009, p.6) critiques, such as the overreliance on econometric techniques that obscure historical context, highlight how postcolonial governance often operates in a vacuum of historical accountability, where policies lack the anchoring of historical precedent that could guide reforms. Nunn’s (2008, p.25) data further reinforce that economic underdevelopment was not merely a product of colonialism but a direct consequence of the slave trade’s role in fostering long-term economic fragmentation, where proximity to slave markets stunted growth by discouraging investment in alternative economic structures. The implication here is that postcolonial Africa’s governance challenges extend beyond formal legal systems to include deep-seated cultural and social barriers that persist even in the absence of direct colonial rule, creating a self-reinforcing underdevelopment cycle where institutional design remains a persistent constraint on growth. These findings underscore that addressing contemporary economic stagnation requires not only redressing formal institutions but also dismantling the entrenched cultural and economic norms that emerged from the slave trade and colonial exploitation. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) report, “In Section II, we turn to a description of the data, before reporting our estimates in Sections III and IV.”

Institutional stability rooted in enforceable property rights serves as the bedrock for sustained economic expansion by ensuring long-term private investment and mitigating transaction costs, as the political economy literature collectively demonstrates. The North and Weingast (1989, p.28) framework shows that the success of representative government, particularly through multiple veto points and the primacy of common law courts, directly shapes the incentives for private actors to commit resources to growth, while institutional rigidity undermines these conditions. This commitment hinges on sovereign credibility, where stable rules prevent opportunistic exploitation by elites or state capture, creating a self-reinforcing loop between formal institutions and market confidence. Credible commitment underpins growth by aligning private incentives with long-term public goods provision, as North (1989, p.9) illustrates through the English financial revolution, where institutional reforms lowered transaction costs and secured property rights’ temporal dimension. The Bank of England’s creation exemplifies how institutional stability reduced uncertainty, enabling capital accumulation that spurred economic dynamism. The downstream effect of this alignment is a reduced risk of wealth hoarding or misallocation, as private actors perceive stable returns on investments over generations.

Elite-driven institutional inequality in pre-industrial societies emerges as a defining mechanism for long-term economic divergence, where structural conflicts over resource distribution shape institutional design in ways that prioritize elite privilege over collective efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that institutions like serfdom and guilds were not neutral artifacts but outcomes of distributive power struggles, with their persistence tied to elite incentives to maintain exclusionary frameworks that preserved inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12) build on this by highlighting how New World societies exhibited unequal institutional trajectories, where elite dominance, through exclusionary policies and concentrated wealth, preserved path-dependent inequalities that stifled inclusive growth opportunities. Bryant (2006, p.8) further refines this by exposing how Eurasian societies’ modernizing paths were structurally unequal from their origins, where institutions like the Mughal patrimonial order systematically excluded capitalistic development, illustrating how distributional conflicts undercut cumulative institutional innovation.

This stream reveals that institutional inequality does not arise from exogenous cultural preferences or accidental historical events but from deliberate elite strategies to entrench dominance. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates how cultural beliefs, such as the Protestant Reformation’s dismantling of guilds, were not neutral but outcomes of shifting elite interests, where institutional collapse mirrored changes in elite power dynamics. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this by arguing that institutional differences across societies were not merely outcomes of initial conditions but were actively shaped by elite-driven exclusion, ensuring that growth-enabling structures remained inaccessible to the broader population. The downstream implication is that institutions designed to protect elite privileges often create rigidities that impede long-term economic dynamism, as elite interests become codified in laws, customs, and cultural norms that resist structural reform. This suggests that sustained economic growth requires not just efficient institutions but those that are themselves shaped by inclusive, not exclusionary, power structures.

Van Zanden and Allen’s findings reveal that the early erosion of skill premiums in Europe, particularly by the 15th century, was not merely an isolated economic fluctuation but a defining institutional feature that later constrained the region’s long-term growth trajectory. Shared evidence (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Allen 2001, p.8) demonstrates that the skill premium, once a robust signal of structural inequality and institutional robustness, collapsed in Western Europe due to institutional failures that prioritized elite protection over inclusive labor markets. The guilds’ restrictive practices, while fostering intergenerational skill transmission, also distorted wage structures by maintaining artificially high skilled labor earnings relative to broader economic dynamics (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dual effect, both the preservation of elite privileges and the undermining of market efficiency, created a feedback loop where institutional rigidity stifled the accumulation of human capital necessary for sustained productivity growth.

Allen’s analysis further clarifies that the divergence in real incomes between early leaders and lagging cities emerged not just in the 19th century but as early as 1500–1750, when stable wages in elite sectors masked widespread stagnation elsewhere (Allen 2001, p.1). The institutional prices Allen cites, though imperfect proxies for actual consumer costs, nonetheless reflect persistent inequality that persisted through centuries of formal economic expansion. The implication is that the collapse of skill premiums did not occur in a vacuum but was systematically reinforced by institutions that codified exclusionary practices, ensuring that economic dynamism remained confined to a privileged class. These findings underscore how early distortions in labor markets and institutional design set the stage for the structural constraints that later limited Europe’s capacity to adopt the more dynamic, inclusive institutions characteristic of other regions.

Endogenous factors embedded in catch-up dynamics reveal how societies actively reshape priorities and institutional capacity over time, beyond the conventional focus on institutional structures in growth theories. The interplay between structural inertia and adaptive responses complicates conventional narratives of institutional determinism, particularly in contexts where rapid convergence and technological opportunity interact (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Abramovitz’s (1986, p.21) analysis of postwar catch-up highlights how countries do not merely adopt advanced technologies passively but actively adjust institutional frameworks in response to evolving social capabilities, demonstrating that institutional changes are not exogenous shocks but dynamic outcomes of endogenous learning. Hopkins’s (2019, p.11) intervention complicates this further by demonstrating that African economic history’s precolonial institutional gaps, once presumed to be insurmountable, were not static but influenced by cumulative, if uneven, social and political processes that enabled subsequent institutional experimentation.

These dynamics suggest that institutions do not merely reflect preexisting economic structures but actively co-construct them through iterative adaptation. Abramovitz’s (1986, p.21) emphasis on "social capability" as a limiting factor on technological adoption implies that institutional design must accommodate not only technical feasibility but also the capacity of societies to internalize new norms and structures (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). The paper further suggests that institutional changes do not occur in a vacuum but are driven by competing pressures, such as trade rivalry, that push countries toward more inclusive or extractive configurations, depending on their prior social fabric. Hopkins’s (2019, p.11) critique of path-dependent determinism reveals that even in contexts with historically entrenched inequalities, institutional experimentation, whether colonial-era reforms or postcolonial adaptations, can introduce variability that disrupts rigid narratives of stagnation. This variability implies that the structural conditions underpinning growth are not static but evolve in response to localized social and economic pressures, where elite-driven institutional constraints can both perpetuate and, under certain conditions, be challenged by grassroots innovation. The downstream implication is that sustained growth trajectories require not only stable property rights but also a capacity for adaptive experimentation, where institutional design is as much a product of endogenous social dynamics as it is a determinant of them.

Pamuk’s work alongside Austin’s critiques highlight that institutional divergence in long-term trajectories arises through cumulative, regionally specific path-dependent processes rather than isolated reversals. These studies refute the assumption of exogenous shocks driving abrupt institutional shifts, instead illustrating how regional histories, including demographic and economic transitions, create structural constraints that amplify or mitigate growth potential. Path dependence here operates as a mechanism whereby initial institutional configurations, reinforced by endogenous pressures, lock in economic behaviors that define long-term divergence. For example, Pamuk (2007, p.2) demonstrates how the Black Death in northwest Europe preserved wage stability in regions like England, enabling capital-intensive industries to thrive while southern Europe’s rigid institutions failed to adapt, thus embedding divergent growth trajectories. Austin (2007, p.2) further refines this by showing that Ghana’s transition from land abundance to scarcity during the cocoa boom triggered institutional innovation, such as labor markets and land-use reforms, where African entrepreneurs adapted to scarcity by institutionalizing voluntary migration and wage labor, which reshaped wealth distribution and economic specialization.

The shared finding that institutions become path-dependent through endogenous adaptation underscores how structural conditions are not static but evolve through localized experiments. Pamuk’s emphasis on the Black Death’s role in preserving wage stability highlights how external shocks can accelerate institutional change when combined with flexible institutional frameworks, while Austin’s focus on Ghana’s labor markets reveals how scarcity-driven institutional reforms can unlock growth paths when combined with entrepreneurial agency. These mechanisms imply that long-term divergence is not merely a product of initial conditions but the cumulative result of how institutions respond to persistent local pressures, where flexibility in adaptation becomes the critical variable. The downstream implication is that sustained growth trajectories require not only resilient institutions but also an institutional capacity to experiment and adjust, particularly in regions where initial advantages or disadvantages create entrenched structural dynamics.

The interaction between ideological alignment and structural equilibria underscores that long-term growth trajectories depend less on rigid institutional design than on systemic support for innovation diffusion. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Temin (2002, p.17) both underscore that the alignment of social norms with technological demands, not just formal institutions, determines how efficiently societies absorb and scale new economic systems. For Kuznets, the compatibility between modern technological requirements and societal values, such as the rejection of slavery or illiteracy, reveals that ideological frameworks act as endogenous constraints on growth paths; (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.4). Temin’s analysis extends this by showing how structural labor misallocations, like surplus agricultural workers, create an artificial demand for industrialization that accelerates growth until equilibrium is restored (Temin 2002, p.17; Temin 2002, p.4). These sources imply that ideological cohesion and structural flexibility create a feedback loop: when societal values and resource allocations align with productivity demands, technological adoption becomes self-sustaining, whereas misalignment leads to persistent inefficiencies that stifle cumulative progress.

Both scholars highlight that ideological alignment fosters institutional flexibility by reducing resistance to institutional experimentation. Kuznets notes that earlier systems, such as those predicated on family enterprises or feudalism, were incompatible with industrialization because their underlying values conflicted with the necessity of large-scale, centralized production (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Temin’s Golden Age growth in post-WWII Europe illustrates how labor surplus economies could rapidly reallocate resources toward industrialization precisely because surplus workers had no competing claims on land or labor markets, allowing wages to stay low and labor supply elastic (Temin 2002, p.17). Together, these findings suggest that when ideological and structural equilibria align, institutions adapt more readily to technological change, whereas misalignment generates rigidities that impede long-term convergence. The downstream implication is that sustained growth trajectories require not only resilient institutions but also societies that can internalize the values and incentives that make innovation scalable.

Institutions shape growth trajectories not as supplementary to technological progress but by actively scaffolding knowledge diffusion and addressing coordination failures to endure. Mokyr’s empirical reframing of the Industrial Enlightenment demonstrates that sustained innovation relied on institutional environments that preserved and amplified human ingenuity, even as Enlightenment philosophers underestimated the role of empirical practice in economic advancement (Mokyr 2005, p.41).

Akyeampong and Fofack’s historical analysis of pre-colonial and colonial African economies complicates this framing by revealing how systemic exclusion of women from institutional structures undermined long-term economic potential (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Their work shows that patriarchal codifications under colonialism, rather than spontaneous institutional adaptation, systematically restricted labor mobility and capital formation, illustrating how structural inequality can erode the very conditions that enable innovation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19). The downstream implication is that institutions must not only protect property rights but also embed mechanisms for inclusive participation, without which even the most disruptive technological shifts risk dissipating through rent-seeking or exclusionary power dynamics. The tension between empirical innovation and institutional adaptation thus hinges on whether societies can internalize inclusive governance as a structural prerequisite for growth, rather than assuming technological progress can thrive autonomously.

The shared understanding among scholars is that long-term economic growth is fundamentally shaped by the institutional frameworks that govern property rights, political participation, and systemic incentives, rather than exogenous shocks or cultural differences alone. Shared findings demonstrate that institutions must be both robust and adaptable to sustain commitment over generations, with property rights enforcement as a foundational mechanism. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) highlights how colonial-era institutional reversals, where extractive structures replaced pre-existing favorable arrangements, directly reversed historical income disparities, proving that institutional design is non-negotiable for enduring growth. North and Weingast’s (1989, p.28) emphasis on *commitment* (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) reveals how stable institutions with multiple veto points ensure that elites cannot exploit short-term gains at the expense of long-term investment, while Austin’s (2008, p.24) critique of the reversal-of-fortune thesis (Austin 2008, p.24) underscores the need to account for path-dependent institutional interactions.

Yet, the debate persists over whether these mechanisms are best understood as *contingent* or *universal*. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that cultural and distributional forces, such as guilds’ structural rules and civic norms, must be included alongside institutional design, showing that even in Europe, non-economic values shaped efficiency. Pamuk’s (2007, p.2) structural analysis of the Black Death’s impact, however, demonstrates that adaptability to exogenous shocks, like flexible institutional frameworks in northwest Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.23), exceeds cultural uniformity. The empirical test of these claims lies in comparing regions with *identical* initial institutions but divergent outcomes, such as England and Spain under the same colonial conditions, where institutional flexibility in England’s common-law courts preserved capital accumulation while Spanish extractive systems stifled it (North, 1989 (North 1989, p.9)). This evidence settles the disagreement: institutions matter, but their effectiveness depends on *interdependencies* between property rights, political commitment, and cultural adaptability.
