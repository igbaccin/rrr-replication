The substantive question at stake in the study of long-term economic growth trajectories through institutions is how enduring structural conditions and institutional mechanisms generate cumulative advantages that shape prosperity beyond short-term fluctuations. The core tension revolves around whether institutional effects on growth are primarily shaped by foundational arrangements, such as secure property rights or constitutional checks, that sustain collective action or by the dynamic interplay of path-dependent historical processes, where earlier institutional choices create self-reinforcing feedback loops. This debate hinges on whether institutions function as static scaffolds or as adaptive systems that evolve in response to exogenous shocks, such as demographic upheavals or colonial interventions, which themselves reshuffle institutional legacies. The live disagreement exists between those emphasizing enduring institutional legacies and those highlighting the adaptive capacity of institutions to absorb and transform exogenous forces.

Shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North 1989, p.9) underscores that institutions must embed security in property rights and political commitments to sustain long-term economic engagement, as North and Weingast’s work demonstrates that constitutional protections emerge from struggles over power that bind rulers to rules. North’s analysis of the Bank of England further reveals how transaction costs, rooted in the stability of property rights over time, directly fuel capital accumulation, a mechanism that transcends neoclassical assumptions about savings alone. These insights collectively argue that institutions must not only preserve but actively incentivize private investment, as their stability becomes the bedrock for sustained economic momentum. The intellectual stakes lie in whether such institutional stability arises organically or is actively engineered through deliberate policy interventions that alter the terms of institutional evolution.

Kuznets’s foundational framework demonstrates that the institutional frameworks embedded in Enlightenment thought directly shape the timing and sustainability of technological deployment by structuring how societies allocate resources toward innovation. The paper’s emphasis on the complementary roles of science, technology, and supportive social attitudes reveals that ideological frameworks do not merely influence growth indirectly but actively define the institutional parameters under which innovations are scaled, such as the replacement of feudal constraints with modern enterprise, as exemplified in Kuznets’s (1973, p.1) contrast between steam and electric power and their incompatibility with slavery or illiteracy. Mokyr (2005, p.41) expands this by highlighting how the "Industrial Enlightenment" emerged as a deliberate policy response to coordination failures, where institutional progress complemented technological breakthroughs, ensuring that rent-seeking did not erode gains. The shared insight from both is that the most durable economic advancements arise when ideological momentum accelerates institutional adaptation, creating a feedback loop where reason and innovation are codified into long-term structural conditions. The paradox Mokyr (2005, p.41) notes, that the West’s success in deploying knowledge was not merely a product of its scientific progress but also its ability to institutionalize it, demonstrates that ideological frameworks do not operate in isolation but must be embedded in policies that sustain their transformative potential. This interplay underscores how the early modern period’s intellectual culture did more than enable innovation; it *embedded* it into the fabric of economic growth, ensuring that later phases could build upon its precedents without reverting to stagnation. The persistence of extractive colonial institutions reshaped long-term economic trajectories by embedding elite dominance into formal and informal governance structures, ensuring that post-colonial reforms failed to dislodge their extractive foundations. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48; 2002, p.34) demonstrate that European settlement prioritized mortality risks over inclusive governance, fostering institutions that prioritized resource extraction over inclusive capital accumulation. Settler mortality, driven by tropical diseases, constrained population density in high-risk zones, which in turn incentivized extractive governance, such as plantation economies and tax systems, that relied on violent enforcement rather than property rights. These institutions, rather than being temporary adaptations, became institutional equilibrium, embedding expropriation risks that persist even after colonial rule ended. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12; 2000, p.2) further refine this mechanism by linking elite dominance to the *path-dependent* development of institutions, showing how persistent inequality preserved extractive structures by privileging elite access to resources and political influence. Their analysis reveals that pre-existing inequalities, rooted in early colonial extraction, determined the institutional trajectories of former colonies, ensuring that later reforms could not dismantle exclusionary systems without dismantling their structural incentives. For example, the survival of plantation economies in Latin America, where European settlers extracted labor through coercion rather than market incentives, became a self-reinforcing cycle of extractive governance, stifling inclusive growth mechanisms that depended on broad property rights and financial stability. These findings suggest that the institutional legacies of colonialism were not merely a one-time disruption but a foundational obstacle to inclusive economic development, embedding long-term constraints in the very architecture of post-colonial states. The downstream implication is that inclusive growth pathways, such as those associated with stable property rights and diversified economies, were systematically undermined by the extractive institutions that colonialism institutionalized, illustrating how structural conditions shaped by colonialism created enduring barriers to sustained prosperity.

Austin’s work reframes the long-term divergence in Eurasian economic trajectories by exposing how historically embedded regional institutional legacies, rather than arbitrary reversals of fortune, determine whether societies sustain cumulative technological and institutional innovation. Joint evidence from Pamuk and Bryant demonstrates that path-dependent institutional structures, shaped by localized historical shocks and cultural dynamics, preemptively constrain or enable future growth trajectories. Pamuk’s analysis of the Black Death’s differential impacts on northwest Europe highlights how exogenous demographic changes preserved labor market rigidities in regions like England and the Low Countries, thereby reinforcing early modern income disparities that persisted as foundational constraints. These rigidities did not simply persist but *structurally* limited the adaptability of local institutions, ensuring that even in the face of external shocks, regional institutional architectures remained anchored in extractive or closed economic systems. Bryant’s critique of revisionist narratives of parallel Eurasian development underscores how the absence of cumulative institutional innovation in non-European societies, such as the Mughal patrimonial order, stemmed not from random external events but from entrenched institutional constraints that precluded the emergence of market-based systems capable of sustaining long-term growth. The shared implication is that institutional divergence in these regions was not merely a result of bad luck but a product of historically embedded, regionally distinct institutional trajectories that rendered later reforms insufficient to undo centuries of cumulative disadvantage. The downstream consequence is that societies with more rigid, path-dependent institutions face persistent barriers to catching up, even if their economic circumstances improve over time. The historical trajectory of economic growth is fundamentally shaped by the enduring stability of constitutional frameworks that anchor secure property rights, enabling sustained private investment and institutional resilience over generations. The commitment of sovereigns to enforce these rights, rather than short-term political gains, demonstrates how institutional design directly determines whether markets can evolve autonomously or remain constrained by rent-seeking incentives (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This mechanism underscores that constitutional stability is not an exogenous condition but a dynamic process where property rights enforcement becomes the linchpin of institutional commitment. The downstream implication here is that societies with historically robust institutions can sustain growth trajectories even amid external shocks, provided their constitutional structures remain resilient to internal erosion.

Entrepreneurial agency in labor markets reshapes institutional frameworks by transforming incentives and social structures, directly fueling divergent growth trajectories. The evidence from Ghana’s cocoa boom demonstrates how shifting labor scarcity and entrepreneurial African agency triggered institutional adaptations, such as the emergence of free wage labor and market-based land use, that replaced extractive systems and spurred long-term economic dynamism. These institutional shifts, however, did not occur in isolation; they were embedded in broader structural conditions where local chiefs retained control over land distribution while allowing voluntary migration, illustrating how entrenched power hierarchies coexisted with novel market mechanisms. The mechanisms here are not merely exogenous cultural shifts but dynamic interactions between agency and institutional feedback loops, as Austin (2007, p.2) shows how the same factor-price shifts that favored land over labor also created the institutional space for labor mobility. These patterns suggest that institutional resilience depends on the capacity to reconcile conflicting incentives, such as those between landowners and free laborers, rather than imposing rigid or extractive structures.

Ogilvie’s framework further refines this argument by emphasizing how institutional conflicts within labor markets and credit systems constrained or accelerated growth. The guilds and communal welfare systems in pre-industrial England and northern Italy, for instance, were not merely efficiency-enhancing but shaped by competing cultural and economic logics. The Protestant Reformation’s dismantling of guilds, while often framed as a cultural decline, reflects how institutional rigidity could stifle adaptability, as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that non-economic values, such as trust and reciprocity, directly influenced the equilibrium of collective action games. Yet these systems also served as engines of capital accumulation when aligned with labor mobility, as van Zanden (2009, p.27) demonstrates through the guilds’ dual role in transmitting skills while also forming cartels that could distort wages. The downstream implication is that institutional frameworks thrive when they balance flexibility with enforcement, where entrepreneurial agency can exploit gaps in rigid systems while simultaneously shaping the structural conditions that sustain them. This tension between agency and institutional constraints reveals that growth trajectories are not predetermined by factor endowments alone but are deeply shaped by how societies reconcile competing demands within labor markets and welfare systems.

African institutions endure as critical nodes in the historical architecture of long-term economic stagnation, where colonial undervaluation of preexisting structures eroded both productive capacities and social cohesion. Hopkins (2009, p.22) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) jointly demonstrate that colonial policies did not simply impose extractive institutions but systematically dismantled or co-opted the precolonial governance frameworks that sustained trade, kinship networks, and entrepreneurial dynamism. Hopkins (2009, p.22) critiques how modern historiography’s reliance on econometric shortcuts oversimplifies institutional evolution, while Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) reveal how colonial-era gendered hierarchies further marginalized women’s contributions to trade and capital formation. The shared finding that precolonial economic systems, often dismissed as "informal" or "tribal", were far more organized than assumed illustrates how colonial misclassification created structural gaps in labor mobility and financial intermediation.

This erosion left enduring governance failures rooted in fragmented institutions that prioritized local elites over national markets. Hopkins (2009, p.22) underscores that colonialism’s disruptions to group cohesion, whether through forced assimilation, displacement, or economic disarticulation, created institutional "stubs" that lacked the depth of preexisting networks. The downstream implication, their work suggests, is that these structural distortions persist in modern governance, where state capacity remains constrained by both historical legacy and persistent gendered inequality.

The implication here is that sustained economic growth hinges on reconstructing institutions that reconcile historical legacies with modern incentives, rather than assuming stagnation is irreversible.

This tension between inherited constraints and adaptive potential shapes the next stream’s inquiry into how regional economic divergence persists, where institutional legacies are both a cause and consequence of divergent growth trajectories.

African economic trajectories reveal that persistent growth mechanisms are not merely cultural but deeply intertwined with the material legacies of slave production, which Nunn (2008, p.3) frames as a structural constraint that outlasted colonial interventions. The evidence suggests that slave markets altered trade dynamics, reinforcing institutional structures that prioritized extraction over inclusive economic systems. In contrast, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that British colonial policies, rather than stifling growth, enabled localized wage expansion, complicating the assumption that institutional rigidities were exclusively extractive. Their findings highlight that structural conditions, such as colonial-era labor systems, did not uniformly suppress development but instead created divergent incentives that persisted in postcolonial economies. This suggests that the impact of slave trade and colonialism was not a one-dimensional drag on growth but rather a complex interplay of incentives that reshaped institutional frameworks in ways that endured beyond formal colonial rule.

The data underscore that the distance from slave markets correlated with long-term economic outcomes in Africa, reinforcing that institutional legacies were not just colonial artifacts but outcomes of preexisting trade systems. These mechanisms imply that institutional divergence in Africa was not merely a function of geography but a consequence of how trade and colonial policies structured economic incentives. The implication is that long-term growth trajectories depend on how these legacies are reconciled with modern incentives, rather than assuming stagnation is an inevitable outcome of historical constraints. The persistence of these conditions suggests that institutional design must account for both historical trade networks and colonial-era labor systems to foster inclusive growth.

Temin’s analysis underscores how mid-20th-century labor surpluses in Europe’s agricultural sectors became the economic catalyst for industrial expansion, yet his framing leaves unaddressed the institutional structures that sustained or enabled those surpluses for decades. Temin (2002, p.17) and Allen (2001, p.8) jointly demonstrate that the same structural misallocation that fueled demand, dramatically expanding agricultural labor forces, was underpinned by divergent wage and price regimes that emerged centuries earlier. Temin’s emphasis on labor exhaustion by the 1970s contrasts with Allen’s deeper historical roots in institutional divergence: cities that retained stable incomes during the 1500–1750 period, like those in early economic leaders, avoided wage stagnation by securing property rights and enforcement that preserved productivity in agriculture. For example, rigid agricultural institutions in some regions kept wages artificially low, while flexible enforcement in others allowed farmers to invest in productivity, creating persistent surplus labor. The downstream implication, shared by both sources, is that institutional design in agriculture, not just policy interventions, defined the capacity for industrial demand creation. This tension between structural surplus and institutional constraints highlights why growth trajectories in late industrializing economies often lagged behind those with historically stable agricultural labor markets.

The shared finding that institutions shape long-term economic trajectories through structural mechanisms, such as the stability of property rights, the efficiency of contracting, and the resilience of welfare systems, remains the most empirically grounded consensus in historical growth scholarship. Shared findings from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate that institutions like those governing extractive or contracting frameworks act as *path-dependent* filters, determining whether surplus labor or capital becomes locked into extractive structures or productive ones. These frameworks persist because they encode commitments to long-term economic incentives, as North and Weingast argue is essential for the "credible commitment" required in public-choice systems, where short-term rent-seeking is balanced by institutions designed to sustain sustained investment.

Ogilvie’s (2007, p.27) (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) analysis of European institutions highlights how *interdependent institutional frameworks*, including labor, credit, and welfare systems, mediate the relationship between cultural norms and economic performance. Putnam’s cultural capital thesis (also cited by Ogilvie (2007, p.12)) reinforces that civic values underpin institutions like guilds and communal welfare, which in turn foster capital-intensive industries, as Pamuk (2007, p.23) shows in his comparison of flexible northwest European responses to the Black Death. These mechanisms reveal that institutions do not operate in isolation; their effectiveness depends on how they align with broader social contracts, where distributional conflicts (as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes) may stifle efficiency if not resolved through stable governance structures.

Austin’s (2008, p.24) (Austin 2008, p.24) critique of the "reversal of fortune" thesis underscores that the global institutional legacy of colonialism, whether extractive or contracting, creates divergent trajectories by systematically reinforcing unequal access to resources. The empirical evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.34) confirms that colonial institutions did not merely extract wealth but shaped long-term institutional endowments, with former colonies in extractive regimes (such as those in Latin America) struggling to escape low-growth equilibria. The critical case of post-colonial Britain and the Caribbean thus serves as empirical proof: institutions designed to enforce private property rights (e.g. the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9)) enabled sustained investment, while extractive alternatives locked resources into short-term exploitation.

The remaining tension centers on whether institutions are primarily *instrumental*, shaping growth through direct efficiency gains, or *structural*, determining the *distributional dynamics* of capital accumulation. While North and Weingast’s (1989, p.28) (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) focus on credible commitments and public-choice systems emphasizes instrumental design, Austin’s (2008, p.24) (Austin 2008, p.4) call for cross-continental comparisons highlights that the African and colonial experiences reveal how institutions also encode *historical legacies* of inequality, where rigid surplus labor or extractive elites persist even when economic conditions change. The settlement evidence from Pamuk (2007, p.2), where flexible institutions in northwest Europe absorbed demographic shocks and capitalized on demand, offers the empirical resolution: institutions that balance efficiency and inclusion create the conditions for sustained structural transformation, while those that prioritize rent-seeking or exclusion do not.
