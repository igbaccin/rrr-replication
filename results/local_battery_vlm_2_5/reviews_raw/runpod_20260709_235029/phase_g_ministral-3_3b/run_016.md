The substantive question at the heart of this inquiry is how institutions, defined broadly as formal and informal rules governing economic and political interaction, systematically shape the trajectory of long-term economic growth by creating conditions for sustained commitment, innovation, and structural adaptation. What intellectual stakes does this inquiry hold? The debate hinges on whether the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are primarily endogenous to their own historical development or whether external shocks and exogenous changes can trigger irreversible institutional reforms that alter future pathways. The core live disagreement revolves around the extent to which path dependence, where prior institutional configurations constrain future options, must be reconciled with the possibility of deliberate institutional design or exogenous disruptions that reset economic systems toward higher growth trajectories.

Institutions as the bedrock of growth trajectories depend on their capacity to foster collective action problems and enforce credible commitments over time, particularly in environments where political and economic elites must balance short-term rent-seeking with long-term investment. Shared findings (North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscore that stable constitutional frameworks, such as those enforcing secure property rights and multiple veto points in governance, arise from struggles between commercially minded elites and authoritarian regimes, ensuring that institutions evolve in ways that mitigate opportunistic exploitation. North and Weingast’s (1989, p.28) analysis illustrates that the success of such institutions stems from sovereign commitment to rule enforcement, a factor that transcends mere formal rules and instead depends on political institutions’ ability to sustain institutional change through checks on elites. Without credible enforcement, even well-designed institutions risk eroding into extractive systems that extract wealth without fostering productivity. Africa’s historical institutional legacies, particularly the interweaving of precolonial governance systems and colonial impositions, systematically constrain long-term growth by embedding enduring governance weaknesses into cultural and structural norms. Hopkins (2009, p.22) contends that these legacies shape modern governance failures by reinforcing social cohesion through non-ethnic loyalties, such as shared economic or religious interests, which obscure the role of elite capture. This institutional specificity ensures that policies rooted in ethnic fragmentation oversimplify the root causes of weak institutions, favoring structural reforms that prioritize historical context over abstract assumptions about fragmentation. Peters (2004, p.5) complements this by highlighting how colonial-era land policies inadvertently deepened systemic exclusion, illustrating that rigid state-customary law separations exacerbated inequality rather than addressing localized autonomy. Together, these sources reveal that African institutions endure as extractive systems not merely because of colonial imposition but because preexisting institutional logics, such as land tenure conflicts and cultural norms, interact with colonial rules to perpetuate exclusionary practices. The result is a feedback loop where systemic exclusion undermines trust in institutions, reinforcing a cycle of underdevelopment.

The colonial period’s institutional interventions further entangled African governance with extractive practices, embedding norms of distrust and opportunism that persist today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that the transatlantic slave trade fostered enduring cultural mistrust through direct exposure to violence and exploitation, shaping societies’ internal norms against cooperation. Their findings suggest that the slave trade’s legacy, rather than colonial decay alone, transcended institutional collapse to shape long-term trust deficits, which in turn hinder economic integration. Nunn (2008, p.3) refines this by showing that the slave trade’s institutional effects were not merely external shocks but deeply embedded within African societies, leaving lasting economic scars through warfare, raiding, and forced labor that stifled productivity. The downstream implication is that these mechanisms persist as proximate determinants of governance failures, where institutional design must account for cultural norms that emerged from historical violence. The implication here is that growth trajectories in Africa are shaped less by abstract institutional reforms than by the specificity of these historical legacies, which demand reforms that address both structural and cultural roots.

van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Allen (2001, p.8) jointly illustrate that the dismantling of guild protections in early modern Europe not only reduced skilled labor demand but systematically undermined wage premiums, thereby defining a persistent productivity constraint. The paper’s empirical focus on wage divergence by the 15th century reveals that these guilds, while initially stabilizing apprenticeships, inadvertently locked in monopolistic pay structures that later eroded under broader institutional stress. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) highlights how guilds’ rigid entry barriers and cartel-like practices reinforced skill inequality, while Allen (2001, p.8) underscores that this institutional collapse left a lasting imprint: by mid-19th-century divergence, even "economic leaders" sustained stagnant wages relative to peripheral regions, where prices remained tied to collapsing demand. The consequence was a feedback loop in which diminishing skill premiums undermined the very institutional frameworks required to sustain cumulative innovation. While demand-driven wage gaps may have persisted, the structural erosion of guilds exposed a deeper tension: institutions that failed to resolve skill disparities early could not later compensate for them with dynamic markets. This mechanism, where institutional fragility undermines human capital accumulation, sets a critical benchmark for understanding how early institutional design shapes enduring growth trajectories.

Austin’s framework highlights how institutions emerge as dynamic systems where economic shocks, such as resource abundance or scarcity, trigger feedback loops between social structures and institutional evolution. Shared findings among Austin’s work and the broader historiography reveal how such transformations, such as Ghana’s labor scarcity following the cocoa boom, did not merely reflect market imbalances but instead catalyzed institutional adaptations that reinforced long-term structural advantages. The paper’s framing of the "reversal of fortune" thesis as a macro-level abstraction masks how these adaptations, driven by entrepreneurial agency and shifting factor prices, embedded new institutional logics that outlasted short-term shocks (Austin 2008, p.24; Austin 2007, p.2). The empirical depth of Austin’s analysis highlights that institutions like land-use markets and hired labor emerged not as neutral equilibria but as deliberate responses to scarcity, embedding cultural and economic practices that later sustained economic specialization. These adaptations, however, were not universal; the same cocoa-driven labor flows also exposed enduring inequalities, particularly in how chiefs and ethnic groups redistributed land benefits, which left lasting asymmetries in institutional access.

The mechanism here lies in the tension between *voluntary migration* and *institutional codification* of labor rights, a dynamic Austin (2007, p.2) traces explicitly. The shift from slavery to seasonal migration did not abolish inequality but restructured it, while freeing Akan-speaking laborers to work lucrative forest lands, it also institutionalized chiefs’ control over land redistribution, creating a duality where labor mobility coexisted with rigid hierarchical access (Austin 2007, p.2; Austin 2007, p.23). This duality later shaped Ghana’s post-colonial economy, where land scarcity persisted as a structural constraint despite the abolition of slavery, illustrating how institutional legacies from earlier transformations could both enable and constrain growth trajectories.

Colonialism’s extractive institutions in the Americas embedded structural incentives that either reinforced or suppressed inclusive growth, while neglect in resource-rich regions enabled flexible institutional adaptation, thus defining long-term economic divergence. The joint analysis of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12) reveals that colonial rule did not merely disrupt pre-existing institutions but *systematically inverted* their institutional legacies, ensuring that prosperous pre-colonial societies faced higher risks of institutional capture by elites, while impoverished regions avoided this fate. For instance, the paper’s institutional reversal hypothesis demonstrates that European colonial powers deliberately established extractive institutions in previously prosperous areas like Latin America, while fostering property rights and market incentives in colonies like the Americas’ settler societies, such as the United States and Canada. Such divergent institutional designs ensured that extractive institutions persisted in regions like Brazil or Mexico, where elite dominance persisted through extractive taxation and monopolistic control of resources, while inclusive institutions in North America facilitated capital accumulation and industrialization.

The joint evidence from these sources also highlights how these institutional divergences became path-dependent, shaping economic opportunities and resource allocation in ways that reinforced inequality. The downstream implication is that these institutional legacies did not simply reflect colonial interventions but *actively constrained* long-term development by reinforcing structural barriers to investment and innovation. Pamuk (2007, p.2) complements this by illustrating that even exogenous shocks, such as the Black Death, interacted with institutional flexibility to determine long-term divergence, regions with rigid institutions struggled to adapt, while those with adaptive structures capitalized on new opportunities. Together, these findings suggest that colonialism did not merely disrupt growth trajectories but *engineered persistent institutional divergence* that continues to shape modern economic disparities.

The persistence of these institutional legacies underscores how colonialism created a feedback loop between institutions and economic outcomes, ensuring that extractive structures became self-reinforcing over time.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) demonstrate that institutions actively shape technological diffusion and social adaptation, ensuring that economic outcomes are not passive reflections but active outcomes of institutional design. These scholars reveal that institutional capacity to absorb and innovate new technologies, rather than passive adherence to inherited structures, determines whether a society accelerates or stagnates in productivity trajectories. Abramovitz’s analysis of postwar catch-up dynamics highlights how institutional flexibility, such as labor mobility and fiscal responsiveness, amplifies the impact of technological shocks, while Kuznets’s framing of modern growth’s requirements underscores that rigid institutional frameworks, like feudalism or slavery, act as persistent barriers to scaling innovations. Together, their work illustrates that growth is not a deterministic process but one where institutions either accelerate technological adoption or become bottlenecks, thereby embedding structural constraints into long-term economic trajectories.

The interplay between technology and institutions becomes particularly acute in contexts where social priorities shift rapidly, as Abramovitz’s emphasis on "wild card" institutional changes shows. When societies prioritize industrialization over agrarian traditions, the institutional architecture must evolve to support mass production and urbanization, otherwise, technological opportunity remains unexploited. Kuznets’s observation that modern economic growth demands institutions compatible with large-scale industrial plants, like centralized governance and skilled labor forces, clarifies that technological diffusion alone is insufficient; the institutions that enable its deployment must emerge simultaneously. This suggests that the most dynamic growth outcomes occur not in societies that merely adopt technology but in those that co-evolve institutions with it, ensuring alignment between structural conditions and technological needs. The downstream implication is that institutional lag, even in technologically advanced periods, can derail growth by creating systemic inefficiencies that persist long after the technological advantage materializes.

The persistence of these dynamics is further reinforced by the idea that institutional change is not linear but iterative, with each generation refining structures based on past failures and successes. Abramovitz’s argument that countries "learn to modify their institutional arrangements" as they gain experience implies that growth trajectories are shaped by cumulative institutional experimentation, where early missteps reinforce rigidities or later adaptations unlock new productivity thresholds. This iterative process suggests that the most resilient growth trajectories are those where institutions are not static but continuously tested and adapted, ensuring that technological opportunities are consistently translated into economic outcomes.

Ogilvie’s framework highlights how guilds and communal welfare systems in medieval England and Europe did not emerge from optimal market designs but from the equilibrium outcomes of collective action games, where norms and cultural beliefs, such as Catholic or Protestant values, directly influenced their efficiency. These institutions thrived by embedding reciprocal obligations into economic interactions, creating a feedback loop where institutional rigidity and social capital mutually reinforced one another.

North and Weingast’s work expands this insight by showing that secure property rights depend on institutions that enforce long-term commitments rather than short-term incentives. Their argument underscores that constitutional frameworks with veto points, like those in the UK, emerged as a response to fiscal crises, ensuring that economic rights were protected not through abstract rules but through the political will to sustain them. The Bank of England’s establishment, for instance, reflects how transaction costs were reduced by a regime bound to enforce property rights over time, thereby unlocking capital formation. This commitment is critical because it transforms institutions from static frameworks into dynamic systems where property rights are not just enforceable but actively institutionalized through recurring political struggles. The interplay between these mechanisms suggests that growth trajectories are shaped by how well institutions can balance stability with adaptability, ensuring that property rights remain a durable foundation for sustained economic activity.

Akyeampong and Fofack’s analysis reveals how colonialism erased precolonial gendered institutional practices, obscuring women’s foundational contributions to trade and agriculture that later became institutionalized under extractive governance. The authors demonstrate how patriarchal alliances between colonial powers and local elites codified patriarchal structures, reducing women’s economic agency to marginalized roles rather than recognizing their enduring expertise in trade and resource management. This erasure created a feedback loop where institutional frameworks reinforced gendered hierarchies, limiting their capacity to participate in capital accumulation. Mokyr’s argument complements this by showing how Enlightenment-era narratives dismissed women’s contributions entirely, framing progress as a masculine, knowledge-driven endeavor that ignored the labor behind material and social progress. Together, these findings indicate that institutional design must account for historical gendered exclusion to avoid repeating cycles of marginalization that stifle long-term economic dynamism. The implication is that growth trajectories are not merely shaped by technological or political structures but are also defined by how institutions either center or silences these excluded contributions. Bryant (2006, p.8) and Temin (2002, p.17) show that labor mobility constraints in premodern Eurasia created structural misallocations, which institutionalized into self-reinforcing cycles of economic stagnation. Together, these sources reveal that institutional rigidity in labor markets not only distorted efficiency but also embedded structural inequalities that delayed modernization. The implication is that institutions that failed to align labor supply with labor demand created self-reinforcing cycles of stagnation, where structural constraints were as enduring as the economic consequences they produced.

Both emphasize that flawed measurement systems and institutional design feedback loops create a self-reinforcing dynamic where growth proxies fail to reflect underlying structural constraints, amplifying stagnation in both historical and contemporary settings. Feinstein’s critique of British real-wage stagnation demonstrates how price indices that overlook household budgets and regional variations misrepresent productivity gains, leaving policymakers reliant on unreliable benchmarks. The mechanisms through which institutions systematically shape long-term economic growth trajectories converge on their foundational role in securing collective action and incentivizing sustained investment. Shared empirical and theoretical evidence demonstrates that stable, inclusive constitutional frameworks, particularly those embedding enforceable property rights and checks on elite extraction, provide the structural backbone for sustained capital accumulation and innovation. North and Weingast’s analysis (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) highlights how the political commitment to enforceable rules, grounded in multiple veto points, transformed Britain’s fiscal crises into long-term economic momentum, illustrating how institutional design directly fuels commitment to long-term economic outcomes. This dynamic is reinforced by Acemoglu et al.’s institutional reversal thesis (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), which shows how extractive institutions in formerly prosperous regions under colonialism collapsed into stagnation, while favorable institutions in marginal areas fostered sustained growth.

The interplay between institutional depth and cultural norms further explains growth disparities. Ogilvie’s framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) exposes how pre-industrial European institutions, such as guilds, communal welfare systems, and credit networks, were not merely economic artifacts but products of interdependent cultural values and institutional frameworks. Putnam’s social capital theory (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) complements this by revealing how civic trust and horizontal networks underpinned institutional resilience, particularly in northern Europe, where cultural norms aligned with capital-intensive industries. These findings suggest that institutions are not static but evolve through feedback loops between economic incentives and cultural equilibrium, where efficiency and social cohesion are mutually reinforcing.

Austin’s critique of the reversal-of-fortune thesis (Austin 2008, p.24) reframes this dynamic by emphasizing path dependence and regional divergence. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.34) emphasize the role of exogenous shocks, such as colonization or demographic upheavals, Austin argues that the thesis overstates the role of global regression models, instead highlighting how localized interactions determine long-term divergence. Pamuk’s case study of the Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.2) underscores this: selective institutional adaptation in northwest Europe, shaped by flexible labor markets and capital mobility, distinguished it from rigid southern systems, proving that exogenous shocks interact with preexisting institutional structures to determine growth trajectories. The empirical comparison thus resolves the debate: regions with preexisting favorable institutions and adaptive cultural norms, like England and the Low Countries, exploited shocks to transition into capital-intensive growth, while extractive or rigid institutions locked into stagnation.
