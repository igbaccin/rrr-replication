The substantive question at stake in this literature is how institutions systematically shape long-term economic growth trajectories through specific mechanisms and structural conditions, and what intellectual consequences emerge from disentangling their causal role. Scholars debate whether enduring institutional architectures, such as property rights frameworks, political commitments, or cultural norms, generate growth by fostering credible commitments, mitigating rent-seeking, or embedding economic activities in stable institutional ecosystems. The live disagreement centers on whether the influence of institutions operates primarily through *formal contracting* (secure rights, enforcement) or *informal relational* mechanisms (cultural trust, social capital), and whether path dependence or exogenous shocks like plagues or colonization are antecedents that amplify or reshape these structural conditions. The tension highlights whether growth trajectories are best explained by institutions as autonomous causal forces or as contingent outcomes of historical interactions.

Shared findings underscore that institutions are not isolated entities but interlocking frameworks that reinforce one another. The work of North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.28) demonstrates that constitutional stability, particularly the enforcement of property rights, is indispensable for long-term economic commitment, as political sovereignty’s commitment to rules shapes market incentives. Credible institutional enforcement, such as the English common law courts, enabled commercial interests to thrive by reducing transaction costs and securing private property, a mechanism that transcends mere rule of law to include the political will to uphold commitments over time. This relational perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.’s (2002, p.48) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34) empirical analysis, which reveals that colonialism reversed pre-existing institutional disparities, embedding extractive institutions in formerly prosperous regions while reinforcing productive ones in poorer areas, thereby sculpting modern economic divides. The critical insight is that institutions do not merely reflect growth but actively sculpt it through their capacity to sustain investments and deter exploitation.

North and Weingast’s argument, grounded in the primacy of constitutional protections, demonstrates that stable property rights act as an anchor for long-term economic commitment by mitigating the risk of opportunistic expropriation. Their analysis reveals that institutional design, specifically the decentralized veto power embedded in representative government, transforms private incentives into sustainable public investment, ensuring that market expansion persists even under political uncertainty (North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North 1989, p.9). Here, the legal enforcement of property rights not only secures capital accumulation but also legitimizes financial innovations, as the Bank of England’s establishment exemplifies. North’s (1989, p.9) empirical evidence shows that transaction-cost reductions tied to stable property rights directly facilitated England’s economic revolution, proving that institutional stability is inseparable from the durability of market systems. The same logic extends to the institutional path dependency observed in public choice systems, where entrenched protections incentivize future compliance rather than short-term exploitation.

These findings underscore how the temporal dimension of property rights, its security over time, becomes a critical resource for sustained growth. Yet North’s acknowledgment of unresolved questions about institutional change highlights a tension: while stability fosters growth, the very mechanisms that sustain it may resist abrupt disruption. The tension between persistence and dynamism emerges as a defining challenge for institutions that must balance historical legacies with adaptive needs, particularly in contexts where property rights remain precarious. This interplay suggests that growth trajectories hinge not just on the existence of stable institutions but on their capacity to endure as conditions evolve.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.3) establish that extractive institutions, rooted in colonial-era exploitative systems, create a feedback loop of suppressed incentives that permanently undermines long-term growth prospects in former colonies. These institutions, shaped by settler mortality risks and the demand for labor extraction, embed rules that prioritize elite capture over inclusive governance, even as postcolonial reforms attempt to dismantle them. Nunn’s (2008, p.3) evidence of slave-market proximity’s enduring negative effects on African economies demonstrates how such institutions persist through institutional inertia, reinforcing extractive equilibria that persist beyond colonial legacies. The shared mechanism here is not just institutional rigidity but the *structural misalignment* between colonial extraction incentives and modern economic demands, which persists in outcomes like persistent inequality or underinvestment in public goods.

Temin (2002, p.17) refines this by showing how post-war structural misallocations, like surplus agricultural labor, could also foster short-term growth spurts, yet only if the underlying institutional architecture supported reallocation. This suggests that extractive institutions don’t merely hamper growth passively but actively *sustain inefficiencies* that require external shocks (e.g. war-induced labor surpluses) to trigger growth. Temin’s emphasis on labor misallocation as a critical driver in Europe’s Golden Age reveals that even in former colonies, the absence of inclusive institutions may limit endogenous growth impulses, as surplus populations remain trapped in extractive circuits. The implication: extractive legacies don’t just stifle growth through direct repression but through the *cultural and structural habits* that make resource reallocation costly or impossible.

Distributional conflicts and their embedded institutional frameworks define how pre-industrial societies shaped long-term economic trajectories without relying on exogenous shocks. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Bryant (2006, p.8) show that the persistence of extractive institutions, like serfdom or guild monopolies, stemmed from enduring power struggles over resource allocation rather than spontaneous efficiency improvements. These conflicts generated structural rigidities that locked labor, capital, and credit into rigid hierarchies, limiting the flexibility needed for capital accumulation. For instance, Ogilvie argues that guilds in early modern Europe were not mere efficiency mechanisms but tools of elite control over craft production, reinforcing vertical hierarchies that constrained horizontal innovation. Bryant’s work further reveals that Eurasia’s uneven development was not accidental but a product of institutions that suppressed competitive markets, such as Islamic patrimonial systems that restricted merchant autonomy. Together, these sources demonstrate that distributional conflicts did not simply disrupt growth directly but embedded inefficiencies into the very institutional contracts governing labor, credit, and property, making endogenous reforms impossible without violent upheaval. The implication: institutions designed to consolidate power often created a feedback loop where conflict perpetuated itself as a prerequisite for institutional change, shaping trajectories of stagnation before the modern era.

African historical institutions did not merely reflect precolonial and colonial power dynamics but embedded systemic biases in governance that persist as constraints on long-term economic growth, as Hopkins (2009, p.22) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate through their analyses of enduring cultural and structural legacies. This mistrust, they argue, persisted beyond formal legal structures, embedding distrust in social interactions that stifled trustworthiness in long-term economic contracts. The downstream implication is that these cultural legacies, rooted in systemic undervaluation of alternative forms of solidarity, continued to shape governance failures well into the postcolonial era, where institutional design often prioritized stability over inclusive economic participation.

These findings intersect with Akyeampong and Fofack’s (2014, p.4) exploration of gendered institutional barriers, which reveal how colonial policies systematically marginalized women’s economic contributions through patriarchal alliances with colonial administrators. The authors trace how kinship and colonial governance codified patriarchal structures, reducing women’s economic agency to rudimentary bartering roles rather than fostering systemic capital formation. This institutional framing, (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19) they contend, created an environment where women’s labor was undervalued, reinforcing gendered inequalities that persist as structural constraints on economic growth. The interplay between these sources underscores how historical institutional legacies did not exist in isolation but interacted with broader cultural and economic systems to create feedback loops that reinforced exclusionary outcomes. The implication for long-term growth is that these legacies, whether through distrust or gendered governance, persist as systemic barriers, demanding policies that explicitly address their historical origins to unlock sustainable development.

Colonialism reshaped institutional foundations in previously wealthy regions by systematically dismantling the very structures that enabled endogenous growth, while simultaneously embedding new extractive governance in areas of marginal or low pre-existing wealth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48; 2002, p.34) demonstrate that European colonizers prioritized institutional design to extract resources or maintain control rather than fostering inclusive economic frameworks, thereby eroding the capacity of elites in prosperous locales to sustain long-term investment. This reversal of institutional architecture, where extractive institutions replaced inclusive ones, created a feedback loop that locked poorer regions into stagnant trajectories while depriving former centers of the capital and trust mechanisms that historically drove cumulative advantage. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12; 2000, p.2) add nuance by showing that elite dominance in post-colonial societies preserved structural inequalities, ensuring that institutional legacies persisted not as neutral tools but as mechanisms that reinforced elite exclusion. Together, these findings suggest that colonialism did not merely impose external shocks but *actively* shaped institutional paths that amplified pre-existing disparities, embedding systemic advantages in favor of the colonizers’ own economies while leaving indigenous societies trapped in cycles of underinvestment and underdevelopment. The implication is that these institutional reversals did not occur in isolation but were deeply intertwined with the economic and political dynamics of the colonized world, where extractive institutions often coalesced with local elites to perpetuate inequality. This structural embedding, rather than being temporary or accidental, became a defining feature of post-colonial institutions, dictating how wealth was distributed and how future growth opportunities were constrained.

Empirical innovation and institutional coordination, rather than abstract Enlightenment progress, defined the transformative dynamics behind sustained economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.41) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) jointly argue. The paper’s empirical focus on industrial experimentation and technological diffusion reframes the role of institutions as not merely enabling but actively shaping the conditions under which innovation thrived, particularly in the transition from agrarian stagnation to manufacturing dominance. The shared finding that institutions functioned as "wild cards" in growth trajectories, influencing which countries ascended to productivity dominance, aligns with Abramovitz’s (1986, p.21) observation that institutional adjustments, such as trade policies and social priorities, emerge dynamically as countries adopt new technologies. Abramovitz’s emphasis on endogenous institutional learning, where constraints imposed by social capability collide with technological opportunity, reveals that growth did not unfold linearly but required iterative policy experiments to prevent coordination failures. This mechanism reveals that the durability of economic progress hinged on institutions that balanced innovation incentives with mechanisms to prevent elite capture, ensuring that technological gains persisted beyond initial extractive phases. These insights underline that the institutional architecture of growth was as much a product of empirical trial-and-error as it was of historical necessity.

The Black Death and subsequent labor market disruptions acted as endogenous shockwaves that reshaped institutional structures through endogenous feedback loops, turning exogenous disruptions into the foundation of long-term growth trajectories (Pamuk 2007, p.2; Austin 2007, p.2). Pamuk (2007, p.2) demonstrates that the mortality crisis preserved labor scarcity and wage stability in northwest Europe, while Allen (2001, p.8) highlights how mid-century wage-price divergence emerged from pre-existing institutional resilience in regions that sustained relative income stability. These mechanisms reveal that institutional adaptability, rooted in labor market responses to scarcity, determined which economies transitioned from short-term disruptions to enduring productivity gains. The empirical evidence suggests that labor mobility and wage preservation underpinned institutional "creative destruction, " not merely exogenous shocks, by embedding incentives for capital-intensive industries that outlasted the crisis. Allen’s (2001, p.8) wage histories further illustrate how institutional stability in early modern leaders preserved investment incentives, while Austin’s (2007, p.2) cocoa boom case shows how land scarcity drove institutional innovation in Africa through voluntary labor markets. These findings underscore that the persistence of growth outcomes hinged on institutional responses that capitalized on labor scarcity’s long-term structural effects, not the shocks themselves.

The shared finding (Pamuk 2007, p.2; Austin 2007, p.23) illustrates that institutional legacies, whether in Europe’s early modern economies or West Africa’s cocoa boom, were shaped by how labor markets mediated between scarcity and surplus, embedding enduring institutional incentives. This suggests that the durability of growth outcomes depended not on the shock’s magnitude but on the institutional mechanisms that sustained labor mobility and wage stability, turning scarcity into a driver of structural reform. The downstream implication is that institutions themselves became path-dependent through endogenous feedbacks, where labor market conditions shaped institutional design, which in turn sustained long-term economic momentum.

The dominant institutionalist frameworks overlook how exclusionary power structures shape even the most formalized legal and economic systems, embedding systemic marginalization into their design rather than merely reflecting their outcomes. Shared evidence from Peters and Hopkins demonstrates that exclusion, whether embedded in colonial legacies, customary law gaps, or bureaucratic inefficiencies, creates feedback loops that distort formal governance and undermine sustainable growth. When customary land tenure systems, for instance, separate formal and customary law, they inadvertently deepen exclusion by allowing powerful actors to control resources while marginalizing those who lack legal protections, as Peters (2004, p.5) shows. The resulting "local autonomy" thus becomes a tool for elite appropriation rather than a mechanism for inclusive development. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further refines this dynamic by exposing how path-dependent institutional gaps, like those between distant past production systems and modern governance, fail to account for the structural inequalities that persist. The downstream implication is that exclusionary institutions do not merely reflect pre-existing disparities but actively perpetuate them through mechanisms that formal frameworks alone cannot address.

These findings reveal how exclusionary structures create a paradox: rigid institutional designs that appear stable often conceal deeper fractures, where marginalized groups are systematically excluded from economic participation. The interplay between weak bureaucratic states and localized elites, as Peters (2004, p.18) details, exposes how even "soft" state structures can be co-opted to maintain power asymmetries, amplifying exclusionary dynamics. Hopkins’s critique of path-dependent determinism further highlights how historical distortions, like the compression of African economic history, mask systemic exclusion, preventing rigorous analysis of its role in shaping growth trajectories. The result is that institutions, rather than acting as neutral mediators, become instruments of exclusion, their formal frameworks hollowed out by structural power imbalances. This suggests that long-term growth trajectories are not solely shaped by institutional rigidity but by the *endogenous* exclusionary conditions embedded within them.

The shared consensus across these sources underscores that institutions systematically shape long-term growth trajectories by embedding structural commitments that transcend short-term efficiency. Austin’s critique of the “reversal of fortune” thesis reframes long-term institutional dynamics as deeply path-dependent, where formal frameworks like those in Africa’s historical compression reveal how exclusionary power structures hollow out institutional potential (Austin 2008, p.24). The same institutional frameworks, such as those in Europe, are not neutral mediators but sites of interdependent contracting and property rights, where rigidities or fluidities in one area ripple through others (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Institutions, thus, operate as endogenous engines of divergence, their stability or collapse defining whether they promote or entrench wealth disparities.

Yet, the debate over causal specificity remains. North and Weingast’s focus on constitutional commitments, particularly the enforcement of property rights through decentralized veto points, highlights that commitment itself is the critical variable, not merely institutional form (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This aligns with Pamuk’s empirical evidence from the Black Death era, where flexible institutions in northwest Europe adapted to shocks like wage preservation, while extractive structures in other regions failed to do so, thereby shaping modern divergence (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Pamuk’s work thus demonstrates that institutional adaptability, rooted in historical conditions, determines whether growth trajectories remain constrained or expand.

Despite these divergent emphases, the underlying evidence ties institutional design to measurable divergence. Acemoglu et al.’s analysis of colonial-era institutions confirms that extractive versus inclusive frameworks, not just exogenous shocks, determine long-term income trajectories, with historical comparisons like those of the United States versus other colonies providing empirical validation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34). The case of Britain’s financial revolution further illustrates how decentralized property rights enforcement, reinforced by fiscal crises, underwrote sustained growth (North 1989, p.9). Shared findings reveal that institutions are not merely outcomes of cultural or exogenous forces but self-reinforcing systems where endogenous exclusionary structures or inclusive commitments define structural conditions for sustained growth.
