The mechanisms and structural conditions through which institutions systematically shape long-term economic growth trajectories remain central to scholarly inquiry, yet they are profoundly contested. The debate hinges on whether institutions operate primarily as foundational enablers of secure commitments, such as stable property rights and institutional frameworks that mitigate short-term rent-seeking, *or* as dynamic outcomes of endogenous path dependence, where their effects are shaped by historical contingencies and systemic interdependencies. This intellectual divide centers on the tension between *institutional causation* (e.g. the idea that enduring structures like constitutional safeguards directly sustain growth) and *institutional mediation* (e.g. the argument that institutions reflect and reinforce prior economic and social configurations). The stakes lie in understanding how these mechanisms unfold over centuries: do they function as self-reinforcing equilibria, or do they emerge from exogenous shocks or cultural legacies that reshaped growth trajectories? Scholars have illuminated how these dynamics interact, particularly in how historical events like colonialism, demographic shifts, or ideological transformations redefined institutional landscapes.

Shared finding (Austin 2008, p.24; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscores that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, advocated by North and Weingast (1989, p.28), places institutions at the core of long-term growth by emphasizing their role in enforcing contractual commitments and preventing opportunistic exploitation. These authors argue that stable constitutional arrangements, rooted in veto points and judicial independence, create incentives for sustained investment and innovation, as political elites must balance competing interests to avoid collapse. Yet their framework risks oversimplifying the complexity of institutional evolution, which Austin (2008, p.24) highlights by pointing to Africa’s divergent historical experiences. The paper’s critique reveals that cross-continental comparisons often obscure how differentiated institutional histories, such as the interplay between serfdom, guilds, and welfare systems, mediate growth outcomes, suggesting that institutions do not act in isolation but as part of interdependent frameworks. This reflects a broader recognition that structural conditions must account for both top-down enforcement and bottom-up cultural norms, as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) demonstrates with European pre-industrial institutions. The interplay between these layers complicates the notion of institutions as purely exogenous forces.

Institutional stability anchored in enforceable property rights emerges as the most durable mechanism shaping long-term growth trajectories, demonstrated by the shared emphasis of North and Ogilvie that enduring commitments to these rights reduce transaction costs and incentivize sustained investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North 1989, p.9). The critical insight from North (1989, p.9) is that the decline in English transaction costs following the Bank of England’s establishment did not stem from technological innovation alone but from the *timing dimension of property rights*, ensuring that capital could be held and redeployed over decades rather than confiscated in short cycles. This stability, North argues, became self-reinforcing when Parliament ceded revenue rights in exchange for fiscal stability, creating a feedback loop between legal protections and long-term capital accumulation. Ogilvie’s (2007, p.27) framework further refines this by showing how *distributional enforcement*, not just abstract rules, must accompany property rights to prevent opportunistic elites from undermining growth. For instance, guilds in northern Europe thrived when their internal norms enforced trust *and* external laws constrained predatory extraction, illustrating how cultural norms and legal enforcement co-evolved to sustain institutional stability. The implication is that growth trajectories hinge on the ability of institutions to internalize commitments to property rights in ways that transcend short-term political cycles, as North and Weingast’s (1989, p.28) argument that veto points in governance prevent arbitrary rule changes underscores. This stability, in turn, shapes the *cumulative advantage* of early adopters, as secure rights allow firms to accumulate knowledge and capital while deterring rent-seeking that would otherwise stifle innovation.

The second mechanism through which institutions drive growth lies in the *decoupling of enforcement from ownership*, a condition where elites are constrained by institutional design rather than personal ties. This separation enabled the emergence of a decentralized financial system where credit became liquid and long-term contracts could be enforced, even as the sovereign’s role in revenue collection remained contested. Ogilvie (207) complements this by showing how *non-economic cultural beliefs*, such as Protestant values in northern Europe, channeled into institutional norms that reinforced property rights by discouraging predatory behavior. The contrast with southern Europe, where feudal ties and Catholic beliefs fostered clientelism, demonstrates that even when rules existed, enforcement efficacy depended on whether institutions embedded cultural norms that discouraged opportunism. Thus, the strength of property rights as a growth driver hinges not only on formal legal frameworks but on the *interplay between cultural norms and institutional design*, ensuring that enforcement remains resilient against elite capture. This interplay suggests that long-term growth trajectories are most favorable where institutions internalize both legal constraints and cultural expectations to sustain stable commitments to property rights.

Colonialism’s enduring impact on institutional design, particularly through differential settler mortality and resource extraction, systematically constrains long-term growth trajectories in postcolonial economies by embedding extractive institutions that persist despite later reforms. These institutional legacies, shaped by European mortality risks and microclimate-driven mortality patterns, lock former colonies into cycles where property rights and economic incentives remain fragile, undermining sustained investment. The work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and (2002) reveals that expropriation risks and high-altitude mortality reduced European settlement in regions already endowed with favorable pre-colonial institutions, forcing the establishment of extractive frameworks in those areas. The shared finding that European powers prioritized resource extraction over private property, such as in plantation economies, demonstrates how colonial institutions were not merely temporary impositions but structural filters that reinforced unequal development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34). This pattern explains why reforms in extractive colonies often fail to reverse stagnation, as institutions remain fundamentally extractive, even when formal legal frameworks evolve.

The mortality-driven institutional reversal further complicates growth outcomes by creating a feedback loop between health risks and economic incentives. The persistence of these institutions, such as mining monopolies or plantation labor systems, creates long-term expropriation risks that deter private investment, as potential entrepreneurs face uncertainty over future property security. The downstream implication is that even where formal property rights emerge post-colonization, their effectiveness depends on whether they align with extractive norms embedded in colonial-era institutions, ensuring that growth remains constrained by these structural constraints. These mechanisms illustrate how colonial legacies do not merely leave "ghosts" in economic development but actively shape the *equilibrium* of institutions in postcolonial states.

The scholarly reassessment of precolonial economic disparities in Africa reveals that historical production gaps, once framed as irreversible, actually encode deeper institutional and structural conditions that persist in long-term growth trajectories. Hopkins and Nunn demonstrate that the intersection of precolonial resource endowments and the transatlantic slave trade did not merely erode African economies but created enduring institutional feedbacks that reinforced economic stagnation. Hopkins critiques the methodological oversimplifications of path-dependent approaches, while Nunn’s empirical work shows that the slave trade’s direct and indirect effects, from forced labor to disrupted trade networks, systematically constrained the development of institutions capable of fostering sustained economic expansion (Hopkins 2019, p.11; Nunn 2008, p.3).

Hopkins’s methodological caution underscores that the past’s influence on present institutions does not operate through deterministic leaps but through discrete, historically bounded mechanisms. The author highlights the limitations of instrumental variable methods in accounting for complex causal chains, particularly when tracing precolonial conditions to contemporary outcomes. This perspective challenges the assumption that institutional convergence would inevitably bridge historical divides, instead emphasizing how precolonial economic structures, such as the prevalence of agricultural labor systems, set the stage for extractive institutional designs. Nunn’s findings further illustrate how the institutional legacy of slave production, including warfare and raiding, created environments where formal property rights lacked credibility. Such institutions, rooted in violence and coercion, made it difficult for postcolonial states to develop trust-based governance structures, as potential investors perceived persistent expropriation risks (Hopkins 2019, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.25).

The trade-off between precolonial resource abundance and slave trade dependence reveals a critical structural tension: while Africa’s extensive arable land and labor capacity could have sustained diversified economies, the slave trade’s industrialization of labor extraction prioritized short-term wealth extraction over long-term institutional stability. Nunn’s empirical evidence indicates that proximity to slave markets, rather than colonial intervention, exerted the most durable negative effects, shaping economic geography in ways that persisted beyond the abolition of slavery. This suggests that the institutional equilibrium in postcolonial states was not merely shaped by colonial legacies but was also a product of the economic and social structures that emerged from precolonial slave-based economies. The downstream implication is that growth trajectories in Africa are not reducible to colonial imprints alone but are instead a function of how precolonial and early modern institutions interacted to constrain or enable institutional innovation.

The enduring exclusion embedded in African economic systems arises from the systemic failure to reconcile precolonial governance with postcolonial institutional frameworks, as Hopkins (2009, p.22) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) jointly argue. The evidence from Hopkins’s critique of modern African historiography reveals that while contemporary governance challenges often appear fragmented or ethically driven, they are instead the product of structural inertia rooted in historical exclusionary practices, particularly those perpetuated through the transatlantic slave trade. Hopkins (2009, p.22) insists that institutional evolution in Africa has been distorted by an overemphasis on ethnic fragmentation rather than deeper social and economic dynamics, such as the cultural norms reinforced by the slave trade. This mechanism, where mistrust evolved as a persistent norm, directly shapes contemporary governance failures, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate through data showing how proximity to the coast, historically tied to the slave trade, correlates with lower trust in institutions.

The institutional separation of customary law further exacerbates systemic exclusion, as Peters (2004, p.5) highlights through land conflicts that expose how formal and informal institutions often operate in tension. The unintended consequence of this institutional gap is that local autonomy, rather than fostering resilience, deepens exclusionary practices by creating disjointed power structures. This tension between state elites and local actors creates a feedback loop where weak bureaucratic states lack the capacity to integrate diverse social interests, perpetuating cycles of marginalization. The implications of this dynamic extend beyond economic underperformance, affecting social cohesion and political stability in ways that persist well beyond colonial legacies. The cumulative effect is a governance framework where systemic exclusion is not merely an artifact of historical events but a defining feature of contemporary African development challenges.

Sokoloff and Engerman’s analysis reveals how elite exclusion in New World societies entrenched cumulative institutional disadvantages that shaped divergent economic trajectories, while Bryant’s critique of revisionist narratives underscores the enduring structural inequalities that underpinned Eurasia’s divergent modernization paths. Both scholars demonstrate that institutional divergence was not merely a function of contingent historical accidents but was instead a product of deliberate exclusionary policies that locked elite groups into positions of dominance, systematically sidelining broader populations (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12; Bryant 2006, p.8). These exclusionary dynamics not only shaped early institutional configurations but also created feedback loops where unequal access to economic opportunities reinforced elite dominance, fostering environments where growth-enhancing institutions, such as secure property rights or inclusive financial systems, remained underdeveloped. For example, in New World societies, the concentration of political and economic power in elite hands impeded the emergence of institutions that would have broadened participation, thereby preserving persistent inequality as a defining feature of long-term development trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Bryant’s work further highlights that even in Eurasia, the absence of comparable institutional inclusivity, despite shared pre-industrial conditions, led to divergent paths toward modernity, illustrating how exclusionary structures could stifle cumulative innovation. The downstream implication here is that the persistence of elite exclusion does not merely retard growth through direct economic constraints but also undermines the social and political conditions necessary for sustained institutional reform. This tension between exclusionary institutions and long-term growth trajectories thus becomes a central mechanism through which structural conditions shape economic outcomes in ways that are both enduring and deeply systemic.

The exclusionary mechanisms outlined by these scholars converge on a critical insight: elite dominance did not merely create imbalances in resource distribution but also reshaped the very fabric of institutional development, ensuring that institutions either reinforced or were co-opted by elite interests. This prioritization left institutions vulnerable to corruption, limited state capacity, and a lack of mechanisms for broad-based economic growth, as elites channeled resources toward their own interests rather than toward infrastructure or public goods that could have spurred collective progress. Bryant’s critique of revisionist narratives further underscores that this exclusionary dynamic was not an exception but a structural feature of many societies, where the absence of inclusive institutional frameworks hindered the accumulation of knowledge and capital that would have otherwise driven modernization. For instance, in Eurasia, the concentration of power in centralized states or within merchant elites often led to institutional arrangements that favored elite interests over the broader population, preventing the emergence of the kinds of meritocratic or participatory structures that could have facilitated long-term economic dynamism. The result was a cycle where exclusionary institutions perpetuated inequality, while the absence of inclusive institutions limited the potential for cumulative economic growth. The persistence of these exclusionary structures thus becomes a defining constraint on long-term economic trajectories, as they systematically undermine the conditions necessary for sustainable institutional reform. This tension between exclusionary institutions and long-term growth trajectories is not merely an historical artifact but a defining feature of how structural conditions shape economic outcomes across diverse societies.

The misconceptions about early economic divergence and wage stagnation in historical narratives obscure how institutions shaped long-term income disparities, with Feinstein’s (1998, p.13) and Allen’s (2001, p.8) work exposing how flawed cost-of-living estimates misled scholars about the persistence of institutional price rigidities. Feinstein (1998, p.13) highlights that historians’ reliance on institutional and wholesale prices, particularly in Britain, distorted perceptions of wage trends, masking the structural constraints that sustained low real incomes for working classes despite rising productivity. These rigidities, rooted in labor contracts and regional price disparities, created artificial barriers that distorted historical growth narratives. Allen (2001, p.8) reinforces this by showing that by mid-19th century, wage-price divergence was already entrenched between economic leaders and peripheral regions, with institutional price inflexibility acting as a self-reinforcing mechanism that perpetuated inequality long before modern indicators emerged. The divergence between Feinstein’s (1998, p.13) critique of overemphasized price stability and Allen’s (2001, p.8) empirical tracing of pre-1750 disparities reveals how misaligned cost estimates obscured deeper institutional mechanisms: rigid price floors in dominant sectors and exclusionary labor protections, which together shaped divergent long-term growth trajectories by eroding consumer purchasing power in peripheral economies.

Feinstein’s (1998, p.13) critique extends to the methodological flaws in early cost-of-living calculations, where reliance on wholesale prices, often tied to elite markets, failed to reflect everyday consumption. These indices, anchored in London or regional hubs, overstated stability by ignoring regional price volatility and supply chain distortions, particularly for basic necessities like food and housing. The 19th-century transition from contract-based wages to standardized labor markets further entangled institutions with wage suppression, as institutional price floors (e.g. minimum wage rigidities) created a feedback loop between elite economic leadership and working-class stagnation. Allen’s (2001, p.8) study of pre-1750 European cities adds precision to this by showing that early economic leaders maintained stable incomes through institutional safeguards, while peripheral cities, exposed to price shocks without corresponding wage adjustments, suffered from cumulative wage erosion. These mechanisms demonstrate that institutional rigidity, rather than mere historical accidents, became a structural feature of early divergence, embedding itself in long-term income dynamics through a combination of price controls, regional exclusion, and elite-driven policy stability. The downstream implication of such rigidities is that economic growth trajectories were not merely a function of external shocks but were systematically shaped by the persistence of exclusionary institutional frameworks, which reinforced disparities over generations.

Labor’s structural misallocation during the post-WWII Golden Age and the colonial-era wage dynamics in Africa reveal a paradoxical yet interdependent mechanism: the same institutional frameworks that constrained labor mobility in Europe also shaped divergent growth trajectories by embedding persistent wage inequalities in colonial economies. Temin’s argument (Temin 2002, p.17)(Temin 2002, p.4) posits that Europe’s rapid industrialization stemmed from a deliberate labor reallocation, surplus agricultural workers exiting agriculture to fuel industrial expansion, while Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) demonstrate that even in British colonies, real wages rose substantially and exceeded subsistence thresholds, undermining the narrative of African stagnation. These findings converge on a key insight: institutional incentives for labor mobility were often calibrated differently across regions, with European policies systematically channelling surplus labor toward industrial sectors, whereas colonial wage structures in Africa inadvertently reinforced structural inequalities by disproportionately favoring settler and migrant groups over indigenous populations. The downstream implication is that economic growth trajectories were not merely a function of policy design but were co-constructed by the unintended consequences of wage dynamics and institutional incentives, revealing how structural labor flows could either accelerate growth or deepen exclusion. The interplay between these mechanisms highlights that institutional legacies, whether historical or colonial, did not operate in isolation but as part of a broader, systemic labor market architecture that dictated long-term economic divergence.

Path-dependent institutional evolution reveals that long-term economic divergence in Europe was not merely a response to exogenous shocks but emerged from the cumulative interaction of demand-driven structural changes and institutional adaptation. Pamuk (2007, p.2) demonstrates that the Black Death’s exogenous demographic collapse preserved wage levels in regions like England and the Low Countries, creating an institutional environment where flexible labor markets could later thrive. This preservation of wages, rather than a purely top-down policy, became a critical catalyst for capital-intensive industries, as Pamuk (2007, p.23) notes, highlighting how demand shifts in skilled labor (e.g. apprenticeship markets) reshaped institutional incentives over centuries. Meanwhile, van Zanden (2009, p.19) refines this mechanism by tracing how guilds, formalized institutions for fair apprenticeship contracts, balanced skill transfer with wage suppression, ultimately reinforcing path-dependent skill premiums that later defined modern productivity gaps. The interplay between these demand-driven wage dynamics and institutional constraints thus illustrates how historical shocks did not arbitrarily determine trajectories but instead set the stage for divergent institutional trajectories, with later reforms or market pressures amplifying or reversing early advantages. These findings suggest that economic divergence was not static but a dynamic interplay between exogenous shocks and endogenous institutional adaptation, where demand for specific labor skills shaped institutional rigidity or flexibility.

The rise of trade rivalry in catch-up economies reshapes institutional priorities beyond mere resource allocation, demonstrating how competitive social pressures directly shape long-term growth trajectories through endogenous adaptation. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Austin (2007, p.2) jointly show that institutional evolution during periods of economic strain does not occur in isolation but is driven by prioritization of trade and labor mobility as strategic imperatives. Abramovitz’s analysis of institutional wild cards reveals how shifts in social capability, such as the adoption of wage labor and land markets, reflect not just technological opportunity but active competition for comparative advantage. These arrangements become self-reinforcing as they reduce transaction costs for labor migration, incentivizing both elite and non-elite actors to institutionalize mobility systems, as Austin (2007, p.2) documents in Ghana’s cocoa boom. The institutional legacies of trade rivalry thus emerge as a *structural condition* for growth, where the persistence of wage labor and land markets in Sub-Saharan Africa created a feedback loop: labor scarcity reduced land as a binding constraint, while new markets for labor capital enabled vertical specialization that sustained productivity gains. These mechanisms highlight how trade rivalry does not merely reflect existing institutions but actively *forces* institutional adaptation to meet shifting demand structures, ensuring that catch-up growth is not just a function of productivity but of how societies organize trade and labor in response to rivalry.

The evidence that technological innovation and empirical empiricism have shaped economic growth trajectories more directly than institutions alone challenges the conventional emphasis on institutional design as the primary driver of long-term divergence. Mokyr’s argument (Mokyr 2005, p.41; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) demonstrates that the Industrial Enlightenment’s breakthroughs in industrialization and scientific progress bypassed formal institutional structures, instead revealing how material and cognitive breakthroughs created their own self-reinforcing momentum. These innovations, rooted in Baconian empiricism, generated feedback loops that transcended institutional fragility, where success depended less on rigid governance than on the adaptability of knowledge diffusion and industrial experimentation. The paper’s critique of Enlightenment optimism highlights how institutional barriers could slow progress, but the core engine remained the *practical application of new techniques*, which often emerged in contexts where formal constraints were less restrictive.

Akyeampong and Fofack’s analysis (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19) further refines this by illustrating how gendered exclusion in Africa’s precolonial and colonial systems undermined broader institutional complementarities. Their work shows that women’s contributions, whether in trade networks or agricultural capitalism, were systematically erased by colonial policies, which codified patriarchal structures to exclude them from long-term economic participation. This undermined not just individual contributions but the *structural conditions* needed for sustained innovation, revealing that institutional design could only fulfill its promise when aligned with the active participation of those with the most creative and productive capacities. The authors’ framing underscores that economic growth in these contexts often depended on the *empirical and social networks* women built, which were later marginalized by formal institutional arrangements.

Both sources converge in their emphasis on how institutional legacies and technological innovation interact in ways that prioritize empirical pragmatism over theoretical rigidity. Mokyr’s findings suggest that the most durable growth trajectories emerged from environments where institutions *complemented* rather than constrained industrial and scientific progress, while Akyeampong and Fofack’s research exposes how gendered exclusion created deadweight in structural conditions that could have otherwise fostered innovation. The downstream implication: the most enduring economic trajectories may not stem from the most "stable" institutions, but from those that *nurtured the conditions for innovation*, whether through open labor markets, inclusive social networks, or the diffusion of practical knowledge. This tension between institutional design and empirical progress demands a reconsideration of how long-term growth is measured, with particular attention to the roles of cognitive and social capital.

The most enduring mechanisms through which institutions shape long-term economic growth stem from their capacity to structure commitment and interaction across time, as North and Weingast (1989, p.28) demonstrate through their analysis of political binding. Their work highlights how institutional frameworks that enforce secure property rights and decentralized veto points foster sustained commitment in public-choice systems, directly linking these structural features to economic evolution. Shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.28; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscores that growth trajectories depend less on isolated rules than on the *interdependency of institutional frameworks*, such as courts, markets, and governance, that enable collective action over generations.

Ogilvie’s (2007, p.27) framework complements this by emphasizing how cultural and social capital act as *structural conditions* that embed institutions in broader systems. Putnam’s (2007) cultural capital thesis, cited by Ogilvie, reveals that civic norms, such as trust and reciprocity, shape the efficiency of guilds and communal networks, which in turn sustain capital accumulation. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show how colonial institutions created extractive structures in former prosperous regions, illustrating how *institutional reversal* both erodes and reshapes growth trajectories through deliberate design. These findings converge on the idea that institutions thrive when they align with *cognitive and social capital*, reinforcing their long-term relevance.

Austin’s (2008, p.24) critique of the "reversal of fortune" thesis refines this by exposing how path dependence and differentiated interactions complicate causal narratives. Austin argues that institutional effects are not uniform but *context-dependent*, particularly across regions with distinct historical legacies. While North and Weingast (1989, p.28) stress the primacy of political stability in enforcing growth-enabling institutions, Pamuk’s (2007, p.2) analysis of the Black Death’s aftermath demonstrates that exogenous shocks can *accelerate institutional adaptability*, in northwestern Europe, flexible structures enabled a transition to capital-intensive industries, whereas rigidity in southern Europe stalled divergence. Shared finding (Pamuk 2007, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.23) highlights that long-term divergence often follows from how institutions *mediate between shocks and structural flexibility*, not just their initial design.

The synthesis of these streams reveals that institutions’ influence on growth trajectories hinges on two interdependent dimensions: the *enforcement of commitment* (as North and Weingast’s (1989, p.28) (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) demonstrate) and the *cultural and structural interdependencies* that Ogilvie’s (2007, p.27) work illustrates. The empirical contrast between colonial extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al.’s (2002, p.48) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34)) and the adaptive resilience of post-plague Europe (Pamuk’s (2007, p.2) (Pamuk 2007, p.23)) settles this tension: the most enduring growth trajectories emerge when institutions are not rigidly extractive but *designed to facilitate collective action and adapt to exogenous pressures*, thereby embedding both enforcement and cultural alignment in sustained economic progress.
