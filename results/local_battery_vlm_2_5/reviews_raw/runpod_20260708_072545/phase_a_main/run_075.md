The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who emphasize the primacy of institutions in fostering economic growth and those who posit alternative causes, such as geographic factors or cultural beliefs. Institutions are understood to encompass the rules, norms, and organizations that structure political and economic interactions, and their impact on growth is often mediated through their influence on property rights, contract enforcement, and the broader economic environment. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has real-world consequences for policy-making and development strategies. For instance, if institutions are the primary drivers of growth, then policies aimed at institutional reform could be pivotal in fostering economic development. If geographic or cultural factors play a more significant role, policy interventions might need to focus on addressing these underlying conditions.

The role of institutions in economic growth is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. and North. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita, illustrating how institutions can persist and shape long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth and development, highlighting how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was instrumental in creating a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic growth. The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges led to falling transaction costs, which were a growing proportion of GNP as specialization and division of labor increased. The evolution of these institutions involved the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. This political commitment to secure property rights was a critical factor in fostering economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic development, indicating that while financial institutions and secure property rights are crucial, their impact can vary based on broader economic and political contexts. This variability highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. These changes were driven by European strategies that varied based on mortality rates and resource extraction opportunities. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted over time, shaping long-run economic outcomes. The extractive nature of these institutions was a deliberate design to maximize rents for European colonists, not merely a byproduct of geographic or climatic factors. This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of private property rights and rule of law, inhibited long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to encourage economic development. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic performance in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The impact of inequality and slave trade on economic development highlights how specific historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This inequality, in turn, shaped institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The centralization of power and the development of markets and trading networks in regions like the Kongo kingdom facilitated trade with Europeans, illustrating how institutional frameworks evolved in response to historical contexts. Nunn (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the absence of centralized governance and well-developed institutions in other regions impeded economic development. The legacy of these historical institutional frameworks continues to influence economic performance in these regions, underscoring the enduring impact of inequality and slave trade on long-term economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the conventional narrative of African economic history by demonstrating that many regions achieved higher per capita incomes earlier than previously thought (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This reevaluation is grounded in the availability of extensive economic data for the colonial era, which has enabled a more nuanced understanding of economic performance in Africa. Broadberry and Gardner emphasize the tight linkage between economic growth patterns and foreign trade, highlighting how export production influenced economic trajectories across various regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This dynamic was shaped by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, illustrating the multifaceted nature of economic development in Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg further argue that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has obscured the true picture of economic development, necessitating a reconstruction of real wage levels to assess the persistence of poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach reveals that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, contra recent literature emphasizing such impediments. The historical economic performance in Africa also underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, better explain pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's research demonstrates that the persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the power dynamics and distributional conflicts among different social groups. For instance, the craft guilds and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe were mechanisms through which powerful groups maintained their control over economic activities, not merely efficient organizational forms. This aligns with Austin's observation that the paths of institutional evolution are deeply influenced by historical patterns and policy experiences, which often reflect underlying power structures and distributional conflicts (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). Austin further illustrates that colonial governments, in the absence of European settlers, often encouraged indigenous commodity production to maintain fiscal security and stability, highlighting the role of institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political actions. This suggests that the adoption of institutions favourable for economic growth is influenced by the need to manage distributional conflicts and maintain political stability, not solely determined by efficiency.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This idea is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who notes that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, indicating that technological progress is a global phenomenon that drives economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, they must be supported by institutional progress to ensure long-term sustainability. Institutional progress helps to reduce access costs and rectify coordination failures, ensuring that technological advancements are efficiently utilized. Therefore, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to reduce transaction costs and secure property rights. North demonstrates that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, which provided secure property rights and effective enforcement, is crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). Austin further emphasizes the importance of institutional evolution, highlighting that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood through patterns of institutional development and policy experience (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6) shows that institutions are pivotal in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the historical evidence of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. They show that colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, experienced lower long-run growth compared to those with inclusive institutions that encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence is a key factor in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where those with extractive institutions failed to industrialize and grow (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe also supports this view, emphasizing that institutions like serfdom and craft guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These findings collectively demonstrate that the nature of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, plays a decisive role in determining long-term economic outcomes.

Institutional progress is essential for the sustainability of economic growth, particularly when complemented by technological advancements. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework provided the necessary conditions for economic development by ensuring that property rights were enforced and that the regime was committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. further argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence from colonial institutions and the evolution of public choice institutions in England both underscore the importance of institutional progress in achieving sustained economic growth. The historical evidence from the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies and the development of capital markets in seventeenth-century England shows that institutional progress is essential for long-term economic sustainability.
