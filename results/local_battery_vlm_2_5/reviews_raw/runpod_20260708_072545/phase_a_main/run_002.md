The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central question in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Proponents of the institutional perspective assert that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, is crucial for fostering economic growth. Other explanations emphasize geographic factors, such as climate and disease, or cultural beliefs, in shaping economic development. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of why some countries thrive while others remain mired in poverty. The stakes are high, as the resolution of this debate could guide efforts to promote economic development and reduce global inequality. Whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, or if other factors such as geography or culture play a more significant role, remains an open question that requires careful examination of the evidence. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that this institutional evolution was critical for economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling transaction costs. This institutional framework supported economic growth by fostering market exchanges and increasing specialization. North (1989, p.6) observes that as the economy grew, the transactions sector became a larger proportion of GNP, driven by increased specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the success of these institutions relied on the regime's commitment to these rules, underscoring the importance of credible commitment in sustaining economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping economic institutions, challenging efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is shared by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The mechanism through which distributional conflicts shape institutions involves the political power of elites, who can influence institutional design to maintain their status and resources. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe, where elites used their political power to maintain institutions that benefited them at the expense of economic efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes that while distributional conflicts are important, they do not function independently. Other factors, such as cultural beliefs and technological changes, also play a role in shaping institutions. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need for a multifaceted approach to understanding economic development. The interplay between distributional conflicts and other factors highlights the importance of considering multiple dimensions when analyzing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate. Colonization strategies varied widely depending on European mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and rule of law, persisted long after the colonial period, shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that in regions where Europeans settled and established law-enforcing institutions, economic growth was more robust. In areas where extractive institutions were imposed, economic stagnation ensued. This pattern underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that these institutions were not merely a result of geographic factors but were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers. The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which they were established. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further illustrate that these institutional differences are robust across various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These findings suggest that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic performance in former colonies, even after controlling for other factors.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) share the finding that African economic development is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including institutional quality and historical legacies, extending the analysis of colonial legacies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. This perspective aligns with the broader literature on the role of institutions in economic growth, emphasizing that institutions shape long-run economic outcomes by influencing investment decisions and economic incentives. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also notes that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the provision of public goods or the development of progressive institutions. This nuance is significant because it suggests that the relationship between ethnic diversity and institutional quality is not straightforward. This perspective challenges the notion that African poverty is an inevitable outcome of historical conditions, instead suggesting that a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic development is necessary to address poverty effectively.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. These adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations and the resolution of growth-induced conflicts, which in turn facilitate sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This perspective suggests that the development of useful knowledge and technology is a fundamental driver of economic growth, not merely a byproduct of institutional changes. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also recognizes that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the interconnected nature of technological and institutional developments in driving long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade induced a culture of mistrust within Africa, a legacy that endures today and significantly impacts contemporary trust levels (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is a deeply ingrained social norm that persists across generations, not merely a byproduct of historical exploitation. Austin supports this view, highlighting that the economic and political actions of colonial governments often reinforced these mistrustful attitudes, thereby perpetuating a cycle of low trust and underdevelopment (Austin 2008, p.15). The mechanism through which the slave trade's legacy of mistrust affects economic development can be traced to the erosion of social capital, which is crucial for fostering cooperation and collective action. This erosion of social capital, in turn, hampers the development of institutions that support economic growth. Austin argues that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This observation suggests that while the legacy of mistrust is profound, it is not insurmountable. Colonial institutions, despite their exploitative origins, could adapt to local conditions, offering a glimmer of hope for institutional reform and economic development.
