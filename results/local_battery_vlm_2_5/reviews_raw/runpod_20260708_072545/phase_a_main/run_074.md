The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high because understanding this role can inform policy interventions aimed at fostering economic development. The debate centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One key position emphasizes that institutions determine the incentives for investment and economic growth by establishing property rights and enforcing contracts. Another position argues that institutions are shaped by deeper historical and cultural factors, which in turn influence economic development. A third position posits that the specific nature of colonial institutions, shaped by the interaction between settlers and indigenous populations, has had a lasting impact on economic development. These positions converge on the recognition that institutions matter, but they diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. The crux of the debate lies in understanding whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether their effects are direct or mediated through other factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of political institutions in 17th-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This period saw the creation of the Bank of England and the advent of new financial instruments, which dramatically lowered the cost of transacting (North 1989, p.9). These institutional developments reflected an increased security of property rights, which is crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Moreover, the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as those that specified and enforced property rights, played a pivotal role in this process (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should be evident in the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GDP (North 1989, p.6).

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were shaped by efficiency concerns. This mechanism of distributional conflicts shaping institutions is further supported by the observation that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was reinforced by institutions that protected elite interests, such as those governing land distribution and labor markets (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The role of institutions in perpetuating inequality is nuanced by the recognition that the specific historical context and the nature of distributional conflicts can vary significantly across different societies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the long-run economic growth of former colonies was significantly influenced by the institutions established during the colonial period. These institutions were shaped by European policies that varied based on mortality rates and the settlers' intentions. For instance, in regions with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in regions with low mortality rates, settlers implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional frameworks had persistent effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) underscore the robustness of these findings by demonstrating consistent results across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, and the efficiency of the judiciary. The specific historical context and the nature of distributional conflicts can vary significantly across different societies, influencing the extent to which these institutions perpetuate inequality. This perspective highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic development, where the interests of politically powerful groups can shape institutions in ways that do not necessarily foster growth. The implications of these findings extend beyond the colonial era, suggesting that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence economic trajectories in former colonies.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective is aligned with Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The authors suggest that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This focus on real wages and their determinants underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that support economic development. The colonial legacy in Africa, including the institutional frameworks established during this period, has significantly influenced the provision of public goods and the overall economic trajectory of the continent. For example, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, while ethnically heterogeneous societies can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. This highlights the complex interplay between ethnic diversity and institutional effectiveness. The extent to which these historical conditions have cast a temporary or structural effect on long-term African welfare development is debated. This perspective suggests that colonial institutions may have had significant impacts, their effects contingent on the specific historical and geographical conditions of each region. The institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence economic trajectories in former colonies, shaping the provision of public goods and the overall economic development of the continent.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49). This progression involves both the accumulation of knowledge and the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate its efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights the necessity of these adjustments, noting that technological advancements require significant changes in societal structures and ideologies to be effectively integrated into the economy (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the development of institutions that support technological progress, is crucial for long-term economic sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). He recognises the potential for coordination failures and suggests that policies to rectify them are essential for maintaining economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also acknowledges the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of a strong political fabric in supporting economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological progress and institutional development suggests that while institutions are important, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth; rather, they work in tandem with technological advancements to drive long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects economic outcomes, offering an alternative explanation to institutional theories of long-run economic growth. The slave trade's impact on trust is deeply rooted in the internal norms of mistrust that developed over centuries. This cultural mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. The external environment, such as the rule of law and trustworthiness of others, also plays a role, but the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that the institutional prescriptions evolved in response to local dynamics. This adaptability suggests that institutions are important, they interact with cultural and historical factors. Therefore, the legacy of the slave trade, manifested in a culture of mistrust, continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa, complicating the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) report, “Moreover, if we compare the point estimates to estimates using the same specification and sample, but not including the interethnic slave exports control (which are −0.104, −0.126 and −0.107, respectively), we find that the point estimates are barely affected by the inclusion of the control.”

Institutions matter for long-run economic growth. This central idea is shared across the literature. North argues that institutions are critical for economic growth, noting that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges and the creation of secure property rights are essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. similarly emphasize that institutions shape economic outcomes, showing that European colonies with extractive institutions experienced lower long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The specific role of institutions varies across historical contexts. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can be influenced by various historical and cultural factors. For instance, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups rather than purely efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. also show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The legacy of the slave trade, manifested in a culture of mistrust, continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa, complicating the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth. This is especially true when considering the impact of extractive institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. While institutions are critical for economic growth, their impact is shaped by historical and cultural factors, and their design is influenced by the interests of powerful groups. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies, which shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
