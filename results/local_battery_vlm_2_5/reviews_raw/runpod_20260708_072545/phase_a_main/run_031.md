The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At stake is the very nature of economic progress: whether it is driven by institutional frameworks that foster growth, or whether other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements are more determinant. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are the primary drivers of economic growth. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures are crucial for sustained economic development. Others posit that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that factors such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs play a more significant role. Additionally, there are rival explanations that emphasize the importance of distributional conflicts and the efficiency of pre-industrial economic institutions. The core of the debate lies in whether institutions are the linchpin of economic growth or merely one piece of a complex puzzle. The institutions that are most effective in promoting growth are those that provide secure property rights and enforceable contracts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast argue that the development of financial institutions and instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the advent of new financial tools were pivotal in this process, as they enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution involved establishing rules for economic exchange and creating mechanisms to enforce and adapt these rules. North and Weingast point out that the commitment of the regime to these rules was crucial for economic growth, a point that resonates with the broader argument that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which reflect the broader economic health of a nation. However, North (1989, p.9) notes that while the reduction in transaction costs is beneficial, the aggregate volume of transactions will increase as specialization and division of labor grow, leading to a larger transactions sector relative to GDP (North 1989, p.6). This implies that while financial institutions are critical, their benefits must be balanced against the increasing complexity they introduce into the economy.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The type of institutions imposed by colonial powers was strongly influenced by the mortality rates they faced. The mortality rate was a crucial factor in determining whether European colonizers would adopt extractive institutions. In regions where mortality rates were high, Europeans established extractive institutions to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development. Where mortality rates were low, colonizers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the colonial institutions persisted long after the colonial period ended. This institutional legacy has had a profound impact on the economic development of former colonies. The persistence of these institutions has created a self-reinforcing cycle where extractive institutions hinder economic growth, perpetuating poverty and underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) substantiate this by demonstrating that the quality of institutions, as measured by various indices, correlates strongly with economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) caution that the relationship between institutions and economic development is complex. Politically powerful groups may resist institutional changes that threaten their rents or political power, further complicating the path to economic growth. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, shaping the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. They highlight the need for a deeper understanding of historical economic paths beyond structural impediments. Their research underscores that the real wage divergence across British Africa was influenced by the expansion of indigenous commodity production, which was often in the interests of colonial governments where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This finding suggests that colonial institutions responded to local economic and political dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing a more nuanced picture of economic development in Africa. This approach reveals that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, as recent literature often emphasizes. Austin (2008, p.2) further supports this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Instead, it is the result of complex interactions between local economic dynamics and colonial institutions. This perspective challenges the notion that African poverty is an inevitable outcome of colonial rule and suggests that a more granular understanding of historical economic paths is necessary to fully grasp the determinants of long-run economic growth in Africa. This nuanced view of African economic history highlights the importance of local agency and the adaptability of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) identify the slave trade as a pivotal factor in shaping African economic development. The slave trade's impact on trust is not solely due to the external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, but also through individuals' internal norms of mistrust. This internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. Nunn (2008, p.19) highlights the importance of centralized governance and well-developed markets in facilitating trade, suggesting that the disruption caused by the slave trade hindered the development of such institutions. This disruption likely exacerbated the mistrust fostered by the slave trade, creating a feedback loop that impeded economic growth. This observation suggests that the presence of strong institutions could mitigate the negative effects of the slave trade on economic development.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate that in New World societies, institutional structures were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This distributional conflict mechanism is evident in the persistence of institutions that favored the elite, such as those that restricted land ownership and political participation to a select few. The implications of this mechanism are profound, as it suggests that institutions designed to maintain elite power can stifle economic growth by limiting competition and innovation.

Technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are pivotal for sustained economic development, as the growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This suggests that the institutional framework must evolve to accommodate technological changes, ensuring that societal conflicts arising from these changes are resolved without excessive costs. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that the interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations is crucial for long-term economic growth. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary to sustain this growth are more complex and subject to reversals.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they establish the framework within which economic activities occur. The creation of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is critical for economic growth. North argues that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who highlight the importance of political institutions that ensure secure property rights and enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of such institutions in seventeenth-century England, for instance, was crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The Bank of England's establishment and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Acemoglu et al. further elucidate that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and can be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is evident in the colonial context, where European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between technological advancements and institutional adaptations is crucial for long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The growth of economic reasonableness, necessary for sustainable economic improvement, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary to sustain this growth are more complex and subject to reversals (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He notes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, changing their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutional changes can be driven by both external and internal factors, and their impact on economic growth can vary depending on the specific context. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, and the need for further research in this area.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between the necessity of technological progress and the complexity of institutional adaptations is the development of the Bank of England and the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). This development shows that institutions that enforce secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and promote economic growth. The interaction between technological advancements and institutional adaptations is crucial for long-term economic growth, and the specific context in which these interactions occur can significantly impact their outcomes.
