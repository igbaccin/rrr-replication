The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with a live disagreement between those who emphasize the primacy of institutions and those who argue for alternative drivers. The institutional view holds that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, is crucial for economic growth. The competing view posits that other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Institutions are often seen as endogenous to political and economic structures, shaped by power dynamics and historical contingencies. The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms can inform policy interventions aimed at fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies. These institutions, in turn, influenced long-run economic development. European settlers in regions with low mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. The persistence of these institutions has had a lasting impact on current income per capita. This perspective underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

These institutional developments in England, which reduced transaction costs, were pivotal in setting the stage for long-run economic growth, as highlighted by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in this regard, as they enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was driven by political factors that ensured the regime's commitment to these rules, which in turn fostered a secure environment for economic exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of political institutions in enforcing and adapting economic rules, which underpinned the success of these financial innovations. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on how the development of political institutions specifying property rights led to a reduction in transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This indicates that although institutional developments were crucial, their impact was part of a broader economic context that included other factors influencing growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) find that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This was achieved through the implementation of policies that favored the elite class, such as those that restricted access to education and political participation for the lower classes. These policies created a self-reinforcing cycle where the elite maintained their power and wealth, while the lower classes remained disadvantaged. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that this institutional framework fostered a culture of mistrust, which further hindered economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust stemmed from the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others, but it was also deeply ingrained in the social norms of the society. This institutional framework and the resulting mistrust created a barrier to economic growth, as it limited the ability of individuals to cooperate and engage in productive economic activities. However, this mistrust was not solely a result of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others, but was deeply ingrained in the social norms of the society. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the long-term effects of the slave trade on Africa's economic development were profound, as it led to the establishment of institutions that perpetuated inequality and hindered economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). This long-term impact resulted from the institutional framework that was established in the wake of the slave trade.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The establishment of these institutions was driven by the settlers' desire to efficiently extract resources and transfer them to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This extractive approach led to the creation of institutions that did not encourage investment or economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) observe that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is thus complex, shaped by both the extractive nature of the institutions and the historical context in which they were established.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He contends that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. He acknowledges that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, irrespective of ethnic diversity. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the role of patriarchal alliances and customary law in codifying the subordinate status of women during the colonial era. They note that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system, which reinforced gender inequalities. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men in access to land and resources, had profound implications for economic development. This dynamic illustrates how colonial institutions, by shaping land access and resource allocation, influenced economic activities and outcomes. Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied depending on the specific policies and practices of different colonial powers. The variation in institutional impacts across different colonial contexts underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue. This perspective highlights technological advancements as crucial for sustained economic development. He suggests that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress. Without such institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that modern technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. He observes that technological progress, economic growth, and the institutional adjustments they necessitate have varied over centuries and regions. Kuznets (1973, p.1) identifies epochal innovations as dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, influencing the pace and sectors affected by technological changes and economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the modern national state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby enabling modern economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) suggests that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This implies that while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, the institutional framework supporting these advancements must be robust and adaptable to ensure long-term sustainability.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting how distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This approach struggles to explain why some countries adopted growth-favorable institutions while others did not. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that exogenous cultural theories can be applied to understand the motivations behind these institutions. This view is complicated by the fact that institutions are not always efficient, and that distributional conflicts can lead to institutions that are not optimal for economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing the critical role of secure property rights and effective enforcement in facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North's work underlines that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the impact of colonial policies on the development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional diversity had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies, with those that received more inclusive institutions achieving higher levels of economic development. Austin challenges the over-simplification of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that the paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience are complex and multifaceted (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's work shows that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more dynamic interaction between institutions and economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts also shape the institutions that govern economic activity. Ogilvie argues that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure of a society, influencing the motivations behind core economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The distributional conflicts underlying these institutions highlight the importance of understanding the political dynamics that shape economic outcomes. This is particularly relevant in the context of colonialism, where Acemoglu et al. show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further supported by the new economic history of Africa, which emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins argues that the experience of East Asia might be more relevant to Africa than that of the United States, indicating the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional reform in different contexts. The specific historical evidence of the English financial revolution shows the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. and Austin by demonstrating that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2).
