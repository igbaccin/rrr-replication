The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies, as it addresses the extent to which institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic progress over extended periods. Institutions are understood as the rules of the game in a society, encompassing formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights) and informal constraints (norms, conventions, and self-imposed codes of conduct) (North 1989, p.6). The live disagreement centres on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. One position argues that institutions matter because they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity, thereby directly influencing growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by scholars who emphasize the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Another position contends that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated through other factors, such as population density and prosperity, which in turn influence the policies pursued by colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This view suggests that institutions interact with other variables in complex ways to determine economic growth. The interplay between these positions highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within broader economic and social contexts.

In particular, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This security is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. This institutional development led to falling costs per transaction, which in turn increased the size of the transactions sector as a growing proportion of GNP. Evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this. The institutional changes in England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, leading to an overhaul of traditional interpretations of British as well as French economic history. If England and France were almost at parity in economic performance, the clear implication is that institutions per se and in particular, the institutional changes we have described were not so revolutionary after all. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and influenced by other factors, including broader economic and social contexts.

Building on this, colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, a finding shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). These scholars argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other instances, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further assert that the type of institutions introduced during colonial rule persisted, shaping current income per capita levels. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

The mechanism through which these institutions influenced economic growth is multifaceted. This extractive nature of institutions meant that they did not encourage economic development but rather maintained a system that benefited the colonizers at the expense of the colonized. The downstream implication of this is that the economic policies implemented during colonial rule had lasting effects on the economic structures of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that the institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who had different incentives, including maintaining their political power or avoiding the costs of institutional change. The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied based on the specific context and incentives of the colonizers. This nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping economic growth underscores the complexity of the relationship between colonial rule and long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share a view that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were shaped by the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain their privileges. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this perspective to the New World, where the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutions that advantaged elites. These institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This perspective highlights the role of power dynamics in shaping institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions are also influenced by shared beliefs and cultural values. These shared beliefs can reinforce institutions that serve to maintain social order and political stability, even if they are not necessarily efficient. This nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping economic growth underscores the complexity of the relationship between distributional conflicts and long-run economic development.

Frankema and Austin highlight that the impact of British colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa is distinct from the broader discussion on institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. The paper by Frankema (2012, p.15) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This is because the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic and political opportunities (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). These dynamics were influenced by the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, which in turn shaped the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations (Austin 2008, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while British colonial rule had a positive impact on education, the broader economic outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of factors.

The profound and enduring impact of the slave trades in Africa on the continent's economic development challenges views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as trust is crucial for various economic activities, including trade and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This extended period of exploitation disrupted social and economic institutions, hindering Africa's ability to develop robust trading networks and centralized governance structures essential for economic growth. The impact of the slave trade on economic development is not uniform across Africa. Nunn (2008, p.2) points out that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with Europeans, suggesting that pre-existing institutional strength could mitigate some of the adverse effects of the slave trade. This highlights the complex interplay between historical events and institutional development in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr contends that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) echoes this by noting that technological progress, driven by knowledge, is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This view suggests that the development of new technologies and the diffusion of knowledge are crucial for economic growth. Kuznets also highlights that the efficient use of these innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This implies that while knowledge accumulation is essential, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. Mokyr elaborates on this point by recognizing the possibility of coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating the effective use of technological advancements. Moreover, Kuznets emphasizes that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, highlighting the role of institutions in managing societal tensions. This suggests that institutions play a critical role in creating an environment conducive to technological progress and economic growth.

North and Weingast highlight the critical role of institutions in economic growth by demonstrating how the evolution of public choice institutions in 17th-century England fostered secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment was pivotal in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore that institutions play a crucial role in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic growth. The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin, however, contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the impact of institutions on economic growth is more complex and context-dependent (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate is settled by the evidence from capital markets, which shows that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary and critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
