The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is an understanding of why some societies thrive while others stagnate. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, and the live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and secure contracts are crucial for economic growth, while others contend that the efficiency of institutions is less important than the distributional conflicts they address. Additionally, there is a debate on whether the reversal of fortune among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or other factors. The question of how institutions influence long-run economic growth is thus complex and multifaceted, with scholars offering varying perspectives on the mechanisms and importance of institutional factors. The debate is further complicated by the interaction between institutions and other variables, such as population density and prosperity, which can influence the policies pursued by colonizers and the resulting economic outcomes. The causal pathways through which institutions shape economic growth are therefore not straightforward and require careful examination. Institutions are not just passive reflections of economic conditions but active shapers of economic outcomes, and understanding this dynamic is crucial for grasping the long-run economic growth of societies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The institutional transformations in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9), were part of a broader pattern of institutional evolution that shaped economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. North notes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement resulted in falling costs per transaction. This, in turn, facilitated market exchanges and increased the aggregate volume of exchanges, which is a growing proportion of GNP as specialization and division of labor multiply. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the regime's commitment to these rules is a critical political factor underpinning economic growth and the development of markets. The success of these institutional changes is evident in the evidence from capital markets, where recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution. This suggests that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary and crucial for economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping the institutional frameworks that either fostered or hindered development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on local mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. In contrast, where Europeans settled in large numbers, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the quality of institutions, measured through various indices such as constraints on the executive and property rights, significantly correlates with current income per capita. This finding underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is linked to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This qualification highlights that the beneficial effects of institutional changes depend on the specific design and objectives of the institutions implemented. The long-term economic performance of former colonies thus hinges on the nature of the institutions established during colonial rule, which in turn were influenced by the strategic and economic interests of the colonizing powers.

In pre-industrial Europe, the establishment of core economic institutions was driven by distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue. These institutions were not merely products of efficiency-driven processes but were deeply rooted in the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need to integrate institutions into economic history research, suggesting that they are indispensable instruments for understanding long-term economic development. This perspective is reinforced by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who highlight that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to the development of institutions that favored elites. The focus on distributional conflicts provides a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolved to serve the interests of powerful groups, rather than promoting efficient economic outcomes. However, the persistence of these institutions over time and their impact on long-term economic development are also influenced by other factors, such as the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption. These factors can either reinforce or undermine the distributional effects of institutions, shaping the economic trajectory of societies in complex ways.

This resilience is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner who argue that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies in the early twentieth century was more robust than previously thought, with per capita income levels above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This perspective is echoed by Broadberry and Gupta in their analysis of pre-1800 economic conditions, which shows that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). In this context, economic growth was significantly influenced by the service sector, highlighting the role of institutional changes in fostering investment and productivity. This pattern of economic development was not uniform across regions. For instance, the timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The impact of these institutions on economic growth was complex. The uneven distribution of economic benefits meant that while skilled laborers saw significant surpluses over basic food needs, unskilled laborers did not experience the same level of improvement (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This variation in economic outcomes underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, where the benefits of institutional changes were not evenly distributed across different segments of society.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades, rather than institutions, were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al. by emphasizing the enduring impact of historical slave trades on economic development. Nunn and Frankema and van Waijenburg both highlight the significant temporal span of the slave trades, which lasted nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted African societies, undermining economic development and institutional stability. Nunn notes that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with Europeans, suggesting that pre-colonial institutions could facilitate trade under certain conditions. However, Austin points out that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic development was not uniform. This adaptive behavior suggests that colonial institutions could be influenced by local conditions, complicating the narrative of institutional determinism.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, with technological and intellectual advancements being more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The institutional adjustments that Kuznets (1973, p.1) refers to include resolving conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies, which can be costly and disruptive. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, institutional frameworks play a supporting role in sustaining this growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, emphasizing the importance of a strong political fabric in maintaining economic growth. The implication is that while technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, institutional frameworks are necessary for sustaining this growth.

The long-run economic growth is significantly influenced by the institutional frameworks that govern societies, as these frameworks shape the incentives and constraints that drive economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent economic underdevelopment, highlighting the critical role of institutions in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast elaborate on this by showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of colonial policies on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by Austin, who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). The shared finding is that colonial institutions, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, have had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2).

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement regarding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the comparative analysis of former European colonies. This analysis shows that colonies with extractive institutions, such as those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, experienced persistent economic underdevelopment, while those with growth-enhancing institutions, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, achieved significant economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence demonstrates that the type of institutions established during the colonial period had a profound and lasting impact on the economic trajectories of these regions.
