The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over time. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement: some scholars assert that the quality of institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce contracts, is crucial for sustained economic growth. Others contend that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables, such as geography or culture, may play equally or more significant roles. Still others propose that distributional conflicts and the resulting institutional arrangements are more critical than efficiency considerations. The interplay between these positions underscores the complexity of economic development, where the causal mechanisms driving growth are multifaceted and often intertwined. The debate surrounding institutions and growth is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic reform. The stakes are high, as understanding the true drivers of economic growth can inform strategies to lift nations out of poverty and foster sustained development. The question of whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, or if they operate in conjunction with other factors, is central to this discussion. Institutions are not static entities; they evolve in response to economic, political, and social pressures. This evolution can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on the nature of the institutional changes and the broader context in which they occur. A key point of contention is whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and how they interact with other factors such as geography, culture, and distributional conflicts. The resolution of these questions is essential for developing a comprehensive understanding of economic growth and for designing effective policies to promote it. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution was pivotal in creating a long-term capital market essential for economic growth. North and Weingast elaborate on this by highlighting the political factors underpinning economic growth, emphasizing the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange. The emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial. North further explains that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges results in falling costs per transaction, with the transactions sector growing as a proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast point out that the effectiveness of these institutions relies on the regime's commitment to these rules, implying that credible commitment supports growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underscores the importance of political stability and institutional commitment in sustaining long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts, driven by the interests of powerful groups, shaped the formation of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, with Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman arguing that institutional development was influenced by these interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were shaped by efficiency concerns. Sokoloff and Engerman, meanwhile, highlight that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is the use of government policies to maintain elite control. This mechanism is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where guilds and other institutions were structured to protect the interests of powerful groups. The downstream implications of this institutional development are qualified by the fact that these institutions could also create barriers to long-term economic growth. For instance, while guilds protected the interests of craftsmen, they also restricted competition and innovation, potentially hindering economic development. This suggests that while distributional conflicts were a driving force behind institutional development, the resulting institutions could have complex and sometimes negative effects on economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run economic growth. This pattern is evident in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were set up to exploit resources and not foster development. The resulting institutions were extractive, focusing on short-term gains and not long-term economic development. The impact of these institutions varied. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this reversal to industrialization opportunities and the interaction between institutions and economic development during the nineteenth century. This suggests that while extractive institutions hindered growth in many colonies, the specific historical and economic contexts also played a crucial role in determining the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic development. These findings underscore the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional changes can shape long-run economic outcomes.

Institutional reforms are proposed as a means to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, but this cluster does not directly engage with the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These institutions are crucial for fostering an environment where investment can flourish, which is essential for long-term economic growth. This understanding is vital for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. This suggests that while institutional reforms are important, the specific historical and economic contexts also play a crucial role in determining the long-term effects of these reforms on economic development. This nuanced understanding of institutional changes and their impact on long-run economic outcomes is essential for designing effective policies to reduce poverty in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores the importance of technological advancements in driving sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on the institutional progress that complements technological advancements, suggesting that without such progress, economic improvements would be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) similarly notes that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by appropriate institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also warns that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This highlights the importance of effective institutions in managing the social and political implications of technological progress. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is thus essential for sustained economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa generated a lasting culture of mistrust that continues to affect trust levels and long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal mistrust hinders collective action and cooperation, which are essential for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These paths, influenced by the slave trade, have led to different institutional outcomes, which in turn affect economic growth. The institutional legacies of the slave trade, such as weak property rights and high levels of corruption, further exacerbate the mistrust and hinder economic development. The enduring mistrust and weak institutions create a vicious cycle where low trust levels perpetuate underdevelopment, and underdevelopment reinforces mistrust. This cycle is particularly challenging to break, as it requires institutional reforms and a fundamental shift in social norms and attitudes towards trust.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and incentives that govern economic activity. The historical origins of institutions, particularly those introduced during colonialism, significantly influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional legacies persist and have a profound impact on current economic conditions. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments illustrate how secure property rights and efficient enforcement can drive economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between institutional frameworks and social norms. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) highlights that shared beliefs and cultural values can shape institutional structures, influencing economic performance. For example, the persistence of extractive institutions in some regions can be attributed to the entrenched beliefs and values that support these institutions, even when they are detrimental to long-run growth. This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of colonial legacies, where institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists have left a lasting impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize short-term gains and not promote long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal mechanisms by compressing different historical periods and paths, thereby missing the nuanced ways in which institutions evolve over time.

Institutional reforms are crucial for breaking the cycle of underdevelopment and mistrust. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) emphasizes that good institutions, which provide incentives for investment and development, are essential for economic growth. The new economic history of Africa demonstrates how institutional reforms can inform policy and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. The historical evidence shows that institutions matter for economic growth, and the empirical comparison of former European colonies illustrates the long-run effects of institutional legacies. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) is that institutions, shaped by historical and cultural factors, fundamentally influence economic growth. The remaining disagreement is whether the specific historical context of colonialism or the broader institutional framework is more determinative. The precise historical evidence of the Bank of England's creation and the development of new financial instruments (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6) shows that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth, settling the disagreement.
