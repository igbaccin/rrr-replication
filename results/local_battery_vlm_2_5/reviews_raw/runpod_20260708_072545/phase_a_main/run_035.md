The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions influence the incentives and constraints that shape economic outcomes, as they are both the rules of the game and the players of the game. The debate focuses on the extent to which institutions drive economic growth, alongside other factors such as geography or culture. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic trajectories, arguing that institutions shape the incentive structures that govern economic behavior and investment. This perspective is often framed in terms of the effectiveness of property rights and the rule of law. The opposing view suggests that institutions are influenced by broader historical and cultural contexts, and are not the sole determinant of economic growth. These differing views underscore the complexity of economic development, where institutions interact with a multitude of factors to shape long-run growth. The disagreement lies in the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, and whether they are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one among many factors. The stakes involve understanding the mechanisms that underpin economic development, which has implications for policy and economic theory. A key question is whether institutions can be designed to promote growth, or if they are merely a reflection of deeper cultural and historical forces. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for the design of economic policies aimed at fostering growth and development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth involves both the rules of the game and the players who enforce and adapt them. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) share the claim that institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. This financial revolution reflected increased security in property rights, a crucial dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. They highlight that the success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, indicating that the institutional changes were revolutionary. This dynamic is driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their ability to adapt and enforce property rights efficiently.

This institutional legacy, shaped by European policies, continues to influence the economic trajectories of former colonies, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The type of colonial institutions established was determined by European mortality rates; high mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, and were not conducive to long-run growth. This dynamic is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were established. These institutions persist and have significantly affected current income per capita in these regions. The persistence of these institutions is due to their equilibrium nature, which means they are stable and resistant to change. This is because the politically powerful groups that benefit from these institutions have little incentive to change them, even if it would lead to long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors. This suggests that the long-run economic growth of former colonies is largely determined by the institutions established during colonialism.

This institutional framework, which favored elites and restricted competition, contributed to the persistence of inequality in New World societies, as argued by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This inequality was exacerbated by the fact that these societies were characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional inequality had profound implications for economic outcomes. By restricting competition, these institutions limited economic dynamism and innovation, thereby stifling long-run economic growth. The persistence of inequality and the lack of competition meant that these societies did not develop the institutions necessary for sustained economic growth. Ogilvie contends that this inequality was rooted in the shared beliefs of the society, which in turn shaped the institutions that governed economic activity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie also notes that institutions are an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, they are not the only factor at play. The interplay between institutions and other factors, such as shared beliefs and economic policies, is complex and multifaceted.

This accumulation of knowledge, as Mokyr argues in conjunction with Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49), is a fundamental driver of economic growth. Kuznets explains that technological progress is the dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, and that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This means that institutions are necessary for the effective use of technology. Mokyr also notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, they are not the sole determinant of economic growth. Kuznets also highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, indicating that institutions are necessary for maintaining social order and political stability during periods of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Hopkins, along with Frankema, assert that colonial rule in Africa significantly influenced institutional change, shaping both negotiated and imposed governance structures (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). These structures were central to how colonial powers, particularly the British, approached education in sub-Saharan Africa. British rule fostered a more conducive environment for formal education development compared to other colonial powers. This educational infrastructure arose from local demand and cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, as well as top-down policies. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, driving its demand (Frankema 2012, p.15). The effectiveness of these institutions in promoting long-run economic growth was not uniform. Hopkins notes that while institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development, the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that while colonial education policies laid the groundwork for institutional development, the long-run economic impact was mediated by complex local dynamics. Hopkins further emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa has gained prominence by highlighting the role of institutions in development, indicating that institutional reforms informed by this history can help address poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa led to a pervasive culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it erodes the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings suggest that the slave trade's impact on trust is deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms of mistrust, and is not merely a result of the external environment, such as the rule of law. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors, highlighting the profound and lasting effects of the slave trade on African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn's analysis further underscores the long-term economic consequences of the slave trade, arguing that Africa's underdevelopment cannot be attributed solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.19). The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are multifaceted, including the disruption of social institutions and the erosion of trust, which are crucial for economic growth and development. The legacy of the slave trade in Africa continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, influencing everything from international trade to political institutions and firm management practices.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) jointly argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of historical factors influencing African economic development. They propose that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, obscuring the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This mechanism underscores the importance of local agency and adaptation, highlighting that institutional evolution is not a linear process dictated solely by colonial legacies. This nuanced view challenges the notion that pre-colonial institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, suggesting instead that a deeper understanding of these interactions is necessary to assess the possibilities of future growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the framework for economic interactions and determine the distribution of resources. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas and inclusive institutions where Europeans settled (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence directly influenced current income per capita, highlighting the lasting impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Austin supports this view, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods, suggesting that long-term growth is influenced by the complex interplay of institutions over time (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin further shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions evolve in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15).

North emphasizes that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North's argument is that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating economic growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, shape core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie's argument complements North's by showing that institutions involve power dynamics and the interests of politically powerful groups. This shared finding (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) underscores the importance of institutions in economic development, as they determine the rules of the game that govern economic interactions.

Acemoglu et al. extend this perspective by arguing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional approach challenges geographic explanations for economic development, showing that institutions are the key factor in determining long-run economic growth. Hopkins builds on this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins shows that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.8) highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the framework for economic interactions and determine the distribution of resources.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement is whether these institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the case of colonial institutions, which were shaped by the interests of European colonists. This is shown by the fact that extractive institutions were established in high-mortality areas, where Europeans did not settle, while inclusive institutions were established in areas where Europeans did settle (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
