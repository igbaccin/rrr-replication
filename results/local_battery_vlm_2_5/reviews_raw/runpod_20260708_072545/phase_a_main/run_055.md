The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies achieve sustained economic progress. A key disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, highlighting how secure property rights and effective governance foster investment and innovation. Another position argues that institutions are shaped by deeper cultural or historical factors, which in turn influence economic performance. A third position focuses on the role of distributional conflicts and power dynamics in determining institutional outcomes. Understanding this debate is crucial for policating effective development strategies and for grasping the complex interplay between institutions, culture, and economic growth.

Institutional quality directly influences economic growth through mechanisms such as secure property rights and effective governance. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.9). These arguments highlight how institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment can lead to long-term economic growth. North and Weingast further illustrate this point by showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in driving economic development.

The creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North argues that the English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This development was critical for enhancing the security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast illustrate this through the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which fostered secure property rights and economic growth. The political factors underpinning economic growth, including the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange, were pivotal in this transformation. The Bank of England, for instance, facilitated market exchanges by providing a stable financial infrastructure, which in turn reduced transaction costs and encouraged investment. This institutional framework was crucial for long-term economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis of the role of credible commitments in economic growth further underscores this point, showing that the regime's commitment to enforce rules governing economic exchange was pivotal. Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. 2001 and 2002 establish that the type of institutions established by colonial powers varied based on their mortality rates and settlement patterns; this variation in institutions is pivotal to understanding long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. 2001 show that where mortality rates were high, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic growth; instead, they maximized short-term rents for the colonizers. Acemoglu et al. 2002 further elaborates that these extractive institutions persisted post-independence, locking former colonies into a trajectory of underdevelopment. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. 2002 also note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes, underscoring the deep and lasting effects of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies, with efficiency being a secondary factor. These institutions were not merely efficient arrangements but were structured to benefit specific groups within society, often at the expense of others. This pattern of institutional design, driven by distributional conflicts, had profound implications for economic development. The persistence of these institutions, which favored elites and restricted competition, contributed to the perpetuation of inequality and hindered economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that these institutions were not monolithic; they evolved over time in response to changing social and economic conditions. This evolution highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their ability to adapt to new challenges and opportunities, even as they continue to reflect the underlying distributional conflicts that shaped their initial formation.

Broadberry and Gardner challenge the conventional narrative of African economic history by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This cluster of scholarship, which includes Frankema and van Waijenburg, shows that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This data reveals that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule has further illuminated the economic development of the region, highlighting the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The role of institutions in shaping these economic outcomes remains somewhat obscure.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development can be attributed to the mistrust it fostered. This mistrust operates both through individuals' internal norms and their external environment, including the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The internal channel of mistrust, which involves individuals' norms and attitudes, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which involves the broader societal and institutional environment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This long duration highlights the deep-seated nature of the mistrust and its enduring impact on African economic development. However, it is important to note that the economic and social institutions of pre-colonial Africa varied widely, and some regions, such as the Kongo kingdom, had well-developed markets and trading networks that could support foreign trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, interacts with the pre-existing institutional frameworks, shaping the contemporary economic landscape in complex ways.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue. Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating this process by fostering an environment conducive to technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge. This is evident in the way institutions can address coordination failures and promote economic reasonableness, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) shows. Kuznets (1973, p.6) suggests that the institutional and ideological adjustments required for efficient use of innovations can be complex and may involve significant social and political challenges. These challenges can lead to conflicts that, if not resolved peacefully, can hinder modern economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth involves creating the right conditions for technological progress and managing the social and political implications of such progress. This highlights the importance of institutions in both promoting and sustaining economic growth through the accumulation of useful knowledge.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in their ability to facilitate the accumulation of useful knowledge. Institutions can significantly influence the trajectory of technological progress by creating an environment conducive to innovation and the efficient use of new technologies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that European colonies with extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth, experienced significant economic underdevelopment. These findings highlight the critical role of institutions in determining the economic outcomes of societies. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9) underscores the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and promoting economic development. This dynamic is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of institutions in enforcing property rights and promoting economic growth.

The role of institutions in managing social and political challenges is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Institutions play a pivotal role in resolving conflicts that arise from the social and political implications of technological progress, in addition to promoting technological progress. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. This highlights the importance of institutions in managing distributional conflicts and ensuring that economic growth benefits a broader segment of society. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) provides a complementary perspective, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) underscores the importance of institutions in managing social and political challenges and promoting sustainable economic growth.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which highlights the importance of institutions in promoting economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.
