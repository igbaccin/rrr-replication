The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographical conditions or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. One live disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, directly impacts economic growth. Others posit that institutions are merely one piece of a larger puzzle, with geographical factors, cultural beliefs, and colonial histories also playing crucial roles. The debate is further complicated by the question of how institutions evolve and whether they can be changed to foster economic growth. This question is pivotal because it determines whether policy interventions aimed at institutional reform can effectively drive economic development. The central disagreement revolves around the degree to which institutions are both necessary and sufficient for long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.9) identifies the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments as pivotal in reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This financial revolution reflects the increased security of property rights and the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. The English financial revolution, which was a product of fiscal crises, exemplifies how political institutions can specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, leading to falling costs per transaction. This dynamic is crucial because, as North (1989, p.9) points out, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by showing how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutions. In colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, economic growth was more robust. This institutional framework, which emphasized property rights and legal enforcement, created an environment conducive to investment and long-term economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the specific context and power dynamics that shape institutional outcomes.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that the development of credible commitment mechanisms, such as the Glorious Revolution, was instrumental in establishing a stable political environment that protected property rights and encouraged investment. This institutional framework ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could be changed only through established procedures, thereby reducing uncertainty and promoting long-term economic development. This institutional stability allowed for the emergence of markets and the development of a robust financial sector, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The persistence of inequality in New World societies shows the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the initial conditions of extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to the evolution of institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby affecting long-term economic development. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding how initial conditions and power dynamics shape institutional outcomes and economic growth.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) both show that economic development in Europe was marked by significant structural changes that shifted economic activity across regions. These changes were not uniform and did not always lead to improvements in living standards. The economic center of Europe shifted northward over time, from the Mediterranean to the North Sea region. This shift was accompanied by changes in the relative price levels of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries after 1740 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland enabled rapid responses to new economic opportunities, allowing for the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This institutional adaptability contrasts with the strengthening of existing monopolies in regions like Tuscany, which hindered economic growth. These structural changes and their varied impacts on different regions underscore the complex nature of economic development and the importance of institutional contexts in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

British colonial education policies played a pivotal role in shaping early twentieth-century economic performance in Anglophone African economies, with formal education systems fostering per capita income levels above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This outcome was partly driven by the demand for missionary education, which depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. These calculations were influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further note that the patterns of economic growth in these regions were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, highlighting the significance of political institutions in facilitating economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while colonial education policies contributed to economic growth, their effectiveness was contingent on specific regional conditions.

Colonial rule and institutional changes significantly impacted economic outcomes in former European colonies, often leading to a reversal of fortune, as observed by Austin and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This reversal was often driven by the presence of European settlers and the colonial governments' fiscal priorities, which influenced institutional prescriptions and economic action. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to adjust their institutional policies in response to local economic and political dynamics. This pattern is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations (Austin 2008, p.15). This adjustment underscores how colonial institutions could adapt to local conditions, shaping economic outcomes in complex ways. The new economic history of Africa demonstrates that the impact of institutions on economic development is multifaceted. Hopkins notes that while good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development, the relationship between institutions and economic outcomes is not straightforward. The presence of poor institutions does not always correlate with a dismal economic record, as societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view highlights that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent on various factors, including local conditions and the adaptability of institutional frameworks.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that distributional conflicts are the fundamental drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They contend that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by power struggles and distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie suggests that these institutions were a product of the struggle between different groups to extract resources from one another, rather than a result of shared beliefs or cultural values. Nunn and Wantchekon extend this idea to cultural impacts, showing that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as it affects both internal norms and external environments, including the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The implications of this mistrust are profound, as it can hinder economic growth by undermining the trust necessary for cooperative behavior and effective institutions. The external environment is affected by the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, but the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channel. This highlights the enduring impact of historical distributional conflicts on contemporary economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly assert. This perspective emphasizes that technological advancements, particularly those that constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, are the mainspring of economic growth. These epochal innovations, which include major breakthroughs in human knowledge, are not merely technical achievements but also require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.6) points out that modern economic growth is possible only if such growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs. This highlights that the institutional environment must adapt to accommodate technological progress, ensuring that the political fabric of society remains strong. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes, is crucial for the sustainability of economic improvement. Without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the benefits of economic improvement could be eroded by rent-seeking behavior. This underscores the importance of institutional adaptability in supporting long-run economic growth. This suggests that the institutional framework must evolve to support the integration of new technologies into the economy, ensuring that the benefits of technological progress are fully realized. Thus, the interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is essential for sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with their ability to adapt and evolve in response to technological advancements. Institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, as seen in the cases of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, have historically supported economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England, which reduced transaction costs and increased the security of property rights, played a crucial role in economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional adaptability is essential for integrating new technologies into the economy and ensuring that the benefits of technological progress are fully realized. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution. Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This suggests that the interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is essential for sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Institutional frameworks that support long-term economic growth are often shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize their own interests over broader economic development. This can lead to the establishment of extractive institutions that maximize rents for these groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics that shape institutional frameworks and their impact on economic growth. The reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies, show that it was related to industrialization, and provide evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Institutional adaptability is also crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring that the benefits of economic growth are widely shared. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving these conflicts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is evident in the case of colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which led to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutions that are responsive to the needs and interests of the population can support long-run economic growth by promoting stability and shared prosperity. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the economic development of both historical and contemporary societies. While there is agreement that institutions matter, the specific mechanisms by which they influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. For instance, the reversal of fortune thesis, which suggests that the economic fortunes of former European colonies were reversed due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, has been critiqued for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).
