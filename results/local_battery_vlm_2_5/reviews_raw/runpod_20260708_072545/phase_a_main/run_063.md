The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding why some regions thrive economically while others stagnate, and whether institutional frameworks can explain these disparities. The debate focuses on the role of institutions versus other factors like geography or culture in driving long-term economic performance. One position emphasises the primacy of institutions in economic development, arguing that secure property rights and effective governance are crucial for sustained growth. Another position contends that while institutions matter, their impact is mediated by other factors, such as the historical context and specific policies implemented. A third position suggests that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle that includes geography, culture, and historical factors. This disagreement highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to influence economic outcomes. The evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights is a critical factor in fostering economic growth, underscoring the importance of credible commitment and secure property rights in driving long-term economic development. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) share the finding that the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement. This was achieved through the development of new financial instruments that dramatically declined the cost of transacting. The evolution of these institutions was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This pattern underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic development, as the regime or sovereign's commitment to these rules is a critical political factor. However, North (1989, p.9) cautions that the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This implies that while institutions reduce transaction costs, the overall economic activity and complexity also increase, presenting new challenges for institutional adaptation.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. document that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, designed to maximize rents for the colonizers rather than promote long-term growth. This institutional framework persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions long after independence. The extractive nature of these institutions, which Acemoglu et al. argue were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, created a barrier to economic development. Acemoglu et al. note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial rule had a profound and lasting effect on the economic trajectories of these regions. However, Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even if they do not encourage economic development. This implies that the persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-term economic growth, as these institutions are not designed to maximize growth but rather to serve the interests of the politically powerful groups that established them.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be traced back to institutions that favored elites and restricted competition. This dynamic is evident in the policies implemented by colonial powers, which often prioritized the interests of the ruling class over broader economic development. The extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset created a framework where institutions evolved to maintain this disparity. This institutional framework, in turn, hindered long-term economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader segments of the population. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that the impact of colonial institutions on African economies was not uniform. The demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa, for instance, was influenced by the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities. This suggests that while colonial institutions may have been extractive in many cases, they also created pathways for economic development in specific contexts. The persistence of inequality in New World societies is a product of the complex interplay between colonial policies and local socio-economic conditions. The impact of colonial institutions on African economies underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is critiqued for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths; Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this critique by noting that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the current economic disparities between Africa and the West are a result of colonial institutions, has been critiqued for its oversimplification of historical processes. A specific mechanism through which institutions shape long-run economic growth is the provision of secure private property rights, which encourages investment and economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their adaptability and responsiveness to local conditions. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that the presence of 'good' institutions is crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This implies that while adaptability is important, the quality of institutions also plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

African economic performance and development are influenced by multiple factors, including the skill premium, per capita income levels, and historical wage trends. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that these factors are crucial in understanding long-term economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) identifies the skill premium as a key predictor of economic growth, noting that institutions such as guilds played a role in the transfer of skills, despite criticisms of their monopolistic tendencies. This skill development is essential for economic growth, as it enhances productivity and innovation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a level of economic development that had not been previously recognized. These findings suggest that African economies were more robust than often portrayed, with significant economic activity and development. This economic activity was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, highlighting the importance of external factors in shaping African economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further support this view by demonstrating that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, showing that economic conditions varied significantly over time and across regions. This variability suggests that African economies were dynamic and responsive to changing conditions. The role of institutions in shaping these economic outcomes remains an open question.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue. This perspective emphasizes technological and intellectual advancements as more fundamental than institutional frameworks. This institutional progress complemented technological advancements, ensuring that economic gains were not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors or coordination failures. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further elaborates on the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring that modern economic growth is contingent on the state's ability to manage such conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where the successful integration of new technologies required significant institutional and ideological changes. However, Kuznets (1973, p.6) acknowledges that these adjustments were not always smooth, and the costs associated with resolving conflicts could be substantial. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver, the institutional environment must be conducive to harnessing these advancements effectively. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intertwined with the broader context of technological and intellectual progress, highlighting the necessity of a supportive institutional framework for sustained economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework not only encouraged investment but also fostered a long-term capital market, which is crucial for sustained economic development. North and Weingast echo this by highlighting that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their success is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, underscoring the critical role of institutions in economic development. The historical evidence from England demonstrates that institutional changes, such as those that established secure property rights, are directly linked to economic growth and development.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism and its lasting effects on former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists adopted different strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions significantly influenced the long-run economic outcomes of the colonies. For instance, colonies with inclusive institutions, such as the United States and Australia, experienced higher economic growth compared to those with extractive institutions, like the Congo. This shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had a lasting impact on economic development. Austin's work on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis provides further insight into how institutional changes introduced by colonialism affected economic outcomes. Austin shows that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and these governments adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on economic development.
