The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions govern the incentives and constraints that shape economic behavior, thereby directly influencing growth. Another position suggests that institutions are merely one of many factors and that their impact is often overstated. The disagreement hinges on the extent to which institutions can be credited with economic growth and whether their role is causal or correlational. The causal mechanisms through which institutions might influence economic growth remain a point of contention, with some scholars emphasizing the role of property rights and legal frameworks, while others point to the importance of political stability and credible commitments. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex, and understanding this dynamic is crucial for policymakers aiming to foster sustainable development. Institutions matter because they provide the framework within which economic agents operate, influencing their decisions and behaviors in ways that can either foster or hinder growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth by shaping the incentive structures that govern investment and economic activity. This institutional framework ensured the security of property rights over time, which is essential for long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate on this by noting that equilibrium institutions, which are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can be extractive and do not necessarily encourage economic development. The effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth hinges on their ability to align with the interests of a broad range of stakeholders, including a narrow elite.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, a finding shared by Ogilvie (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a pivotal moment in this evolution, as it established a constitutional monarchy that limited the power of the sovereign and ensured that property rights would be protected. This institutional change involved a fundamental alteration in the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement, which North and Weingast describe as a credible commitment to property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The significance of this institutional framework is underscored by the development of capital markets, which thrived under the secure property rights regime. Ogilvie emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were part of a broader process of institutional evolution (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and other scholars (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, highlighting the role of colonial institutions in shaping educational outcomes. This institutional framework influenced African political leaders' cost-benefit calculations regarding formal education, altering the perceived benefits of investing in this sector. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule shifted the calculus for both macro-level political leaders and micro-level families, making formal education more appealing. This shift in perceived benefits was part of a broader institutional evolution that included the development of capital markets and secure property rights. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Frankema 2012, p.15) further underscore the importance of these institutions by noting that the timing and scale of economic growth in export production varied across countries due to factors like local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development, suggesting that the impact of colonial rule was shaped by specific institutional arrangements. The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that the legacy of British colonial rule, particularly in fostering formal education, continues to influence contemporary economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply ingrained and operates through both internal norms and external environmental factors. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this argument by highlighting that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, spanning nearly 500 years. The duration and intensity of the slave trades significantly shaped Africa's economic trajectory, contrasting with the relatively shorter period of colonial rule. The institutional legacy of the slave trade, particularly the erosion of trust, continues to hinder economic development by undermining social cohesion and reducing the effectiveness of public institutions. This legacy is particularly evident in the challenges faced by African countries in fostering robust and inclusive economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causal relationships, a critique shared with Hopkins (2009, p.18). Both authors argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not adequately explain why different historical periods and paths lead to distinct economic outcomes. This is because the adoption of growth-favorable institutions varies across countries, suggesting that historical and contextual factors play a significant role. The role of colonial administrations in shaping institutional frameworks is crucial. In cases where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which fostered economic growth. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this by emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa, which focuses on institutional reforms, does not directly address how institutions cause long-run economic growth. This gap underscores the need for further research to understand the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic development over the long term.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth and that institutions are secondary to it (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress and ensure the sustainability of economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while institutions play a role, they are not the fundamental cause of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved peacefully, underscoring the importance of institutions in managing societal changes brought about by technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that although institutions may not be the primary driver, they are crucial for the stability and sustainability of economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the stability and sustainability of economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established in colonies directly impacted their economic trajectories, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive ones fostering it. This finding aligns with North's assertion that institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and economic development is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to institutional changes introduced during colonialism. These changes, in turn, influenced the ability of these colonies to industrialize and develop economically. The importance of institutions in managing societal changes brought about by technological advancements is underscored by the need for peaceful resolution of growth-induced conflicts, as highlighted by Kuznets (North 1989, p.9). This suggests that while institutions may not be the primary driver of economic growth, they are essential for ensuring its stability and sustainability.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions were shaped by the political and social dynamics of the time. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, underscoring the dynamic nature of institutional change. This dynamic is further evidenced by the financial revolution in England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and reflected increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments illustrate how institutional changes can facilitate economic growth by lowering transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This is consistent with North's argument that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further supported by the argument that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This highlights the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in economic growth. The shared finding across these sources is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The specific disagreement lies in the extent to which institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations versus distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from the Bank of England's financial revolution and the dynamic institutional changes in colonial administrations settles this disagreement, showing that both efficiency and distributional conflicts play significant roles in shaping institutions that influence long-run economic growth.
