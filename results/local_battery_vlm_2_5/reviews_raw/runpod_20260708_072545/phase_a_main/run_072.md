The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study in economic development, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, such as geography or culture, or if they are endogenous to the economic and political processes of societies. Scholars argue that institutions can either foster growth through secure property rights and effective governance or hinder it through extractive practices and poor enforcement. This disagreement highlights the importance of understanding the causal pathways through which institutions operate, as it informs policy decisions aimed at promoting economic development. A key point of contention is whether institutions are merely a reflection of deeper cultural or geographic determinants, or if they have an independent effect on economic growth. This debate is not merely academic, as it has significant implications for the design of economic policies aimed at fostering development. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth involves a nuanced examination of how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, affect economic outcomes over the long term. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, countering neoclassical theories which focus on population and savings. They assert that the evolution of institutions, particularly those governing property rights and public choice, significantly impacts economic development. This institutional change was spurred by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was pivotal. North and Weingast further underscore the importance of credible commitments in fostering secure property rights, emphasizing that the development of institutions governing public choice is crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This focus on political factors underpinning economic growth contrasts with views that attribute economic development primarily to economic factors. However, North and Weingast acknowledge that the evolution of institutions is complex and can be influenced by various factors, including the regime's commitment to enforcing rules and the degree to which the sovereign is bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This complexity is evident in the historical context of France and England, where similar economic performance suggests that institutional changes alone may not be revolutionary. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by noting that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should reduce transaction costs, even as the size of the transactions sector grows proportionally to GNP (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic highlights how institutional evolution can drive economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions, determined by European policies, played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth in former colonies. The European settlers' different colonization strategies led to the establishment of divergent institutions. In some colonies, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, shaped by the settlers' interests, significantly influenced the long-run economic trajectories of these colonies, with extractive institutions hindering economic development. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of these institutions, which continued to affect income per capita long after colonial rule ended. The influence of these institutions persists today, as demonstrated by the continued economic disparities among former colonies. The persistence of these institutions is due to the fact that they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This implies that the institutions established during colonialism were designed to serve the interests of the European colonists. The impact of these institutions on economic development is evident in the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge is a fundamental driver of economic growth. Mokyr's emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge highlights the role of technological progress in sustained economic improvement. Kuznets further elaborates on the necessity of institutional adjustments to accommodate technological advancements. Kuznets points out that "modern technology" is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, necessitating significant institutional changes for efficient use of innovations. These institutional adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts arising from technological progress, ensuring that economic growth is not impeded by social or political instability. Kuznets emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a critical role in peacefully resolving these growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating modern economic growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex relationship between knowledge accumulation and economic development. Kuznets also notes that the pace and impact of technological changes vary across regions and periods. Mokyr's work complements this by recognizing the potential for coordination failures in the absence of institutional progress, suggesting that without complementary institutional advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements could be limited. This highlights the necessity of both technological and institutional progress for long-term economic growth. Mokyr notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, indicating that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by robust institutional frameworks to ensure sustained economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. The slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) also note that both internal and external channels are important in transmitting the effects of the slave trade on trust, but the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. The internal norms of mistrust developed during the slave trades have had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development, as they continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, located just south of the Zaire river. Because the Kongo kingdom had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, it was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the adverse effects of the slave trades on economic development were not uniform across Africa, and that the presence of strong institutions could mitigate some of the negative impacts.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9); (Pamuk 2007, p.23) argue that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to significant economic divergences within Europe by 1450. The Black Death caused a significant labour shortage, which led to increased wages for surviving workers. This wage increase was particularly pronounced in regions with flexible institutions that could quickly adapt to changing economic conditions. Pamuk (2007, p.23) notes that Holland, with its high degree of institutional flexibility, was able to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities. This institutional flexibility allowed for the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing, which in turn drove economic development. This suggests that the economic divergence within Europe was due to both geographical factors and the institutional environments that could facilitate or hinder economic adaptation. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) also note that high levels of investment in agriculture were crucial for economic development, indicating that institutional change was not the only factor at play. Overall, the presence of strong, flexible institutions was a key factor in determining which regions of Europe were able to take advantage of the economic opportunities presented by the Black Death and achieve long-term growth.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional change created incentives for African political leaders and families to invest in education. It is important to acknowledge that the impact of colonial institutions on education was not uniform across all societies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that in New World societies, institutions that emerged in response to different factor endowments and initial conditions often advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic could also be relevant to African societies, where colonial institutions might have had varying effects depending on local contexts and the specific policies implemented. The extent to which British colonial institutions facilitated educational development in sub-Saharan Africa thus depended on the interplay between these institutions and the local socio-political landscape.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) assert that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They find that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding is supported by the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors, including local endowments and political institutions, played a significant role in shaping the economic trajectory of African countries during the colonial period. The reconstruction and comparison of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965) further support the argument that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Understanding these historical patterns can help identify the specific historical or geographical conditions that have affected long-term African welfare development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, are the key drivers of pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This limitation underscores the importance of understanding the underlying distributional conflicts that shape institutional outcomes. These institutions were not merely responses to economic needs but were shaped by the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. Austin (2008, p.2) elaborates on this by showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating how local power dynamics influenced institutional outcomes. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the distributional conflicts that arise from the interactions between different social and political groups.

The institutions that foster economic growth are those that provide secure property rights and enforce rules that facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical to long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast emphasize the importance of credible commitment in underpinning growth, as seen in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise mechanisms by which institutions foster growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that the institutions that foster growth are those that align with the interests of powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence shows that institutions that provide secure property rights and enforce rules that facilitate market exchanges foster long-run economic growth.
