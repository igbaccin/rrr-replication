The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are a primary driver of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established in a society, whether extractive or inclusive, directly influences economic outcomes. Others posit that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, where geographic factors and cultural beliefs are antecedents that shape institutions. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth in developing countries. Understanding the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth can help identify effective strategies for institutional reform and economic development. The stakes are high, as the effectiveness of policies hinges on the accuracy of this understanding. Institutions are dynamic entities that evolve in response to economic and political pressures, shaping and being shaped by the societies they govern. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The institutional evolution in seventeenth-century England, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9), was pivotal in establishing secure property rights, which then became a cornerstone for economic growth. Their analysis highlights how the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction was a direct result of increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. North elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction, while the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplied due to increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and underpinned the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic activity. North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, illustrating the political factors that drive economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The robustness of these institutions is further corroborated by historical evidence that underscores the pivotal role of institutional changes in shaping long-run economic growth, even when compared to other economic powerhouses like France. This institutional framework encompassed the mechanisms enforcing and adapting the rules of economic exchange, ensuring a credible commitment to property rights and economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) illustrate that the distinct institutional frameworks resulting from different colonization strategies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, were determined by European mortality rates and settlement patterns, which in turn influenced the design of colonial institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in areas with lower mortality rates, institutions were set up to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development, as extractive institutions hindered long-term growth by maximizing rents for European colonists. The persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era further solidified their impact on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) underscored that these institutional differences continued to influence current economic outcomes, including income per capita. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linked to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, highlights the enduring effects of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. This dynamic suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is a critical factor in understanding the economic disparities observed today.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight the need for a more nuanced approach to the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, emphasizing the importance of historical context and varied causal factors. Austin (2008, p.2) posits that the thesis fails to account for the complexity of institutional evolution and the varied responses of colonial administrations to economic and political incentives. Austin’s (2008, p.2) analysis of colonial administrations demonstrates that institutions evolved in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The thesis also fails to capture the nuances of indigenous economic and political action, which could influence institutional prescriptions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further underscores the importance of understanding the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This suggests that while institutions play a critical role, the historical context and data availability are also essential for a comprehensive understanding of economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) reveal that distributional conflicts were instrumental in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. In this view, these institutions were not merely efficiency-driven; rather, they emerged as a result of power struggles and the need to maintain social order. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the shared beliefs of a society make up part of its institutional structure and that the persistence of these institutions can be linked to the underlying power dynamics. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this perspective to colonial societies, where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power shaped institutions that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. The distributional conflict perspective suggests that institutions often reflect the interests of powerful groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that institutions should be viewed as indispensable tools in the economic historian's toolkit, implying that distributional conflicts are crucial in institutional development. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional formation and its impact on economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa, as documented by Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development. This impact extends to the cultural and institutional legacies that persist to this day. This mistrust is deeply rooted and manifests in various aspects of social and economic life, affecting both intergroup and intragroup relations. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates on the adverse effects of the slave trade, noting that the period of the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a duration far longer than the era of colonial rule. This extended period of exploitation disrupted economic and social institutions, leaving a lasting imprint on African societies (Nunn 2008, p.2). The disruption of economic and social institutions is further illustrated by the case of the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets. The Kongo kingdom's ability to support trade with the Europeans underscores the potential for economic development in Africa, had it not been for the disruptive effects of the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). The negative impact of the slave trade on economic development varies across Africa. The varying effects of the slave trade on different regions and societies highlight the importance of contextual factors in shaping economic outcomes. This variability suggests that while the slave trade had a significant impact on economic development, its effects were mediated by local conditions and institutions, which in turn shaped the trajectory of economic growth in different parts of Africa.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasize that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are fundamental to long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts. One specific mechanism through which technological progress drives economic growth is the reduction of access costs to knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that the development of formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, played a significant role in reducing these access costs. The sustainability of economic improvement hinges on both technological and institutional progress. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting the economic benefits of technological advancements. Thus, while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its long-term sustainability is contingent on complementary institutional developments.
