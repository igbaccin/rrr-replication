The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is a central debate in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding the mechanisms that drive economic development and the role of political and economic institutions in this process. One side of the debate emphasizes the critical role of institutions in fostering economic growth. This perspective highlights how secure property rights, effective enforcement mechanisms, and stable political systems can create an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Institutions are seen as the bedrock upon which economic progress is built, facilitating the efficient allocation of resources and encouraging long-term planning. A rival position suggests that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and social factors, and are not the primary drivers of economic growth. This view posits that institutions are endogenous to economic development, reflecting broader societal changes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the cause or the consequence of economic development, with significant implications for policy and theoretical frameworks. The resolution of this debate could inform strategies for economic development, particularly in regions struggling with poverty and underdevelopment. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a pivotal area of inquiry, with far-reaching implications for economic theory and policy. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, both of which are critical for economic growth. The political factors underpinning this economic growth included the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underlines the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the necessary framework for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. The institutions that emerged were designed to redistribute wealth and power. The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiency, can be explained by the power dynamics at play. For example, serfdom was maintained because it benefited the landowning elite, who had the political power to enforce it. Similarly, guilds were maintained because they benefited the craft and merchant elites, who had the power to enforce membership restrictions and control competition. These distributional conflicts were deeply rooted in the social structures of the time, and the institutions that emerged were a reflection of these power dynamics. The institutions that evolved in New World societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, were designed to advantage the elites through various government policies that restricted competition. These policies contributed to the persistence of inequality and hindered economic growth, as the institutions were not designed to promote efficiency or innovation. This dynamic highlights the importance of considering distributional conflicts when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This process was driven by the differential mortality rates of European settlers, which led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that extractive institutions, which "do not encourage economic development" were the result of a political dynamic where the powerful European settlers designed these institutions to preserve their advantages. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) explain that the European settlers did not necessarily aim to maximize long-run growth. This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) who emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights how the institutional changes introduced by colonialism had lasting effects on economic development. This institutional framework, designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The long-run effects of these institutions are evident in the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, which was related to industrialization. The persistence of these institutional structures, shaped by the political dynamics of the time, continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision and does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the notion that ethnic diversity inherently leads to institutional dysfunction. This historical analysis aims to understand the long-term picture of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income. The implications of these findings suggest that specific historical or geographical conditions may have cast a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development. This nuanced view of African economic history underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay between institutions, economic development, and historical context.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting social and economic dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it affects both internal norms and external environments. The persistence of this mistrust, therefore, may explain why some African countries have struggled to adopt and maintain institutions that support economic development. This variability suggests that while the culture of mistrust is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of institutional outcomes.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that economic growth is fundamentally propelled by technological advancements, which require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This perspective suggests that the accumulation of knowledge and technological breakthroughs are essential for sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also acknowledges that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that technological progress introduces conflicts that need to be resolved to achieve modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This indicates that while technological progress is a key driver, the institutional framework plays a crucial role in managing the conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. Therefore, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is vital for long-run economic growth.

The institutional framework plays a pivotal role in managing conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. Institutions are dynamic entities that adapt to technological progress and societal changes. Acemoglu et al. highlight that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The type of institutions established significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). As a result, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This shows that institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the work of Ogilvie and Austin. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the underlying conflicts and power dynamics that shape institutions. Austin, on the other hand, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This argument suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutional changes over time is necessary to fully grasp their impact on economic growth. Austin also shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This further emphasizes the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic outcomes.
