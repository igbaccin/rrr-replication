The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the centrality of institutions and those who argue for the primacy of other factors. The institutional perspective posits that well-functioning institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are essential for sustained economic growth. Rival views suggest that factors such as geography, culture, and exogenous shocks are critical in determining economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or if they are exogenous factors that can be manipulated to achieve growth. One side argues that institutions are the key to long-term economic growth by providing the necessary framework for investment and economic activity. The other side contends that institutions are merely one of many factors and that their impact is often overstated. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and shapes our understanding of economic development. The central question remains: do institutions shape long-run economic growth, or are they merely a byproduct of other underlying factors? Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was pivotal in enhancing the security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. Their analysis underscores that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. This institutional framework governed economic exchange and ensured the enforcement and potential change of these rules, underpinning economic growth. Moreover, the commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical political factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical context shows that the institutional changes in England led to a significant improvement in economic performance. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and establishing secure property rights.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This influence was mediated through the differential establishment of extractive versus growth-oriented institutions, which were in turn driven by European mortality rates and settlement patterns. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that in regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to facilitate rapid resource transfer to the metropole. Regions with low European mortality, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional framework fostered economic development and shaped the long-run economic trajectory of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further illustrate that the robustness of these findings holds across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This robustness underscores the profound and enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is bidirectional. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is also contingent on historical and economic contexts.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights into institutional reforms that can alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that ethnically homogeneous societies are not necessarily dysfunctional, nor are ethnically heterogeneous societies incapable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective is aligned with Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who emphasize that African poverty is not an inevitable consequence of historical or geographical conditions. Instead, they argue that poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon. This shared finding suggests that the long-term economic development of Africa is not predetermined by historical or geographical factors. The implication is that institutional reforms can significantly improve economic outcomes, as good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions operate are still an area of active research. Englebert's conclusion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments further supports the idea that institutions can be reformed to improve economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the potential for institutional reforms to drive economic growth and reduce poverty in Africa.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr also acknowledges that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, its benefits can be enhanced or hindered by the institutional environment. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is complex, as technological changes introduce conflicts that need to be resolved for modern economic growth to be possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, even within a framework that prioritizes technological progress.

The mistrust generated by the slave trade operates through both external and internal channels, with the internal norms of mistrust being at least twice as impactful as the external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Distributional conflicts fundamentally shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They trace this back to the fact that institutions often reflect the power dynamics and interests of dominant groups. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and the structure of guilds can be understood as outcomes of conflicts over resource distribution. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who show that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, restricting competition and maintaining inequality. They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped the institutions that evolved, perpetuating these inequalities over time. This focus on distributional conflicts does not negate the role of institutions entirely. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes that institutions should be seen as indispensable instruments in economic history, suggesting that while distributional conflicts shape institutions, these institutions in turn influence economic outcomes. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between power, institutions, and economic development, where institutions are both shaped by and shaping of economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay between power dynamics, economic outcomes, and institutional evolution. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. They show that extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality led to persistent economic underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further support this by demonstrating that different measures of institutions, such as constraints on the executive and property rights, all correlate with economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). North argues that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are critical for economic growth. He shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). North's work highlights that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Together, these findings illustrate how institutions shape economic growth by influencing the security of property rights and the efficiency of market exchanges. Ogilvie emphasizes that institutions should be seen as indispensable instruments in economic history (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She argues that distributional conflicts shape institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between power, institutions, and economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He shows that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that institutions evolve in response to economic and political dynamics. Austin's findings align with North's emphasis on the importance of institutions in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He shows that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes and the need for institutional reforms to address poverty. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth and the need for institutional reforms to address poverty. The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Austin shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). The remaining disagreement is that some scholars argue that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts, while others emphasize the role of efficiency in shaping institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the evolution of institutions in response to economic and political dynamics, as shown by Austin. This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth and the need for institutional reforms to address poverty.
