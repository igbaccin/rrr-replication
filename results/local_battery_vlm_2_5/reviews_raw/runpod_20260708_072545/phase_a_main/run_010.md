The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. This question is marked by a live disagreement between those who emphasize institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who argue for alternative factors. Some scholars, like Acemoglu et al. posit that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or growth-enhancing, directly influences long-term economic outcomes. This perspective is challenged by those who highlight the importance of other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic conditions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This debate underscores the need to understand how institutions interact with other variables to shape economic trajectories. Institutions are dynamic entities that evolve in response to economic and political pressures, influencing economic growth through their design and enforcement. This evolution can be traced through the interaction of institutions with other factors, such as colonial policies and cultural beliefs, which shape their development and impact. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This institutional evolution, in turn, set the stage for the development of secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9); (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional change was a response to the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The evolution of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should be evident in the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic is crucial for understanding how the institutional framework in England supported economic growth. For example, the commitment of the regime to these rules, as well as the mechanisms for enforcing them, was a critical political factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment underpinned the development of markets and the rules governing economic exchange. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic development, highlighting that while institutions are crucial, they are not the only factor at play.

These settlement patterns, influenced by European mortality rates, were pivotal in determining the type of colonial institutions established, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). When Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the institutional framework was geared towards rapid resource transfer to the metropole. In regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to more robust economic development. These institutional differences, shaped by initial settlement patterns and mortality rates, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions depended on the broader economic context. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic growth, highlighting that while institutions are crucial, their effects are mediated by other economic factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, are strongly correlated with current income per capita, reinforcing the idea that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes today.

This historical context highlights how distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Serfdom and guilds were maintained because they served the interests of the ruling elites who benefited from the control and extraction of resources from the lower classes. This perspective is crucial because it underscores that the persistence of certain institutions can be attributed to their role in maintaining social hierarchies and power structures. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implication is that the efficiency of institutions is often secondary to their role in maintaining social order and power dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes that these institutions are not immutable; they can be transformed into indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that while distributional conflicts have historically shaped institutions, understanding these dynamics can also inform efforts to reform institutions for more equitable economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) build on this by challenging the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that the 'reversal' is a series of complex processes. They assert that the timing and nature of institutional changes in Africa were not uniform and that the economic impact of colonial institutions varied significantly across different regions and periods. Austin (2008, p.2) observes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, and that this dynamic was influenced by the presence or absence of European settlers. This mechanism highlights the importance of local agency and the adaptability of institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to provide incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. This implies that while local adaptations are important, the ultimate success of institutions in promoting economic growth hinges on their capacity to foster investment. The complexity of institutional change in Africa underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of historical processes and the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further illustrate this by showing that the slave trade in Africa had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day. This mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary social dynamics. Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, emphasizing that the prolonged period of the slave trades, nearly 500 years, exerted a more significant influence on African development than the relatively shorter period of colonial rule. The slave trades disrupted local economic and social institutions, stifling the development of centralized governance and well-developed markets. This disruption impeded Africa's ability to support trade with external entities, as evidenced by the Kongo kingdom's success in facilitating foreign trade due to its centralized government and well-developed trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). The persistence of mistrust and the disruption of institutional development underscore the deep-seated challenges Africa faces in achieving sustained economic growth.

This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr also notes that technological progress is more irreversible and monotonic than economic reasonableness, implying that while technological advancements are steady, the institutional frameworks supporting them may face setbacks (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This qualification suggests that while technology and knowledge are primary drivers of economic growth, the institutional environment must adapt and evolve to fully realize their potential. Kuznets further elaborates that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, underscoring the importance of a stable political fabric (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) shows that the development of economic institutions, such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly lowered transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects increased security in the time dimension of property rights, a key factor in fostering a long-term capital market (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the establishment of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is essential for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions create a stable environment that supports long-term economic development by providing secure property rights and effective contract enforcement (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The specific types of institutions that emerge are heavily influenced by historical and political factors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights how the design of institutions can maximize rents for politically powerful groups, even if these institutions do not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This shared finding underscores the importance of understanding the political and historical context in which institutions are formed, as these factors significantly influence their impact on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The historical evidence shows that institutions matter for economic growth, but the precise mechanisms by which they operate remain a subject of debate. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can vary significantly depending on the historical context.
