The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the field of economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies achieve sustained prosperity. One position posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights, effective enforcement, and political stability. This view is supported by scholars who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and the enforcement of property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth. Another position suggests that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes. This perspective highlights the role of shared beliefs and distributional struggles in the development of core economic institutions. The debate hinges on whether institutions drive economic growth through efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms, or whether they are merely a reflection of deeper cultural and political dynamics. The live disagreement centres on the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, with some scholars arguing for a direct causal link and others suggesting a more complex interplay involving cultural and distributional factors. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a multifaceted issue that requires a nuanced understanding of both institutional frameworks and the broader socio-political context. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This institutional change was crucial for fostering a long-term capital market and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to enforce these rules, a critical political factor in economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling costs per transaction, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is a direct result of these political changes. This suggests that while institutional changes reduce individual transaction costs, they also stimulate economic growth by increasing the overall volume of transactions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutional frameworks in societies with high inequality have historically favored elites, shaping economic policies that restrict competition and perpetuate disparities. The slave trade in particular, which Nunn (2008, p.2) points out lasted for nearly 500 years, created conditions that entrenched inequality. This inequality was perpetuated through institutions that favored the elite, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. The slave trade influenced the economic development of Africa and molded the institutions that regulated economic activity. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which further hindered economic development by reducing social capital and trust, essential components for economic growth. This mistrust, combined with institutional frameworks that favored elites, created a vicious cycle where economic opportunities were limited, and the broader population remained marginalized. This dynamic illustrates how historical inequalities and the slave trade have left lasting impacts on economic institutions, perpetuating disparities that continue to affect economic development today.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Their work demonstrates that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and the ease of settlement. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that these institutional differences, rooted in colonial policies, have persisted and continue to affect current income per capita. This perspective is crucial for understanding how colonial legacies have shaped economic development trajectories. The institutional frameworks established during colonial times created enduring structures that continue to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the profound and lasting impact of colonial policies on economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) find that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. These calculations were shaped by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. For instance, the establishment of colonial administrative structures created opportunities for Africans to engage in formal education, as it provided access to new economic and political roles. This mechanism contrasts with other colonial powers, which often had different approaches to education and governance. One downstream implication is that the educational legacies of British colonial rule have had lasting effects on African economic development, as they have shaped the skills and human capital of the population. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this by noting that the persistence of poverty in Africa cannot be solely attributed to colonial legacies, but rather to a complex interplay of historical, geographical, and structural factors (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, a process that requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective suggests that while institutions may facilitate growth, it is the accumulation of knowledge that propels it. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) support this by showing that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, highlighting the role of the service sector in economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the accumulation of useful knowledge, combined with institutional adjustments to facilitate its application, is a necessary condition for sustained economic growth. Without such adjustments, modern economic growth may not be possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) share a critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They point out that the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes the political delivery of secure private property rights as crucial for long-term economic growth, oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the limitations of this approach, noting its difficulty in testing quantitatively and its insufficiency in explaining why certain institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates this point through the example of colonial governments' responses to indigenous economic and political action, showing that institutional prescriptions were often adjusted in response to local incentives. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that shared beliefs and cultural values, often invoked to explain economic differences, are themselves part of the institutional structure and cannot be treated as exogenous factors. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are simply tools for economic efficiency, suggesting instead that they are deeply embedded in social and political contexts. This nuanced view of institutions as products of distributional conflicts and social contexts underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and political conditions under which institutions evolve.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show how the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. This is consistent with North's (1989, p.9) (North 1989, p.6) emphasis on the importance of secure property rights and efficient political institutions in fostering economic growth. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is crucial for economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also pivotal. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) demonstrate that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed. This credible commitment to rules is essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their impact. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) further illustrates this through colonial governance, where the interests of colonial administrations and indigenous populations shaped institutional outcomes in ways that were not always predictable or efficient. This nuanced view of institutions as products of distributional conflicts and social contexts underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and political conditions under which institutions evolve.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This is consistent with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie shows that institutions like serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by these conflicts. Together, these findings highlight the importance of understanding the historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve and their impact on long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions matter in economic growth and development. North (1989, p.6) shows that institutions are critical to the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. This is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) who demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions shape economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the role of distributional conflicts. This is consistent with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparison of institutional outcomes in colonies with different mortality rates, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions, as demonstrated by Austin (2008, p.15).
