The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with far-reaching implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: is the primary driver of long-run economic growth best explained by the political and economic institutions that underpin a society, or by other factors such as cultural beliefs, geographic endowments, or exogenous shocks? Proponents of the institutional perspective argue that the quality of institutions, such as the rule of law and property rights, directly influences economic performance by determining the incentives for investment and innovation. This perspective is often contrasted with views that emphasize the role of exogenous factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, in shaping economic outcomes. Another rival cause for long-run economic growth is the role of population density and prosperity, which influence the policies pursued by colonizers, rather than the institutions they introduce. The debate is further complicated by the idea that the reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This debate is crucial because it informs policy recommendations for economic development, particularly in regions with a history of colonial rule. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can guide efforts to foster sustainable development and reduce poverty. The question is whether the institutions that underpin a society are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, a critical factor for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) traces the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues as a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. They focus on the development of markets and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. However, North (1989, p.9) notes that the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This implies that while secure property rights are crucial, the efficiency gains from reduced transaction costs must be balanced against the growing complexity of the economic system.

The enduring impact of these colonial institutions is evident in the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with low mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions is crucial, as Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional design, rooted in colonial policies, shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies by determining the extent to which property rights were enforced and investment was encouraged. The impact of these institutions varies. This interaction suggests that while institutional frameworks set the stage for economic growth, the ability to industrialize within these frameworks was a critical downstream factor. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al., underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) share the finding that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa, a dynamic that was shaped by the interplay of political institutions and economic incentives (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This development was further supported by the growing demand for export production, which was tightly linked to foreign trade and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). While British rule facilitated educational development, the broader economic impact of colonial institutions varied across different African regions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the economic development of British Africa was influenced by specific historical and geographical conditions, which had both temporary and structural effects on long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while British colonial rule had a positive impact on education, its overall economic impact was more complex and varied across different regions.

The economic contributions of African women, as emphasized by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18), reveal that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be nuanced by gender dynamics. Their work underscores that women were instrumental in agricultural activities, including farming and migration, which propelled economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This implies that the economic performance of African countries is not determined by ethnic diversity but rather by the quality of institutions and the incentives they provide for investment. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further elaborates that the prominence of institutions in development policy enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists, emphasizing the crucial role of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) note that the codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal alliances, which in turn marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities. This qualification suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their impact can be mitigated by gender-specific barriers.

The divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution, finance, and climate (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This is evidenced by the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe, which was facilitated by efficient merchant networks and financial systems. Pamuk (2007, p.23) builds on this by highlighting the structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These structural changes, including increased urbanisation and institutional flexibility, enabled regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. However, Pamuk (2007, p.23) qualifies this by noting that the institutional flexibility in regions like Holland was unusual and not universally present across Europe. This suggests that while merchant distribution, finance, and structural changes were significant drivers of economic divergence, their impact was mediated by regional-specific factors such as institutional flexibility.

There is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.1; van Zanden 2009, p.19). This perspective is shared by van Zanden (1999, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) who argue that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards. One mechanism that drives this decoupling is the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that institutions such as guilds played a crucial role in the intergenerational transfer of skills. These institutions ensured the fair execution of apprentice contracts, which was essential for maintaining skill levels and economic productivity. However, these same institutions were also criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades, which could artificially inflate wages and create local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). For instance, despite significant economic growth, the relative price level of basic foodstuffs increased, which could offset the benefits of economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This decoupling suggests that while institutions like guilds could enhance skill levels and productivity, their restrictive practices could also limit the broader economic benefits, thereby affecting living standards.

The restrictive practices of institutions like guilds, as discussed by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), illustrate how institutional efficiency can be tempered by distributional conflicts and social contexts. This perspective highlights how institutions are shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups, not just by efficiency concerns. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were shaped by the struggles between elites and other social groups. This view challenges the conventional belief that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to the New World, arguing that societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The institutional structures that emerged in these societies were thus shaped by the distributional conflicts and the resulting power dynamics. The downstream implication of this perspective is that institutions can perpetuate inequality by entrenching the interests of dominant groups. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) qualifies this by emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they can also be instruments for economic historians to analyze long-term economic development. This perspective underscores the importance of considering the political and social context in which institutions operate, rather than viewing them solely as efficient responses to economic conditions.

The culture of mistrust in Africa, stemming from the slave trade, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), provides an alternative lens through which to view the long-run economic growth, challenging purely institutional explanations. This mistrust has deep historical roots, stemming from the transatlantic slave trade. Nunn and Wantchekon found that the slave trade significantly reduced trust within African societies, and this reduction in trust is a crucial factor in understanding the region's economic underperformance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is not merely a consequence of the slave trade's economic disruption but is also deeply ingrained in the social fabric, affecting interpersonal relations and collective action. Some regions, particularly those with different colonial experiences, show varying levels of trust and economic outcomes. This variation indicates that while the slave trade played a significant role in shaping mistrust, other factors, such as colonial policies and indigenous economic actions, also influenced the development of trust within African societies. These findings underscore the complexity of African economic development, highlighting that mistrust is just one of many factors shaping economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the historical evidence that shows the impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks, which significantly influenced current economic outcomes. These strategies ranged from settler colonies with strong property rights to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) extends this analysis by highlighting the importance of indigenous economic actions and colonial policies in shaping institutional development. Austin shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that indigenous actions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) shows how colonial policies and indigenous economic actions together shaped the institutional landscape, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth.

The institutional framework developed by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) provides a deeper understanding of how institutions matter in economic growth. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. This decline in transaction costs reflects the increased security of property rights, which is essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) further emphasizes the role of institutions by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie shows that institutions like serfdom, the community, and the craft guild were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. This shared finding (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) shows that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of political power, economic interests, and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

The impact of colonialism on institutional development is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) builds on this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8) shows that colonial institutions had a lasting impact on economic development, and that understanding this impact is crucial for informing institutional reforms.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is clear. Institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial policies and indigenous economic actions together shaped the institutional landscape. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions, economic opportunities, and long-run economic growth.
