The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic progress. Institutions, defined broadly as the rules of the game in a society, can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that shape economic behavior. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, and whether they are primarily shaped by cultural factors, geographic determinants, or political dynamics. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geographic endowments and cultural beliefs playing equally significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions are molded by the interests of politically influential groups, who craft them to boost their own gains and long-run growth is not the primary goal. This debate underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate in different contexts. Institutions are dynamic entities that evolve in response to economic, political, and social changes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The establishment of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 1694, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England, demonstrating the transformative power of institutional innovations. These institutions were critical in reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and enabling long-term capital markets to flourish. The financial revolution in England, which included the establishment of the Bank of England, was a response to the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This institutional framework underpinned the economic growth of England and provided a model for other nations. The success of these institutions was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This article focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets-not simply the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This institutional framework, which included the Bank of England and other financial innovations, was essential for the long-term economic growth of England.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to swiftly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences, shaped by European mortality rates and colonization strategies, have had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions has been a key factor in determining current income levels in former colonies. The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule continue to influence economic outcomes, with extractive institutions hindering growth and investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) stress that these institutions were crafted to maximize rents for European colonists. This focus on short-term extraction has left a legacy of underdevelopment in many former colonies. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was pivotal in the long-run development of these regions. This interaction underscores the intricate relationship between institutional frameworks and economic growth, demonstrating that the path to development is influenced by both initial conditions and the specific institutions in place.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that the thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The thesis' central claim is that today's economic disparities are largely a result of colonial policies that were detrimental to economic growth. Austin and Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrate that this perspective overlooks the complexity of historical institutional development. The thesis assumes that the colonial period was uniformly negative for economic growth, but Austin and Frankema and van Waijenburg reveal that this is not the case. Austin demonstrates that colonial administrations could adopt institutional prescriptions that responded to local economic and political incentives, leading to varying outcomes across different regions (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was contingent on local conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg extend this insight by showing that poverty in Africa has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their study of real wage levels in British African colonies highlights the variability of economic conditions and the potential for growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Institutional frameworks were influential, but they interacted with local contexts in complex ways. Hopkins further qualifies this view by emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional arrangements and their interactions with local conditions in shaping long-run economic growth. It is clear that the legacy of colonial institutions is more nuanced than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests, and that local contexts played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Technological progress is a necessary condition for sustained economic growth. Institutions facilitate this process by resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes. This is because the introduction of new technologies often disrupts existing social and economic structures, leading to conflicts that can hinder growth if not managed properly. Kuznets (1973, p.1) suggests that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving these growth-induced conflicts, thereby enabling modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This is because institutional progress helps to reduce access costs and coordinate efforts, making technological progress more effective and sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of useful knowledge is more monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This is because technological progress can be reversed or slowed down by various factors, such as wars, economic crises, or political instability. The growth of useful knowledge is resilient and can continue despite these challenges. This is because useful knowledge is cumulative and can be built upon over time, even if there are temporary setbacks.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade undermined the development of institutions that foster trust and cooperation, which are crucial for economic growth. This mistrust significantly impedes economic development by reducing the effectiveness of institutions that rely on social capital. The internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the historical experience of the slave trade, are at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust creates a barrier to the formation of effective institutions, as it reduces the willingness of individuals to engage in cooperative economic activities. The legacy of the slave trade in Africa is evident in the deep-seated mistrust that persists in many African societies today, hindering the development of institutions that are essential for economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) establish that distributional conflicts were central in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. The persistence of inequality in the New World is linked to the initial factor endowments and the institutions that evolved from them, which in turn shaped the economic trajectory of these societies. This unequal distribution of wealth and power led to the creation of institutions that favored elites, such as those that restricted competition and consolidated political power. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is evident in the formation of serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe. These institutions were influenced by the power dynamics between different social groups. For example, craft guilds, which were ostensibly designed to ensure the quality of goods and services, also served to limit competition and maintain the economic dominance of skilled artisans. This dynamic is also seen in the New World, where institutions that evolved in societies with extreme inequality tended to perpetuate that inequality. The mechanisms by which these institutions maintained inequality varied, but often included policies that restricted access to resources and opportunities for the majority of the population. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and power dynamics that shaped the institutions of a society. The legacy of these institutions can be seen in the long-term economic development of these regions, highlighting the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities within a society. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted the economic trajectory of colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persistently affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further stress that these institutions were crafted to maximize rents for European colonists. This perspective is aligned with North’s (1989, p.9) view that institutions facilitating market exchanges and securing property rights are crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in the 17th century led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth. The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.9).

The historical context and power dynamics that shaped institutions are critical in understanding their enduring impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and Austin (2008, p.15) both highlight the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions were established. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the results of institutional impact on economic growth are consistent across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Austin 2008, p.15) underscores the importance of considering the historical context and power dynamics in understanding the impact of institutions on economic growth. The legacy of these institutions can be seen in the long-term economic development of regions, highlighting the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the work of Ogilvie (2007, p.3) and Hopkins (2009, p.8). Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that institutions should be an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, emphasizing their role in understanding long-term economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) shows that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. Good institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8) underscores the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment and fostering economic growth.
