The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high intellectually, as the debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely secondary factors. One key position is that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and efficient markets. This view is supported by scholars who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as those in seventeenth-century England, was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Some scholars argue that institutions are influenced by broader historical and cultural factors, and that distributional conflicts often overshadow institutional efficiency. This debate is central to understanding the mechanisms through which economic growth occurs and the policies that can promote it. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they are influenced by other factors, remains unresolved. The answer to this question has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 was a critical institutional development that facilitated economic growth by providing a stable financial environment. This institutional change was driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The political institutions that emerged from this trade were instrumental in specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn fostered economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical political factor. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, such as those that emerged in England, should result in falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. This institutional framework supported increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). While the institutions of seventeenth-century England were instrumental in fostering economic growth, the role of these institutions is not isolated from other factors. Nonetheless, the institutional framework that emerged in England provided a robust foundation for economic development, underscoring the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in driving long-run economic growth. The evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this institutional impact.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies. These frameworks were shaped by mortality rates and the settlers' intentions, ranging from extractive states that rapidly transferred resources to the metropole to more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that in colonies with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The persistence of these extractive institutions highlights a key mechanism: the long-term impact of initial institutional frameworks set by colonial powers. This pattern underscores that the nature of colonial institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions are both a legacy of the past and a determinant of current economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) found that the results with other covariates are also very similar. This finding suggests the robustness of the institutional impact on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutional legacies means that the economic growth of former colonies is intrinsically linked to the historical institutional frameworks imposed during the colonial era.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) highlight that the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World was distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, suggesting a deeper layer of institutional complexity. The persistence of inequality in New World societies, rooted in the initial distribution of resources and power, shaped institutions that favored elites. This inequality was reinforced through government policies that restricted competition and maintained elite advantages. These institutions, designed to protect elite interests, hindered economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader economic participation. This mechanism aligns with Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who posits that institutions such as serfdom and guilds were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were shaped by the need to manage conflicts over resource distribution (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The persistence of these institutions over time suggests that their distributional effects were deeply embedded in the socio-economic fabric, as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note in their analysis of New World societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth was profound, as they created barriers to innovation and economic mobility, thereby perpetuating inequality and stifling growth.

Austin's analysis of African economic development, which challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, underscores the profound impact of historical factors such as slave trades and colonial policies on Africa's economic trajectory. Austin and Nunn both argue that the slave trades and colonial policies have had lasting effects on African economic development. Austin's work shows that in some cases, colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, leading to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development varied depending on local conditions and responses. Moreover, Nunn highlights that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on the continent's economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context is crucial for understanding the current state of African economies, as it reveals that the roots of underdevelopment are deeply embedded in the region's history. Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize that the slave trade also fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and has implications for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal norm of mistrust, they argue, accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, underscoring the complex and enduring legacy of historical factors on contemporary economic conditions in Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, examine African economic performance and the impact of colonial rule, focusing on historical data and real wage levels (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Both studies highlight that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their analyses reveal that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance, which often emphasized persistent underdevelopment. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule supports this perspective (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The data show that economic growth in these regions was tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, with variations in growth patterns driven by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while colonial institutions may have influenced economic outcomes, they were not the sole determinants of growth. The timing and scale of export production varied significantly, indicating that local factors played a crucial role in shaping economic development. This gap in the literature highlights the need for more comprehensive analyses that integrate historical economic data to understand long-term African welfare development.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets highlights that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This technological progress, driven by the advancement of human knowledge, requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use and diffusion of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustaining economic improvement. For example, Mokyr notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the gains made through technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further underscores the importance of resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, highlighting the role of the sovereign state in maintaining political stability during periods of rapid technological change (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is fundamental, the institutional framework that supports and regulates this progress is equally important for long-term economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, challenging the primacy of institutions in driving long-run economic growth. They identify merchant distribution and finance as pivotal factors in the economic growth of north-western Europe, highlighting the role of the service sector in enhancing productivity. Pamuk (2007, p.16) further elaborates on this divergence by underscoring the structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which created a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap, in turn, fostered institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, enabling rapid economic adaptation and growth. The institutional changes in north-western Europe, driven by economic opportunities and the need for investment, led to higher agricultural productivity. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that the high productivity in southern Indian agriculture resulted from significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. However, the differing trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe suggest that the impact of investment on economic development varied significantly across regions, with institutional frameworks playing a crucial role in determining outcomes. The service sector's contribution to economic growth during the early modern period, as noted by Broadberry and Gupta, indicates that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is more nuanced than previously thought, with structural changes and economic opportunities influencing institutional development and, consequently, economic performance.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is highlighted by the diverse pathways that European colonies took. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional frameworks that significantly impacted current income per capita. In colonies with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established, whereas in areas like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were implemented. This variation in institutional development directly influenced economic outcomes, with secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms fostering growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.29) underscore the importance of credible commitment in economic growth, demonstrating that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which reflect the increased security of property rights and the falling transaction costs that facilitated economic growth. The institutional changes in England, particularly the creation of the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in this economic transformation (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how different historical periods and paths influence institutional evolution. The historical evidence and empirical comparisons show that the precise interaction between institutions and economic opportunities is key to understanding long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies and the enforcement of property rights in low-mortality colonies directly influenced economic development. This is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.29), who show that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of how different historical periods and paths influence institutional evolution. These findings are further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth, but the precise mechanisms and historical contexts in which they operate vary significantly.
