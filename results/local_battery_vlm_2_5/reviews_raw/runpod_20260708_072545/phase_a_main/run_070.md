The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions, as the rules of the game governing social interactions, can profoundly influence economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints under which individuals and organizations operate. A key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions themselves are the driving force behind economic growth, or whether they are merely one factor among many, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs. Institutions shape the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior, and thus their quality and design can either foster or hinder long-term economic development. This question is central to debates about the effectiveness of policy interventions and the sustainability of economic growth.

Institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, not necessarily to promote long-run growth. The nature of colonial institutions, for example, was often shaped by the interests of European colonists, who established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach was particularly evident in colonies with high mortality rates, where Europeans adopted different colonization strategies. Where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, the outcomes were markedly different. This dynamic highlights how the initial design of institutions can have lasting effects on economic development, as seen in the persistence of these institutional legacies in current income per capita. This is particularly evident in the contrast between colonies where Europeans settled and those where they did not, underscoring the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonial powers implemented diverse institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, shaped by the desire to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted and had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to the policies pursued by Europeans, influenced by factors like population density and prosperity. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that specific institutional developments can significantly impact transaction costs and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) shows that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional innovation facilitated economic growth by enabling long-term capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further support this view by highlighting that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn supported economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework ensured that the regime was committed to and bound by the rules governing economic exchange, thereby reducing uncertainty and transaction costs. One specific mechanism through which these institutions operated was the provision of credible commitments. They argue that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought, suggesting that the economic performance of England and France was nearly at parity before the French Revolution. This qualification underscores the importance of considering the broader economic and political context when assessing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These scholars posit that the economic institutions of these societies were shaped more by the power dynamics and conflicts between different social groups than by considerations of economic efficiency. Ogilvie highlights how institutions like serfdom, the community, and craft guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be understood as outcomes of struggles between elites and other groups. For instance, she notes that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights played a significant role in shaping institutions. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who demonstrate how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This distributional conflict mechanism is supported by evidence from Latin America, where institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement were blocked by a system of shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights. This perspective is qualified by the context-dependence of institutions. For example, the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to the impact of institutions on the course of development through their influence on wealth, human capital, and political power. This shows that while distributional conflicts are a key factor, the broader economic and political context also plays a crucial role in shaping institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. For example, Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns, necessitating significant institutional changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress complementing technological advancements, economic improvement would be unsustainable in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts. For instance, the sovereign state plays a pivotal role in peacefully resolving such conflicts, ensuring that economic growth is not hindered by excessive costs or political instability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that although technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the stability and sustainability of this growth are contingent on the ability of institutions to adapt and evolve in response to technological changes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) both demonstrate that historical factors have significantly influenced African economic development, shaping the continent's economic trajectory in profound ways. Austin argues that colonial policies, particularly those related to commodity production and labor competition, played a crucial role in determining the economic outcomes of African regions. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade has had lasting effects on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. One specific mechanism through which these historical factors exerted their influence was the alteration of institutional frameworks. Colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local economic and political dynamics, which in turn shaped the institutional landscape of African economies. For instance, colonial governments that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous economic activities, thereby influencing the development of local institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional adaptation was not uniform across Africa, leading to varied economic outcomes. The impact of the slave trade on African economies extends beyond institutional changes. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that the slave trade's legacy includes a deep-seated mistrust within African societies, which has impeded economic development. This mistrust is rooted in internal norms, which have been shaped by the historical experience of the slave trade. This internal mistrust has had a significant impact on economic development, as it affects trustworthiness and trust in the rule of law. The implications of this historical mistrust are far-reaching, as it influences various aspects of economic life, including trade, political institutions, and management practices. This historical mistrust, however, is not the sole determinant of economic outcomes. This responsiveness suggests that while historical factors have significantly shaped African economies, the role of institutions in long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional adaptation continues to shape the economic trajectories of African nations.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought. These findings suggest that African economies were more robust than often portrayed, with significant economic activity and growth during the colonial era. This economic dynamism was driven by the integration of African economies into global markets, particularly through export production. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that patterns of economic growth and decline in many African countries were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. This integration fostered economic development by creating demand for local goods and services, stimulating productivity, and generating revenue for investment. The extent and impact of this integration varied across regions and over time, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth and the structural effects of historical conditions. This understanding is crucial for evaluating the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it provides a more nuanced view of Africa's economic past and the factors that have driven its development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of income and the structural effects of historical conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonists established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with extractive states in high-mortality areas and growth-oriented institutions in low-mortality regions. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on current income per capita, showing that the type of institutions introduced during colonial rule significantly impacted long-term economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that understanding the long-term picture of economic development requires a nuanced view of Africa's economic past, including the distribution of income and the factors that have driven its development. The key takeaway from these studies is that institutions matter for economic growth, as they shape the economic landscape over the long term.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's (1989, p.9) (North 1989, p.6) work, which argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development. He shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This, in turn, facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) extend this argument by focusing on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets. They show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their effectiveness. Together, these studies demonstrate that institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges are essential for long-term economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She contends that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of income per capita in former European colonies, which shows that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century.
