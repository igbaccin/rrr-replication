The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The debate centers around two primary positions: those who emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth, and those who argue that other factors, such as geographic conditions or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. The first group posits that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and provide secure governance, are crucial for sustained economic development. The second group, however, contends that institutions are merely one among many factors, and that geographic endowments or cultural attitudes may be more determinative. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors. Understanding this question is essential for policymakers seeking to foster economic development in various regions. The debate on institutions versus other factors such as geography or culture is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions are shaped by the underlying economic and political structures, while others see institutions as independent variables that can drive economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The Bank of England's creation in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional change was a product of fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues. North and Weingast's argument aligns with North's broader claim that institutions matter in economic growth and development. North (1989, p.6) argues that political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should reduce transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and economic growth. North (1989, p.6) also notes that as the economy grows, the size of the transactions sector will increase, thereby multiplying the aggregate volume of exchanges. This implies that while secure property rights are crucial, the complexity of transactions will also rise, potentially requiring further institutional adaptations to sustain economic growth. This interplay between institutional evolution and economic growth underscores the dynamic nature of property rights and their enforcement.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. They demonstrate that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to a dichotomy between settler colonies with inclusive institutions and extractive states in regions with high mortality. This institutional divergence, rooted in colonial strategies, has had enduring effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) illustrate that these institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, shaped long-term economic trajectories by influencing investment and economic growth. This institutional legacy persists, as evidenced by the correlation between historical institutions and contemporary economic performance. The persistence of these institutional effects highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. This dynamic interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were designed to secure the interests of the ruling elites, who used these institutions to maintain their power and control over resources. This perspective is further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.3), who advocates for a more nuanced understanding of institutions as tools that can be used to analyze long-term economic development.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, challenging the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective highlights the enduring economic impact of the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, compared to the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule. The slave trades disrupted economic development by decimating populations, destroying social and political institutions, and creating long-lasting regional disparities. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to the expectations of some theories of colonial exploitation (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior suggests that the long-term impact of slave trades on African economic development was influenced by the evolving dynamics of colonial rule and indigenous responses. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize that understanding these dynamics requires a deeper examination of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This approach acknowledges the complexity of African economic development and the need to consider multiple factors beyond the slave trades.

Frankema argues that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered the development of formal education, a perspective shared by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational advancement was driven by the establishment of new social and political institutions under British rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. These institutions created a demand for missionary education, which in turn facilitated the spread of formal education across the region (Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial administration's emphasis on education contrasted with other colonial powers, which often prioritized resource extraction over educational development. This divergence in colonial policies had significant implications for the economic trajectory of sub-Saharan Africa. The educational infrastructure laid during the British colonial period set the stage for future economic growth by equipping the population with essential skills and knowledge. The impact of British rule on education varied across regions, as local conditions and political institutions played a crucial role in shaping educational outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr posits that the accumulation of knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological progress is the main driver of long-term economic growth. This process is contingent on institutional adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets elaborates on this by highlighting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to peacefully resolve growth-induced conflicts. Kuznets further explains that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts, which are inevitable during periods of technological and economic growth. Mokyr notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be limited in its sustainability. Mokyr suggests that the growth of economic reasonableness was far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This implies that while technological progress is a necessary condition for economic growth, institutional adjustments are essential for sustaining this growth.
