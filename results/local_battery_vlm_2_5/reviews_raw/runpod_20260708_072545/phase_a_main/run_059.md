The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The extent to which institutions drive economic prosperity is at stake. One position is that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, emphasizing the importance of property rights and rule of law in fostering investment and economic development. Another position is that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors, such as geography and culture, and thus are not the primary drivers of economic growth. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic growth or exogenous to it. This question has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is particularly salient in the context of colonial legacies and post-colonial economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that financial institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This institutional evolution is crucial for economic growth, as it facilitates market exchanges and supports a long-term capital market (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the success of these institutions hinges on the regime's commitment to enforcing the rules governing economic exchange, highlighting the political factors underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, the importance of these institutions is not absolute; North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that while institutional changes were significant, they were not revolutionary on their own (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Instead, the broader economic performance and the effectiveness of these institutions depend on the political context and the degree to which the regime is committed to these rules. This interplay between institutions and political commitment is essential for understanding the long-run economic growth dynamics.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism's impact on long-run economic growth was mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions, which have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The nature of these institutions was shaped by European mortality rates and settlement patterns. Where mortality rates were high, Europeans established extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer, while lower mortality rates led to more investment-friendly institutions. This institutional divergence was a direct result of colonial policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to maximize resource extraction for the metropole. These institutional differences have had lasting effects on economic development. Evidence supports the perspective that institutional changes were crucial in reversing the relative incomes among former colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic growth, underscoring the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks in assessing current economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective is evident in the role of technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge in sustaining economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this by noting that modern technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interaction between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and maintaining political stability, which is essential for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by pointing out that institutional progress must complement technological progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. Without this complementarity, the benefits of technological advancements could be eroded by rent-seeking behavior. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting and sustaining the growth driven by technological advancements.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa have been identified as a significant factor in shaping the continent's economic development, with adverse effects on economic growth. The mechanism through which the slave trades operated is argued to be the erosion of trust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) point out that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and continues to impact economic development. This mistrust is not merely a result of external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, but is deeply embedded within individuals' internal norms. Nunn (2008, p.19) supports this view by noting that sustained trade in Africa was only possible in regions with centralized governance and well-developed markets, suggesting that the disruption caused by the slave trades impeded the establishment of such institutions. This implies that the long-term effects of the slave trades extend to the institutional and cultural fabric of African societies. The legacy of mistrust, as a result, continues to hinder economic growth by undermining the social capital necessary for cooperative economic activities. This historical perspective underscores the enduring impact of the slave trades on Africa's economic trajectory.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share the view that distributional conflicts among elites and the broader population are critical in shaping core economic institutions. The key mechanism through which this occurs is the influence of elites on policy formation. In pre-industrial Europe, elites often controlled institutions such as serfdom and guilds, which were designed to maintain their economic and political dominance. This elite influence was driven by a desire to preserve their status and wealth. In the New World, similar dynamics played out, with institutions in societies characterized by high inequality favoring elites. This was achieved through policies that restricted competition and maintained the status quo, thereby perpetuating inequality. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that while institutions are crucial, their study should be integrated thoughtfully into broader historical research programs to avoid superficial analyses. The focus on distributional conflicts underscores the importance of understanding power dynamics in shaping economic institutions.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) show that British rule in Africa fostered the development of formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. This educational expansion was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial administration's emphasis on formal education created a more educated workforce, which in turn facilitated economic development. The economic performance of African countries under colonial rule was not uniform. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This economic growth was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in economic performance across different African colonies highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that the impact of colonial rule on economic development was not monolithic (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies reveals a more nuanced picture of economic development during the colonial period. This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional frameworks and economic conditions that shaped long-run economic growth in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting the complexity of historical paths and institutional evolution in Africa, arguing that simplistic causal frameworks oversimplify the dynamics of economic development. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, and fiscal security motivated institutional changes that reflected local economic conditions. This nuanced view of colonial rule suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth was not uniform across Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this perspective by emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which are essential for development. This points to a broader implication: the effectiveness of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth depends on their ability to adapt to local contexts and provide the right incentives for investment. This adaptive capacity is crucial for understanding the varied economic outcomes across African societies.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives for investment and the overall economic environment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas and more inclusive institutions where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law systems (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional legacy persists, affecting current income per capita. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic development.

The adaptive capacity of institutions is crucial for understanding varied economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which are essential for development. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment lead to better economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The effectiveness of these institutions depends on their ability to adapt to local contexts. Austin (2008, p.2) points out that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the need for institutions to be flexible and responsive to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is key to understanding why some societies have thrived while others have struggled.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by North's (1989, p.9) argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development. North (1989, p.9) shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not encourage economic development, as they are shaped by politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of institutional changes in Europe and their impact on economic growth provides a clear example of how institutions can drive long-run economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in Europe show that institutions can facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth and highlights the need for institutions to be adaptive and responsive to local conditions.
