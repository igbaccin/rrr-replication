The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, reducing transaction costs, and allocating resources. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth. One position argues that institutions directly influence growth by promoting secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. Another position contends that institutions are shaped by underlying political and social factors, which in turn influence economic outcomes. A third position posits that the efficiency of institutions is less critical than their distributional consequences. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is intellectually significant because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering development and addresses the enduring question of why some societies prosper while others stagnate. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely reflect deeper structural factors. The question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth remains a crucial point of contention, with implications for both historical analysis and contemporary policy. North and Weingast argue that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth, highlighting the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework not only governed economic exchange but also enforced and adapted the rules governing these exchanges, ensuring a credible commitment to property rights. In the broader context of economic development, the institutional changes in England were not merely about economic rules but also about the political commitment to enforce these rules. The institutional framework in England, with its secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, set a foundation for long-term economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, with Acemoglu et al. showing that European colonists established extractive institutions where mortality rates were high, and Acemoglu et al. demonstrating that these institutions persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional framework set up by colonial powers, therefore, was not merely a function of geographic factors but a direct result of colonial policies that prioritized resource extraction over sustainable economic development. Acemoglu et al. further note that population density and prosperity influenced the policies pursued by Europeans, suggesting that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts shaped institutions in ways that served to maintain the status quo and the interests of the powerful. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are merely the outcome of efficiency-driven processes. The differential paths of development in Latin America and the United States, for example, can be traced back to the institutional frameworks that either restricted or encouraged competition. The implications of these institutional designs are profound. For instance, the persistence of inequality in Latin America can be directly linked to the institutions that were shaped by the distributional conflicts of the past. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that this institutional framework is often overlooked in favor of cultural explanations, which might obscure the role of power dynamics in shaping economic outcomes. While Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges that beliefs can influence institutions, she argues that the focus should be on the material interests and power dynamics that underlie these beliefs. This suggests that the long-run economic growth of societies is deeply influenced by the institutional structures that emerge from distributional conflicts.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) contend that the impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic trajectory is more nuanced than the simple "reversal of fortune" thesis suggests. Specifically, Austin (2008, p.2) posits that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how colonial policies could align with local economic interests, fostering growth in certain sectors. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) build on this by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial policies could lead to economic development under specific conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The variability in growth patterns across different regions and countries suggests that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions also played significant roles (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema (2012, p.15) further qualifies this by noting that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, indicating that the type of colonial administration mattered significantly (Frankema 2012, p.1). This implies that the long-run economic growth of African economies was shaped by a complex interplay of colonial policies, local economic conditions, and the specific institutional frameworks imposed by different colonial powers.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today. This mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is not solely due to the effect of the slave trade on the trustworthiness of others. Instead, it is rooted in internal norms of mistrust that have been deeply ingrained within African societies. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, contra those who might attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. This is because the slave trades disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to a decline in productivity and economic stagnation. These findings suggest that the long-run economic growth of African economies was shaped by a complex interplay of the slave trade, local economic conditions, and the specific institutional frameworks imposed by different colonial powers.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective posits that technological advancements create a virtuous cycle where innovation leads to increased productivity, which in turn fuels further economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This adjustment is necessary because technological progress is often incompatible with traditional social structures, such as the rural mode of life and extended family patterns. This is because institutional progress helps in reducing access costs and addressing coordination failures, which are critical for the sustainability of economic growth. The implication here is that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be accompanied by institutional changes that facilitate their integration into the economic system. This suggests that while technological progress can be sustained over long periods, the institutional changes required to support it may face more significant obstacles.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the integration of technological advancements into the economic system. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, with some colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others set up extractive states focused on rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced the economic trajectories of these colonies, highlighting the critical role of institutions in economic development. North (1989, p.9) further argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term capital markets and economic growth, underscoring the importance of institutions in fostering a conducive environment for economic development.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The successful enforcement of property rights and the commitment to these rules by the regime are critical for economic development. This is evident in the historical context of seventeenth-century England, where institutional changes were pivotal in fostering economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, emphasizing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The historical evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of the impact of institutions on economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the success of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England is evident in the development of capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence shows that institutions per se, and in particular the institutional changes described, were crucial for economic growth.
