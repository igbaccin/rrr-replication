The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At stake is the identification of the causal mechanisms that drive economic progress over extended periods. A key disagreement revolves around the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic growth. Others contend that institutional frameworks are less critical, or that they are influenced by broader historical and cultural contexts. Still others propose that the impact of institutions is contingent on specific historical and geographical factors. The debate highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other determinants of economic growth, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within different contexts. This interplay is evident in the varying paths that different regions have taken toward development or stagnation. These debates share a common theme: institutions evolve over time, influenced by political, economic, and social forces. This evolution can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on the specific institutional arrangements in place. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This dimension is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. The success of the English financial revolution was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. This political commitment underpins economic growth and the development of markets. The elaboration of bureaucratic structures, such as those inherited from Louis XIV, was not a hindrance to economic growth. Recent research that has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution has led to an overhauling of traditional interpretations of British as well as French economic history. The implication is that the institutional changes described were not as revolutionary as they might seem. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show how Europeans adopted different colonization strategies depending on mortality rates, leading to a range of institutional outcomes. In colonies with high European mortality, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Lower mortality rates in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand encouraged European settlement and the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences, rooted in the initial colonization strategies, persist and have significantly influenced current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further argue that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, has had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that these institutional effects are robust across various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This robustness underscores the pervasive impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, highlighting that the legacy of colonial rule continues to shape economic trajectories in profound ways.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions. This perspective highlights how the distribution of power and resources among different social groups shaped the development of economic institutions. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and distributional conflicts among landlords, peasants, and urban elites. Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic underscores how distributional conflicts can drive institutional change. Austin (2008, p.2) also points out that the presence of European settlers significantly influenced the expansion of commodity production, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts can be mediated by external factors. This nuance indicates that while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor, they operate within a broader context of political and economic influences. The legacy of colonial rule and the dynamics of power distribution continue to shape economic trajectories in profound ways.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust, they argue, is deeply embedded within African societies and has had profound effects on economic development. The slave trade created a pervasive environment of mistrust that extended beyond economic interactions, affecting social and political institutions as well. This mistrust is not simply a product of the slave trade's impact on the external environment, such as the rule of law, but also stems from internal norms of mistrust that have been ingrained over centuries. The internal norms of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon show to be at least twice as large as the external channel, have had a lasting impact on trust levels within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn supports this argument by highlighting that the slave trades in Africa, spanning nearly 500 years, significantly influenced the continent's economic development. This period of slave trades is notably longer than the era of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years, indicating that the effects of the slave trade on economic development are more profound and enduring (Nunn 2008, p.2). The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development extend beyond direct economic impacts to the broader social and institutional fabric of African societies. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade has hindered the development of strong, cohesive institutions that are essential for sustained economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade continues to shape African economic trajectories, affecting economic outcomes and the social and political environments in which economic activities take place. This legacy underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which contemporary economic challenges are embedded.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) re-evaluate historical economic performance in Africa, highlighting periods of growth and decline tied to foreign trade and export markets. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that national accounting data for Africa, once thought scarce, are in fact abundant for the colonial era and beyond. This data reveals that economic growth in many African countries was closely linked to the dynamics of foreign trade and export markets. These patterns of economic fluctuation were driven by a variety of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complexity of African economic history and the need for nuanced analysis that considers multiple factors influencing growth and decline.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological progress is a fundamental driver of economic growth, with institutional and ideological adjustments enabling the efficient use of innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.49) supports this view, emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. These technological advancements spread to a substantial part of the world, driving economic growth. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial. Kuznets (1973, p.6) highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The growth of economic reasonableness, while less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is essential for sustaining the benefits of technological advancements. The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress and resolving conflicts underscores their importance in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological progress and resolve conflicts, thereby creating an environment conducive to sustained economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutions, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states. These institutions, in turn, significantly influenced current income per capita. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework is critical for both long-term capital markets and overall economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that the institutionalist approach emphasizes the political delivery of secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth. The theory must also explain why institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries. This points to the complexity of institutional adoption and its impact on economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further illustrate this by focusing on the political factors underpinning economic growth, particularly the degree to which the regime is committed to enforcing rules. This commitment is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the belief that institutions were designed primarily for efficiency. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) support this by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups. This highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design. North (1989, p.6) adds that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs and increased economic activity. This institutional framework is essential for facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic growth. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.6) is that institutions matter profoundly in economic growth and development, shaping the economic landscape through their role in facilitating technological progress and resolving conflicts.

The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions are adopted and their impact on economic outcomes. The historical evidence of colonial policies and their long-term economic effects, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), settles this disagreement. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
