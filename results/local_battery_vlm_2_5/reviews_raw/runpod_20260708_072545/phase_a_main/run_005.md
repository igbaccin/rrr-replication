The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it speaks to the very mechanisms that drive prosperity and poverty. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the primary factors that influence economic growth. Some scholars emphasize the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth by providing secure property rights and enforcing contracts, while others argue that institutions are shaped by deeper cultural or historical factors. Furthermore, there are rival explanations that propose geographic or demographic factors as the primary drivers of economic growth, independent of institutional frameworks. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development. The interplay between institutions and growth is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions are the product of strategic interactions among powerful groups, while others see them as emergent properties of cultural beliefs and historical trajectories. This interplay is crucial for understanding how institutions can be designed or reformed to promote long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional change was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The evidence from capital markets shows that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) qualify that while the institutional changes in England were significant, they were not revolutionary in isolation. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV in France was not such a hindrance to economic growth, implying that the institutional framework alone does not determine economic performance.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) illustrate the profound impact of these institutional changes on current economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and (2002) argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers varied widely and had lasting impacts on economic development. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In contrast, where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was more robust. This pattern suggests that the extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers in regions with high European mortality created persistent barriers to economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the impact of these institutions is not uniform across all measures of institutional quality. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies highlights the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

This underscores how colonial policies not only shaped institutional frameworks but also influenced long-term development trajectories in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) show that the institutional legacies of colonial rule in Africa were not uniform and that the type of colonial power had a significant impact on the development of institutions. For instance, British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, altering the perceived benefits of formal education and influencing long-term development trajectories. This is because the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and incentives for investment. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further underscore the significance of these institutional frameworks by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the economic performance of the region was more robust than previously characterized. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, particularly those related to education and governance, played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic development in Africa.

These historical inequalities, shaped by institutions, have resulted in persistent economic disparities, as documented by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the institutional frameworks in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional bias towards elites created a self-reinforcing cycle where economic power remained concentrated, hindering broader economic development. This mechanism is further illuminated by Nunn and Wantchekon, who demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, significantly impacting economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of mistrust, stemming from historical inequalities, creates an environment where trust is low, which is detrimental to economic growth. However, Nunn and Wantchekon's findings suggest that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This highlights the deep-seated nature of institutional effects on economic development, where internalized norms of mistrust can override external improvements in governance and legal frameworks.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800. In the early modern period, Europe's economic growth was driven by high productivity in the traded goods sector, facilitated by merchant distribution and finance. This productivity was not limited to manufacturing but also extended to the service sector, which played a crucial role in economic development. The economic growth in Europe during this period was uneven, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled laborers. This divergence was not merely a result of geographical factors but also due to institutional changes that led to investment in agriculture and high agricultural labor productivity. van Zanden (1999, p.9) supports this view by noting that economic development in Europe shifted to the countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England, and that this shift led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. However, the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period is not straightforward. This suggests that while institutional changes and productivity gains were crucial for economic growth, they did not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards for all segments of the population.

Institutional and ideological adjustments play a pivotal role in the efficient use of innovations, suggesting a pathway to long-run economic growth that complements the role of institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts, such as those arising from the incompatibility of modern technology with traditional rural lifestyles. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Institutional progress helps to mitigate coordination failures and reduce rent-seeking behaviors, ensuring that technological progress translates into sustained economic growth. This dynamic interplay between technological innovations and institutional adjustments underscores the complex nature of long-run economic growth, where institutions facilitate but do not solely determine economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the oversimplification of causal mechanisms in institutional theories (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They argue that historical paths and periods are compressed in institutional theories, which can lead to a misleading understanding of the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, which posits that institutions explain the divergence between the economic paths of formerly colonized countries, oversimplifies causation by collapsing different historical periods and paths. This simplification can obscure the nuanced ways in which institutions evolve and interact with economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, are better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency. She posits that these institutions were shaped by the power dynamics and conflicts between different social groups, rather than by a straightforward pursuit of economic efficiency. This perspective suggests that the distributional implications of institutions are crucial for understanding their long-term effects on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) provides a concrete example of this dynamic, showing that colonial administrations in Africa adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation indicates that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local conditions and power struggles. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be seen as an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This underscores the importance of incorporating institutions into long-term research programs, ensuring that their complex and dynamic nature is fully appreciated. The evolution of institutions in response to local conditions and power struggles highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of their role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and incentives that guide economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive, which significantly influenced current income per capita. These findings underscore the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that wealthy regions in the past remain wealthy today, oversimplifies historical causation. This suggests that understanding the dynamic evolution of institutions over time is crucial for grasping their role in economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) provide evidence that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, account for the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This emphasizes the importance of institutions in determining long-term economic trajectories.

North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development by providing secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. This perspective highlights the foundational role of institutions in fostering economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) points out that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local conditions. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This underscores the practical implications of understanding institutional dynamics for policy and development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a complex interplay of historical context, power dynamics, and adaptive responses. The shared finding is that institutions are indispensable for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement is that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal mechanisms behind economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
