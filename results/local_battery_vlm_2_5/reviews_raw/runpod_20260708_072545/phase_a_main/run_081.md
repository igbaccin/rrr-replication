The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the persistence of prosperity and poverty across regions. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules and informal norms, are pivotal in determining economic outcomes. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as culture or geography, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are essential for economic growth, while others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that cultural factors or geographic endowments are more influential. The debate is further complicated by the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are a key driver of economic growth, shaping the environment in which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established during colonization had a profound impact on long-run economic development. This pattern suggests that the quality of institutions matters more than just their presence. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that institutions shaped by colonial powers often maximized rents for the colonists rather than promoting long-run growth, illustrating how the extractive nature of these institutions hindered economic development in many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, suggesting that institutional changes can have varied effects depending on the economic context (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange was critical. This institutional framework ensured that economic actors could rely on the stability of property rights, which in turn encouraged investment and innovation. The role of these institutions in promoting economic growth was not uniform across different contexts. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight that the factor endowments of New World societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved in these societies, often favoring elites and restricting competition. The persistence of inequality, therefore, had a profound impact on the institutional landscape and economic development. This underscores the complex interplay between institutional evolution and economic outcomes, where the initial conditions and factor endowments can significantly influence the trajectory of growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) share a critique of institutional theories, arguing that these theories oversimplify causal mechanisms by compressing different historical periods and paths. These scholars suggest that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis obscures the complexity of institutional evolution by collapsing diverse historical experiences into a single narrative. This simplification fails to account for the varied paths of institutional development and the specific contexts in which institutions emerge. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this critique by arguing that the efficiency rationale for institutions is insufficient. Instead, distributional conflicts, where different groups vie for resources and power, better explain the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective highlights how institutions are shaped by the power dynamics and interests of various social groups. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates this point by examining colonial administrations that adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic shows how institutions are not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but are also shaped by the interplay of power and interests. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by emphasizing that institutions are not just tools for distributional conflicts but also part of a broader institutional structure that includes shared beliefs and cultural values. This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of institutions and their evolution.

Broadberry and Gardner, alongside Broadberry and Gupta, challenge conventional narratives about economic development by examining historical economic performance across various regions, highlighting periods of growth and divergence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). They demonstrate that the economic history of Africa and Asia reveals significant levels of economic activity and growth in the early 20th century and before 1800, respectively, challenging the notion that these regions were uniformly poor or stagnant. Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early 20th century, a finding that underscores the role of foreign trade and export markets in driving economic growth. This pattern of growth was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner's analysis of economic growth in Anglophone Africa highlights the importance of export production and foreign trade in shaping economic development. They document how the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability in growth patterns suggests that the economic performance of African countries was shaped by specific economic and institutional conditions. Broadberry and Gupta's work on the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 complements this perspective by showing that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as by production. This highlights the crucial role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly. Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia underscores the importance of institutional change in driving investment in agriculture and high agricultural labour productivity. However, Broadberry and Gupta also note that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was not solely due to geographical factors but also to high levels of investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This qualification suggests that while institutional change played a crucial role in driving economic growth, other factors such as investment and geographical conditions also influenced economic performance.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as it erodes the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Nunn notes that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, contra those who might attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. The adverse impact of the slave trade on economic development is evident in the long-term effects on institutions and social norms. For example, the slave trade disrupted local economies and social structures, leading to a decline in productive activities and a weakening of institutions that could have supported economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings suggest that the internal channel of mistrust, where individuals' internal norms of mistrust are affected, is at least twice as large as the external channel, where the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others are impacted. This internal mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the trust necessary for economic transactions and cooperation. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are not limited to the past but continue to affect contemporary Africa, shaping its institutions and social norms in ways that hinder economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective suggests that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that technological progress is not compatible with traditional societal structures and requires significant adjustments, such as the transition from a rural mode of life to an industrialized society. This implies that the adoption of new technologies necessitates broader societal changes, including shifts in family patterns and the veneration of nature. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, they must be supported by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth. This indicates that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, it must be complemented by effective institutional frameworks and policies to address coordination failures and ensure sustained economic development. Economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological advancements, which require supportive institutional frameworks to address coordination failures and ensure long-term sustainability.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and property rights. North argues that economic growth is fundamentally tied to the development of efficient institutions that reduce transaction costs and enforce property rights (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is reinforced by North and Weingast, who emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic development. Acemoglu et al. further highlight that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which can lead to the establishment of extractive institutions that maximize rents for these groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks the complex interactions between institutional evolution and economic development over different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity is further illustrated by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts often drive the establishment of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These insights collectively show that institutions are not merely static frameworks but dynamic entities that evolve in response to political and economic pressures.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive, based on mortality rates and the presence of European settlers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutional differences have had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive institutions fostering it. This perspective is supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships between institutions and economic development by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from colonial settings shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has had a profound and enduring impact on economic outcomes. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the variation in income per capita across former colonies, which can be attributed to the different institutional frameworks established during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the divergent economic trajectories of colonies with inclusive versus extractive institutions.
