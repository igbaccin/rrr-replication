The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant intellectual stakes. Understanding how institutions influence growth can illuminate the pathways to prosperity and the obstacles that hinder it. At the heart of this debate are competing views on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. One perspective emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on economic outcomes, arguing that certain institutional frameworks foster growth by promoting investment, secure property rights, and efficient markets. This view is supported by scholars who highlight the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic efficiency. Another perspective, however, posits that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural and historical factors, which in turn influence economic development. This perspective suggests that the effectiveness of institutions is contingent on broader social and political contexts, and that institutions alone may not be sufficient to drive long-run economic growth. The debate between these views underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions function within different contexts. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether they are themselves shaped by deeper structural factors, remains a live disagreement in the field. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is dynamic and multifaceted, influenced by a range of factors including historical legacies, cultural beliefs, and political dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional change was driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues. The political factors underpinning economic growth extended beyond the rules governing economic exchange to include the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their effectiveness. This implies that while institutions were crucial, their impact on economic growth was not singularly transformative. The broader implication is that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and influenced by multiple factors, including the broader economic and political context. The institutional framework that evolved in England facilitated market exchanges and ensured that the costs per transaction were falling, supporting economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the nature of these institutions depended on European mortality rates, which in turn shaped colonization strategies. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions did not foster long-run economic growth, as they were optimized for rent extraction and development. The extractive nature of these institutions is further underscored by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and promote long-run growth. This institutional framework persisted, significantly impacting current income per capita. The legacy of colonial institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, a phenomenon Acemoglu et al. attribute to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions affect economic growth remain complex and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. highlight that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can vary based on historical and political contexts. This variability suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effects are mediated by a range of factors.

This process, however, was far from uniform, with different regions experiencing varying degrees of institutional change and economic impact. Hopkins and Frankema both argue that the impact of colonial rule on Africa's economic development varied significantly based on the policies implemented by different colonial powers (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments was not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, suggesting that the institutional legacies of colonial rule played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Frankema, meanwhile, demonstrates that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African families and political leaders. The variation in colonial policies led to different institutional trajectories, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule could support economic development. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms by which colonial institutions influenced economic growth in different contexts.

Distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is the formation of guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that guilds were not simply efficiency-enhancing organizations but were also tools used by craftsmen to restrict entry and maintain their monopolies. This restricted competition and maintained the status quo, benefiting those already in power. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by noting that the impact of these institutions on economic growth was not uniform. In some cases, the restrictive nature of these institutions may have hindered economic growth by limiting innovation and competition. This suggests that while distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping economic institutions, their impact on long-run economic growth was complex and varied. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions highlights the importance of power dynamics in economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development, a position shared by Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade led to the development of a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply ingrained as an internal norm and a response to the untrustworthiness of others. The internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel. This finding complements recent studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The mechanism by which the slave trade shaped Africa's economic development is through the erosion of social capital. The slave trade disrupted economic activity and undermined the social fabric necessary for economic growth. This mistrust, in turn, hindered the development of institutions that rely on social cooperation and collective action. Nunn qualifies the extent of this impact, noting that the slave trade's effect on trust works through both internal norms and external environments, with the internal norms playing a larger role. The persistence of this mistrust suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to affect Africa's economic development long after the trade itself has ended.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, and they spread to a substantial part of the world. This technological progress requires institutional adjustments to ensure economic growth, such as resolving growth-induced conflicts. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that these conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs, and the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further qualifies this by recognizing the possibility of coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them. Without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, it must be accompanied by appropriate institutional adjustments to ensure sustained and sustainable economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activities. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacts long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Specifically, colonies with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment experienced higher income per capita, whereas those with extractive institutions faced economic stagnation. This finding is reinforced by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development by specifying and enforcing property rights (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. These institutional frameworks are critical because they provide the necessary security and predictability for long-term economic activities. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth, highlighting the importance of institutional design in economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is consistent with North's argument that institutions must specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement to reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

Institutional development is also crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as shown by North and Weingast. They demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was essential for securing property rights and promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is supported by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of ongoing debate. For example, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that institutional development is influenced by the distribution of power and resources within society and efficiency considerations. This disagreement highlights the complexity of institutional dynamics and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic growth. The historical evidence from colonial institutions and the empirical comparisons of economic performance in different regions show that institutional quality is a key determinant of long-run economic growth.
