The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists. At stake is the understanding of what drives sustained economic development. One position argues that institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic interactions, play a pivotal role in fostering growth. This perspective emphasizes that institutions that protect property rights, enforce contracts, and ensure political stability create an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Another position suggests that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables, such as geography or culture, may be more influential in determining economic outcomes. These conflicting views highlight the need to delve into the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence growth, and to evaluate the relative importance of institutions compared to other factors. The debate is complicated by the fact that institutions are not static; they evolve over time and are shaped by historical, political, and economic contexts. Therefore, understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires a nuanced analysis that considers both their endogenous and exogenous determinants. This review will examine how scholars have approached the study of institutions and economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms proposed and the evidence presented to support these mechanisms. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and political frameworks in 17th-century England significantly lowered transaction costs, which in turn facilitated long-run economic growth. The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights led to falling costs per transaction, even as the size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework fostered a commitment to secure property rights, which underpinned economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Their research demonstrates that European colonizers implemented different strategies based on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole, with extractive institutions that discouraged investment and economic development. Regions with low mortality, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences, shaped by European policies, had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions into the modern era underscores their enduring impact on economic development. These findings are consistent with the broader historical evidence that the nature of colonial institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, has been a critical determinant of long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Nunn and Wantchekon, further illustrate how the legacies of inequality and slave trades created institutional frameworks that either bolstered elite interests or impeded overall economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This institutional impact is evident in how the slave trades created a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, influences the development of institutions by undermining the trust necessary for effective governance and economic cooperation (Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman further argue that the extreme inequality in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, characterized by policies that protected elite interests, stifled economic growth by limiting the opportunities for broader societal advancement. The lasting effects of these institutions are evident in the enduring disparities in economic development between regions with different historical experiences of inequality and slave trades (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) suggest that the interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments offers a different pathway to long-run economic growth, distinct from the colonial experiences. Kuznets and Mokyr both argue that economic growth is driven by technological progress and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This process involves resolving conflicts between new technologies and existing social structures. Kuznets notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns, requiring significant adjustments in institutions and ideologies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further elaborates on this point by highlighting the role of institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, which is essential for technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.49). These adjustments involve political and social changes as well as technical ones. Kuznets emphasizes the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological advancements do not weaken the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr acknowledges that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements would be unsustainable, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological innovations and institutional adjustments underscores the importance of a coordinated approach to economic development, where institutions facilitate the efficient use of new technologies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) provide a counterpoint to the colonial narratives, highlighting periods of growth in Africa before 1950 that were facilitated by internal institutional factors. They demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding supported by recent historical national accounting efforts. This economic growth was tied to export markets, with the timing and scale of growth varying across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) underscore the significance of real wage trends in understanding long-term African welfare development, suggesting that pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates are crucial for assessing the persistence of poverty. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that growth patterns were closely linked to foreign trade and export markets, indicating that external economic factors also played a significant role in shaping African economic development. The interplay between internal institutional factors and external economic influences underscores the complexity of African economic history, where institutions facilitated growth but were also subject to broader economic forces.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) extend this analysis by examining how colonial education systems in Africa, shaped by distinctive institutional frameworks, influenced long-term economic outcomes. The British colonial administration fostered formal education more extensively than other colonial powers. African political leaders and families actively engaged in cost-benefit analyses, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional context was conducive to developing human capital, which is crucial for long-term economic growth. The growth in military spending in the post-independence era, particularly in response to regional instability and domestic dissent, diverted resources from other productive sectors (Simson 2019, p.11). This shift highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and post-independence economic policies, where military expenditures often displaced investments in public goods and services.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that the contributions of women to African economic growth have been historically significant. They document that during the colonial period, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gender-based division of labor was reinforced by colonial institutions, particularly through the codification of customary law that privileged men. The colonial era introduced a dual legal system, with European courts operating alongside native courts, which further entrenched patriarchal structures. This institutional framework marginalized women and limited their access to land and resources crucial for economic advancement. Hopkins (2009, p.8) suggests that the new economic history of Africa has highlighted the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment. This emphasis on institutions aligns with the findings of Akyeampong and Fofack, who show that the codification of customary law under colonial rule had profound implications for gender dynamics and economic development. The institutional framework shaped by colonial powers thus played a pivotal role in determining who had access to economic opportunities and who was marginalized. This institutional bias had lasting effects on African economic development, as it perpetuated inequalities that hindered growth.

Austin and Ogilvie argue that institutional theories of long-run economic growth oversimplify the historical record by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They argue that distributional conflicts provide a more nuanced explanation for the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. In particular, Austin underscores the importance of colonial administrations responding to indigenous economic and political actions, which shaped institutional prescriptions in ways that contradicted the efficiency-driven models of institutional change. This perspective highlights the agency of local actors and the dynamic nature of institutional development, where colonial powers adapted to local conditions and pressures. For instance, Austin shows that when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial administrations encouraged the expansion of indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between colonial powers and local actors challenges the notion that institutions were merely imposed from above. Ogilvie extends this critique by arguing that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the institutional framework of pre-industrial Europe was a product of ongoing struggles over resources and power. This distributional conflict perspective offers a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolved in response to the complex interplay of local and colonial forces, shaping long-run economic growth in ways that institutional theories often overlook.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay of historical and contemporary factors. Institutions, as North argues, are critical for economic growth because they define the rules of the game that shape economic behavior (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional framework is a dynamic force shaped by historical events and distributional conflicts. The historical evidence shows that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which often prioritize short-term gains over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with Ogilvie's argument that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These findings underscore the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions evolve, as they play a pivotal role in determining long-run economic growth. The reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies, for example, is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin further elaborates on this by showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between institutions and economic behavior highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between the roles of institutions and distributional conflicts lies in the measurement of the institutional changes introduced by colonialism in different regions. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. provides a clear example of how institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, rather than promote long-run growth, have had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
