The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it challenges conventional wisdom that attributes growth primarily to factors such as population, savings, and technology. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely complementary factors. One position emphasizes the centrality of institutions in fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity, while the other views institutions as secondary to other factors like geography and culture. The debate is crucial because it shapes policies aimed at fostering economic development in both developed and developing nations. Institutions, by shaping the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior, can either facilitate or hinder growth. Understanding this relationship is essential for designing effective policies. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development. Some scholars argue that institutions are endogenous, shaped by economic and political forces, while others view them as exogenous, influencing economic outcomes independently. This tension highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they provide a stable framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The Bank of England's role in fostering economic growth highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

This variation in institutional frameworks directly affected the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were not merely a result of geographic factors but were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions highlights how colonial policies can have long-lasting effects on economic growth, shaping the economic landscape of former colonies for centuries.

This evolution of institutions was crucial in establishing secure property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that this institutional development was a response to the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues underscores how political institutions can shape economic growth by providing a framework for secure property rights. These institutions facilitated market exchanges and lowered costs per transaction, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional framework was crucial for the development of a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic growth. This institutional framework not only governed economic exchange but also ensured that the rules were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of their effectiveness. The implications of these institutions extend beyond economic growth, influencing the broader economic landscape by fostering a stable and predictable environment for investment and economic activity.

This perspective challenges the notion that economic efficiency was the primary goal of pre-industrial Europe's institutions, highlighting the role of distributional conflicts (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Distributional conflicts are the result of power struggles between different social groups over the distribution of resources and economic opportunities. These conflicts shaped the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. Ogilvie shows that these institutions were designed to maintain the power and privileges of the ruling elite, rather than to promote economic efficiency. These institutions were the result of political negotiations and compromises between different social groups, and they reflected the balance of power between these groups. Austin notes that the colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe evolved in response to changing economic and political conditions. The institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were not always efficient. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe were not always designed to promote economic efficiency, but rather to maintain the power and privileges of the ruling elite.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) argue that colonial powers influenced formal education systems in Africa and the New World, thereby shaping long-run economic growth. This is because demand for missionary education was influenced by African political leaders' and families' cost-benefit calculations (Frankema 2012, p.15). These calculations were shaped by the institutions implemented under colonial rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) elaborate on this mechanism by highlighting how differences in factor endowments across colonies led to varying degrees of inequality. This inequality, in turn, influenced the institutions that evolved, impacting the course of development. However, the impact of colonial educational policies on long-run economic growth is qualified by the varying effectiveness of these policies across different colonial administrations. For example, while British rule generally promoted education, the specific policies implemented in different colonies could vary significantly in their effectiveness. This variation suggests that the impact of colonial educational policies on long-run economic growth depended on the specific institutional context of each colony.

Technological advancements are a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) both show. This is because the growth of useful knowledge fuels economic development by enabling innovations that enhance productivity and efficiency. For example, Kuznets highlights that technological progress is not only about inventing new tools but also about the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate the adoption and diffusion of these innovations. This perspective underscores that the economic benefits of technological advancements are contingent on the societal ability to integrate them into existing structures. Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge requires institutional progress to complement technological progress, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. However, Kuznets qualifies this by noting that the pace and impact of technological changes vary across different regions and time periods, suggesting that the effectiveness of technological advancements in driving economic growth depends on the specific institutional and ideological context of each society. This variation implies that while technological progress is crucial, its economic benefits are shaped by the institutional environment in which it occurs.

This mistrust, in turn, hindered the development of effective institutions and economic cooperation. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent factor that continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings indicate that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the enduring impact of historical events on current economic conditions. Nunn notes that the slave trade's duration and scale dwarfed the period of colonial rule, suggesting that its effects were profound and long-lasting. The adverse economic impact of the slave trade is compounded by the fact that it disrupted the development of local economic and social institutions, leaving Africa with a legacy of underdevelopment that persists to this day.

Institutions matter in economic growth and development, as shown by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6). North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They show that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets. This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. The historical evidence and empirical comparisons presented in these studies show that institutions shaped by colonialism and distributional conflicts have enduring effects on economic development. This evidence settles the disagreement over whether institutions or geographic factors are the primary determinants of long-run economic growth, demonstrating that institutions play a crucial role.
