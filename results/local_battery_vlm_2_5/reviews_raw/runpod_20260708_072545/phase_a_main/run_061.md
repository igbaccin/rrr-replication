The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, where institutions are defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that shape human interaction. This question is intellectually significant because it addresses the fundamental drivers of economic prosperity and poverty. There are two major positions: one argues that institutions are the primary causal factor in economic growth, while the other posits that economic growth is driven by other factors, such as geography or culture. The causal mechanisms proposed by these positions differ significantly. The first position emphasizes the role of institutions in creating stable and predictable environments that encourage investment and innovation. The second position suggests that other factors, such as geography or culture, are more influential in determining economic outcomes. The debate between these positions is crucial because it shapes our understanding of how to promote economic development in underdeveloped regions. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is essential for designing effective policies that can foster sustainable development. This question involves both economic theory and practical policy implications. The stakes are high, as the outcomes of this debate can influence global economic policies and the lives of millions of people. The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The implication is that while the development of financial instruments and political institutions in England played a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth, the specific institutional arrangements may not be universally applicable, and other factors, such as the commitment of the regime to enforcing rules, also play a significant role. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) contend that European colonizers established different institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional framework, characterized by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities, persisted long after the colonial period ended. These institutions had profound effects on economic development, as they discouraged investment and innovation. This mechanism is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions explains why many former colonies continue to struggle with economic development today. The specific impact of these institutions can vary. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the effects of colonial institutions on economic outcomes can be measured using various indicators of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These findings underscore the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional legacies shape economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) jointly argue that institutional reforms in Africa are pivotal for poverty reduction, though the literature does not directly address long-run economic growth. They highlight that specific institutional interventions, such as improving governance and strengthening the rule of law, can mitigate poverty, but the effectiveness of these reforms depends on the broader historical and structural contexts. This insight underscores the importance of tailored institutional reforms that consider the unique historical and social contexts of African countries. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further elaborate on this by emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. They argue that historical and geographical conditions have cast a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development, implying that structural impediments are not insurmountable. This perspective suggests that while institutional reforms are crucial for poverty reduction, their impact on long-run economic growth may be mediated by historical and structural factors that require further investigation.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary engine of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights the role of technological progress in driving economic growth, as innovations in knowledge lead to advancements in production and efficiency. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized, suggesting that economic growth is contingent on the successful integration of new technologies into existing social structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This integration involves resolving conflicts that arise from the adoption of new technologies. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the benefits of technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) explains that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of political stability in facilitating economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). While technological progress is the primary driver, the ability of societies to adapt and integrate these innovations is critical for sustained economic growth. This adaptation requires not only institutional changes but also a coordinated effort to manage the social and political implications of technological advancements, ensuring that economic growth is both efficient and sustainable.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting contemporary trust levels (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade instilled a deep-seated mistrust among African populations, which continues to shape social and economic interactions. This mistrust is not merely a historical artifact but a pervasive factor influencing modern behaviors and institutions. The legacy of the slave trade has created a climate where individuals are less likely to trust one another, which in turn affects economic development. Trust is a critical component in fostering economic growth, as it facilitates cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and encourages investment. The persistent mistrust in African societies, therefore, hinders the development of robust institutions that support economic activity. Austin highlights that the colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous economic and political action, but the deep-seated mistrust from the slave trade era complicated these efforts. This mistrust undermines the effectiveness of institutional reforms aimed at promoting economic growth, as it erodes the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Consequently, the historical legacy of the slave trade continues to pose a significant barrier to economic development in Africa, as the mistrust it engendered persists in contemporary social and economic structures.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the efficiency-driven view of institutions, which posits that institutions evolve to maximize economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to the New World, showing that societies with greater inequality had institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This distributional conflict approach highlights how institutions can be manipulated by powerful groups to maintain their advantages, rather than evolving to promote economic efficiency. This perspective suggests that understanding the distributional conflicts that shape institutions is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is not always straightforward. This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between distributional conflicts and institutional development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in the literature, with a shared emphasis on the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic development. Institutions matter because they create the necessary conditions for economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn reduce transaction costs and encourage investment. North argues that the development of institutions that enforce property rights and lower transaction costs is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who highlight that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is a key factor in long-run economic growth, as seen in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The institutional framework also influences economic growth through its impact on distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between institutions and distributional conflicts is complex, as shown by the persistence of inefficient institutions despite the potential for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on economic outcomes.

The historical evidence and empirical comparisons used in these studies provide a clear picture of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence shows that the institutional framework of a society, shaped by distributional conflicts and the enforcement of property rights, is a key determinant of economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The case of European colonies illustrates how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn affected long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence settles the disagreement by showing that the institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts and the enforcement of property rights, is a key determinant of economic growth. The empirical comparison of different colonization strategies provides a clear picture of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.
