The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental drivers of prosperity and inequality. Institutions are the rules of the game that structure economic interactions, and their quality can significantly influence a country's economic trajectory. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key determinant, emphasizing their ability to foster secure property rights and economic stability. Others contend that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, where geographic factors or cultural norms also play crucial roles. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective contrasts with views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors, suggesting that the institutional framework is not the sole determinant of economic success. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing that the path to economic development is multifaceted and cannot be attributed to a single factor. This debate highlights the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of economic growth and the role that institutions play within this complex system. The interplay between institutions and other factors, such as geography and culture, remains a central theme in the literature.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. Their work emphasizes that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates and the potential for settlement. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The extractive nature of these institutions significantly hindered long-run economic development. In contrast, colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to more favorable economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the persistence of these institutions has had enduring effects on current income per capita. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) qualify this by noting that the impact of these institutions is robust across various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, and rule of law. This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial times has had a pervasive and lasting influence on economic development, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies long after independence. The interplay between colonial institutions and long-run economic outcomes underscores the critical role that institutional design plays in economic development, highlighting the need for institutions that promote investment and growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) extend this idea by emphasizing that specific institutional developments were crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This reduction in transaction costs is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement is crucial. The size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges, thereby lowering costs per transaction. The institutional changes in England, particularly the creation of the Bank of England, highlight the role of secure property rights in economic development. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their effectiveness. The political factors underpinning economic growth include the regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. This commitment is a critical factor in the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights. North and Weingast's analysis shows that the institutional changes in England were pivotal in fostering economic growth. The institutional framework established during this period continues to influence economic development, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional design on long-run economic outcomes.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share this view. Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that economic growth is fundamentally rooted in the expansion of useful knowledge, which fuels technological advancements. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights that technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustained economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes, requires not only technological progress but also institutional progress to complement it. Without such institutional adjustments, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as technological progress alone cannot ensure long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is essential for maintaining the political fabric of society and ensuring modern economic growth. This highlights that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutional frameworks play a critical role in facilitating and sustaining economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade had a profound impact on African societies, creating deep-seated mistrust that continues to influence contemporary economic interactions. This mistrust is a persistent barrier to economic development. This internal mistrust is at least twice as impactful as external factors, underscoring the depth of its influence on social and economic dynamics. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this by noting that the slave trades in Africa spanned nearly 500 years, a duration far longer than the period of colonial rule. This extended period of exploitation and conflict likely entrenched mistrust within African societies, making it a persistent feature of social interactions. The centralised government and well-developed trading networks of the Kongo kingdom, for instance, facilitated trade with Europeans, illustrating how institutional structures can mitigate the adverse effects of exploitation. However, the broader impact of the slave trade on African economic development extends beyond immediate exploitation, creating a legacy of mistrust that continues to hinder economic progress. The enduring nature of this mistrust suggests that addressing its roots is crucial for fostering long-term economic growth in Africa.

This analysis builds on the understanding that inequality in the New World shaped economic outcomes by influencing the development of institutions that favored elites, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis, along with Broadberry and Gupta's work (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Their joint claim is that the initial factor endowments in New World colonies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, created a context where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power emerged early on. This inequality was reinforced through institutions that concentrated power and resources in the hands of elites, who used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their privileged status (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the way institutions evolved to protect elite interests, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. This dynamic is evident in the unequal distribution of economic gains, where skilled laborers benefited more than unskilled laborers, further entrenching social and economic disparities (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). However, the specific mechanisms by which inequality influenced institutional development varied across regions, with some societies experiencing more pronounced effects than others.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) further complicate this narrative by challenging the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that the historical economic development paths of different regions must be examined separately. These authors suggest that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography, which focuses on secure private property rights as the key to long-term economic growth, needs to account for the varied institutional responses to local economic and political conditions. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the importance of local agency in shaping institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is evident in the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to expectations, illustrating how local conditions can influence institutional prescriptions. This finding underscores the complexity of historical economic development paths, where local responses to colonial policies can lead to varied economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further qualify this pattern by arguing that the persistence of poverty in Africa is not solely due to structural impediments but rather a result of specific historical and geographical conditions that have temporarily affected long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This qualification suggests that while local agency plays a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes, the broader historical and geographical context also influences economic development paths.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with settler colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly impacted long-term economic development. North (1989, p.9) further illustrates that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth. These findings underscore the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the success of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, showing that the regime's commitment to rules was critical for economic growth. This commitment to institutional rules is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who highlight the importance of trust for economic development. Their findings show that both external and internal channels of trust are important, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel. This suggests that the cultural and institutional environment created by colonial rule has lasting effects on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) complements this by showing that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This indicates that local agency plays a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes, even within the broader historical and geographical context.

Austin (2008, p.2) notes that most economists see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialization of Europe. This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6) underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and do not encourage economic development, as they are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. This highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, where the type of institutions established can have lasting effects on economic development paths. The historical evidence of the slave trade causing a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, settles the disagreement. This is evident in the empirical comparison of the internal and external channels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
