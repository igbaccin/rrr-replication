Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms through which they exert this influence are subject to intense debate. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth through secure property rights and those who argue that institutions are shaped by underlying political and social factors. The former group posits that institutions, by providing a stable framework for economic activity, enable long-term growth. The latter group contends that the nature of institutions is determined by the interests of powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term gains over long-term economic development. This debate is crucial for understanding the historical trajectories of economic growth and development, as well as for designing effective policies to promote economic progress. The stakes are high: a clear understanding of the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policy decisions that aim to foster sustainable development and reduce economic disparities. The discussion begins with the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

This role has been extensively studied. Institutions are central to economic growth because they provide the framework within which economic activity occurs. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is evident in the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which significantly lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated long-term economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. build on this by highlighting how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes in European colonies. In some colonies, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The theme of institutions shaping economic growth persists as we turn to the role of distributional conflicts in institutional development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were critical in this regard. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed the trading of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, laying the groundwork for a more stable economic environment. The extent to which these institutional changes were revolutionary is debated. Recent research has shown that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was significantly upgraded, suggesting that institutional changes in England might not have been as groundbreaking as previously thought. This challenges the notion that England's institutional changes were uniquely transformative, implying that other factors, such as bureaucratic structures, might also play a significant role in economic growth.

These frameworks, in turn, were influenced by the institutional legacies of colonial powers, which varied significantly across different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European colonizers implemented varied institutional strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. A key mechanism through which these institutions influenced growth was their impact on property rights and the rule of law, which were crucial for long-term economic development. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, as these institutions shaped the political and economic landscape of the colonies in ways that persisted long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the specific measures of institutions, such as constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all yield similar results. This suggests that the type of institutions matters less than their overall quality in influencing economic growth.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share the view that institutions are not merely efficiency-enhancing mechanisms, but are deeply rooted in the power dynamics and distributional struggles of societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie's analysis highlights how institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by the conflicts over resource distribution. This perspective is complemented by Sokoloff and Engerman's work, which shows that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The implications of this perspective are profound, as it suggests that the persistence of inequality can be traced back to the institutional frameworks established during the early phases of societal development. This perspective underscores how early institutional frameworks continue to influence modern economic growth and development.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that Africa's underdevelopment is primarily attributable to the long-term effects of the slave trades. The slave trades, which spanned nearly five centuries, had profound and lasting impacts on African societies, shaping their economic trajectories in ways that continued long after the end of the trades. The mechanism through which the slave trades influenced economic development involved the destruction of social capital and the disruption of political institutions. The slave trades led to the depletion of human capital, as large numbers of able-bodied individuals were forcibly removed from their communities. This depletion included the direct loss of labor and the erosion of social networks and the disruption of local governance structures. The loss of human capital and the weakening of institutions hindered the development of productive capacities, as communities struggled to rebuild and establish stable political and economic systems. The institutional framework established during colonial rule, however, did not fully compensate for these losses. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could have mitigated some of the adverse effects of the slave trades. This encouragement varied across colonies, and the overall impact of colonial institutions on economic development was limited by the legacy of the slave trades. The persistence of poverty in Africa, therefore, cannot be solely attributed to colonial institutions but must also be understood in the context of the deep and enduring effects of the slave trades.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered the development of formal education, a legacy that distinguishes it from other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This shift was driven by the introduction of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of education, making it more attractive to African families and leaders. This educational development was not uniform across Africa; the timing and scale of growth in educational attainment varied between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, indicating that the educational policies implemented during British rule had tangible economic benefits. The legacy of British rule is complex, and the economic benefits of education were not evenly distributed across the continent. The development of formal education under British rule had long-lasting effects on African societies, shaping economic outcomes as well as social and political institutions.

The growth of useful knowledge is pivotal in driving economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly assert. This perspective posits that technological and intellectual advancements are the primary drivers of sustained economic improvement. Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the efficient use of innovations, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues. The growth of knowledge itself is not merely a function of institutional progress. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that the sustainability of economic improvement hinges on the coordination of technological progress with institutional advancements. Without this coordination, the benefits of technological progress can be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors. This implies that while institutions are important, their role is complementary to the primary driver of economic growth: the accumulation and application of useful knowledge.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different scholars highlighting various aspects of this relationship. Institutions are the rules governing economic exchange and the mechanisms enforcing these rules and allowing them to be changed. This emphasis on the enforcement of rules is crucial for understanding economic growth. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments are examples of institutional changes that reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The expansion of indigenous commodity production and the role of colonial administrations in changing institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action are key examples of how institutions adapt to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation is crucial for understanding the long-run effects of institutions on economic growth.

The interaction between institutions and technological progress is another critical aspect of economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the sustainability of economic improvement hinges on the coordination of technological progress with institutional advancements (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Without this coordination, the benefits of technological progress can be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors. This implies that while institutions are important, their role is complementary to the primary driver of economic growth: the accumulation and application of useful knowledge. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics underlying institutional changes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the role of political and social factors in shaping institutions and their impact on economic growth. The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter in economic growth and development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. The role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs is well-established (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The debate centers on whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this debate is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
