The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, are critical because they influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives and constraints. The live disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions drive economic growth and the mechanisms through which they do so. Some scholars emphasize the direct causal impact of institutions on economic growth, arguing that institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts are essential for sustained economic development. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic growth, and that their impact is often mediated by other variables, such as geography or cultural beliefs. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, with some arguing that institutions are shaped by economic and political forces, while others view them as relatively stable and independent variables. The central question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth remains unresolved, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of economic development in different regions. This question is particularly salient in the context of former European colonies, where the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The reduction in transaction costs and establishment of secure property rights in 17th century England, as highlighted by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29), were pivotal in fostering long-run economic growth, setting a precedent for institutional development in other regions. The development of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This suggests that the institutional changes in England were pivotal in creating a stable environment for economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment to secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms was a key factor in the long-run economic growth of England.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to varied economic outcomes. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in areas with lower mortality, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that these institutional differences persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies hindered economic growth by preventing investment and development. Acemoglu et al. also note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while extractive institutions initially hindered growth, the potential for industrialization could mitigate some of these negative effects. The persistence of these institutions and their impact on economic development highlight the enduring influence of colonial policies on long-run economic growth.

Austin shows that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth must be understood through the lens of diverse historical contexts. Austin highlights that colonial administrations' institutional prescriptions were often responsive to local economic and political dynamics. Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) both stress the importance of local agency and institutional adaptation, which could lead to higher production equilibria despite initial extractive policies (Austin 2008, p.15; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) add a crucial dimension by highlighting the enduring impact of historical traumas, such as the slave trade, on contemporary economic outcomes. This suggests that while local adaptations can mitigate some negative effects of colonial institutions, deep-seated cultural legacies can still hinder long-run economic growth.

The influence of colonial institutions on public goods provision in Africa, as explored by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on economic development. This insight is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. One specific mechanism through which institutions shape economic outcomes is the provision of public goods, which in turn affects investment incentives and development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that "good" institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. However, the relationship between institutions and economic development is complex, and the impact of institutions can vary across different historical and geographical contexts. This deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for evaluating the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary or structural effect on long-term African welfare development. This suggests that while institutional changes can influence public goods provision, the long-run economic growth in Africa is also shaped by a myriad of other factors, including historical and geographical conditions.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). The source of technological progress, the particular production sectors that it affected most, and the pace at which it and economic growth advanced, varied over centuries and among regions of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This mechanism is visible in the emergence of formal institutions aimed at reducing access costs to knowledge and promoting technological innovation. These institutions include scholarly societies and journals that facilitated the dissemination of new ideas and technologies. This institutional progress, while important, is a complement to technological progress. Without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited and in the end might have been frittered away and eliminated by the relentless erosion of rent seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further supports this view by highlighting the necessity of institutional adjustments to efficiently use innovations. Technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and on a community of feeling-in short, the modern national state-plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, institutional adjustments play a crucial role in sustaining and maximizing the benefits of technological innovations.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that these conflicts were the driving force behind the development of institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The pattern of inequality in the New World societies, for example, led to institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective highlights how the distribution of power and resources within societies can lead to institutional arrangements that reflect the interests of dominant groups. However, Ogilvie acknowledges that institutions are not merely the product of distributional conflicts but also serve as tools for economic historians to understand long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). By transforming institutions into indispensable instruments, economic historians can better grasp how these conflicts shape economic outcomes over time.

Institutional arrangements, shaped by historical and political forces, play a crucial role in determining long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they set up extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer. This variation highlights how initial institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic development. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This institutional change reflects increased security of property rights, a critical factor in long-term economic growth. The creation of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is essential for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which in turn supports economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further illustrated by the impact of colonial policies on long-run development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. This dynamic nature is also evident in the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement lies in the measurement of economic outcomes in former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that colonies with extractive institutions have lower income per capita today, directly linking initial institutional frameworks to long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The variation in colonial strategies and their lasting effects on economic development illustrate the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
