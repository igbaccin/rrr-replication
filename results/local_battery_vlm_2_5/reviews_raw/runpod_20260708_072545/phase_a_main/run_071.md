The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and the extent to which they are causal. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement over whether institutions operate as primary drivers of economic growth or merely as facilitators of other underlying factors. One camp argues that institutions directly shape economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and efficient governance. This view posits that institutions reduce transaction costs and foster an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Another camp contends that institutions are secondary to other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, and that their impact on economic growth is overstated. This perspective suggests that institutions are more likely to be endogenous to economic development rather than exogenous drivers. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economies and for designing effective policies to promote economic growth. The following review synthesizes the evidence on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms proposed by different streams of scholarship. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was instrumental in fostering a long-term capital market and driving economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for these institutional developments. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that as political institutions specify and enforce efficient property rights, the costs per transaction decrease, while the aggregate volume of exchanges increases. This dynamic is crucial for economic growth, as it facilitates market exchanges and supports a growing economy. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that the success of these institutions depends critically on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and how these rules are enforced. This commitment underpins the stability and effectiveness of the institutions, ensuring that they continue to support economic growth. The implications of these institutional changes are profound, as they not only foster economic development but also shape the broader political and economic landscape of a nation.

These extractive institutions, which Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show to be a legacy of colonialism, have had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that European colonizers implemented divergent institutional strategies based on mortality rates and resource extraction potential. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This pattern is exemplified by the Congo and the Gold Coast, where institutions were tailored to maximize colonial rents rather than foster long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions post-colonialism is crucial, as they continue to shape the economic trajectories of these regions. This institutional legacy has profound implications for the economic development of former colonies, as the extractive nature of these institutions continues to impede growth and development in many regions. However, the impact of these institutions is not uniform. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while extractive institutions generally hinder economic growth, the specific historical context and opportunities for industrialization can mitigate some of their negative effects.

This historical context underscores how distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share this posture. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by the power struggles and distributional conflicts between different social groups. For example, serfdom and craft guilds can be understood as mechanisms that protected the interests of the ruling elite and privileged groups by restricting competition and maintaining their economic dominance. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. These distributional conflicts often resulted in institutions that limited economic growth by stifling innovation and competition. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight how these institutions, by restricting competition and maintaining the dominance of elites, contributed to the persistence of inequality. The implications are significant, as this perspective suggests that the long-term economic growth of pre-industrial Europe was hindered by institutions designed to maintain the status quo rather than promote efficiency. This dynamic is crucial for understanding the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic development, as it underscores how the institutional framework can be shaped by power dynamics rather than economic efficiency.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that the complex interplay of historical factors in African economic history challenges simplistic views of causation, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution. One specific mechanism by which institutions shape economic development in Africa is through colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic and political action. For instance, colonial governments that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's findings suggest that colonial institutions were not monolithic, and that understanding their evolution requires a nuanced approach that considers local dynamics. This nuanced approach is crucial because it underscores how the institutional framework can be shaped by power dynamics rather than economic efficiency alone. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this by noting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves key questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This highlights the need for further research into the long-term picture of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) together with Nunn (2008, p.2) provide a stark example of how historical events, such as the slave trades, can shape cultural attitudes and economic development over centuries. This mistrust is not merely a byproduct of the slave trade's effect on the trustworthiness of others, but rather a deeply ingrained internal norm. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, and that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel. This internal norm of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as trust is crucial for various economic activities, including international trade and firm management practices. However, Nunn (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that the impact of the slave trade on economic development may have been mitigated in regions with strong institutional frameworks.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlight the pivotal role of technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge in sustaining long-term economic development. Mokyr traces the development of formal institutions designed to reduce access costs to knowledge, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements would be unsustainable. Kuznets elaborates on this by emphasizing the need for institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the political stability necessary for modern economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) underscores the importance of the modern national state in peacefully resolving such conflicts, suggesting that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in maintaining the political fabric of society. However, Kuznets qualifies this by noting that the pace of technological progress and economic growth, as well as the institutional adjustments, differ over centuries and among regions. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in fostering economic growth is contingent on their adaptability to specific historical and regional contexts.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activity. Institutions provide a framework that influences investment, innovation, and productivity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that institutions shaped by colonial powers, such as extractive states in high-mortality regions, have lasting impacts on economic outcomes. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than foster long-run growth, illustrate how institutional design can either hinder or promote economic development. The persistence of these institutions over centuries underscores their enduring influence on economic trajectories. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) further emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are critical for economic growth. The development of institutions like the Bank of England and the consequent decline in transaction costs highlight how institutional changes can facilitate economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights and reducing transaction costs.

The adaptability of institutions to specific historical and regional contexts is crucial for their effectiveness in promoting economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which influenced the path of economic development. This adaptability is essential for institutions to remain relevant and effective in fostering growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) argues that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa demonstrates that institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) suggests that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This highlights the importance of understanding the underlying motivations and conflicts that shape institutional design and their impact on economic growth. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.9) is that institutions matter for economic growth. However, the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth and the specific historical contexts that shape these mechanisms remain debated. The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is particularly contentious. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that extractive institutions designed by colonial powers have had lasting negative effects on economic development. In contrast, Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.
