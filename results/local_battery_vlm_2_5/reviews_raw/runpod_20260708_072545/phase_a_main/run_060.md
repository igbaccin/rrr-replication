The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue among scholars, with some arguing that institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, while others contend that geographic factors, cultural beliefs, or distributional conflicts play a more significant role. The debate hinges on whether institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure economic and political interactions, are exogenous or endogenous to economic growth. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions are exogenous and that their quality determines economic outcomes. On the other hand, others posit that institutions are endogenous and that economic factors shape their development. This disagreement is crucial because it informs policy decisions and our understanding of historical economic development. The stakes are high: if institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, then policies aimed at institutional reform could have profound effects on economic development. If institutions are merely a byproduct of economic factors, focusing on institutional reform might be misguided. The central question remains: what role do institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth? The answer lies in understanding the complex interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography, culture, and distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The Bank of England's establishment in 1694 marked a pivotal moment in economic history, as it introduced new financial instruments that dramatically reduced the cost of transacting. This financial revolution was underpinned by political institutions that specified and enforced efficient property rights, creating a stable environment for long-term capital markets to thrive. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the success of these institutions hinged on the regime's commitment to upholding the rules governing economic exchange, a critical factor in fostering economic growth. However, the evolution of these institutions was not a straightforward process; North (1989, p.6) notes that the transaction costs per transaction would fall as economic institutions facilitated market exchanges, but the aggregate volume of exchanges would increase, reflecting the growing specialization and division of labor. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between institutional development and economic growth, where the reduction in transaction costs is just one facet of a broader institutional framework that supports long-run economic development.

However, the role of distributional conflicts in shaping these institutions in pre-industrial Europe suggests that efficiency was not the sole determinant, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue. These institutions often reflected the interests of the dominant elites, who used them to maintain their control over resources and labor. Similarly, in the New World, the institutional frameworks that emerged were heavily influenced by the initial conditions of inequality. The extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to institutions that favored the elite, restricting competition and reinforcing the existing power structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that perpetuate inequality. The persistence of inequality and the lack of competition could hinder innovation and economic growth over time.

This institutional legacy of colonialism, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33), has had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, persisted and had lasting economic impacts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) illustrate this through various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, which show similar results to those reported in their main findings. These extractive institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, has hindered economic development in many former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, suggesting that the timing and nature of industrialization may have mitigated some of the negative effects of these institutions.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) propose institutional reforms as a means to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. They argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform these reforms, highlighting the crucial role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, suggesting that functional institutions can emerge even in heterogeneous societies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is supported by the work of Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They reconstruct real wage levels to understand long-term economic development and its distribution, aiming to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Instead, they focus on addressing immediate poverty reduction, leaving the deeper structural changes required for sustained economic growth largely unaddressed.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets observes that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional changes is critical for economic growth. This tailoring is essential because technological progress alone is not sufficient for sustained economic growth; it must be complemented by institutional progress to avoid the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further emphasizes the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of political stability in harnessing technological advancements for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Thus, the efficiency of technological progress in driving economic growth depends on the adaptability of institutions to technological changes and the political stability that supports these changes.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa, as shared by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2), illustrates how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, impacting long-run economic growth through persistent mistrust. The mistrust that developed as a result of the slave trade has been shown to significantly affect trust levels in contemporary Africa, a finding that aligns with broader research on the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings underscore that the internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the historical experience of the slave trade, are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust can hinder economic cooperation and investment, thereby affecting long-run economic growth. Austin's examination of colonial institutions highlights that indigenous economic and political actions could influence institutional prescriptions, suggesting that while historical mistrust is a significant barrier, local agency can still shape institutional outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Historical institutions, such as those established during colonial periods, significantly influence current economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, shaped the economic trajectories of colonies, with inclusive institutions fostering growth and extractive institutions hindering it (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Similarly, North argues that the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The shared finding that institutions matter for economic growth is further underscored by Austin, who highlights that indigenous economic and political actions could influence institutional prescriptions, suggesting that local agency can still shape institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were efficient and instead suggests that they were shaped by the interests of powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions were formed and how they continue to influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical experience of the slave trade, for example, has shaped internal norms of mistrust that can hinder economic cooperation and investment, thereby affecting long-run economic growth. Similarly, the new economic history of Africa, as argued by Hopkins, shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. This is evidenced by the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which local agency can influence institutional outcomes. Austin shows that colonial administrations could change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, suggesting that local agency can still shape institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of colonial institutions, which shows that indigenous economic and political actions could influence institutional prescriptions. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium, demonstrating the influence of local agency on institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15).
