The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions, self-imposed codes of conduct), are widely recognized as critical factors in economic performance. A lively debate exists regarding the mechanisms through which institutions shape growth. Some scholars emphasize the importance of institutions in securing property rights and providing a stable framework for economic activity, while others highlight the role of institutions in mediating distributional conflicts and shaping economic outcomes. This debate is particularly relevant in the context of colonial legacies, where the institutions imposed by colonial powers have had lasting effects on economic development. The debate also extends to the relative importance of institutions compared to other factors such as geography and culture. A central point of contention is whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by the interests of powerful groups. This review explores these competing perspectives, focusing on how different institutional frameworks have influenced long-run economic growth in various historical and geographical contexts. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is essential for informing policy and development strategies aimed at promoting sustainable economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The institutional changes in 17th century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), demonstrate that the development of new financial institutions and political structures can significantly reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering long-term economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, marked a pivotal shift in economic governance. This transformation, known as the English financial revolution, was instrumental in enhancing the security of property rights over time. As North (1989, p.6) elaborates, this period saw the development of political institutions that specified and enforced efficient property rights, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. This institutional evolution was crucial for establishing a robust capital market and driving economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) stress that the effectiveness of these institutions depended on the regime's dedication to maintaining the rules of economic exchange. This commitment was not merely about the rules themselves but also about the institutions that enforced and adapted them. The evidence from capital markets underscores this point, showing that the institutional changes in England were pivotal in fostering economic growth, even if the economic performance of England and France was initially comparable. The institutional framework in England provided a stable environment for investment and economic activity, setting the stage for sustained economic development.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, a finding shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Where Europeans settled in large numbers, they created institutions that upheld the rule of law and promoted investment. These institutional differences persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of the colonies. This institutional framework, characterized by extractive policies, hindered economic development in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) underscore that the interplay between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial for the long-term development of these colonies. This suggests that while extractive institutions initially impeded growth, the subsequent industrialization efforts could mitigate some of these negative effects. The legacy of colonial institutions thus continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

This nuanced view challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) both assert. For instance, the presence of European settlers did not always hinder indigenous commodity production; in some cases, colonial governments actively encouraged it. This dynamic is evident in colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments could opt for higher production equilibria when faced with the right incentives, demonstrating that institutional responses were not monolithic. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) add that the legacy of the slave trade also shaped contemporary economic issues, such as trust, which is crucial for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal norm of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, has had a profound effect on African economies, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This suggests that the historical legacies of colonial institutions and the slave trade continue to influence current economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, had lasting effects on economic development. For example, the codification of customary law under colonial rule marginalized women's access to land, which was crucial for cash crop farming and economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.15) further qualifies this by showing that the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities. This suggests that the type of colonial institution mattered significantly in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with British rule being more conducive to formal education than other colonial powers. The variation in institutional frameworks across different colonial powers underscores the importance of specific institutional designs in driving economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) examine various aspects of African economic history but do not directly address how institutions influence long-run economic growth. They focus on factors such as the skill premium, per capita income levels, and historical wage trends. The skill premium, for example, is a critical indicator of long-term economic performance, reflecting the costs and benefits of investing in human capital. The guilds in western Europe played a significant role in the intergenerational transfer of skills, but their monopoly on these skills could also restrict entry and maintain higher wages above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dual role of institutions in both facilitating and hindering economic growth underscores the complexity of their impact. The institutional frameworks that governed apprenticeship contracts and skill development in Europe highlight how specific institutional designs can shape economic outcomes over the long term. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) extend this perspective by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This suggests that the economic performance of African economies was more nuanced and varied than often portrayed, with factors such as foreign trade and political institutions playing crucial roles. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) warn that the absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates creates substantial gaps in understanding Africa's long-term economic development. This underscores the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully grasp the determinants of economic growth in the region.

This perspective is complemented by Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), who propose that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. They argue that technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This process of technological progress is not uniform across regions or time periods. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress is accompanied by significant institutional and ideological adjustments, which vary across different regions and historical periods. He emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets (1973, p.6) adds that resolving growth-induced conflicts is vital for modern economic growth. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver, the institutional framework plays a critical role in managing the societal changes that accompany economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are a fundamental force shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. They propose that the core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds in Europe and elite-advantaging policies in New World societies, are better explained by the struggles over resource distribution among different social groups rather than by efficiency or cultural beliefs. This emphasis on distributional conflicts highlights the role of power dynamics in institutional development. For instance, in pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the ability of dominant groups to maintain their control over resources and labor, despite the potential efficiency gains from alternative institutions. This dynamic is further illustrated by the New World societies, where institutions evolved to maintain the status and power of elites. The persistence of inequality in these societies was reinforced by government policies that restricted competition and limited economic opportunities for the majority of the population. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) add that initial factor endowments, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, also significantly influenced these institutional outcomes. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a crucial driver, the interaction between power dynamics and initial economic conditions is essential for understanding the development of institutions that support long-run economic growth.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by determining the incentives for investment and the allocation of resources. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the long-term economic trajectory of colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, were shaped by the initial conditions and the strategies adopted by European colonizers. Inclusive institutions, which enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, foster long-term economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. Extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources to the metropole, hinder economic development. This dynamic is evident in the differing outcomes of colonies like the United States and the Congo, where the former developed inclusive institutions that supported growth while the latter remained mired in extractive practices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the importance of property rights and transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) highlights that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments reduced transaction costs, facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework provided the security necessary for long-term investments and market exchanges, which are essential for economic development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) complements this view by emphasizing that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, often drive the establishment of core economic institutions. This perspective suggests that the power dynamics and conflicts within societies play a significant role in shaping institutions that either support or hinder economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) further shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This indicates that the interaction between power dynamics and initial economic conditions is essential for understanding the development of institutions that support long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

The historical evidence and empirical comparisons presented in these studies settle the debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise remaining disagreement is whether the initial factor endowments or the institutional frameworks established during colonization played a more significant role. The historical evidence of colonies with high mortality rates adopting extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, shows that institutional frameworks established during colonization played a more significant role (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence and empirical comparison, which Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) have used, settles the disagreement.
