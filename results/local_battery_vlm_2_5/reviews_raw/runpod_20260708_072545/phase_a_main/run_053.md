The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. At stake is the explanation of why some nations thrive economically while others stagnate. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic conditions, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures are crucial for fostering economic development. Others posit that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that factors such as cultural attitudes towards trust and cooperation, or the legacy of historical events like the slave trade, are more determinative. The debate is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and other economic and social factors, making it challenging to isolate the causal effects of institutions. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth is essential for developing effective policies aimed at promoting sustainable development. This question is particularly relevant in the context of historical institutional changes and their enduring effects on contemporary economic outcomes. The debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), since they shape the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. This institutional change facilitated market exchanges and supported the development of a long-term capital market, which in turn promoted economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by the Europeans, providing evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). North (1989, p.9) explains that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges.

North and Weingast, along with Austin, emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the success of seventeenth-century England in fostering secure property rights hinged on the regime’s commitment to and enforcement of rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Austin further argues that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were responsive to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting that the evolution of institutions was not a unidirectional process but rather a dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local economic conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The institutional changes in England facilitated the development of capital markets, which in turn supported economic growth. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic conditions illustrates how the evolution of institutions can shape long-run economic growth.

Mokyr's argument that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge contrasts with the view that institutions are the primary driver (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight the importance of technological advancements in driving economic growth, with Mokyr emphasizing the role of new knowledge and Kuznets focusing on the need for institutional adjustments to accommodate technological changes. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations is crucial for sustained economic growth. Mokyr notes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological innovations are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' observation that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, or "epochal innovations, " have been the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized, as Kuznets explains that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural modes of life and large family patterns. The institutional adjustments needed to support technological progress are not merely reactive but are essential for the peaceful resolution of growth-induced conflicts. This dynamic is further complicated by the potential for rent-seeking behaviors to erode economic improvements, as Mokyr warns that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets' argument that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting technological advancements.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that British colonial rule had a more positive impact on education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that institutional differences shaped by colonial rule influenced long-run economic growth. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities and social mobility. This dynamic was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, who saw formal education as a means to adapt to the changing institutional landscape. This finding suggests that the institutional framework established by British colonial rule, particularly in education, played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing new economic opportunities and social mobility. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered regarding the long-term picture of economic development in Africa.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust operates through both internal norms and external environments, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one. The internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the legacy of the slave trade, create a pervasive environment where trust is diminished, directly hindering economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets and trading networks. This observation underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic growth, even in the context of historical adversity. The legacy of mistrust from the slave trade, however, complicates the establishment of such frameworks, as it erodes the social capital necessary for effective institutional development. This dynamic suggests that the enduring effects of the slave trade on African economic development are not merely economic but deeply rooted in the social and institutional fabric of the continent.

Distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, a perspective shared by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). They argue that these institutions were shaped by the struggle for power and resources among different social groups, rather than by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie emphasizes that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by the conflicts between landlords and peasants, and between different guilds. The merchant guilds, for instance, were formed to protect the interests of merchants against the encroachment of other groups. These institutions were not the result of efficiency considerations, but rather the outcome of power struggles. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this analysis to the New World, showing that societies with greater inequality were characterized by institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is the control over political power. In pre-industrial Europe, the landed elite controlled political power, which allowed them to shape institutions in their favor. This control over political power was a key factor in the persistence of inequality. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) qualifies this pattern by noting that the role of institutions in economic development is complex and multifaceted, and that institutions can have both positive and negative effects on economic growth. The impact of institutions on economic growth is not straightforward, and it depends on a variety of factors, including the specific historical and cultural context.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) underscores the critical role of institutions in long-term economic development. These studies collectively illustrate that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has lasting effects on economic growth, shaping the economic landscape of former colonies for centuries.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) corroborate this view, showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings highlight that institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges are essential for economic growth. Together, these studies demonstrate that the development of institutions that specify and enforce property rights is a key factor in long-term economic growth.

The complexity of institutional effects on economic growth is further illustrated by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to extractive institutions that do not maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.2) adds to this complexity by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not straightforward and depends on a variety of factors, including the specific historical and cultural context. Austin (2008, p.2) also shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which influenced institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This evidence indicates that institutions can have both positive and negative effects on economic growth, depending on the context and the interests of the powerful groups involved. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the empirical comparison of former European colonies, which shows that institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
