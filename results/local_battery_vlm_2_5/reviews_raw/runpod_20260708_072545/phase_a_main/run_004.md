The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding whether and how the rules and norms that govern societies can drive sustained economic prosperity. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One camp emphasizes the role of extractive institutions, which can hinder growth by concentrating power and resources in the hands of a few. Another camp focuses on the importance of inclusive institutions, which promote broader participation and economic opportunity. A third camp argues that the efficiency of institutions, rather than their inclusiveness or extractiveness, is the key determinant of economic growth. Furthermore, some scholars contend that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is mediated by other factors, such as cultural beliefs and historical paths. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they operate in conjunction with other variables. This question is crucial for policy-making and for understanding the complexities of economic development. If institutions are indeed the primary drivers, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could have profound effects on economic growth. If other factors are equally or more important, a more nuanced approach to economic development is needed. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is thus not just academic but also deeply practical, with implications for how societies can achieve sustained prosperity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly argue that institutions that reduce transaction costs are pivotal for long-run economic growth, as they establish secure property rights and enforce market exchanges. This decline reflects increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. Their analysis of capital markets provides evidence that the institutional changes in England were crucial for its economic performance. The size of the transactions sector in the aggregate would be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. This implies that while transaction costs per unit might decrease, the overall economic activity and complexity increase, driving long-run growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show. The nature of these institutions was determined by the Europeans' mortality rates in the colonies, leading to different colonization strategies. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in areas with low mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence, driven by European mortality rates, had profound implications for the long-term economic trajectories of the colonies. This institutional design, in turn, shaped the economic performance of these colonies over the long run. The persistence of these institutions and their impact on economic growth varied across all former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights that while institutions were crucial, their impact was mediated by other factors such as the potential for industrialization.

In Africa, the historical institutions shaped by inequality, as identified by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15), have played a critical role in determining economic outcomes. They show that the colonial era introduced institutions that reinforced existing inequalities, with the elite using their political power to shape policies that limited competition and maintained their dominance. The British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa, however, altered the perceived benefits of formal education by introducing new social and political institutions. These institutions affected the cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders and families, making formal education more desirable. This shift in the value of education was driven by the implementation of new institutions that changed the incentives and opportunities available to Africans (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights the role of British rule in fostering education, suggesting that this was a significant factor in shaping African development. This institutional legacy, while beneficial in promoting education, did not uniformly benefit all sectors of society. The persistence of inequality, as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) argue, meant that the institutions put in place during colonial times often favored the elite, limiting broader economic development. The legacy of colonialism and slavery in Africa thus highlights how institutions shaped by inequality have influenced economic outcomes, with the educational benefits of British rule being one notable example.

Broadberry and Gardner have highlighted that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding shared by Broadberry and Gupta (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This perspective challenges the prevailing narrative that African economies were uniformly stagnant prior to the colonial era. The economic performance of these regions was intricately tied to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This dynamic was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The service sector, in particular, played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, as it does in the modern period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This sector's contribution to economic development was significant, with merchant distribution and finance being key drivers. The high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe, for instance, was as much a result of merchant activities as it was of production. The economic benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled laborers enjoying a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers. The role of institutions in shaping these economic outcomes is not explicitly addressed in this literature. This omission leaves open the question of how institutional factors might have influenced the patterns of growth and divergence observed in these regions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis oversimplifies historical causation by flattening distinct periods and trajectories. The thesis fails to explain why some countries adopted growth-friendly institutions while others did not. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped pre-industrial European institutions. The craft and merchant guilds, for example, were not merely efficient; they were also products of power struggles. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates that colonial administrations sometimes responded to local economic and political actions by altering their institutional prescriptions. This dynamic highlights that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local conditions and power dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that institutions should not be reduced to mere fashion accessories. Institutions are indispensable tools for understanding long-term economic development, and their study requires careful integration into historical research programs. This approach underscores the complexity of institutional change and its role in shaping economic outcomes.

Technological and intellectual advancements are fundamental drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights how the growth of useful knowledge, rather than institutional frameworks, is the mainspring of economic growth. This useful knowledge is generated through innovations, and its diffusion through economies is contingent on institutional and ideological adjustments. Kuznets (1973, p.1) agrees that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, are the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations are not merely technological but also encompass intellectual advancements that transform economic structures. This perspective suggests that technological progress, when complemented by institutional progress, can lead to sustainable economic improvement. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional environment must adapt to support and sustain these advancements. This dynamic interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation underscores the complex relationship between knowledge, institutions, and economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. Institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. North argues that the development of economic institutions that lower transaction costs is a critical factor in economic growth. In particular, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was pivotal in fostering a long-term capital market and economic growth. North and Weingast further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which shows that the regime was committed to enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment to secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is a key factor in sustaining long-run economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of either inclusive or extractive institutions in their colonies. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were often shaped by indigenous economic and political action. For instance, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were not uniform and were influenced by local conditions and actions. The variation in institutional outcomes across colonies underscores the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth.

Furthermore, the role of institutions in economic growth is not limited to colonial contexts but extends to pre-industrial Europe as well. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups and were not necessarily designed to maximize long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. also highlight that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to powerful groups rather than promoting economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the institutional environment must be conducive to economic growth, and extractive institutions can hinder long-run development.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise mechanism by which institutions shape economic growth remains a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific historical evidence that settles this debate is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were a key factor in their long-run development.
