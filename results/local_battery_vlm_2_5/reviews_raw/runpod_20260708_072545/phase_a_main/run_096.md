The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship, with debates centering on how institutions influence economic outcomes over extended periods. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules (constitutions, laws, regulations) and informal constraints (norms, conventions, self-imposed codes of conduct), are central to understanding economic development. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions matter and the mechanisms by which they exert their influence. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide a stable framework that encourages investment and innovation. Others contend that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they evolve organically or are shaped by external forces. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policy decisions and development strategies aimed at fostering sustainable economic progress, especially in regions lagging behind in development. This question is pivotal for distinguishing between the enduring impact of institutions and transient factors that may temporarily affect economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and (2002) (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) agree that institutions are crucial for economic growth. North's work illustrates how institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges can reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth. North (1989, p.6) highlights the development of economic institutions that "specify 'efficient' property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement" as a critical factor in reducing transaction costs. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this idea by showing how different colonial strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. They argue that European settlers in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, established extractive institutions that hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The legacy of these institutions continues to affect current income levels in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic development. The persistence of these institutions underscores the importance of initial institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of economic growth. This process encompasses the invention of new technologies and the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their efficient use. These innovations necessitate adjustments in societal structures, such as the shift from rural to urban lifestyles and the adaptation of family patterns, to accommodate the new technological paradigms (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be limited and potentially eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the interdependence of technological and institutional developments (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that the sovereignty of the state plays a pivotal role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that the political fabric of society remains strong enough to support long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations is essential for sustaining economic growth over the long run.

This interplay is exemplified by North and Weingast, along with Sokoloff and Engerman (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), who highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework enabled the development of markets by establishing rules that governed economic exchange and ensuring that these rules were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. The commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical political factor. Credible commitments were essential for the development of capital markets, as they provided a stable environment for investment and economic activity. This stability was instrumental in driving economic growth, as it allowed for the coordination of efforts and the mitigation of coordination failures. The evolution of these institutions in England contrasts with the institutional frameworks in other societies, such as those in the New World. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that the factor endowments in the New World led to societies with extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, highlighting the importance of initial conditions in shaping long-run economic growth. The implications of these findings are significant, as they underscore the need for institutions that foster secure property rights and credible commitments to drive economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15), shows that institutional change, driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, significantly influences the provision of public goods. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for economic advancement (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, such as colonial rule, which introduced a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the de-privileging of women in access to land, which had implications for economic growth and development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This suggests that while institutional change can drive economic growth, it is crucial to consider the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate and the conditions under which they are effective.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue. This mistrust is not merely a residual effect of historical events but a persistent cultural norm that shapes contemporary social and economic interactions. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that this mistrust is deeply ingrained and significantly influences economic development. This internal norm of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon identify as a key mechanism, is at least twice as large as the external channel of mistrust, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust can hinder economic development by reducing cooperation and social capital, which are crucial for economic growth. This long-term impact is evident in the underdevelopment of economic institutions and social structures that are essential for sustained growth. The historical absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets, as noted by Nunn, exacerbated the economic stagnation caused by the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical legacy of mistrust and institutional underdevelopment continues to shape Africa's economic landscape today.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contend that distributional conflicts shaped the institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie posits that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft and merchant guilds were the product of power struggles and the negotiation of interests among different social groups. Austin further underscores this perspective by noting that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were often responsive to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the role of local agency in shaping institutional outcomes. This focus on distributional conflicts offers a nuanced understanding of institutional development, as it acknowledges the complex interplay of power dynamics and interest negotiations that underpin institutional change. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
