The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that lies at the heart of the debate between neoclassical and institutionalist theories of economic development. At stake is the understanding of the fundamental drivers of economic progress and the mechanisms through which societies achieve sustained growth. A central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinants of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. In one view, institutions are seen as the key to economic development, providing the necessary framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and efficient markets. Some scholars posit that institutions are just one of many factors, and their influence depends on various other conditions. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and the allocation of resources aimed at fostering economic growth. Understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes is essential for designing effective policies. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving a range of factors that include colonial legacies, cultural beliefs, and the evolution of political and economic systems. By examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, scholars can gain insights into the historical and contemporary factors that drive economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the development of institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 1694 significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution facilitated a long-term capital market and sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.9) notes that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the Hundred Years' War was a pivotal moment in this process. The political institutions that specified and enforced property rights were critical for economic development. This suggests that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary. However, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) qualifies this by noting that the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate would be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This implies that while secure property rights were crucial, the broader economic context, including the growth of market exchanges, also played a significant role in long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. These changes were rooted in the differing mortality rates of European colonizers, which led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. Colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subject to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The institutional framework established during colonial rule had long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. The legacy of these institutions persists, influencing contemporary economic performance. This relationship is robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) add that equilibrium institutions may be extractive even if they do not promote economic development. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term gains over long-term growth. Therefore, the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies is a critical factor in understanding their economic trajectory.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, shared by Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenges the oversimplification of causation in different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Austin argues that the thesis fails to account for the diverse institutional trajectories of former colonies, which were influenced by both local and global factors. This critique is supported by Hopkins, who underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and political contexts of African countries to inform institutional reforms and eliminate poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin (2008, p.15) demonstrates that colonial administrations frequently adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies further illustrates the complexity of economic development trajectories, highlighting that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced perspective on institutional change indicates that the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, as noted by Acemoglu et al., results from both colonial policies and complex interactions between local and colonial actors. This perspective implies that institutional reforms aimed at promoting economic growth must be tailored to the specific historical and political contexts of each country.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective suggests that the accumulation of knowledge and its application in technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress often requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. This implies that while institutions may facilitate growth, it is the advancement in technology that drives it. This growth is not merely about the accumulation of knowledge but also about the institutional progress that complements technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.49) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them. This recognition underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting technological progress. This qualification suggests that while institutions are important, they are not the primary drivers of economic growth. Economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress, with institutions playing a supportive role. The implication is that policies aimed at promoting economic growth should prioritize investments in technology and knowledge accumulation, while also ensuring that institutional frameworks are in place to support these advancements.

The slave trade in Africa significantly hindered economic development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) arguing that it fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. Nunn (2008, p.2) elaborates on this by noting that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period much longer than the roughly 75 years of official colonial rule. This extended duration allowed the slave trade to deeply embed detrimental economic and social institutions, which colonialism alone cannot fully account for. The institutional damage wrought by the slave trade is not merely a matter of historical record; it continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, the mistrust engendered by the slave trade is not solely a product of environmental factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), play a crucial role in perpetuating this legacy. This internal mistrust has wide-ranging implications, influencing economic development, political institutions, and firm management practices. The depth of this institutional damage suggests that policies aimed at fostering economic growth in Africa must address the enduring effects of the slave trade, which include the entrenched mistrust that continues to hinder development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) assert that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. These institutions, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, were the product of power struggles and the distribution of wealth and resources. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the entrenched interests of elites who benefited from maintaining the status quo. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) elaborate on this dynamic, showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, highlighting how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that perpetuate economic disparities. The institutional arrangements that emerged from these conflicts had profound implications for economic growth. For instance, the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption were often shaped by these distributional conflicts, affecting long-term economic development. The institutional structures that emerged from these conflicts were not always efficient, as they often favored the interests of elites over broader economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were designed for economic efficiency, suggesting instead that they were the result of power dynamics and distributional conflicts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of extensive scholarly inquiry. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, play a crucial role in economic development. North shows that institutions matter because they determine the costs of transacting, and the emergence of efficient property rights can significantly reduce these costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, had a profound impact on long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption were often shaped by distributional conflicts, affecting long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This highlights that institutions are not merely structures but are deeply intertwined with power dynamics and the interests of various groups within society.

The institutional perspective also underscores the importance of historical context in understanding economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This historical lens is crucial because it shows that institutions are not static but evolve over time in response to various factors, including political dynamics and economic pressures. For instance, the institutional structures that emerged from colonial rule were not always efficient, as they often prioritized the interests of elites over broader economic efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were designed for economic efficiency, suggesting instead that they were the result of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. The new economic history of Africa shows that institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, regardless of the historical context (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North 1989, p.9).

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is a recurring theme. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These insights show that institutions are not always designed for economic efficiency but rather are the result of complex power dynamics and distributional conflicts. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The institutional perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical and political context in which institutions emerge and evolve.
