The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. Institutions are the rules of the game, the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction, and the incentives that determine the outcomes of economic activity. The live disagreement in the literature concerns the degree to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography, culture, or technology, are the primary drivers of economic growth. One position holds that institutions are the key determinant, with different institutional frameworks leading to vastly different economic outcomes. This perspective argues that well-functioning institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and ensure secure contracts, are essential for sustained economic growth. Conversely, another position argues that institutions are important but not the sole determinant, and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographical conditions, also play a significant role. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and development strategies, shaping how economists and policymakers approach economic growth. Therefore, understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth is essential for designing effective policies. Institutions matter because they create the framework within which economic actors operate, shaping incentives and outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) share a view that institutions are pivotal for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, in other colonies, extractive institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which had long-term detrimental effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores that the nature of institutions, whether growth-enhancing or extractive, significantly influences long-term economic development.

This institutional evolution, which fostered secure property rights, was crucial for economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue. In this view, the Glorious Revolution of 1688 was pivotal in establishing credible commitments to property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term economic growth. This institutional change was underpinned by a political settlement that ensured the sovereign was bound by the rules governing economic exchange, thereby fostering a stable environment for investment and growth. This institutional framework fostered domestic investment and drew in foreign capital, thereby boosting economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the impact of these institutions varied across different regions and periods. For instance, in areas where European settlers were not present to compete for local labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, highlighting the contextual nature of institutional impacts (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced understanding underscores that while secure property rights were instrumental in fostering economic growth, their effectiveness was contingent on local economic and political dynamics.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, demonstrate that African economies had higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These findings challenge conventional views on African economic history. The high per capita incomes and real wages were driven by the expansion of export production. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complex interplay between local economic conditions and external factors. The degree to which institutional factors sustained or altered these economic patterns remains uncertain. For instance, the role of colonial institutions in shaping these economic outcomes is not fully elucidated by these studies, leaving room for further investigation into how specific institutional arrangements influenced long-term economic growth in Africa.

Distributional conflicts are a pivotal factor in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, a perspective shared by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). These authors contend that economic institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations. This institutional setup, favouring the elite, restricted economic opportunities for the broader population, thereby influencing long-term economic growth. The role of distributional conflicts and inequality in shaping these institutions is complex. The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiencies, suggests a strong influence of power dynamics and elite interests in institutional design. The implications of this perspective are significant: it highlights the need to consider the political and social context in which institutions emerge and evolve, rather than focusing solely on economic efficiency. This is particularly relevant in understanding the long-term economic growth in regions where institutional arrangements were deeply entrenched and resistant to change.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth. This mistrust is not merely a result of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others but is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. Nunn (2008, p.2) further underscores the historical significance of the slave trade, noting that it spanned nearly 500 years, far outlasting the period of official colonial rule. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade shaped Africa's economic development in ways that are still evident today. The persistence of this mistrust has broader implications for economic development, as it affects various aspects of social and economic life, including trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). However, Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this by noting that sustained trade did not occur until the presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets, suggesting that the impact of the slave trade on economic development was not uniform across Africa. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, is complex and multifaceted, with enduring effects on trust and economic growth that vary across different regions and historical contexts.

Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This technological advancement is not a standalone phenomenon but requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets observes that technological progress necessitates significant institutional and ideological changes, which can vary across regions and historical periods. This is crucial because the major technological breakthroughs that drive sustained economic growth often require substantial adjustments in societal structures. Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the effective use of technological advancements. Kuznets notes that resolving growth-induced conflicts is crucial for modern economic growth. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, the institutional framework that supports and integrates these advancements is equally important for sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the profound impact of colonial institutions on modern economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European settlers established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, which significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, characterized by secure property rights and effective enforcement, facilitated economic development by reducing transaction costs and promoting investment (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England, for instance, led to a financial revolution that enhanced the security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.9) further elaborates on how the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges results in falling transaction costs, which in turn supports long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework not only encourages investment but also resolves conflicts that arise from economic growth, ensuring stability and continuity (North 1989, p.6).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a crucial role in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlights the significance of institutional design in economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.2) complements this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, emphasizing the need to consider different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The expansion of indigenous commodity production in the absence of European settlers and the fiscal security sought by colonial administrations further illustrate how institutional prescriptions can be shaped by local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15) underscores the importance of institutions in mediating the impact of economic and political actions on long-run economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in this regard, as they provided a credible commitment to property rights and market enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that economic actors can rely on stable and predictable institutional frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, which reflects the broader economic impact of secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) adds that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts, which provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The shared belief systems and political legitimacy that underpin institutions are critical for understanding their role in economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity and resolving conflicts that arise from growth. The historical evidence from colonial institutions and the development of capital markets in England shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups can hinder long-run economic growth, while those that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement support it.
