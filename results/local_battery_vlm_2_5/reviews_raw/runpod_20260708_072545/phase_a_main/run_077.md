The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. Some scholars argue that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by providing the framework for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges, thereby fostering growth. Others posit that institutions are merely one piece of a broader puzzle, where cultural beliefs and political choices play equally, if not more, significant roles. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors remains a contentious issue.

For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.29) argue that secure property rights, enforced through credible political commitments, are crucial for economic growth. They highlight the political factors underpinning economic growth, focusing on the institutions that enforce rules and govern their change. This perspective is echoed by North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.9), who emphasizes the importance of transaction costs and the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and supporting economic growth. This view underscores the direct role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for efficient market functioning.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. Inclusive institutions, which enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, were established in colonies where Europeans settled, such as the United States and Australia. Extractive states were established in colonies such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, with the main objective of swiftly transferring resources to the metropole. This distinction highlights how the type of institutions established during colonization can have enduring effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutional legacies underscores the long-term impact of institutions on economic growth.

This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by providing a more secure environment for investment. The degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules is a critical political factor. This commitment ensures that property rights are established and effectively enforced, fostering a stable economic environment. The evolution of these institutions in England highlights how political stability and credible commitment can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. The institutional changes in England were not merely about economic rules but also about the political framework that ensured these rules were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. This political framework was crucial for the long-term economic growth that followed.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and guilds, (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of these institutions can be explained through the lens of distributional conflicts, which ensured that existing power structures were maintained. The community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were all institutions that emerged from these conflicts. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of those in power. The community structure, for example, ensured that resources were distributed in a way that maintained the status quo, while the craft and merchant guilds regulated competition and protected the interests of their members. This perspective highlights the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and power within society. The impact of these institutions on economic growth varied. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This shows that the specific configuration of institutions mattered for economic outcomes. These institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, had lasting effects on economic development, demonstrating the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions emerge.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that the quality of public goods in Africa is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, as dysfunctional governance can persist even in homogeneous societies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Meanwhile, Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British colonial rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, suggesting that the type of colonial institution mattered for educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.15). The mechanism by which these institutional differences translated into long-run economic growth is unclear. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) provide insight into this gap by examining the role of gender dynamics in land access during colonial rule, noting that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered land access pattern, reinforced by colonial customary law, had significant implications for economic development. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched patriarchal alliances, altering the economic landscape in ways that are not fully captured by broad institutional measures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These findings suggest that the specific configuration of colonial institutions, including gender dynamics, played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes in Africa.

The slave trade in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon's work highlights the devastating impact of the slave trade, which engendered a culture of mistrust within Africa, persisting to this day and accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as trust is crucial for various economic activities, including trade and investment. Nunn's research complements this by emphasizing the prolonged duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, compared to the relatively brief period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This temporal perspective underscores the deep and enduring impact of the slave trades on African economic development, suggesting that the legacy of mistrust and institutional degradation persists long after the end of the slave trades. The specific mechanisms through which the slave trade impacted economic development are complex and multifaceted. Nunn and Wantchekon's study shows that the slave trade's effect on trust operates through both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding suggests that the internalization of mistrust within African societies has had a profound and lasting impact on economic development. The historical context of the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, illustrates how the absence of such institutions in other regions may have hindered economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) identify the growth of useful knowledge as a mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes the importance of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts, which can otherwise weaken the political fabric of society. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes the role of the modern national state in peacefully resolving such conflicts, ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates that without institutional progress complementing technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, necessitates not just technological advancements but also the development of institutions that facilitate the dissemination and application of this knowledge. This interplay between technological progress and institutional development is essential for long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights a complex interplay between colonial institutions and local economic development, where the initial extractive nature of institutions could be modified over time by the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profound, with the nature of colonial institutions playing a pivotal role in this process. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.6) show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates. In areas with high mortality, extractive institutions were set up to maximize resource extraction for the metropole. Where mortality was low, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic development. This distinction highlights the direct impact of institutional design on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) builds on this by arguing that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between colonial institutions and local economic development. This suggests that while initial institutions might be extractive, they can evolve over time, influenced by the incentives created by local economic activities. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) further emphasizes the importance of institutions by arguing that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. This perspective underscores that the design of institutions is influenced by the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to institutions that maximize rents for these groups. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development. This highlights the enduring impact of institutional quality on economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development. He shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This indicates that secure property rights and effective enforcement are key to economic growth. The shared finding of the literature is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups often hindering long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The extent to which these institutions can be modified over time by indigenous economic and political action is a point of disagreement. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations seeking fiscal security, and encouraging their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. The historical evidence shows that the initial extractive nature of institutions could be modified over time by the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, as seen in the case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher production equilibrium.
