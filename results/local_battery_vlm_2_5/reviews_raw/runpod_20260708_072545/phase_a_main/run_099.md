The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical and contemporary economic trajectories. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that govern human interactions, can influence economic performance by shaping incentives, reducing transaction costs, and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Institutions matter in economic growth and development. The live disagreement centres around the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. One key position emphasises the direct impact of institutions on economic outcomes, arguing that well-designed institutions can foster growth by promoting investment and innovation. This view is supported by scholars who highlight the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in driving economic development. A competing position focuses on the indirect effects of institutions, suggesting that they shape economic growth by mediating the influence of other factors, such as geography and culture. Proponents of this view contend that institutions can either amplify or mitigate the effects of these underlying determinants, depending on their design and implementation. This debate is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, and for designing effective policies to promote development. The question of whether institutions directly cause growth, or whether they operate through other variables, remains unresolved. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

Building on this, North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets were crucial, as they governed not only the rules of economic exchange but also how these rules were enforced and changed. The regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of this transformation. The impact of these institutional changes varied across Europe. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, challenging traditional interpretations of British and French economic history. This suggests that while institutions played a significant role, other factors also contributed to economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, highlighting that institutional changes alone do not guarantee economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They argue that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. In areas with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic development, as they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than foster growth. In areas where Europeans could settle more safely, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita. The long-run impact of these institutions varied. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact was mediated by other factors, such as the ability to industrialize.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This perspective challenges the notion that ethnic diversity inherently leads to poor institutions, suggesting that other factors, such as governance structures, play a crucial role in shaping institutional outcomes. Their work underscores the importance of understanding the real wage divergence across British Africa, which provides insights into the economic conditions that influenced institutional development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates highlights the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully grasp the determinants of African poverty. The new economic history of Africa, by focusing on institutions and governance, has the potential to inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The effectiveness of these reforms depends on a deeper understanding of the specific historical and geographical conditions that have shaped Africa's economic trajectory.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the economic inequality of New World societies. The paper's central claim is that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's observation that the economic performance of many Anglophone African economies was characterized by levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This suggests that the institutional frameworks in these societies, despite their colonial origins, were capable of supporting economic growth. The persistence of inequality in these societies, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman, indicates that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed. This unequal distribution of wealth and power likely hindered the development of inclusive institutions that could have fostered more sustainable and widespread economic growth. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in New World societies is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both the initial conditions of inequality and the subsequent evolution of institutional frameworks.

The slave trade had a direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development, offering an alternative explanation to those who attribute underdevelopment solely to institutions or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn both argue that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and significantly impedes economic growth. The impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic development is a persistent force that continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes. This internal mistrust, fostered by the slave trade, has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for productive cooperation and economic growth. This long-term impact highlights the enduring legacy of the slave trade and its role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are further compounded by the lack of centralized governance and developed markets in many African societies, as noted by Vansina (Nunn 2008, p.19). This lack of institutional support exacerbates the challenges posed by the slave trade, creating a complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly propose that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial. For instance, technological progress can lead to growth-induced conflicts that need to be resolved peacefully, and the modern national state plays a crucial role in this resolution. This highlights the importance of institutional stability in sustaining economic growth driven by technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that economic reasonableness, which includes institutional progress, is essential for the sustainability of economic improvement. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutional frameworks are necessary to complement and sustain this progress, ensuring that economic growth is not frittered away by rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex relationship between knowledge, technology, and economic growth, where technological advancements are the primary drivers, but institutional stability is crucial for sustaining this growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) jointly contend that the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe was shaped by complex interactions between economic, political, and social factors, which led to diverse institutional outcomes across different regions. This dynamic is further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, were a primary driver of core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective is crucial because it underscores how institutions in pre-industrial Europe were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply embedded in the social and political fabric of societies. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that the institutional evolution in Europe was influenced by patterns of investment and policy experience dating back to the original industrialization, suggesting a long-term institutional path dependency (Austin 2008, p.2). This historical context is important because it shows that the institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe were not static but evolved over time in response to various economic and political pressures.
