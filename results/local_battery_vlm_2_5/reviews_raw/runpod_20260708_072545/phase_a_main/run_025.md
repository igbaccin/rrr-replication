The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of economic prosperity and stagnation. One live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars emphasize the role of secure property rights and the rule of law, arguing that these institutions foster investment and economic development. Others posit that the efficiency of institutions is less critical than their distributional effects, suggesting that institutions may be shaped by underlying social conflicts and power dynamics. The debate extends to the historical context, with some scholars arguing that institutions are path-dependent and shaped by colonial legacies, while others contend that institutional change can be driven by endogenous factors within societies. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, involving both the creation of institutions and their enforcement over time. This complexity is evident in the varying historical experiences of different regions, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is closely tied to the broader issue of economic development and the persistence of poverty in certain regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during this period led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The cost per transaction would fall, but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate would be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This institutional framework underpins the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange. The economic performance of England and France before the French Revolution suggests that institutions per se were not the sole determinant of economic growth. The bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth, indicating that other factors, such as political stability and credible commitment, played significant roles.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were designed for efficiency, and instead posits that they were shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups. For instance, the persistence of serfdom and guilds can be understood as outcomes of struggles between landlords and peasants, or between craft producers and merchants. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were also tools used by dominant groups to maintain their power and control resources. Distributional conflicts thus had a profound impact on the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe, shaping the very rules that governed economic life. However, the role of distributional conflicts extends beyond Europe. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had significant implications for economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that institutions are not merely accessories but indispensable tools in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and power dynamics that shaped institutions, and how these institutions in turn influenced economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) highlight the multifaceted impacts of colonial rule on African economic development, emphasizing the role of women and the influence of education under British rule. The institutional legacy of colonialism, particularly the codification of customary law, significantly shaped economic trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that the quality of public goods and institutions in Africa is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Instead, the effectiveness of institutions hinges on broader governance frameworks that can either uphold the rule of law or perpetuate dysfunction. Frankema (2012, p.15) builds on this by noting that British rule fostered formal education more than other colonial powers, altering the perceived benefits of education and thus influencing economic development (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework, however, was not uniform across Africa. Thus, while British rule had some positive impacts on education, the broader institutional changes often reinforced existing inequalities, particularly for women. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development in Africa, where the benefits of education were not evenly distributed.

The slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2), challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for productive economic activity. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings are bolstered by Nunn's assertion that the slave trades had a profound impact on economic development, lasting for nearly 500 years and affecting the social and economic institutions of the continent. This long-term impact is not merely a result of the immediate disruption caused by the slave trades but also of the enduring cultural and institutional legacies they left behind. The persistence of mistrust, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon, suggests that the slave trades created a deep-seated barrier to economic growth that continues to affect Africa today.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) assert that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. This perspective is supported by the observation that technological progress often requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, recognizing the possibility of coordination failures that necessitate policies to rectify them (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while institutions may facilitate the diffusion of technological knowledge, the primary engine of economic growth is the advancement of technology itself. However, the growth of economic reasonableness, which is closely linked to institutional progress, is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, the institutional framework must evolve to support and sustain these advancements over the long term. The implication is that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent on their ability to adapt to and facilitate technological progress.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that the divergent economic trajectories of former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). A key mechanism in this process is the differential mortality rates of European settlers, which led to the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than foster long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern of colonial institutional development highlights how the initial conditions set by European settlers shaped the long-term economic outcomes of these regions.

The impact of these colonial institutions persists over time, influencing current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this perspective by noting that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between colonial powers and local populations. This dynamic highlights that while colonial institutions were initially extractive, they were not static and could evolve in response to local pressures, which may have mitigated some of the negative long-term economic impacts. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and local economic development, indicating that the legacy of colonialism is not solely determined by the initial institutional frameworks but also by the subsequent adaptations and interactions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme that emerges from the interplay between colonial institutions and local economic development. The institutional framework established by colonial powers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In contrast, colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, saw the development of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional frameworks highlights the direct impact of colonial policies on long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, demonstrates their enduring influence on economic development. Austin supports this view by noting that colonial administrations could adapt their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This adaptability suggests that while initial institutional frameworks were crucial, subsequent interactions and adaptations played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

North emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth by arguing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic development. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Ogilvie, who argues that institutions are indispensable tools for economic historians, underscoring their role in shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie's work highlights that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, often drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. This insight suggests that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule were not merely efficient but were shaped by the power dynamics and interests of the ruling elite (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The dynamic interaction between colonial powers and local populations further complicates the role of institutions in economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This indicates that the legacy of colonialism is not solely determined by the initial institutional frameworks but also by the subsequent adaptations and interactions. These adaptations could mitigate some of the negative long-term economic impacts, as seen in the case of the Gold Coast, where colonial administrations responded to local pressures by changing their institutional prescriptions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins builds on this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. This perspective underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes and the potential for institutional reforms to address poverty and economic stagnation (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The shared finding among these sources is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The precise disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations versus distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence of colonial adaptations, such as the changes in institutional prescriptions in the Gold Coast, settles this disagreement by showing that both efficiency and distributional conflicts played significant roles in shaping institutional outcomes. This evidence highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and local economic development, indicating that the legacy of colonialism is not solely determined by the initial institutional frameworks but also by the subsequent adaptations and interactions.
