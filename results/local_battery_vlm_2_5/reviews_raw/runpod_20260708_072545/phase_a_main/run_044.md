Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, yet the precise mechanisms by which they exert this influence remain a subject of intense debate. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, are more determinative. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established in a region, whether extractive or inclusive, directly impacts economic outcomes. Others posit that the historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate are equally, if not more, influential. This debate is crucial for understanding the fundamental determinants of economic development and for designing effective policies to foster growth. The resolution of this debate hinges on identifying the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic growth and the conditions under which these pathways are most effective. Institutions are shaped by the political and economic contexts in which they emerge, and in turn, they shape the trajectory of economic development. The extent to which institutions can be deliberately designed to promote growth is a key question that underpins this intellectual dispute. This question is particularly salient in the context of colonial legacies and their enduring effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality are a specific type of institution that significantly affected current income per capita. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction rather than fostering long-term economic development. The Europeans' decision to set up extractive states in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, as opposed to investing in institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment as they did in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, had lasting effects on economic outcomes. This pattern of institutional design was driven by the Europeans' desire to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, often at the expense of the local population's economic prospects. The persistence of these extractive institutions over time has been a critical factor in the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize their own rents, rather than promoting long-run growth. This institutional legacy continues to influence current economic conditions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, both of which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political factors underpinning economic growth extend beyond the rules governing economic exchange to include the institutions governing enforcement and change of these rules, with the regime's commitment to these rules being a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The development of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement should manifest in the growth of the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework not only facilitated market exchanges but also ensured that the costs per transaction were falling, which is essential for sustained economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and other scholars (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) show that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more positive impact on formal education compared to other colonial powers. The British colonial administration fostered a unique institutional environment that encouraged the development of formal education systems, driven by the demand for missionary education. The British colonial administration's focus on formal education was more pronounced than that of other colonial powers, such as the French, who often prioritized other aspects of colonial administration, such as resource extraction and military control. This institutional focus on education was a key factor in the development of human capital in sub-Saharan Africa, which in turn facilitated economic growth. However, this focus on education did not always translate into immediate economic benefits, as the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that economic divergence between regions was already pronounced before the 19th century. The authors suggest that this divergence was driven by shifts in economic activity, particularly the role of merchant distribution and finance, which facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe. The service sector thus played a pivotal role in economic growth during the early modern period, contributing to the uneven distribution of economic benefits across different regions. This perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gupta's observation that "the wages of skilled labourers in tables 1 and 2 yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than the wages of unskilled labourers" (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This pattern indicates that economic development during this period was not uniform, and the benefits of growth were concentrated among specific segments of the population. Van Zanden also notes that the focus of economic development shifted to countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England, where economic growth led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift suggests that regional economic dynamics played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. However, this focus on regional shifts and economic activity does not account for the institutional frameworks that underpinned these developments.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are intertwined drivers of economic growth, with innovations requiring efficient use through institutional changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets shows that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, or epochal innovations, are the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. Mokyr explains that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, and that institutional progress complements technological progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. The institutional adjustments that Kuznets discusses are not merely passive responses to technological change but active mechanisms that facilitate the efficient use of innovations. These adjustments help resolve conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies, enabling modern economic growth. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further qualifies this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, and might be frittered away and eliminated by the relentless erosion of rent-seeking. The growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, highlighting the need for continuous institutional adjustments to sustain economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has persisted and impacted economic outcomes. This culture of mistrust is not merely a historical artifact but a living legacy that continues to affect contemporary economic development. The mechanism through which the slave trade shaped mistrust involves both external and internal channels. The external channel pertains to the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, while the internal channel involves individuals' norms of mistrust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel, suggesting that the norms of mistrust are deeply ingrained and not easily altered by external factors. Austin (2008, p.15) complicates this picture by showing that colonial administrations sometimes responded to indigenous economic and political action, which could influence institutional prescriptions. This suggests that while the culture of mistrust is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of economic outcomes. The interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional responses highlights the complex nature of economic development in Africa, where trust and institutional frameworks are intertwined in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of inquiry in economic development. Institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are pivotal in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as seen in the development of the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution which reduced transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, which included secure property rights, was essential for economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). The evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how political factors, such as secure property rights, are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings are supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical impact of institutions is further illustrated by Austin, who shows that colonial administrations sometimes responded to indigenous economic and political action, influencing institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional responses underscores the complex nature of economic development in Africa, where trust and institutional frameworks are intertwined in shaping long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence shows that the development of institutions that enforce property rights and rule of law is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is whether the culture of mistrust is a significant factor or if the impact of colonial institutions is more direct. This historical evidence settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the culture of mistrust is significant but not the sole determinant of economic outcomes, as the interplay between historical legacies and contemporary institutional responses highlights the complex nature of economic development in Africa.
