The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. This question is intellectually significant because it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive economic prosperity over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the causal pathways through which institutions influence growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, arguing that institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for fostering investment and innovation. Another position contends that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to adapt to changing economic conditions and societal needs. A third position highlights the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes, suggesting that institutions are often the result of power struggles rather than efficiency considerations. The debate over these causal pathways underscores the need to understand how institutions evolve and interact with economic growth, as well as the broader implications for policy and development strategies. The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of this relationship. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in 17th century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. These institutional changes were a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War and were critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This institutional shift involved economic rules and the political factors supporting economic growth and market development. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is essential for ensuring that the rules governing economic exchange are enforced and can be changed in a predictable manner. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that European colonizers established different institutions depending on their mortality rates. Where mortality was high, extractive institutions were established to facilitate rapid resource extraction. In contrast, where mortality was low, settlers established more inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This pattern of institutional development had lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further demonstrate that these institutions persisted over time, significantly affecting current income per capita. As a result, the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies were shaped by the initial institutional frameworks imposed during the colonial period. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. This pattern of institutional persistence and its impact on economic growth is supported by the findings of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This highlights the crucial role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they can either facilitate or hinder economic development depending on their design and enforcement.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors. The slave trade in Africa is estimated to have involved the forced migration of over 12 million people, a significant portion of Africa's population at the time. The slave trade's impact on economic development was multifaceted, including the disruption of local economies, the destruction of social institutions, and the loss of human capital. This disruption extended beyond the immediate period of the slave trade, having long-lasting effects on African economies. One specific mechanism through which the slave trade affected economic development was the erosion of trust within African societies. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it can hinder cooperation, reduce investment in public goods, and undermine economic institutions. Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates on the role of institutions in economic development, noting that the Kongo kingdom's centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks were crucial for supporting trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the absence of such institutions in other African societies, possibly due to the disruptive effects of the slave trade, may have hindered their economic development. The enduring impact of the slave trade on economic development highlights the importance of understanding the historical context of economic institutions and the long-term effects of disruptive events on economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are fundamental in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for long-term economic development. The continuous adaptation of institutions to technological progress is crucial for maintaining economic growth over the long term.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) both argue that the institutional frameworks set by colonial powers significantly influenced educational development in colonised regions. Frankema (2012, p.15) demonstrates that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, noting that the demand for missionary education was shaped by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) show that in New World societies, institutions that emerged in contexts of high inequality favoured elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional bias towards elites had long-term effects on economic development, as it limited the broader population's access to education and economic opportunities. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions operated differed across various colonial contexts. This institutional bias towards elites had long-term effects on economic development, as it limited the broader population's access to education and economic opportunities.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) offer nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena in pre-industrial Europe by examining specific institutional arrangements and their impacts, without directly addressing the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the complexity of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over time. This approach highlights that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political actions by indigenous populations, rather than imposing uniform policies. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium in commodity production illustrates how colonial administrations could respond to local incentives. This adaptive behaviour suggests that institutional changes in pre-industrial Europe were influenced by local economic dynamics and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie contends that institutions like serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were shaped by the need to manage conflicts over resources and power. For example, Ogilvie argues that the rules of guilds were motivated by beliefs about the afterlife, highlighting how cultural factors could influence institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This approach underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate, rather than applying universal theories. This nuanced view of institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe provides a more complex understanding of how institutions shaped economic development in the region, offering insights into the mechanisms through which these institutions influenced long-term economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and by influencing the distribution of resources and power. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly impacted long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions determine the extent to which property rights are protected and economic activities are encouraged, thereby influencing economic growth trajectories (North 1989, p.6). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, for example, was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic development.

The specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate are critical in understanding their impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks in their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutions requires considering the specific historical and cultural contexts in which they operate, rather than applying universal theories. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) shows that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups and the historical context in which they operate.

One point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth and development. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). There is a remaining disagreement on the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence long-term economic outcomes. This is countered by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the measurement of income per capita in former European colonies, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
