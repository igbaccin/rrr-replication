The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At stake is the ability to identify the key factors that drive economic growth and development over extended periods. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors, determine economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers, while others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, if not secondary to other causes such as geography or culture. Another point of contention is the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, with some scholars focusing on the role of property rights and transaction costs, while others emphasize the impact of political institutions and the distribution of power. The debate also extends to the historical context in which institutions evolve and their interaction with other factors such as population density and prosperity. These disagreements highlight the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic development. This discussion is critical for understanding the historical evolution of economic systems and for informing policy decisions aimed at promoting economic growth and development. The literature on this topic is extensive and varied, with some scholars focusing on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, while others emphasize the importance of other factors such as geography, culture, and population density. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This development was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the capital markets of the time. They highlight that the political factors underpinning economic growth included not just the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This commitment was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement reduced transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization and division of labor. This process multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional factors in economic development. The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is nuanced, with secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms playing a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the colonization strategies of Europeans varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, which were set up in colonies with low European mortality rates, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Extractive institutions, established in colonies with high mortality rates, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional variation, rooted in European policies, had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by colonial policies, significantly affected the long-run economic growth of former colonies. However, the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were not uniform across all colonies, and the specific mechanisms through which these institutions influenced economic growth varied. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the results hold across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This consistency across different institutional measures underscores the robustness of the institutional framework in shaping long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is primarily driven by technological advancements and the diffusion of knowledge. They observe that technological progress requires complementary institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations, suggesting that institutions are not the fundamental cause of growth. Kuznets highlights that technological progress introduces conflicts with existing social structures, necessitating institutional changes to resolve these conflicts peacefully. This peaceful resolution is crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth. For example, Kuznets notes that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Mokyr's argument is that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the development of institutions that facilitate the use of new knowledge, is essential for sustaining technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr also acknowledges that this institutional progress is not always monotonic or irreversible. This suggests that while institutions are necessary for sustaining technological progress, they are not the primary drivers of economic growth.

This perspective shifts the focus from institutions to the underlying social dynamics, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) propose that the distribution of power and resources within a society determines the institutional structures that emerge. This is exemplified by the way in which serfdom, the community, and guilds in Europe were shaped by the conflicts and power dynamics among different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) also demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This suggests that the institutional landscape is not merely a product of efficiency considerations but is deeply influenced by the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts. For instance, the persistence of inequality in Latin America can be attributed to institutions that were shaped by the interests of the elite, who used their power to maintain their advantage. This perspective offers a critical insight into how economic institutions are not neutral entities but are shaped by the societal power structures that underpin them. The implication of this dynamic is that the institutions that emerge from such conflicts may not always be conducive to economic growth, as they often reflect the interests of dominant groups rather than broader societal efficiency.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This critique underscores the complexity of historical legacies and their impact on contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, altering institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how historical legacies can shape contemporary economic outcomes by influencing the development of institutions over time. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this perspective by demonstrating that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, rooted in historical legacies, can impede the formation of institutions that foster trust, a critical factor for economic growth. This finding suggests that the historical legacies of mistrust can have profound and lasting effects on institutional development, potentially hindering economic growth by creating barriers to trust and cooperation. Understanding these historical legacies is essential for comprehending the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) demonstrate that the legacy of these colonial institutions in Africa is more complex than previously thought. These institutions influenced the provision of public goods and the development of formal education, both crucial for economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa, which focuses on institutional reforms, can inform policies to eliminate poverty. This focus on institutions aligns with the broader literature on economic development, which underscores the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, a crucial factor in development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). The role of institutions is not straightforward. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, which often marginalized women. This legal system, codified under colonial rule, had significant implications for land access and economic activities. For example, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor, shaped by colonial institutions, had downstream effects on economic development. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied depending on the specific policies and practices of different colonial powers. The variation in institutional impacts underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, provide a counter-narrative to the conventional views of African economic history, showing that per capita incomes in Anglophone Africa were above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This finding is significant because it shows that African economies were more dynamic than previously thought, with economic growth driven by foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variation in economic growth across different regions and countries can be attributed to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg further emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that the economic performance of African countries was influenced by a complex interplay of historical, geographical, and institutional factors, which had significant implications for long-term welfare development. The historical evidence of higher per capita incomes in Anglophone Africa before the mid-20th century underscores the importance of considering the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes, and highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of African economic history.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) offer a different lens through which to view European economic development, emphasizing regional shifts and wage dynamics rather than uniform improvement. Van Zanden examines wage data across European cities and regions, concluding that economic development did not correlate with changes in the standard of living during this period. This finding challenges the notion of an 'industrious revolution' in Western Europe. Pamuk reinforces this view by highlighting the long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk's analysis suggests that institutional flexibility played a crucial role in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. This institutional adaptability, in turn, facilitated the rise of urban centers and commercial activities. This economic dynamism was not uniformly distributed across Europe. Van Zanden notes that after 1700, the focus of economic development shifted to countries bordering the North Sea, particularly England. This shift was accompanied by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a complex interplay between economic development and regional price dynamics. The economic growth in England, for example, was characterized by higher wheat prices due to abundant supply and export subsidies, which influenced the relative economic performance of different regions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented, with scholars emphasizing their critical function in economic development. North argues that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is reinforced by Austin, who highlights that the evolution of institutions is key to understanding long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, exemplifies how secure property rights can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). These institutions foster an environment conducive to investment and economic activity, thereby promoting sustained growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly influenced the long-run development of former colonies, demonstrating the power of institutions in shaping economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional legacy has had lasting effects on the economic performance of these regions. Additionally, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework helps explain the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial in the long-run development of these colonies.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into the role of institutions in economic development. Hopkins argues that understanding the historical experience of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who advocates for transforming institutions into indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence from Africa shows that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa highlights the importance of 'good' institutions in providing the necessary incentives for investment and growth. The historical record of Africa demonstrates that the presence of poor institutions is linked to dismal economic performance (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This historical evidence settles the debate on the role of institutions in economic growth, showing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for long-term economic development.
