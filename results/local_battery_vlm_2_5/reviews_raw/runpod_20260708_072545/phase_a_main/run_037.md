The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Understanding this relationship is crucial for explaining the divergent economic trajectories of nations. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and ensure security encourage long-term economic growth. Others contend that the influence of institutions is overstated, and that factors like population density, prosperity, and colonial policies are more critical. There is also a perspective that institutions are molded by underlying cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, with economic needs being a secondary factor. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the complexity of institutional impact on economic development. This review will explore these conflicting views to understand how institutions influence long-run economic growth. North and Weingast, alongside North, argue that the evolution of institutions in 17th-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period significantly decreased transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional development was driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. North and Weingast emphasize that this institutional change was critical for economic growth, as it provided a stable framework for market exchanges and facilitated long-term capital markets. Furthermore, the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which shows that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary. North and Weingast also highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for enforcing rules and ensuring that the regime was committed to these rules. This commitment was a critical factor in fostering economic growth and the development of markets. However, North notes that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges would result in a growing proportion of transactions in the aggregate GDP, as specialization and division of labor increased the volume of exchanges. This implies that while institutional changes were pivotal, the economic landscape also saw increased complexity and transaction volume, which could potentially challenge the efficiency of these institutions.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping the institutional frameworks that persist to this day (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and rule of law, stifled investment and hindered economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. This institutional design, rooted in the colonial era, has left a lasting imprint on the economic landscape of these regions, perpetuating economic underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize the role of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies, which further influenced the institutional frameworks established in these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The effects of these institutions are not consistent across all former colonies. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al., highlights the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic growth. This reversal underscores the importance of understanding the institutional legacies of colonialism in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of these institutions can be understood through the lens of the distributional conflicts that underpinned them. For instance, the craft guilds, which are often viewed as efficiency-enhancing, were actually mechanisms through which artisans secured their economic interests and restricted competition from outsiders. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) provide a parallel account for New World societies, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and thereby contributed to the persistence of inequality. This pattern indicates that the institutions that emerged often addressed the need to manage and mitigate distributional conflicts, with efficiency considerations being a secondary factor. The persistence of inequality and the institutions that maintained it had long-term effects on economic development. These institutions, by their nature, often hindered economic growth by restricting competition and innovation, thereby creating a feedback loop where initial inequality led to institutional arrangements that perpetuated and even exacerbated that inequality. This dynamic highlights the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on long-run economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which can distort the understanding of long-term economic growth in Africa. This quantitative challenge is compounded by the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates, which leaves key questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This gap in data makes it difficult to assess the long-term picture of economic development and the distribution of income, which are crucial for understanding the possibilities of future growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) address this by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, providing a more nuanced view of economic development during colonial rule. This approach reveals that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that economic growth in Africa is more complex and dynamic than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis implies.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade in Africa had a profound impact on its long-term economic development. The slave trade created a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, significantly affecting institutional structures. The slave trade operated over a period of nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, and involved four distinct slave trades, a duration that far exceeds the timeframe of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This extended period of exploitation disrupted local economic and social institutions, hindering Africa's ability to support trade and development. The legacy of the slave trade is evident in the enduring mistrust that has impeded the formation of strong, cohesive institutions necessary for economic growth. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered the development of effective governance and economic policies, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment. The persistence of this mistrust is a critical factor in understanding Africa's economic trajectory, as it directly influences the quality of institutions and their ability to foster growth. It is important to note that the slave trade's impact on trust is not solely attributable to the external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, but also to internal norms of mistrust that were ingrained during this period.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share this view, arguing that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by the observation that major technological breakthroughs, or "epochal innovations," have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations have spread to a substantial part of the world, driving economic growth and necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. The pace and sectors affected by technological progress have varied across centuries and regions. The interplay between technological changes and the institutional adjustments required for efficient use of innovations has been crucial in shaping economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the early periods of industrialization among currently developed countries, where the resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs has been vital for modern economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) notes that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. This underscores the importance of institutional stability in complementing technological progress. The sustainability of economic improvement is contingent on institutional progress that complements technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that without such institutional progress, economic improvements might be frittered away by rent-seeking behaviors, emphasizing the need for coordination failures to be rectified through policies that support both technological and institutional advancements. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for sustainable economic growth.
