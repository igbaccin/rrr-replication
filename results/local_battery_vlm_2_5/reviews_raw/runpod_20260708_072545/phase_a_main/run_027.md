The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies can achieve sustained economic progress. One position holds that institutions, such as property rights and the rule of law, are fundamental drivers of economic growth, while another position argues that the efficiency of institutions is secondary to other factors, such as cultural beliefs and political dynamics. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors. This question is live because the answer determines how policymakers should prioritize institutional reforms in their development strategies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or growth-promoting, significantly influenced current economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on long-term economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional development was crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional change fostered a more stable economic environment, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) observe that the success of these institutions depended on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and how these rules were enforced. This commitment was a critical political factor in underpinning economic growth and the development of markets. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement would lead to falling costs per transaction, with the transactions sector growing as a proportion of GNP. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that institutions were fundamentally influenced by the distribution of power and wealth within societies. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions evolved primarily to enhance economic efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that shared beliefs within a society can form part of its institutional structure, influencing how economic institutions are shaped and maintained. These shared beliefs, however, are not the only determinant; the distribution of political and economic power also plays a crucial role. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and the structure of guilds can be understood as outcomes of distributional conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to the New World, where initial factor endowments and the resulting inequality shaped the development of institutions. The extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in many New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic is crucial because it shows how the initial distribution of resources can lock in institutional structures that perpetuate inequality, affecting long-term economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions are indispensable instruments in economic history, suggesting that while distributional conflicts are important, the specific mechanisms by which institutions shape economic outcomes are complex and multifaceted. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on long-run economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). By contrast, in colonies where Europeans settled, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were set up to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over broader economic development. This institutional framework persisted, influencing the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions affected growth varied. This robustness underscores the pervasive influence of institutional frameworks on economic development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering economic growth. This internal mistrust, which is at least twice as large as the external factors, has significant repercussions for economic development. This finding is supported by Nunn, who argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. This effect is not isolated to the historical period of the slave trades but persists through the enduring mistrust, which continues to hinder economic growth. The historical legacy of the slave trade is a continuing influence on contemporary economic structures.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) find that colonial education in sub-Saharan Africa developed along distinct lines under British rule. British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, a phenomenon tied to the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and social mobility. This process was driven by demand for missionary education, which depended on the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This alteration in perceived benefits was a key factor in the development of formal education systems in the region. This complexity is evident in the varying real wage levels and trends across British African colonies during their period of colonial rule. The long-term picture of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income, is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The reconstruction and comparison of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965) provide insights into the structural effects on long-term African welfare development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered, highlighting the need for further research to fully understand the determinants of the real wage divergence across British Africa and its relation to other parts of colonial Africa.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the historical paths of African economies by compressing different periods and causal mechanisms into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). When European settlers were absent, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with their fiscal security goals. These administrations adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political actions, demonstrating a more nuanced relationship between colonial powers and African economies than a simple institutional framework would suggest (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this perspective by highlighting that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, thereby helping to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the diverse historical contexts and institutional adaptations that shaped African economies. The emphasis on institutional reforms must be balanced with an acknowledgment that 'good' institutions alone are not sufficient for development; they must also provide incentives for investment, which is essential for economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as institutions provide the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, which maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting economic development, can persist and significantly impact current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For example, institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, such as those established by European settlers in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, have led to higher income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North argues that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is because such institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering a growing economy (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for instance, reflect increased security of property rights and have been instrumental in economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in providing the necessary incentives for investment and economic development. The historical evidence shows that the persistence of extractive institutions can be traced back to colonial policies, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by the observation that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence shows that institutions that were seemingly favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others, highlighting the complexity of institutional development (Austin 2008, p.2). In addition, Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the diverse historical contexts and institutional adaptations that shaped African economies. The emphasis on institutional reforms must be balanced with an acknowledgment that 'good' institutions alone are not sufficient for development; they must also provide incentives for investment, which is essential for economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The shared finding that institutions matter in long-run economic growth is clear (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions affect growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can persist and significantly impact current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence of colonial policies and their impact on institutional development in former colonies settles this disagreement. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
