The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. Institutions, as the rules of the game, can either foster or hinder economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that shape individual and collective behavior. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or economic policies play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods have enduring effects on economic development, while others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing growth. The debate also involves whether institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts, and how these dynamics interact with economic outcomes. Institutions actively shape economic growth through their impact on property rights, transaction costs, and the enforcement of contracts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of financial institutions and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial innovation reflected an increased security of property rights, which is crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political model outlined by North (1989, p.9) suggests that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War was instrumental in this process (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further elaborate on this by emphasizing the role of political institutions in specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces costs per transaction (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of these institutions involved both economic rules and the political mechanisms that ensured these rules were enforced and could be adapted over time. This institutional framework was critical for the remarkable success of England's capital markets, as evidenced by the significant upgrade in France's economic performance before the French Revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The development of these institutions fostered a stable environment where economic growth could flourish, underscoring the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in long-run economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. Colonial powers established institutions that maximized their short-term gains. For instance, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, has had lasting effects on economic outcomes. This institutional legacy has significantly impacted the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views on African poverty and economic development by examining historical data and re-evaluating the impact of colonial policies. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their work underscores the importance of understanding the long-term dynamics of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income and the role of historical conditions. This approach extends the analysis to indigenous economic and political action, revealing that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local incentives. This dynamic is evident in the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial policies adapted to local contexts, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. This responsiveness highlights the complexity of colonial institutions and their varied effects on economic development.

This focus, however, risks overlooking the broader institutional context that underpins these interventions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that the impact of colonial institutions on African development has been multifaceted, with varied effects on economic outcomes. Hopkins discusses that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. The presence of poor institutions is linked to Africa's economic record, but the relationship is complex. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa has informed institutional reforms and poverty reduction efforts. Hopkins shows that the role of institutions has become prominent in development policy, with 'good' institutions providing incentives for investment essential for development.

Frankema shows that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. Colonial institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions. The implementation of these institutions depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. Colonial institutions adapted to local contexts, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. The varied effects of colonial institutions on African development highlight the complexity of institutional impact on long-run economic growth. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes in Africa.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is a persistent cultural norm that influences contemporary economic interactions. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade's impact on trust is primarily internal, shaped by individuals' norms of mistrust. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors, suggesting a deep-seated cultural impact that transcends immediate historical context. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade likely entrenched mistrust within African societies, creating a cultural legacy that continues to influence economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) qualifies this impact by highlighting that sustained trade and economic development were possible in regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom. This qualification suggests that while the slave trade fostered mistrust, the presence of strong institutions could mitigate its economic impact, pointing to the complex interplay between historical events and institutional strength in shaping long-run economic growth.

This perspective shifts the focus from purely economic factors to the political and social dimensions that underpin institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggest that institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by the need to manage conflicts between different social groups. The institutions in these societies were structured to maintain the power of elites and to manage the conflicts that arose from the unequal distribution of wealth and resources, creating a system that perpetuated inequality. This system is exemplified in the institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were designed to manage distributional conflicts, ensuring that elites maintained their control over resources. The guilds, for example, restricted competition and maintained the power of elites by controlling access to markets and resources. This approach contrasts with the idea that institutions are designed for efficiency. These institutions restricted competition and maintained the power of elites, contributing to the persistence of inequality. The institutions of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were designed to manage conflicts between different social groups and to maintain the power of elites. This approach suggests that institutions are designed to manage distributional conflicts. In New World societies, the institutions were shaped by the need to manage conflicts between elites and other social groups. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and power. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) describe how European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional frameworks. In some cases, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In others, they established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9), who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth and development by providing secure property rights and effective enforcement, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in managing distributional conflicts is also crucial. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) both highlight that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the need to manage conflicts between different social groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for core economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, and the craft guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) points out that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions were designed to manage conflicts and maintain elite control over resources (Austin 2008, p.15). This approach shows that institutions are designed to manage distributional conflicts, ensuring that elites maintain their control over resources.

The persistence of inequality is another key aspect of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) both show that institutions restricted competition and maintained the power of elites, contributing to the persistence of inequality. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional frameworks, which in turn affected economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) provides examples of how colonial administrations adapted their policies to maintain control, further reinforcing the role of institutions in perpetuating inequality (Austin 2008, p.15). The shared finding is that institutions designed to manage conflicts and maintain elite control have a lasting impact on economic development and inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

Institutions play a key role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and power, managing distributional conflicts, and perpetuating inequality. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are designed to manage distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement is whether institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in colonization strategies and the resulting institutional frameworks, which show that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
