The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate, with implications for policy and historical interpretation. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture, drive economic outcomes. One position argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while another contends that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geography and culture playing significant roles. A third position posits that the impact of institutions is mediated through other variables, such as trust and social norms. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they operate through other variables, is central to this discussion. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. They actively shape economic outcomes, not merely serving as a backdrop to economic activity. One key mechanism through which institutions influence growth is by reducing transaction costs. As North (1989, p.9) explains, the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights can dramatically lower the costs of market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This reduction in transaction costs facilitates economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this argument by showing that the type of institutions established in former European colonies significantly impacted their long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) observe that institutions' impact varies across different contexts. They report that in regions with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions that persist to this day, significantly affecting current income per capita.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) underscore the significance of credible commitment in economic growth, arguing that the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange was a critical political factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment fostered a stable environment where property rights were secure, encouraging investment and economic activity. They argue that the institutional environment in New World societies, characterized by extreme inequality, advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the initial conditions and strategies of colonization, including the establishment of institutions, play a crucial role in shaping the institutional environment and, consequently, long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) assert that African poverty was not a persistent phenomenon due to structural impediments, and many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This perspective challenges the notion that Africa's economic underperformance was inevitable. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These variations highlight the importance of specific historical and geographical conditions in shaping economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that the absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This gap in data underscores the need for further research to fully understand the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and its relation to other parts of colonial Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that the reversal of fortune thesis, while highlighting shifts in relative incomes among former European colonies, does not directly illuminate the role of institutions in fostering long-run economic growth. He notes that most economists see the behaviour of older industrial economies in the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe. In a similar vein, Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This implies that understanding the historical evolution of institutions in Africa can guide current institutional reforms aimed at fostering economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the institutionalist approach encounters challenges in quantitative testing and explaining the adoption of certain institutions in some countries. Therefore, while the reversal of fortune thesis provides a framework for understanding shifts in relative incomes, it does not fully capture the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development in Africa, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to factors like colonialism or more recent events. This impact is not merely an artifact of the slave trade's effect on the trustworthiness of others. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trades caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply ingrained, functioning through individuals' internal norms as well as their external environments. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This historical context underscores the significance of the slave trades in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, while official colonial rule lasted only about 75 years. This temporal disparity highlights the enduring impact of the slave trades on Africa's economic development. The legacy of the slave trades in Africa continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, shaping the continent's development path in profound ways.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, contrasting with the view that institutions are the primary driver (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is complemented by Kuznets, who points out that technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets highlights that technological progress and economic growth are intertwined, with the pace and impact of technological advancements varying across centuries and regions. This interplay between technological progress and economic growth requires institutional changes to resolve conflicts and ensure sustainable growth. Mokyr further elaborates on the role of technological progress, noting that coordination failures can impede economic improvement. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure the sustainability of economic growth. Mokyr notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. Therefore, while technological advancements are a significant driver of economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on the complementary role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining economic improvements.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and sustainability of economic improvements. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutional differences, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are critical for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is supported by North's argument that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which facilitated secure property rights and financial transactions, was instrumental in economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework encourages investment and creates a stable environment for economic activities, which is essential for sustained growth. Furthermore, North and Weingast highlight the importance of political institutions that ensure secure property rights and enforce economic rules, which are fundamental for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underscores the idea that institutions that provide credible commitments are essential for long-term economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The role of institutions in economic growth is further emphasized by Hopkins, who argues that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Hopkins 2009, p.8) underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise role of institutions in economic growth remains a subject of debate. Austin qualifies the role of institutions by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is complex and multifaceted.
