The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, shape economic incentives and constrain behavior, thereby influencing economic outcomes. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who argue for the significance of other factors, such as geography or culture. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlight the critical role of institutions in fostering economic growth, while Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) question the singular focus on institutions, pointing to the complexities and multiple factors influencing economic development. The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms behind economic growth can inform policy decisions and development strategies. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide a historical perspective on how institutions governing public choice can foster secure property rights and economic growth. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play a more significant role. This question is pivotal in shaping our understanding of economic development and the design of effective policies to promote growth.

The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which indicates a commitment to property rights. This commitment was underpinned by political factors that ensured the regime was bound by the rules governing economic exchange. North (1989, p.9) observes that economic growth leads to an increase in the transactions sector, mirroring greater specialization and division of labor. This implies that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on the broader economic context and the ability to adapt to changing conditions. Institutions play a dynamic role in economic growth, necessitating ongoing adaptation to safeguard property rights and encourage long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that distributional conflicts, not efficiency concerns, drove key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the persistence of institutions like guilds in medieval Europe can be attributed to the power of elites who benefitted from the restrictions they imposed. These institutions were structured to preserve the status quo and safeguard the interests of the powerful. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that in New World societies, institutions were shaped by the unequal distribution of resources and power, leading to policies that advantaged elites and restricted competition. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality and the failure of some economies to develop. The influence of these institutions on economic growth is complex. This tension between short-term elite interests and long-term economic growth is a critical aspect of how institutions shape economic development. The persistence of inequality in these societies suggests that the institutions that emerged from distributional conflicts had a profound and lasting impact on economic trajectories.

The institutions established by European colonizers varied widely based on their mortality rates and settlement patterns. Where Europeans settled in large numbers, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In areas where Europeans encountered high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that these extractive institutions persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions endure because they were created to maximize benefits for European colonists, not to stimulate long-term economic growth. This is because the institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over long-term economic gains. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that the impact of these institutions is robust across various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, and the efficiency of the judiciary. Their research indicates that the long-term effects of colonial institutions stem from the institutional legacies of European colonizers, not just geographic factors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the economic performance of African economies during the colonial era has been misunderstood. They contend that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the prevailing narrative of persistent poverty. This economic growth was primarily driven by export production, which was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports. The timing and scale of this growth varied significantly between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) support this view by reconstructing and comparing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. Their findings suggest that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, as recent literature has emphasized. Instead, the economic development of these colonies was influenced by a range of factors that affected the distribution of income and long-term welfare development. The influence of these factors on long-term economic growth is still debated.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust. This mistrust is a product of both external and internal factors. The external factors are rooted in the historical context of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and created an environment of distrust. The internal factors, however, are more profound and enduring. They stem from the psychological and cultural legacies of the slave trade, which have embedded mistrust deeply within African societies. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates this by showing that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which in turn influenced institutional prescriptions. This dynamic reveals how historical events can shape contemporary institutional frameworks, even when those events are not directly related to the current economic context. Mistrust persists, a living legacy that continues to shape African societies today.

The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1), (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks, with Kuznets (1973, p.1) noting that modern technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Institutions facilitate the spread and use of new knowledge, not drive growth directly. This dynamic is evident in the historical interplay between technological progress and institutional development, where institutions adapt to accommodate new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, or "epochal innovations", constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. This complementary relationship underscores the importance of institutions in supporting technological advancements, which in turn drive economic growth. The implications of this relationship are significant, as it suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their role is secondary to the growth of useful knowledge. The sustainability of economic growth, therefore, depends on the continuous interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce contractual obligations. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.6), who highlights how the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement led to a decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during this period reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The interplay between institutional development and economic growth is further underscored by the fact that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years War (North 1989, p.9). Furthermore, the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, as well as the falling costs per transaction, show that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The importance of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. In some cases, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, while in others, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional frameworks had significant long-term effects on the economic development of the colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the results with other covariates are very similar, using a variety of alternative measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, an index of law and order tradition, a measure of property rights, a measure of rule of law, and the efficiency of the judiciary. The consistency of these results underscores the robustness of the institutional framework in shaping economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The thesis emphasizes the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by the Europeans, showing that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the concept of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) maintains that institutions are essential tools for every economic historian. She emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs. Austin (2008, p.2) shares this view, highlighting that most economists see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialization of Europe. This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that these beliefs can significantly impact economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. In the context of the development experience of the former colonies, this implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical evidence and empirical comparisons that show how different institutional frameworks led to varying economic outcomes. The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are the primary driver of economic growth or if they are secondary to the growth of useful knowledge. North (1989, p.9) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth by fostering secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. The historical evidence of the Bank of England's creation and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution settle the disagreement, demonstrating that institutions are crucial for economic growth, but their role is secondary to the growth of useful knowledge.
