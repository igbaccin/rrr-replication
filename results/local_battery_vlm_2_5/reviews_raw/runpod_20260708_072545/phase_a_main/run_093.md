The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or merely secondary to other factors. One live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions directly shape economic growth by providing the necessary framework for investment and economic activity. Others posit that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and political factors, and that their impact on growth is mediated through these factors. Institutions are seen as both causes and effects in complex feedback loops that drive economic development over the long term. This interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the historical and contextual factors that shape institutional development. The debate underscores the need to understand how institutions are formed, how they evolve, and how they interact with other economic and political factors to influence long-run economic growth. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing effective policies that can promote sustainable economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. establish that extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality are a specific type of institution that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their work demonstrates that European colonists established extractive states in regions with high mortality rates, aiming to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, persisted and had lasting effects on economic development. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where the institutional framework was not conducive to investment and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further elaborate that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from the existing rent-seeking structures, thereby inhibiting institutional changes that could foster economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. also highlight that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the enduring impact of these institutions on economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, both critical factors for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional evolution was a product of fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also stress the importance of a regime's commitment to these rules, highlighting that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This emphasis on credible commitment extends beyond the economic realm. North (1989, p.6) argues that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, alongside falling transaction costs, would result in a growing proportion of GNP being allocated to the transactions sector. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was therefore pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, illustrating how institutional changes can foster economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over resources and power. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this view to New World societies, showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutions in ways that hinder economic growth. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that institutions should be treated as an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, suggesting that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how distributional conflicts and government policies interact with institutions to shape long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, as presented by Austin (2008, p.2); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2), examines how historical patterns and colonial influences have shaped Africa's economic development. This perspective highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional frameworks in response to indigenous economic and political actions, rather than imposing uniform policies. Austin notes that colonial governments often encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent, and adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner corroborate this by showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied based on local conditions and adaptations. However, Broadberry and Gardner also point out that the growth in export production was tied to foreign trade and was influenced by a range of factors, including political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This qualification underscores that while colonial institutions played a role, their effect on economic growth was mediated by broader economic and political contexts.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that women played significant roles in Africa's economic growth and development, while the impact of colonial powers on education varied. Akyeampong and Fofack's focus on women's contributions reveals that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law in a way that privileged men. The British, in particular, influenced the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.1) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. This is because the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education. Hopkins (2009, p.18) concludes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, and that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was mediated by broader economic and political contexts. The role of women in economic development and the varying impacts of different colonial powers on education highlight the complex interplay between gender, colonial rule, and institutional development in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade not only decimated populations but also eroded social cohesion, creating a pervasive mistrust that persists today. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. The internalization of mistrust within African societies has been particularly detrimental. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) posit that this internal mistrust is at least twice as impactful as external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust has deepened the challenges faced by African economies, making it difficult to establish the institutional frameworks needed for sustainable growth. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, extends beyond immediate economic losses to include long-term institutional and social deficits.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective is anchored in the idea that advancements in technology and knowledge are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth. Kuznets highlights that technological progress is not compatible with traditional rural modes of life or extended family patterns, necessitating significant institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate efficient use of innovations. These epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, require societal adaptations to resolve conflicts and ensure political stability. Mokyr elaborates on this by recognizing the potential for coordination failures in the absence of institutional progress, which could undermine the sustainability of economic improvements. He emphasizes that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress is crucial to complement it and prevent the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors. The interplay between technological and institutional progress is complex, with technological advancements often outpacing institutional adaptations. This dynamic is illustrated by the growth of useful knowledge and the establishment of formal institutions designed to reduce access costs, as seen in the proliferation of scholarly societies in Europe between 1600 and 1799. However, the growth of economic reasonableness is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress, indicating that while technology drives growth, institutions play a critical role in sustaining it.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and norms that govern economic interactions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted the long-term economic trajectories of colonies. This is evident in the contrasting outcomes between colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, such as the United States, and those where extractive states were set up, like the Congo. The differences in institutional frameworks directly influenced current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of early institutional choices. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) reinforces this by highlighting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but by the presence of progressive institutions. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18) demonstrates that the institutional environment is crucial for economic development, as it provides the incentives necessary for investment and growth.

The creation and enforcement of property rights are central to understanding how institutions drive economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the development of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century exemplify how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and facilitate economic growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who emphasize that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth. The historical evidence from the colonial period shows that institutions designed to extract resources from colonies hindered their economic development, further illustrating the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) complements this by noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions are dynamic and can evolve based on local conditions. This dynamic nature of institutions is crucial for understanding their role in long-run economic growth, as it shows that institutions can be adapted to promote economic development under the right conditions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) reinforces that institutions are shaped by shared beliefs and distributional conflicts, which can either promote or hinder economic growth depending on the institutional framework in place.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical and empirical evidence presented by these studies. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are shaped by exogenous cultural factors or by endogenous political and economic processes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from the colonial period and the empirical comparisons of income per capita between colonies with different institutional frameworks settle this disagreement. The case of the United States, where institutions that enforced the rule of law were established, versus the Congo, where extractive institutions were imposed, shows that endogenous political and economic processes are more influential in shaping institutions than exogenous cultural factors.
