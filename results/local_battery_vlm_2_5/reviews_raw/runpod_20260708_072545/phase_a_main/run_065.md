The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high as institutions govern the rules by which economic activity occurs, and their design can either facilitate or hinder growth. The debate centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One position holds that institutions matter fundamentally because they structure incentives and constrain behavior in ways that either promote or impede economic growth. Another position argues that institutions are merely epiphenomenal, reflecting deeper structural factors such as geography or culture. A third position suggests that institutions evolve in response to economic development, which is endogenous to it. Understanding which view is correct has profound implications for policy, as it determines whether institutional reform can be a lever for economic growth. The evidence suggests that institutions do indeed play a crucial role in economic development, but the nature of this role and the mechanisms through which it operates remain hotly debated. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. North (1989, p.9) traces this dynamic to the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically lowered transaction costs. These institutional changes reflect an increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. This institutional evolution was both an economic and a political phenomenon, as the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues resulted from the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that a critical political factor in this process was the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing them. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which shows that England's economic performance was due to both its institutional changes and the political factors underpinning them. This institutional framework was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for economic activity.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. Extractive institutions, which focused on resource extraction and rapid transfer to the metropole, were established in colonies with high mortality rates. Settler colonies with low mortality rates developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence, driven by European policies, had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions over time significantly affected current income per capita, illustrating the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional design, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, resulted in extractive institutions that hindered economic development. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is a direct result of the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) both highlight how institutions shaped economic development by influencing the distribution of wealth and power. The institutional arrangements in New World societies with greater inequality often favoured elites through policies that restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. This institutional bias towards elites, which was particularly pronounced in regions with abundant land and natural resources relative to labour, created a political environment where elites could maintain their dominance by controlling access to resources and opportunities. These institutional frameworks hindered economic growth and prevented the emergence of a broad-based middle class that could drive innovation and entrepreneurship. The persistence of inequality in these societies was thus deeply rooted in the institutional structures that favoured the elite. Pamuk (2007, p.16) also notes that institutional flexibility, as seen in late medieval Holland, could facilitate rapid economic responses to new opportunities. This suggests that while institutional arrangements that favour elites can hinder growth, institutional flexibility can mitigate these effects by allowing for more dynamic economic adjustments. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is therefore complex, influenced by both the distribution of power and the flexibility of institutional frameworks. The institutional change in African colonies was influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, impacting the quality of public goods provided by African governments, as noted by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights how the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shift in education demand was pivotal as it influenced the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The availability of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence has limited the extent to which Africa appears in comparative historical work on economic growth and development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge simplistic causal narratives by highlighting diverse historical paths and institutional evolutions, offering a more nuanced view of economic development. This approach underscores the complexity of African economic history, which cannot be reduced to a single reversal of fortune. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, a critique that extends to Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. Specifically, Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, leading to institutional prescriptions that responded to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic is evident in the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional responsiveness suggests that colonial policies adapted to local contexts, challenging the notion of a uniform reversal of fortune. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further complicate this picture by arguing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, revealing a more nuanced economic landscape than previously acknowledged (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This deeper understanding of real wage divergence across British Africa helps assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development.

The slave trade directly impeded Africa's economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust, and is not merely a result of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a duration far exceeding that of official colonial rule. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted economic and social institutions, hindering Africa's ability to develop robust trading networks and centralized governance structures. This erosion has had profound implications for Africa's economic trajectory, as the lack of trust and weak institutions continue to impede economic growth and development.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks, as discussed by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49). The development of new technologies and the dissemination of knowledge lead to increased productivity and economic growth, as technological progress drives the expansion of useful knowledge. This process involves the sovereign state playing a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological advancements are integrated into the economic system without excessive costs. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex relationship between knowledge, technology, and economic growth, where the growth of useful knowledge is fundamental to economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions are crucial for specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn reduces transaction costs and fosters economic growth. This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), who demonstrate that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment has been pivotal in the economic development of certain colonies. These findings highlight that the institutional framework supports and drives long-term economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and market exchanges.

The historical evolution of institutions further illustrates their impact on economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) show that the political factors underpinning economic growth, such as secure property rights and credible commitments, are essential for the development of markets and sustained economic progress. This is evident in the case of seventeenth-century England, where institutional changes fostered a credible commitment to property rights, leading to significant economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is linked to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which shaped long-run development trajectories.

Institutional reforms can also play a critical role in addressing contemporary economic challenges. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution and its impact on economic outcomes. The shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Austin 2008, p.15) is that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and efficient market exchanges (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some scholars emphasizing the role of colonial institutions (Austin 2008, p.15) and others focusing on the political factors underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence of seventeenth-century England and the empirical comparison of former European colonies settle this disagreement by demonstrating that credible commitments to property rights and institutional changes introduced by colonialism are crucial for long-term economic development.
