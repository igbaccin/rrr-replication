The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development studies. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules and informal norms, can significantly influence economic outcomes. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions drive economic growth primarily through efficiency considerations or through distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that efficient institutions, characterized by secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, are crucial for economic growth. Others contend that distributional conflicts, stemming from the power dynamics among different groups, are the primary drivers of institutional change and, consequently, economic growth. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable development. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is closely tied to the broader question of how societies evolve and adapt over time. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on the underlying mechanisms. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) report that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth. This development of economic institutions facilitated market exchanges, which in turn made a growing proportion of the economy's transactions more efficient. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a pivotal moment in this process, as it led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution, which was underpinned by the creation of new financial instruments, reflects increased security of property rights over time. This dimension is critical to both the long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North and Weingast also note that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the capital markets of the time. The political factors underpinning economic growth included the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. The regime or sovereign's commitment to these rules is a critical political factor. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that the success of these institutions varied across Europe. They point out that if England and France were almost at parity in economic performance, the clear implication is that institutions per se, and in particular, the institutional changes we have described, were not so revolutionary after all. This suggests that while institutions played a crucial role in fostering economic growth, other factors such as bureaucratic structure and economic performance also played a significant role.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established institutions that varied widely based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This variation led to different institutional frameworks, ranging from inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, in turn, shaped economic outcomes in the colonies. This institutional framework persisted even after the colonial period, continuing to affect economic development in the former colonies. The persistence of these institutions varied across all colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. They highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, suggesting that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism had lasting effects on economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The legacy of these colonial institutions is evident in the current economic performance of many former colonies, where the extractive nature of these institutions continues to hinder growth and development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). They jointly challenge the narrative that Africa has been perpetually poor by revealing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This evidence suggests that Africa’s economic performance was not as stagnant as previously thought. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence, supports this view (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variation in economic growth across different African regions highlights the importance of local conditions and institutions in shaping economic trajectories. This nuanced view of African economic history contrasts with recent literature emphasizing structural impediments, offering a more dynamic picture of Africa's economic development.

The slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) arguing that the slave trades had long-lasting effects on trust and economic institutions. The slave trades disrupted African societies, creating a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. One of the key mechanisms through which the slave trades impacted economic development was by fostering mistrust within African societies. The slave trades led to a significant reduction in intergroup trust, which in turn hindered economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings show that the slave trades reduced trust both internally and externally, with the internal effects being more pronounced. This reduction in trust is a significant barrier to economic development, as trust is crucial for economic transactions and the functioning of markets (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trades also had a lasting impact on economic institutions, as the disruption of traditional social and economic structures hindered the development of effective institutions. Nunn's research also highlights the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, and its relative length compared to the colonial period, which lasted approximately 75 years. The enduring impact of the slave trades on economic development is evident in the persistence of mistrust and weak institutions. Furthermore, the slave trades also affected the ability of African societies to engage in sustained trade, as the disruption of traditional social and economic structures hindered the development of effective institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). This highlights the importance of understanding the long-term effects of historical events on economic development.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible than institutional progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that the pace of technological progress and the institutional adjustments vary across regions and centuries. This variation implies that the efficiency of institutional adjustments is contingent on the specific historical and geographical contexts. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving conflicts induced by technological progress, but this role is not always straightforward. For instance, the conflicts arising from technological progress can weaken the political fabric of society if not managed properly. This underscores the importance of effective institutional frameworks in harnessing technological progress for sustained economic growth.

Distributional conflicts, more than efficiency, provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) jointly argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, thereby altering institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This mechanism underscores the importance of local economic and political contexts in shaping institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) elaborates on this by arguing that institutions are indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian. This perspective is crucial for understanding how institutions evolve and adapt to changing economic and political landscapes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that institutions are shaped by shared beliefs and cultural values, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutions, culture, and economic development, suggesting that a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth requires a nuanced analysis of these interconnected factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profound, as they create the framework within which economic activities unfold. Institutions govern the enforcement of property rights, the structure of political decision-making, and the broader economic environment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional setups. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast saw the establishment of extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights how the nature of institutions can significantly influence economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) further underscores the importance of institutions by arguing that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions is deeply intertwined with cultural and political factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs and cultural values play a crucial role in shaping institutions, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is aligned with North's argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize their own interests over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interplay between institutions, culture, and economic development suggests that a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth requires a nuanced analysis of these interconnected factors.

Institutional change is often driven by the need for credible commitments, which are essential for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the necessary conditions for economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical record, which shows that the evolution of institutions is a complex process influenced by a variety of factors. While there is agreement that institutions matter, the precise mechanisms by which they influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as evidenced by the development of financial institutions in England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the measurement of transaction costs and their impact on economic growth, as shown by North (1989, p.6).
