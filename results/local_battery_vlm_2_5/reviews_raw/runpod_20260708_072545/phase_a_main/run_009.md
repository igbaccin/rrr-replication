The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. The stakes are significant because institutions are the rules of the game that structure economic interactions. The live disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. One position emphasizes the role of extractive institutions in perpetuating economic stagnation. Another position focuses on the importance of property rights and secure transactions in fostering economic development. A third position highlights the impact of political factors on the evolution of institutions and their subsequent effects on economic growth. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for designing effective policies that promote sustainable economic development. The key to resolving these debates lies in disentangling the causal pathways through which institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional transformation was driven by the development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional change was both an economic adjustment and a political one, reflecting a trade of certain rights to Parliament for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The political commitment to these rules was crucial, as North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize, for the regime's credibility in enforcing them. The evolution of these institutions fostered a long-term capital market, which was essential for economic growth. The evidence from capital markets supports this, as the development of markets and the rules governing economic exchange were underpinned by political factors that ensured the regime's commitment to these rules. However, the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as traditionally thought, as recent research has shown that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was significantly upgraded, indicating that institutions per se were not the sole determinant of economic growth. This suggests that while institutional changes were critical, other factors also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They argue that the European settlement patterns and the resulting institutions were pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. In colonies where Europeans settled in large numbers, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In contrast, in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, as seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The institutional legacy of colonialism, therefore, played a crucial role in determining the long-run economic growth of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) note that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups, who designed them to maximize their own rents rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional framework, characterized by extractive practices, hindered economic development in many former colonies.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts led to the creation of institutions that served the interests of the powerful, even if they were not efficient. For example, craft guilds were designed to protect the interests of skilled artisans, but they also restricted competition and limited economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that in New World societies with greater inequality, elites used various government policies to advantage themselves, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies were designed to protect the interests of the powerful, even if they were not efficient. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were designed to promote economic efficiency.

To better understand these historical economic performances, Broadberry and Gardner, along with Broadberry and Gupta, delve into specific case studies. These studies highlight that economic growth and development in these regions were influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, which challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. These patterns of growing and shrinking were tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports, as well as to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This suggests that while institutions played a role in shaping economic growth, other factors were also crucial. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This divergence was driven by high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe, which arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This highlights the role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period, as in the modern period. However, the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled labourers yielding a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled labourers. This uneven distribution of economic benefits suggests that while institutions may have facilitated growth, they also contributed to inequality. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) report, “Historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa Despite its central importance in the modern economic history of most regions, there has until recently been relatively little use of national accounting in the economic history of Africa ( Manning 1987 : 51).” Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) report, “Note, further, that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe may be seen as arising through merchant distribution and ﬁnance as much as through production.”

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades, rather than institutions, were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, challenging the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, left a lasting impact on Africa's economic development. The slave trades disrupted African societies, leading to a loss of human capital and the destruction of social institutions. This disruption was significant because it prevented the development of complex, centralized political structures that could support trade and economic growth. For instance, the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the absence of such structures in other parts of Africa, due to the slave trades, hindered economic development. However, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the impact of the slave trades may have been more nuanced. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, and tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This implies that the impact of the slave trades on economic development may have been mediated by colonial policies and the responses of African societies to those policies.

The role of technological and intellectual advancements in driving economic growth is central to understanding long-run economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) share a view that the growth of useful knowledge underpins economic growth. This perspective posits that technological progress, through its role in the generation of new ideas and innovations, is a primary driver of economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, indicating that while technology is a primary driver, its effects are mediated through institutional frameworks. Mokyr (2005, p.49) suggests that technological progress can lead to coordination failures, which can only be rectified by institutional progress. This implies that while technological advancements are fundamental, their impact on economic growth is conditioned by the institutional environment. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that the effectiveness of institutions in managing technological change is critical for sustained economic growth. This points to a nuanced relationship between technological progress and institutional frameworks, where the latter facilitates the former's contribution to economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate technological progress and manage growth-induced conflicts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, illustrating how institutions can either foster or hinder economic development. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The importance of secure property rights is further emphasized by North and Weingast (1989, p.29), who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of their effectiveness (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscores the critical role of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic growth.

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. They emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments in England shows that the creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is crucial for sustained economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Austin (2008, p.2) is the development of capital markets in England, which provides a striking indication of the effectiveness of institutions in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
