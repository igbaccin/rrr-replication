The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic development. Others posit that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that factors such as population density, prosperity, or the specific historical contexts of colonization and industrialization are more determinative. Additionally, there are rival explanations that highlight the role of distributional conflicts and shared beliefs in shaping economic institutions. The debate centres on whether institutions are the key to understanding long-run economic growth or if they are merely one piece of a complex puzzle influenced by a multitude of factors. This question is especially pertinent for understanding the economic trajectories of former European colonies, where the legacies of colonial institutions continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth. Institutions influence economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution reflects an increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both capital market development and economic growth. The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights should manifest in the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction fall while the size of the transactions sector grows, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the success of these institutions is underpinned by the regime's commitment to enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional stability is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth may be more nuanced and context-dependent.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Specifically, European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and the ease of settlement, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction for the metropole. These institutions, characterized by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities, persisted over time and had lasting effects on economic development. This institutional framework, shaped by the interests of the colonizing power, significantly influenced the economic trajectory of former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also highlight that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted. This interplay between institutional frameworks and industrial opportunities underscores the nuanced role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that the ability of societies to innovate and apply new technologies is a crucial element that propels economic growth. This interplay is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where societies that successfully resolved conflicts arising from technological changes and adapted their institutions accordingly experienced sustained growth. For instance, the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, played a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Thus, the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress and mitigating coordination failures is essential for long-term economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting and sustaining the benefits of technological advancements, thereby shaping economic outcomes over the long run.

This historical context is crucial for understanding the broader implications of institutional frameworks on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is driven by both internal norms and external environments, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel. This pattern of mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has profound implications for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.19) highlights that the Kongo kingdom's centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks facilitated trade with the Europeans, suggesting that robust institutions could have mitigated the adverse effects of the slave trade. The broader impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic development extended beyond institutional factors. The slave trade disrupted social and economic structures, leading to a legacy of underdevelopment that persists to this day. This legacy includes the erosion of trust and the disruption of economic and social institutions, which are crucial for sustainable economic growth. The slave trade's long-term effects on Africa's economic development underscore the importance of addressing the historical roots of underdevelopment, including the legacy of mistrust and institutional disruption.

Sokoloff and Engerman show that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions shaped economic growth by creating barriers to entry for new competitors, thereby limiting market competition and innovation. The unequal distribution of political power reinforced these economic barriers, as elites used their influence to maintain policies that favored their interests. This dynamic is evident in the way factor endowments, such as land and natural resources, were distributed unequally from the outset, leading to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these inequalities over time had profound effects on the institutions that evolved, particularly in terms of property rights and financial sector structures. Frankema (2012, p.15) extends this by showing how the colonial legacy of inequality affected educational outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa, where the demand for missionary education was influenced by the new social and political institutions implemented under British rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the institutions shaped by colonial policies maintained economic inequality and influenced the development of human capital, a critical factor in long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the view that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors. Both authors highlight that the colonial legacy has had a profound impact on African institutions, shaping their development and influencing long-run economic growth. This perspective underscores that the quality of institutions, whether good or bad, plays a crucial role in providing incentives for investment and fostering development. The authors suggest that understanding these long-term economic trends is essential for assessing future growth possibilities. The persistence of poverty in Africa is deeply rooted in the institutional legacies of colonial rule, not solely due to structural impediments. The specific impact of these institutions on economic growth can vary. For instance, while some institutions may hinder development, others can facilitate it by providing the necessary incentives for investment and innovation. This variability underscores the complex relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth in Africa.

Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts were central to the formation of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Shared with Austin, this perspective highlights that the evolution of institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds was driven by the need to manage conflicts over resources and power (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions emerged as solutions to distributional conflicts, where different groups sought to protect their interests through formalized rules and structures. For example, guilds provided a means for artisans to control entry into their professions and maintain high standards, thereby protecting their economic interests. This mechanism of institutional formation was driven by the need to manage and resolve conflicts between different social groups, not efficiency alone. Austin's argument that colonial governments adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions underscores this point. Colonial administrators who sought fiscal security and stability often adapted their policies to align with the interests of local populations, demonstrating how institutional change can be influenced by the need to manage distributional conflicts (Austin 2008, p.15). This process is not straightforward. Ogilvie cautions that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted, noting that institutions can both facilitate and hinder growth depending on the context and the specific conflicts they address (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This complexity suggests that while distributional conflicts are a key driver of institutional evolution, the impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth is not always predictable. This nuanced view of institutional evolution in Europe highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical and social contexts in which institutions emerge.

This institutional framework provided the necessary conditions for market exchanges to flourish, thereby driving economic development. These institutional changes were economic and political, reflecting the power dynamics and interests of the colonizing powers. This perspective underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, where institutions are both a product of and a driver for economic development. The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is not a simple relationship. The case of colonial institutions demonstrates that the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth is influenced by the specific historical context and the interests of powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
