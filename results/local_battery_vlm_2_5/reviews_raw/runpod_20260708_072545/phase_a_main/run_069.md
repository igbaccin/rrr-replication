The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. Institutions are the rules of the game that structure political, economic, and social interactions, and the live disagreement concerns whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or secondary to other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological change. A key aspect of this debate is the extent to which institutions can be shaped by historical and political processes, and whether they can adapt to foster economic growth over the long run. This debate has significant implications for understanding both past and present economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are a crucial factor in determining economic growth, and that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast, however, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The debate is not merely academic: understanding the role of institutions in economic growth has practical implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. The stakes are high, as the outcomes of this debate can influence how governments and international organizations approach economic development, with potential impacts on global poverty and inequality. The scholarly disagreement is between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, and those who argue that other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological change, are more important. The disagreement is not merely about the relative importance of institutions, but also about the mechanisms through which they operate and the extent to which they can be shaped by historical and political processes. The question is not just what institutions are, but how they work and how they can be changed to foster economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. These divergent institutions, shaped by historical and political processes, had lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They contend that equilibrium institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. This perspective highlights the enduring influence of institutions on economic outcomes, even after the colonial period.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. These institutions were pivotal in creating a stable environment where economic activities could thrive. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) discuss how the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and the subsequent constitutional changes led to a significant shift in political dynamics. This shift ensured that the monarchy was bound by the rules, fostering a credible commitment to property rights and economic growth. However, this institutional framework was not universally beneficial; Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) note that in societies characterized by extreme inequality, such as those in Latin America, institutions often advantaged elites. This dynamic restricted competition and hindered economic development. The differential outcomes in economic growth between societies can be traced back to the initial conditions and the institutional frameworks that evolved from them. These frameworks, shaped by historical and political processes, had lasting impacts on economic development, illustrating how institutions can both foster and impede growth depending on their design and enforcement.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the fundamental drivers of economic growth, according to Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These authors argue that the advancement of technology, along with the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate its efficient use, are crucial for sustained economic growth. Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets (1973, p.6) supports this view, noting that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby enabling modern economic growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for economic development. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, suggesting that while technological advancements are steady, the institutional framework must continually adapt to support economic growth. Thus, the dynamic relationship between technology and institutions is pivotal for long-run economic growth. This relationship underscores the importance of not only technological innovation but also the adaptive capacity of institutions in fostering economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) examine specific factors influencing African economic development, such as the contributions of women and the historical determinants of poverty, and thus address the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth only obliquely. The historical determinants of poverty and the contributions of women are key factors that have shaped the trajectory of African economic development. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities, is one such determinant. This process was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and the introduction of a dual legal system that privileged men. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) document how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system, where European courts operated alongside native courts. This system, combined with customary law, often codified the subordinate status of women, limiting their economic participation. For instance, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor, reinforced by colonial institutions, had profound implications for economic development. However, it is important to note that the economic contributions of women were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This suggests that the institutional framework, while limiting, did not entirely erase women's economic agency. The historical determinants of poverty, as explored by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), further illustrate the complex interplay between institutions and economic development in Africa. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income. This understanding is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This internal mistrust, in turn, is a significant barrier to economic development. The internal norms of mistrust that developed during the slave trade have persisted over centuries, shaping African societies in ways that continue to hinder economic growth. Nunn supports this perspective by highlighting the enduring impact of Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, on the continent's economic development. The extensive duration of these slave trades, coupled with the internal mistrust they fostered, has left a lasting imprint on Africa's economic trajectory. The long-term mistrust has been a significant barrier to economic development. The internal norms of mistrust have impeded the development of strong institutions, which are crucial for sustained economic growth. This mistrust has made it difficult for African societies to build the necessary social capital and institutional frameworks required for economic progress.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are pivotal in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie and Austin argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were driven by distributional conflicts rather than by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's examination of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe underscores how these institutions were products of power struggles and distributional conflicts rather than efficiency-driven developments. Austin's analysis of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further illustrates that the path of institutional evolution was shaped by political and economic actions that responded to indigenous pressures. These actions were often driven by the need to maintain fiscal security and manage the interests of local populations. This highlights how institutional development can be a response to the need for stability and control, rather than purely efficiency-driven. However, Austin notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation process reveals that while distributional conflicts are a significant driver, the specific outcomes of these conflicts can be influenced by the local context and the actions of governing bodies. This dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional adaptation underscores the complexity of institutional development in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, with various scholars emphasizing different aspects of this relationship. Institutions not only facilitate economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms but also play a pivotal role in the political economy of growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). For instance, the Bank of England's establishment in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution reflects the increased security of property rights over time, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The political model outlined by North highlights how the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of fiscal crises, underscoring the importance of institutions in economic development (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is further supported by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that the adoption of different colonization strategies by Europeans led to the establishment of distinct institutions in their colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly impacted the long-run economic performance of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the work of Austin and Acemoglu et al. Austin notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation process reveals that while distributional conflicts are a significant driver, the specific outcomes of these conflicts can be influenced by the local context and the actions of governing bodies (Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This highlights the complex relationship between institutions and economic outcomes, where political power and rent-seeking behavior can shape institutional design and economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The complexity of institutional development is further underscored by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their success is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of the role of institutions in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie adds that institutions are shaped by shared beliefs and distributional conflicts, providing a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations, highlighting the importance of political and social factors in institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes remain a subject of debate.
