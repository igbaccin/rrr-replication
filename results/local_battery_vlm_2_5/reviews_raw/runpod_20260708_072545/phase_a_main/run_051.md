The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and why some economies flourish while others stagnate. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure social interactions, drive economic growth. One position emphasizes the causal power of institutions in determining economic outcomes, highlighting how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder growth. This perspective is often linked to the idea that institutions shape incentives, which in turn influence economic behavior and productivity. A rival position, however, suggests that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic growth, and that other variables, such as geographic factors or cultural beliefs, may play a more significant role. This debate underscores the need to carefully examine the mechanisms through which institutions operate and the conditions under which they exert their influence. The resolution of this debate has far-reaching implications for policy and economic theory, as it informs our understanding of how to promote sustainable economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments, such as the English financial revolution, were pivotal in this process. These institutions and the consequent decline in transaction costs reflected an increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This security was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This institutional evolution involved the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. North (1989, p.6) notes that as economies grow, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This dynamic suggests that while institutions reduce transaction costs, the complexity of economic interactions also increases, potentially requiring further institutional adaptations to maintain efficiency.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) posit that European policies led to different colonization strategies, resulting in either inclusive or extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, influenced economic outcomes by affecting investment and resource allocation. This focus on short-term gains led to extractive institutions that hindered economic development. For instance, in colonies with high European mortality rates, colonizers often implemented extractive institutions to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. These institutions persisted long after colonization, continuing to impact economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also acknowledge that the design of these institutions was influenced by the political power dynamics and the reluctance of colonizers to initiate changes that would not directly benefit them. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, the specific outcomes were also contingent on the political and economic contexts of the colonies.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The thesis posits that the economic success of former colonial powers and the underdevelopment of former colonies are due to the differential impact of European colonial institutions. Austin's work demonstrates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not fully account for the complexities of institutional evolution. Austin shows that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, and did not impose uniform extractive institutions. This adaptation was evident in cases where European settlers were absent, and colonial administrations encouraged indigenous commodity production to ensure fiscal security. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to the expectations of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view highlights that colonial institutions were not monolithic but evolved in response to local contexts, thereby influencing long-run economic growth in varied ways. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further elucidate how historical legacies, such as the slave trade, shaped contemporary economic outcomes through the development of a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of colonial institutions and historical events, such as the slave trade, thus continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes by influencing social norms and institutional frameworks.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight the critical role of institutions in shaping African economic development, noting that while institutional quality significantly impacts the provision of public goods, the historical legacies of colonial rule have often led to dysfunctional institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions, shaped by colonial policies, have influenced the allocation of resources and the development of social norms, which in turn affect economic outcomes. For example, colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that often reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary laws, which marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) illustrate that despite the marginalization of women, they still made significant contributions to economic growth, particularly in the pre-colonial and colonial periods, by engaging in food farming and cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This highlights that while colonial institutions may have hindered overall economic development, they also created opportunities for certain groups to contribute to economic growth. The enduring legacy of colonial institutions thus continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes by influencing social norms and institutional frameworks, even as it opens pathways for economic contributions from marginalized groups.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments, not institutions as the fundamental cause, are crucial for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is evident in the historical record, where societies that successfully navigated these changes experienced sustained economic growth. Without such institutional adjustments, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments thus reveals the dynamic nature of economic growth, where institutions must evolve to accommodate technological innovations for sustained development.

Distributional conflicts, not institutional efficiency, fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contends that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were the product of power struggles among elites rather than efficiency-driven adaptations. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of institutions that favored elite interests, such as those that secured property rights but limited broader economic participation. The influence of distributional conflicts is further illustrated by the enduring impact of inequality on institutional development. For instance, in New World societies, the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped institutions that maintained these disparities over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the role of power dynamics in shaping economic institutions, as these dynamics can lead to institutional arrangements that are not necessarily efficient but serve to maintain the status quo.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate economic transactions. North argues that institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic growth by specifying property rights and lowering transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Austin, who emphasizes that institutions are crucial for long-term economic growth and that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the complexity of historical institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). Both scholars highlight that institutions are not static but evolve over time, influenced by political and economic factors. The creation of institutions such as the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, the development of financial instruments and the specification of property rights reflect increased security and efficiency, which are critical for economic growth. This shared finding shows that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth by creating an environment that encourages investment and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; Austin 2008, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is not always straightforward. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may not always prioritize economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, in New World societies, the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped institutions that maintained these disparities over time. This dynamic illustrates that institutions can be designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates further underscores how initial conditions and power dynamics can influence institutional development and economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and influenced by a variety of factors, including power dynamics and distributional conflicts.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement between the efficiency-driven and distributional conflict perspectives is the case of the New World societies. Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to significant differences in current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison demonstrates that the initial conditions and power dynamics that shaped institutions in these colonies had a lasting impact on their economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies further illustrates how institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence highlights the importance of understanding the role of power dynamics in shaping economic institutions, as these dynamics can lead to institutional arrangements that are not necessarily efficient but serve to maintain the status quo. The historical evidence from the New World societies shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and influenced by a variety of factors, including power dynamics and distributional conflicts.
