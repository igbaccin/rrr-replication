The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the economic literature. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules, informal norms, and their enforcement, are critical in determining economic outcomes. The disagreement centers on how institutions influence economic growth, with some scholars asserting that institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, while others argue that institutions are secondary to other factors. For instance, some scholars contend that institutions shape long-run economic growth by determining the distribution of resources and opportunities, while others emphasize that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency. This debate is crucial because it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can inform the design of policies that promote sustainable economic development. The central question is: how do institutions shape long-run economic growth? One position maintains that the nature of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, is pivotal in determining economic outcomes. Another position argues that institutions matter by reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency. The debate is nuanced, with some scholars emphasizing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth through the distribution of resources and opportunities, while others focus on the efficiency-enhancing effects of institutions. This debate is far from settled, as evidenced by the diverse views on the role of institutions in economic growth. The key debate is whether institutions shape long-run economic growth primarily through their impact on resource distribution and opportunities, or through their efficiency-enhancing effects. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth. This commitment is evident in the development of financial instruments and the Bank of England, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) share a finding that the establishment of financial institutions and financial instruments can significantly lower transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th-century England reduced transaction costs dramatically and can be described as the English financial revolution. This revolution reflected an increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights should facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction. However, the aggregate volume of exchanges increases due to specialization and division of labor, resulting in a growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which highlights the political factors underpinning economic growth. Evidence from capital markets indicates that the institutional changes in 17th-century England provided a foundation for economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Colonial powers established extractive institutions in high-mortality regions, which persisted and shaped long-term economic trajectories. This institutional divergence is evident in the contrasting strategies employed by European colonizers. In regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. Conversely, in areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, inhibiting long-term development. The persistence of these institutions is crucial. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutional framework is the primary determinant of economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional framework established during colonialism not only shaped immediate economic conditions but also had lasting effects on the economic development of these regions. For example, the extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not encourage long-run growth, instead perpetuating economic stagnation. This institutional impact is evident in the varying levels of economic development observed in former colonies today.

This influence extended beyond economic policies, affecting social structures and cultural norms. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that women's contributions to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men were more involved in cash crop cultivation. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to privilege men. Women's economic activities, such as migration in search of farmland for cocoa, played a crucial role in economic development. For instance, in the Gold Coast, women's migration for cocoa farming contributed to the region becoming the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. Frankema (2012, p.15) further elaborates that the demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education. The formal education system introduced by the British was more conducive to development than other colonial powers. This altered the gender dynamics as well, as men and women were differently affected by the new social and political institutions. The implications of these gender dynamics and educational policies are profound, as they shaped the economic landscape of African regions. The new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. The codification of customary law under colonial rule had long-lasting effects on gender dynamics and economic development in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists to this day. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines social cohesion and cooperation, which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.19) supports this view by highlighting that the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period much longer than the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule. This prolonged period of exploitation and mistrust has had enduring effects on African economic development. The impact of the slave trade varies across Africa. The presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, mitigated some of the adverse effects of the slave trade. This variation in institutional strength across African regions suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is complex and intertwined with the pre-colonial political and economic institutions. These institutions, in turn, shaped the response to colonial rule and the subsequent economic trajectory of African regions.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) show. The accumulation of technological advancements, which Mokyr terms "useful knowledge," is a key factor in sustained economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who argues that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This knowledge, when applied effectively, can lead to significant economic improvements. The efficient use of these innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. Kuznets highlights that modern technology is not compatible with traditional rural modes of life or large family patterns, necessitating significant changes in societal structures. For instance, the introduction of new technologies often conflicts with existing social norms and political structures, requiring adjustments to resolve these conflicts peacefully. These adjustments are crucial for the sustainability of economic growth, as Mokyr notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements may be limited and ultimately unsustainable. Kuznets further emphasizes the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of a strong political fabric in supporting economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency concerns, are the key drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. These conflicts shape institutions by determining how power and resources are allocated among different groups in society, and these institutions, in turn, influence long-run economic outcomes. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that institutions should be treated as a crucial tool for economic historians. This focus on distributional conflicts is particularly relevant in the context of New World societies, where initial conditions and factor endowments led to extreme inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) point out that the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor in these societies resulted in high average living standards but also in stark wealth disparities. These disparities were exacerbated by institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had lasting effects on economic development. However, the persistence of these inequalities and their institutional underpinnings highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on economic growth. This is particularly evident in the case of Latin America, where historical inequalities have had profound and enduring effects on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, which specifies efficient property rights and provides effective enforcement, is crucial for reducing costs per transaction and increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast similarly emphasize the importance of credible commitment in economic growth, highlighting how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which underscores the significance of institutional changes in driving economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This shared finding (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) shows that institutions play a pivotal role in economic development by ensuring the enforcement of property rights and reducing transaction costs.

Distributional conflicts also play a significant role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups that sought to maximize their rents, often at the expense of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. further illustrate how extractive institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, persisted in former colonies and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had lasting effects on economic development, as seen in the case of Latin America, where historical inequalities have had profound and enduring effects on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical evidence from Latin America and other former colonies shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that it was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe further supports this by highlighting how distributional conflicts shaped economic institutions in ways that maximized rents for powerful groups, often at the expense of long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence of colonial institutions in Latin America and the interaction between institutions and industrialization in former colonies settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
