The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic in economic history and development economics, with scholars debating whether institutions are a primary driver of growth or if they are merely epiphenomenal, reflecting deeper forces. The disagreement centers around whether institutions cause growth or are themselves caused by other factors, such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that institutions are the fundamental cause of economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective governance foster investment and productivity. Some scholars argue that institutions are molded by fundamental economic and political forces, with their influence on growth being less direct. This debate is crucial because it informs policy recommendations and our understanding of economic development trajectories. The stakes are high: if institutions are the primary cause of growth, then institutional reform should be a priority for development efforts. If institutions merely mirror deeper forces, then prioritizing institutional reform could be misplaced. The question of whether institutions cause growth or are caused by other factors remains unresolved. This is where the debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth becomes particularly salient. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The pivotal role of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), underscores the enduring impact of secure property rights on economic development. This institutional evolution was driven by a political model where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues, addressing fiscal crises and establishing a foundation for long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specified and enforced property rights led to falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of market exchanges in the economy (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework was crucial for economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for investment and market exchanges. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which showed a remarkable commitment to secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of these institutions highlights the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The institutions that European settlers established in their colonies varied widely, from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states that rapidly transferred resources to the metropole. These institutions were not simply a consequence of geographic or climatic conditions; they were intentionally crafted to maximize benefits for European colonists. The long-run economic growth of these colonies was significantly shaped by the institutions that were put in place, which in turn were influenced by the mortality rates that Europeans faced in these regions. Colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were more likely to have extractive institutions, while those with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, were more likely to have institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This pattern resulted from deliberate European policies, which were influenced by the population density and wealth of the regions they colonized. This highlights the complex interplay between institutions, colonial policies, and long-run economic growth.

The institutional landscape of Africa was influenced by a combination of internal and external forces, as noted by Hopkins (2009, p.18); (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the significance of endogenous factors, such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation, in shaping African institutions. He argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective underscores the complex interplay between ethnic diversity and institutional quality. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) complement this view by emphasizing exogenous factors, such as colonial rule, in influencing African economic development. They reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. Their findings indicate that structural barriers, not enduring historical factors, have influenced African poverty. This implies that the institutional landscape in Africa has been profoundly influenced by both internal dynamics and external forces. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also observes that emphasizing the role of institutions allowed the new economic history of Africa to engage development specialists. This attention has led to a deeper understanding of how 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

Building on this, Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) provide detailed insights into how the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe influenced economic outcomes, offering a rich context for understanding historical economic phenomena. This is despite the fact that their focus is not directly on the long-run growth outcomes of modern economies. The mechanisms through which institutions operate in this context are complex. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments sometimes adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting a degree of institutional adaptability. This adaptability stemmed from both efficiency and distributional conflicts, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues. This adaptability and the influence of distributional conflicts suggest that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were responsive to a variety of factors beyond simple efficiency metrics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also points out that institutions are a crucial tool for every economic historian. This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, even if the focus is not on long-run growth. The nuanced understanding of institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe provides a framework for examining how institutions can influence economic development in various contexts, including those beyond Europe.

The adverse impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic development, as argued by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Nunn 2008, p.2), presents a counterpoint to institutional explanations for underdevelopment. Nunn and Wantchekon identify a culture of mistrust as a key mechanism through which the slave trade impeded economic development. They argue that the slave trade led to a persistent reduction in intergroup trust, which in turn hindered economic growth. This mistrust is shown to account for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, suggesting a significant impact on economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn supports this view by highlighting the extensive duration of Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, which far outlasted the period of colonial rule. This long-term exposure to the slave trade created enduring social and economic conditions that persistently affected development. This broader view underscores the complexity of economic development, where multiple historical factors interact to shape long-term outcomes.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both argue that technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth. Technological progress is fundamentally driven by the growth of useful knowledge. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, it is also dependent on a society's ability to adapt its institutions and ideologies to accommodate new technologies. Mokyr further qualifies this perspective by highlighting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, and without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that while technological progress is crucial, it must be supported by institutional changes to ensure long-term economic growth. For instance, Kuznets argues that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is essential for modern economic growth. Mokyr suggests that the growth of economic reasonableness is less consistent and irreversible than technological progress, showing that while technological progress drives economic growth, it is also affected by various external factors that can influence its sustainability.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the historical trajectories of nations. North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they define the rules that shape economic interactions. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is essential because it provides the necessary security for property rights, which in turn facilitates economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Austin agrees that institutions matter, but he also emphasizes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which means that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across different contexts (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.9) shows that institutions play a fundamental role in economic growth, but their effects are mediated by historical and contextual factors.

The specific types of institutions that are established also play a significant role in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of various institutions. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in other cases, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This difference in institutional frameworks had a significant impact on the long-run economic growth of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. also show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth, as the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on the economic development of these regions.

The persistence of these institutions over time is another critical factor in understanding their impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) shows that the persistence of institutions is closely tied to the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to the establishment of extractive institutions that hinder economic growth.
