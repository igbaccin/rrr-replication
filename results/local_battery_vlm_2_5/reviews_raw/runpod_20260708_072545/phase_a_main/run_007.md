The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, encompassing the rules and norms that govern social interactions, are widely recognised as critical factors in fostering economic development. The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain contentious. One prominent debate revolves around the extent to which institutions are shaped by exogenous factors, such as geography or culture, versus endogenous factors, such as political and economic dynamics. Some scholars argue that institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors, while others contend that endogenous processes play a more significant role. This debate has profound implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic growth and for designing policies aimed at promoting development. This live disagreement centres around the causal pathways through which institutions operate. Do institutions shape growth through their impact on property rights and investment, or are they merely a reflection of deeper cultural or geographical determinants? The resolution of this debate is crucial for understanding the fundamental drivers of economic growth and for informing policy interventions aimed at fostering development. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of the various factors at play. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, and that the type of institutions established in a society can significantly impact its long-run economic trajectory (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions provided a stable framework for economic activity, enabling long-term capital markets to develop and thrive. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impacts, as the same institutions that facilitate growth in one context may hinder it in another, depending on the underlying power dynamics and the specific design of the institutions.

North and Weingast, together with Ogilvie, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, particularly in the context of seventeenth-century England, was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast assert that the success of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on the regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange and the political factors underpinning this enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie echoes the importance of institutions, advocating for their integration into economic history research to better understand long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie's emphasis on distributional conflicts as a primary driver of economic institutions suggests a nuanced view of institutional evolution. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is determined by their formal structure and the broader social and political context in which they operate.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1)  argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, not the institutional framework (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements are the mainspring of economic growth. Technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are the primary driver, institutional adjustments play a supportive role in harnessing the full potential of technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that the political fabric of society remains strong (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not as direct as technological advancements but is still necessary for managing the social and political implications of economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial institutions and inequality played a pivotal role in shaping economic development by influencing the evolution of institutions that either facilitated or hindered growth. The New World societies with greater initial inequality, such as those in Latin America, saw institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional framework was shaped by the factor endowments and the social structures that emerged from the colonial period. These institutions, in turn, affected long-run economic growth by determining the security of property rights, the prevalence of corruption, and the structures of the financial sector (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). However, Nunn (2008, p.2; 2008, p.19) qualifies this by showing that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound adverse effect on economic development, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions was not uniform across Africa. The legacy of slavery and the subsequent colonial rule created a complex institutional landscape that continues to influence economic development in Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), challenge the persistent narrative of African poverty by examining historical economic performance and poverty levels before 1950. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon, and that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. By reconstructing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, they demonstrate that economic growth was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has limited our understanding of long-term economic development in Africa. This underscores the need for deeper historical analysis to grasp the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and to explore how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa.

The literature on colonialism and institutional reforms in Africa highlights the complexities of historical paths and the role of African initiatives in shaping institutional change (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin's work (2008) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and that colonial institutions were not uniformly imposed but adapted in response to local conditions. This adaptation mechanism is crucial as it shows that colonial institutions were not static but evolved in response to indigenous economic and political action. Austin illustrates this with the example of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to the typical hypothesis. This dynamic interaction suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depended on their ability to adapt to local contexts and incentives. Hopkins underscores this point by emphasizing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, highlighting the importance of understanding these historical dynamics for contemporary policy. However, Hopkins also notes that the concept of 'good' institutions, crucial for providing incentives for investment, must be carefully considered in the African context, where the relationship between institutions and economic development is complex and multifaceted.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by setting the rules that govern economic activity and determine the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutions, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced current income per capita in former colonies. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlights that the development of institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are key components of institutional frameworks that facilitate market exchanges and foster economic development.

The historical dynamics of institutional change are complex and multifaceted, with different paths leading to varied economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional contexts and how they evolve over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) further emphasizes that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, often drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2) shows that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which can lead to the establishment of extractive institutions that do not encourage economic development. The role of institutions in economic growth is therefore contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they operate.
