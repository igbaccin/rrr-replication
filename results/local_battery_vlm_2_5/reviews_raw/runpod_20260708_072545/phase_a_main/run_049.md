The central question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth has been a focal point of scholarly debate, with significant implications for understanding the dynamics of economic development. At the heart of this debate is a disagreement over the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are pivotal in facilitating long-term growth by providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. Others contend that the efficiency of institutions is often secondary to distributional conflicts and the political dynamics that shape them. Additionally, there is a rival perspective that emphasizes the role of cultural beliefs and shared values in determining institutional effectiveness. These differing views highlight the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within diverse historical and political contexts.

One stream of scholarship argues that institutions are critical in determining long-term economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity. Institutions, as described by North (1989, p.9), help reduce transaction costs and enforce property rights, which are essential for fostering economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) who note that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutional frameworks in European colonies, with significant implications for current income per capita. The extractive institutions imposed by Europeans in some colonies, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, differed from the more inclusive institutions established in settler colonies. These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes over the long term. By highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions, this stream of scholarship demonstrates that the legacy of historical institutional choices can persist and influence economic development for centuries.

This historical perspective is further supported by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, both driven by the need to address fiscal crises, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This decline, in turn, reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The political institutions that emerged during this period specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement. These institutions facilitated market exchanges by lowering transaction costs, which grew as a proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework was crucial for fostering economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for market exchanges and investment. However, North and Weingast note that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, but also highlight the critical political factor of regime commitment to these rules. This commitment underpins the stability and predictability necessary for sustained economic growth.

Building on this, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Kuznets 1973, p.1) show that institutions in societies with greater inequality have been shown to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism is evident in the New World societies, where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power was a persistent feature from the outset. The institutional framework that evolved in these societies reflected the interests of the dominant elite, who used their power to shape policies that maintained their privileged status. This institutional bias restricted competition and innovation, as the elite had little incentive to support policies that would foster broad-based economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) highlights the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that the stability and predictability provided by strong institutions are crucial for sustained economic growth. However, in societies with high inequality, the state's role is often compromised by the elite's influence, leading to institutions that perpetuate inequality rather than promoting inclusive growth. This institutional capture by elites can hinder the adoption of policies that would otherwise drive technological progress and economic growth, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates and the ease of settlement. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, rather than fostering long-term economic development. This institutional framework persisted, affecting current income per capita. These extractive institutions were not designed to maximize long-run growth but to maximize rents for European colonists, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. However, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not just about the type of institutions imposed, but also about the interplay between these institutions and local conditions. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth was not uniform but varied depending on the specific historical and economic context of each colony. Thus, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic trajectories, with some former colonies still grappling with the consequences of extractive institutions imposed centuries ago.

Extending this analysis, the literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision, including institutional change driven by both African initiatives and exogenous causes (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion by informing institutional reforms, while Frankema contends that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was not uniform but varied depending on the specific historical and economic context of each colony. For instance, the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for African populations (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, Simson notes that post-independence expenditure trends were influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments, indicating that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic trajectories (Simson 2019, p.2; Simson 2019, p.11). This complex interplay between colonial institutions and post-independence policies underscores the enduring influence of historical factors on African economic development.

The historical legacy of these institutions is further underscored by the profound and enduring impact of the slave trades in Africa, shaping societal norms and institutions in ways that persist to this day. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to hinder economic progress. This mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show, is not merely a reaction to the untrustworthiness of others but is rooted in internal norms that have been shaped by the historical experience of the slave trades. These internal norms of mistrust have significant implications for economic development, as they can undermine the social capital necessary for cooperative economic activity. This cultural legacy of mistrust, however, is not the only factor at play. Nunn (2008, p.19) points out that the economic and social institutions of pre-colonial African societies varied widely, with some kingdoms like the Kongo having well-developed markets and trading networks. This variation suggests that the impact of the slave trades on economic development was not uniform across Africa, and that the legacy of the slave trades interacts with pre-existing institutional structures in complex ways.

This historical context is crucial, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) show that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by a complex interplay of historical legacies and political dynamics. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) jointly demonstrate that the institutional environment was influenced by the political and economic incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This nuanced understanding of institutional change in pre-industrial Europe reveals that the adoption of institutions favourable for economic growth was not uniform across countries, and that the legacy of colonial rule interacted with pre-existing institutional structures in complex ways. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further illustrates that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, although he repudiates the game-theoretic view, according to which 'beliefs are institutions'. This suggests that the institutional environment was shaped by a complex interplay of historical legacies and political dynamics, and that the adoption of institutions favourable for economic growth was not uniform across countries. This nuanced understanding of institutional change in pre-industrial Europe reveals that the legacy of colonial rule interacted with pre-existing institutional structures in complex ways.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic actors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly impacted the long-term economic trajectories of colonies. The extractive institutions imposed in high-mortality regions, designed to maximize resource extraction for European benefit, persisted and hindered economic development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) reinforces this perspective, showing that the institutional legacies of colonial rule interacted with pre-existing structures in complex ways, shaping economic outcomes. This interaction highlights that the adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth was not uniform across countries. The legacy of colonial rule, therefore, was crucial in determining the institutional environment and subsequent economic performance of former colonies.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's (1989, p.9) (North 1989, p.6) emphasis on the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in Europe led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating economic growth. These institutions provided the necessary framework for long-term capital markets and economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) adds that shared beliefs and political dynamics significantly influence the institutional structure of a society. However, she also critiques the game-theoretic view that equates beliefs with institutions, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of how beliefs and institutions interact.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) highlights that the new economic history of Africa shows the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. The shared finding is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the institutional legacies of colonial rule were the primary determinant of economic outcomes or whether other factors, such as pre-existing institutional structures and political dynamics, played a more significant role (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their interaction with pre-existing structures in Africa shows that the legacy of colonial rule was crucial in determining the institutional environment and subsequent economic performance of former colonies.
