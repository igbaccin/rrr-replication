The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can illuminate pathways to prosperity and the underlying forces driving economic divergence. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth and those who propose that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographical determinants, play a more significant role. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy and development strategies. Institutions, according to one perspective, provide the framework for economic activity, shaping incentives and constraints that influence investment, innovation, and overall economic performance. Another perspective posits that institutions are shaped by broader cultural and geographical factors, which in turn influence economic growth. The interplay between these factors is complex, and understanding their relative importance is crucial for designing effective policies to promote economic development. This literature review will explore the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Understanding the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is essential for addressing the persistent disparities in economic development observed across regions and countries. North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the institutional evolution in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of the Bank of England in 1694 and the advent of new financial instruments marked the English financial revolution, significantly lowering transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional change was not merely an economic development but a political one, as it reflected a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The institutional framework that emerged from these political negotiations was crucial for economic growth, as it provided a foundation for a long-term capital market. This is evident in the evolution of English institutions, which facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development. The success of these institutions is underscored by the evidence from capital markets, which shows that the institutional changes in England were not merely revolutionary but also effective in promoting economic growth. Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The nature of these institutions varied widely depending on the colonization strategies employed by European powers, which in turn were influenced by factors such as mortality rates and the ease of settlement. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, which was closely tied to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. The institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape the economic landscapes of former colonies today.

The impact of colonialism and slavery on African development underscores how historical institutions shaped by inequality have influenced economic outcomes, as detailed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), Nunn (2008, p.2), and Frankema (2012, p.15). This institutional framework, which emerged from the colonial era, has had lasting effects on economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) finds that the slave trades in Africa, which spanned nearly 500 years, significantly impacted economic development. The prolonged and extensive nature of these trades disrupted social and political institutions, creating conditions that hindered economic growth. The disruption of African societies through slavery was both a demographic and a profound institutional shock. This disruption was compounded by the fact that the slave trades occurred over a prolonged period, allowing for deep-seated institutional changes that were difficult to reverse. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the impact of colonial rule varied significantly across different regions, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education in Africa was influenced by new social and political institutions introduced by colonial powers, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions, particularly those related to education, continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa today.

The work of Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenges the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by examining historical economic performance and poverty levels before 1950. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, contra recent literature emphasizing such impediments. The authors find that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding is supported by the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, which enables a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. The data reveals that the growth of export production was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, highlighting the interconnectedness of global economic forces and local economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The authors explore the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which is crucial for understanding the distribution of income and the long-term picture of economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) identifies a specific mechanism that colonial administrations were responsive to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This responsiveness led to changes in institutional prescriptions, such as Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This distributional conflict perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by the power dynamics and interests of different groups within society, not just by economic efficiency or responsiveness to incentives. The implication is that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is more complex and multifaceted than simple efficiency arguments suggest.

The growth of useful knowledge, as a primary driver of economic growth, is highlighted by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight that technological advancements are fundamental to long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are key drivers of economic growth, they must be supported by a robust institutional framework to ensure long-term sustainability.
