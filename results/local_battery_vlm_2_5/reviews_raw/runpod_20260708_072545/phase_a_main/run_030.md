The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some nations prosper while others stagnate, and whether the roots of these disparities lie in the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity. One live disagreement centres on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they define and enforce property rights, thereby fostering investment and economic activity. Others contend that the impact of institutions on growth is contingent on historical and cultural contexts, and that institutions themselves are shaped by underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Another disagreement is whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and the extent to which they can be deliberately designed to promote development. The debate is further complicated by the challenge of identifying the specific institutional arrangements that are most conducive to growth, and whether these arrangements are universally applicable or context-specific. This review will explore these disagreements by examining how scholars have conceptualised the role of institutions in economic growth, and the evidence they have marshalled to support their claims. The review will show that there is a consensus that institutions matter for growth, but that the mechanisms through which they operate and the conditions under which they are effective remain hotly contested. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) share this posture (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) suggest that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies depending on mortality rates, and these institutions significantly influenced current income per capita. The presence of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand is a key mechanism through which these institutions promoted economic growth. Extractive institutions in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast impeded economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, were not conducive to long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and do not encourage economic development. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex and depends on the specific context and the interests of the politically powerful groups.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They contend that the political factors underpinning economic growth, including the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and the enforcement of those rules, were pivotal. Evidence from capital markets supports this, as the political changes in England facilitated the development of markets and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) elaborates on this by suggesting that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that cultural beliefs play a significant role in the formation of institutions, which challenges the notion that institutions are solely determined by political factors. This suggests that while political institutions are crucial, they are not the only determinants of economic growth. Instead, a complex interplay of political, cultural, and economic factors shapes the institutional environment and, consequently, long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a perspective on the role of technological innovation in driving economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that technological progress requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that technological progress involves the diffusion of new ideas through society, which in turn demands institutional and ideological adaptations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) extends this idea by highlighting the importance of coordination failures and the role of institutions in rectifying them. He suggests that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological innovation is crucial, it must be supported by appropriate institutional frameworks to fully realize its economic benefits. Kuznets (1973, p.1) acknowledges that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that the state's role in managing technological change and institutional adjustments is vital for sustained economic growth.

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) show that the economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not follow a consistent path when assessed through wage data and urbanization rates. The shift in economic development to countries bordering the North Sea, initially Holland and later England, led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift highlights a key pattern: regional economic disparities and structural changes, such as urbanization, were significant drivers of economic development during this period. These structural changes were not uniformly beneficial; they led to varying outcomes across different regions. For instance, while Holland experienced rapid economic growth due to its institutional flexibility, Tuscany faced stagnation due to its rigid monopolistic structures (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Pamuk (2007, p.16) notes that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that significantly influenced economic development. These changes led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, indicating that institutional flexibility and urbanization rates were critical factors in shaping economic outcomes. The institutional environment, therefore, played a pivotal role in determining how regions responded to economic opportunities and challenges. This suggests that while technological progress and institutional frameworks were crucial, the specific structural changes and institutional settings in different regions were equally important in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) both demonstrate that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. The British colonial administration established institutions that facilitated the spread of formal education, altering the perceived benefits of education for African political leaders and families. The implementation of these institutions, which included the establishment of schools and the training of teachers, was a key factor in the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. These institutions also played a role in shaping the economic outcomes of the region, as they provided Africans with the skills and knowledge necessary to participate in the colonial economy. This institutional framework was not uniform across all colonial powers, as other powers did not establish similar institutions or provide the same level of support for formal education. This variation in institutional frameworks led to different economic outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa, with British colonies generally experiencing higher levels of economic growth and development than other colonies. This is consistent with the finding that British rule was more conducive to economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. This institutional framework also had implications for the long-run economic growth of the region, as it provided a foundation for future economic development. The role of British colonial institutions in fostering formal education in sub-Saharan Africa highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provided the necessary framework for economic development and growth. This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) agree that colonial institutions have shaped African economies in ways that are not directly tied to long-run economic growth. Colonial rule in Africa was a complex process that varied across regions and periods. This variation led to different institutional outcomes, which in turn influenced economic development. For example, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations responded to local economic and political actions, adjusting their policies to incentivize indigenous production. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth depended on local contexts and adaptations. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to have a material interest in the status quo, leading to institutional changes that supported economic activity. Hopkins (2009, p.18) elaborates on this by noting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the broader institutional framework. This framework, shaped by colonial rule, has enduring effects on economic outcomes. The relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth is complex. Austin (2008, p.2) points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic development. This complexity is further highlighted by the fact that institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. This variability underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have influenced African economies. The new economic history of Africa, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues, can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) contend that African economic history is profoundly shaped by factors such as gender roles and the legacy of the slave trade, which influence long-run economic growth through social and cultural mechanisms. Women's contributions to economic growth in Africa were significant during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and their access to land for cash crops was limited by colonial rule's codification of customary law, which privileged men. This codification reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, further marginalizing women's economic roles. The slave trade also had a lasting impact on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of its reduced-form effect on trust. This mistrust is rooted in both external environmental factors and internal norms, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one. The implications of this mistrust are vast, affecting economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The exact mechanisms by which these historical factors influence contemporary economic outcomes require further investigation. This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how historical and social factors, such as gender roles and the legacy of the slave trade, continue to shape African economies.
