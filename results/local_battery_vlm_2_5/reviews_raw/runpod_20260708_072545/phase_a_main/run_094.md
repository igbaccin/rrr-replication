The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central question in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive economic outcomes. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies progress economically over extended periods. The debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural traits, play a more significant role. One position emphasises the critical role of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic development, while another highlights the influence of external factors that shape institutional frameworks. The interplay between these factors is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions are shaped by external forces, while others contend that institutions themselves can influence these forces. This literature review explores these dynamics, focusing on how institutions have been shown to influence long-run economic growth. The central theme is the causal relationship between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, with a particular emphasis on how different types of institutions can either foster or hinder economic development. The establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This process was underpinned by the emergence of political institutions that specified "efficient" property rights and provided increasingly effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). The Bank of England's creation in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution increased the security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change was pivotal, as it ensured that the regime was committed to and bound by the rules governing economic exchange. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which reflects the broader economic performance. However, the institutional changes in England were not unique; recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought. This implies that while institutions are crucial, the specific institutional arrangements may not be as determinative as initially believed.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The specific strategies employed by European colonizers were not uniform but varied significantly based on mortality rates and the economic potential of the colonies. In regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic growth. Conversely, in regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The differences in institutional frameworks led to divergent economic outcomes, with extractive institutions in high-mortality regions resulting in lower current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions has had a lasting impact on the economic development of these regions. The variation in colonial strategies underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. However, the long-term effects of these institutions are not solely determined by their initial design but are also influenced by subsequent economic and political developments. This dynamic further complicates the relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth, as the initial institutional framework can evolve over time in response to changing economic and political conditions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by examining the specific historical and economic contexts in Africa, arguing that previous characterizations oversimplify causation and misrepresent economic performance, thereby complicating the narrative of colonial impact. This thesis often overlooks the dynamic nature of colonial institutions, which could adapt in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, colonial administrations seeking fiscal security might adjust their institutional frameworks to align with indigenous economic interests. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that these economies were more robust than previously thought. This challenges the notion that African economies were uniformly stagnant or underdeveloped during the colonial period. The dynamic interplay between colonial institutions and local economic conditions reveals that African economies were not merely passive recipients of external forces but actively shaped their own trajectories. This dynamic interplay underscores the importance of considering local agency and institutional adaptation in understanding long-run economic growth in Africa.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) present evidence that the British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, providing a more nuanced view of colonial impacts on African education systems, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes. This structured approach to education under British rule laid the groundwork for post-colonial educational systems, which continue to influence African economies today. Nevertheless, the extent to which colonial education systems have contributed to long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustained growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world and required significant institutional and ideological adjustments. This implies that while technological advancements are essential, they must be supported by appropriate institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) also recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them. This underscores the importance of not only technological progress but also the need for institutional mechanisms to facilitate economic growth. The growth of economic reasonableness, while less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is nonetheless crucial for sustaining economic improvements. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is complex, with each reinforcing the other to drive long-term economic growth.

This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating economic exchanges and supporting long-term capital markets.
