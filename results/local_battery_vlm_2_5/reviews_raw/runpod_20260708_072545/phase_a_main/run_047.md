Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms by which they operate remain a subject of intense debate. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement over whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and culture, or whether they are endogenous to economic and political processes. One position argues that institutions are shaped by the economic and political incentives facing powerful groups, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. These institutions, in turn, influence economic growth by determining the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Another position, however, posits that institutions are largely the result of historical and cultural factors, which can either facilitate or hinder economic development. This disagreement has significant implications for understanding the roots of economic growth and for designing effective policies to promote development. The stakes are high because the answer to this question can inform policies aimed at fostering economic growth and reducing poverty. A comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in economic growth requires a nuanced examination of the complex interplay between historical, cultural, and economic factors. The debate between Acemoglu et al. and Austin highlights the importance of considering the historical context in which institutions develop. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution were pivotal in declining the cost of transacting and securing long-term property rights. This security was crucial for the development of a long-term capital market, which in turn drove economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) describe the English financial revolution as a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This institutional change involved economic exchange and the enforcement and alteration of rules. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges. This was evident in the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, highlighting the critical role of political factors in underpinning economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The nature of these institutions depended on the European settlers' mortality rates in the colonies. Where mortality rates were high, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic growth. In colonies with lower mortality rates, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, promoting economic development. This pattern of institutional development, driven by settler mortality rates, highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that these institutional differences continue to affect current income per capita, underscoring the long-run impact of colonial institutions. This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions were pivotal, their impact on economic growth was mediated by the specific interests and power dynamics of the colonizing powers.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the conventional view that these institutions were shaped by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions, which appear inefficient, can be understood as the outcome of power struggles between different groups in society. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further argues that these institutions were shaped by the interests of powerful elites who used them to maintain their control and extract rents. The persistence of these institutions, despite their apparent inefficiency, can be attributed to the ability of elites to enforce them through coercion and manipulation of the legal system. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to New World societies, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective suggests that the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth is a function of their efficiency and the power dynamics and distributional conflicts that shape them. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that these institutions evolved over time in response to changing economic and political conditions.

Austin and Hopkins, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is more complex than a simple cause-and-effect relationship. One key mechanism in this process is the role of indigenous economic and political action in shaping institutional responses. The interests of colonial governments and the actions of indigenous populations created a dynamic interplay that influenced institutional outcomes. Austin notes that when European settlers were not present, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive process shows that institutions evolved in response to changing economic and political conditions. The role of colonial governments in shaping institutions varied across Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has limited our understanding of long-term economic development in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth may vary significantly depending on the historical and geographical context, and that a deeper understanding of these contexts is necessary to fully appreciate the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa had a profound impact on its long-term economic development, creating a culture of mistrust that persists to this day and significantly affecting institutional structures (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade's impact on trust is a deeply ingrained internal norm of mistrust. This mistrust significantly impedes economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. The slave trade also disrupted African societies' institutional structures, leading to the erosion of centralized governance and well-developed markets. Nunn shows that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that the disruption of such institutions by the slave trade had lasting effects on Africa's economic development. The legacy of the slave trade influences contemporary economic outcomes through its enduring impact on social and institutional structures.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) agree that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that the accumulation of technological knowledge is the primary determinant of long-run economic growth. Mokyr explains that technological progress, which is the accumulation of useful knowledge, drives economic growth. Kuznets further elucidates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) illustrates that the source of technological progress, the particular production sectors that it affected most, and the pace at which it and economic growth advanced, differed over centuries and among regions of the world. These differences highlight the importance of technological progress in shaping long-run economic growth. The implications of technological progress are significant, as it necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.6) qualifies that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs and without a long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. This qualification underscores the importance of institutional stability in sustaining economic growth driven by technological progress.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and enforcement of property rights, as well as the overall political and economic stability of a society. North argues that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who note that the presence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The stability of institutions is further highlighted by North, who points out that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional stability is essential for sustaining economic growth driven by technological progress, as it ensures that growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society (North 1989, p.9). Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is paramount, as they provide the framework necessary for economic development and stability. Institutions actively shape the economic landscape through their impact on property rights and political stability.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly influenced the long-run development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy has persisted and continues to affect the economic outcomes of these regions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by the work of Hopkins, who notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a lasting impact on the economic development of former colonies, underscoring the importance of understanding the institutional legacy of colonialism in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their impact on property rights and political stability, as well as the legacy of colonial institutional changes. North shows that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they provide the framework necessary for economic activity and stability (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical evidence and empirical comparisons of income per capita across regions show that institutional stability and the development of efficient property rights are crucial for long-run economic growth.
