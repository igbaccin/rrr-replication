The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in the study of economic development. At stake is the explanation for persistent economic disparities between countries, and the identification of policies that can foster sustainable growth. Institutions matter in economic growth and development, but the precise mechanisms by which they operate remain a subject of intense debate. A key point of disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the extractive nature of institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, is crucial in determining economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is primarily due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and geographic factors play a lesser role. A further perspective posits that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Understanding these debates is essential for grasping how institutions influence economic growth over the long term.

A common finding is that institutions evolve in response to political and economic pressures (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different institutions based on mortality rates, with extractive states in high-mortality areas and institutions enforcing the rule of law in low-mortality areas. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on current income per capita. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. point out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not to promote long-run growth. This perspective underscores the importance of political power dynamics in shaping institutions. North and Weingast also emphasize the role of institutions in economic growth, noting that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. Their work shows that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, and that the degree to which the regime is committed to these rules is a critical political factor. This commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989 (North 1989, p.9); North and Weingast 1989 (North and Weingast 1989, p.29)). This financial revolution was pivotal in enhancing the security of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The Bank of England's establishment and the development of new financial instruments facilitated market exchanges by specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement. These developments reflect the broader political and economic changes that underpinned England's economic growth during this period. The significance of these institutions is underscored by the remarkable success of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the institutional changes' impact. This institutional framework governed economic exchange and ensured that the rules were enforced and could be changed, highlighting the critical role of political commitment in economic growth. The success of these institutions in fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs was a key factor in England's economic growth, as evidenced by the development of capital markets.

Building on this foundation, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and (2002) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) extend the analysis to colonial contexts, where European policies shaped institutions that had lasting impacts on economic trajectories. This influence was primarily mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, which persisted and affected current income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to foster long-run growth. The European settlers established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they encountered. In colonies with low mortality rates, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up with the intention of rapidly transferring resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) further support this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all align with the economic outcomes predicted by their model. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) qualify this by emphasizing the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, highlighting that the long-run development of former colonies was centrally influenced by this interaction. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact is modulated by other economic factors, such as industrialization opportunities.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that these historical inequalities, rooted in institutional frameworks, have enduring effects that continue to shape economic outcomes over generations. This is evident in the New World societies, where institutions that favored elites through various government policies restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. The legacy of these institutions is seen in the contrasting growth records of Latin America and North America, with Latin America's institutions reinforcing inequality and North America's institutions fostering more inclusive growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this perspective by exploring how the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust, a product of historical inequalities, has significantly impacted economic development by eroding social capital and undermining trust in institutions. Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this by noting that the impact of the slave trade on economic development is not uniform across Africa. The presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets in some regions, such as the Kongo kingdom, mitigated the adverse effects of the slave trade. This variation underscores the complex interplay between historical inequalities, institutions, and economic outcomes, highlighting that the institutional impacts of slavery and inequality are not monolithic but vary based on local conditions and historical contexts.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule played a significant role in shaping African education systems and economic development (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). These findings underscore the importance of colonial institutions in driving economic development, as the growth of export production in African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify these effects by noting that the persistence of poverty in Africa cannot be attributed solely to structural impediments, suggesting that the long-term impact of colonial institutions on economic development is more complex than previously thought (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This complexity indicates that while British colonial rule had positive effects on education and economic development, the overall impact on African welfare was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies historical causation by compressing distinct periods and paths, which leads to a flawed understanding of the institutional evolution. The thesis does not account for the complexities of local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions that shape economic outcomes. The shared beliefs and institutions of a society are part of its institutional structure, but the thesis does not fully explain why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not in others. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to have a material interest in the status quo, which led to changes in institutional prescriptions. This contrasts with the thesis' assumption that colonial institutions were uniformly imposed and did not adapt to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution that takes into account the complex interplay of local factors and distributional conflicts.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasize that beyond institutions, the relentless march of technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge are the ultimate engines driving sustained economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, while Kuznets highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. This perspective underscores the importance of coordination between technological and institutional advancements in driving economic growth. This variation suggests that the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is complex and context-dependent.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) highlight the critical role of institutions in shaping economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonies with high mortality rates established extractive institutions, which significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North underscores that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Both perspectives emphasize that the nature of institutions, whether they enforce the rule of law or are extractive, directly influences long-term economic outcomes. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9) illustrates that the institutional framework set by colonial powers had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of their former colonies. The institutional legacies of colonialism, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) show, are central to understanding the economic disparities observed today.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) complicate this narrative by introducing the role of historical context and distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the institutional evolution and economic growth are more complex and context-dependent (Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, and not efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The importance of historical context and distributional conflicts, as shown by Austin and Ogilvie, means that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across different historical periods and regions. The institutional frameworks that emerged from colonialism, as well as the conflicts and historical contexts that shaped them, are crucial for understanding the long-term economic outcomes of former colonies. This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) shows that the institutional environment is not just a product of colonial policies but also a result of the interplay between historical contexts and distributional conflicts.

Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as evidenced by the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The role of credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise measurement of the economic outcomes of former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29), reveals that the institutional frameworks set by colonial powers, and the credibility of their commitments, are central to understanding the long-term economic trajectories of these regions. The empirical comparison of economic outcomes among former colonies, as well as the measurement of institutional quality and credible commitments, settles the disagreement on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
