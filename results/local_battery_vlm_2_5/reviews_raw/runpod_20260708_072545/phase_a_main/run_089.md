The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. It is at stake whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. One position argues that institutions are endogenous, shaped by economic and political forces, and thus influence economic growth through credible commitment mechanisms (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Another position posits that institutions are exogenous, determined by factors such as geography or culture, and that they directly impact economic outcomes. The interplay between these views underscores the complexity of understanding how institutions function within economic systems, and how they might be harnessed to promote growth. This interplay is particularly evident in the context of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This was achieved through the creation of new financial instruments that increased the security of property rights, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The Bank of England's establishment was a response to fiscal crises, reflecting a broader trend in political and economic institutions that facilitated market exchanges by specifying and enforcing property rights. This institutional change led to a decline in transaction costs, which in turn supported economic growth by enabling more efficient market operations. North and Weingast further emphasize that the success of these institutions depended on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange, highlighting the political underpinnings of economic growth. They argue that the thesis's focus on institutional factors, such as colonial administrations and the slave trade, overlooks the complex interplay of historical and cultural factors that shape economic outcomes. One specific mechanism through which these factors operate is the development of a culture of mistrust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it affects both internal norms and external environments, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The narrative of persistent poverty in Africa is challenged by historical evidence on economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner, alongside Frankema and van Waijenburg (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), assert that poverty has not been a constant feature of Africa's economic history. This perspective is supported by empirical data showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The economic growth in these regions was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. This dynamic was influenced by a variety of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in the timing and scale of export production growth underscores the complex interplay between economic and institutional factors. Long-term economic development in Africa, including income distribution, continues to be a subject of ongoing investigation. This is crucial for understanding the potential for future growth and the impact of historical and geographical conditions on African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The availability of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence has facilitated a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history, highlighting the need for deeper analyses of real wage trends and the determinants of economic divergence across the region.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the legacy of colonialism is a critical factor in shaping the long-run economic growth of former colonies. The institutions established during colonial rule, particularly those that were extractive in nature, have had lasting impacts on current income per capita. This is apparent in regions where Europeans established institutions aimed at maximizing resource extraction, fostering long-term economic development. For instance, in colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, which prioritized the interests of colonial powers over local economic development, have persisted and continue to influence economic outcomes. This institutional framework, shaped by the political and economic interests of colonial powers, has led to a divergence in economic performance among former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions, which did not encourage investment or economic growth, has resulted in a persistent economic underdevelopment in many regions. This underscores the importance of understanding the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic growth. The persistence of these institutions highlights the need for policies that can address the historical legacies of colonial rule and promote more inclusive and growth-oriented institutions. The mechanisms through which these institutions affect economic growth are complex and multifaceted, involving factors such as property rights, rule of law, and the efficiency of the judiciary.

Technological progress, fueled by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary engine of economic growth, as argued by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that technological progress is not compatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns, necessitating significant societal changes to integrate new technologies effectively. These conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs to maintain the political fabric of society, allowing for modern economic growth. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) shows that without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting technological innovations, even if technological progress itself is the primary driver of economic growth.

The institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations, as argued by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). These authors highlight that the core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts within society. This perspective challenges the traditional view that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea by showing how extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power shaped the institutions that evolved in New World societies. The implication is that institutions are not merely tools for economic efficiency but are also instruments of power and control, reflecting the underlying social and political dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) advises against viewing institutions as mere fashion accessories, stressing the importance of a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within the broader socio-economic context. This nuanced understanding is crucial for appreciating the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth.
